# A laser cutting snapshot

[TARİH: 01.12.2018 The Fabricator]

A Deep Dive The Operations

Laser cutting, metal fabrication’s irreplaceable process, offers unprecedented levels of productivity. If a technician who left the industry 15 years ago returned today to operate a new solid-state laser cutting machine, he or she very likely would peer through the green-tinted enclosure and stand agog and amazed by the speed and ease of use that the newest laser machines offer. But then he’d look to the offload stations and see a familiar sight: operators shaking, breaking, and manually sorting part after part after part.

If you’re looking to buy a laser, you can find a spec sheet and look into its features. And then you have best practices for machine nesting, operation, and maintenance, information for which is all readily available.

What is not so easy to find is a resource that details the entire laser cutting process, start to finish, from order entry to secondary processing. A fabricator can have the fastest laser around, but all that cutting horsepower really is just a single piece to a much more complicated puzzle. Looking at the complete picture, you have everything from order entry to programming and nesting to material handling to the actual cutting process and, finally, sorting and transporting those cut parts to the next operation downstream.

How do orders flow, from order entry to nesting and programming, then into and out of the machine, and on to the next downstream operation? That’s what this study aims to find out by diving deep into five operations. We reached out to a selection of companies representing a true cross section of the business: OEMs, contract fabrication, and job shops. They serve different geographic areas, work with different customers, and have very different business models.

These companies weren’t chosen because they have certain levels of automation or software technology. In fact, you’ll notice that no fabricator here has a material handling tower or automated storage and retrieval system (though one plans to invest in one soon).

The study does have one big takeaway, and it’s one that has significant potential for improved throughput. It has to do with part sorting, or de-nesting. In one sense, a laser cutting operation isn’t "finished" until the cut parts reach the next operation downstream. Parts flow quickly from an automated storage system to a cutting table, then to an offloading table—where workers grasp, shake, even hammer the cut parts out of the nest. Deburring is another challenge. For instance, some parts may require a rounded edge that the laser just can’t produce. Those cut parts are then sent through a secondary machine that, if it’s slower than all other processes in the cutting department, governs the throughput.

If anything, this study shows that while it’s easy to point out best practices when it comes to machine maintenance and operation, the same can’t be said of part flow.

The five customers covered here represent a cross section of the fabrication business. Franke Foodservice Systems cuts components for new food service equipment and to replenish the company’s spare parts inventory. General Stamping & Metalworks offers a range of services, but its sweet spot lies in contract work and repeat orders. Sureway Tool & Engineering services various industries too, but it cut its teeth on the point of purchase business. On opposite sides of the country, MillerMetal Fabrication and Richards Sheet Metal Works are custom fabricators that serve a wide variety of customers, from piece parts to complex assemblies.

Imagine a laser cutting operator who happens to jump from one of these companies to another. The machines would be similar. In fact, all five offer solid-state laser cutting, a testament to how quickly the industry has adopted the technology. Outside the machine itself, though, operators would find the details of their workday to be quite different. One company uses printed paper travelers to manage work-in-process; another uses tablet computers. One company brings all cut nests to a central location for sorting and scrap disposal; another sorts parts and skeletons directly off of the laser worktable after cutting. Some companies have jib cranes and suction hoists for loading raw material; others have automated material loading and unloading equipment.

The operators also would find some companies in transition, particularly when it comes to software. Some use homegrown systems, from estimating software built in-house to Excel spreadsheets for managing inventory, while others are moving away from legacy systems to more automatic software solutions. The ability to manage orders and manufacturing capacity rapidly and transparently from the front office is often the driving force behind these efforts. In particular, most shops have automated nesting to some degree, but how those orders are pulled from enterprise resource planning (ERP) varies greatly. Most companies seem to be moving away from manual order management and toward more automatic connections between ERP and CAD/CAM.

In essence, the operator would see similar machine tools but different material handling methods and, most significantly, a wide variety of strategies for managing work flow and information.
