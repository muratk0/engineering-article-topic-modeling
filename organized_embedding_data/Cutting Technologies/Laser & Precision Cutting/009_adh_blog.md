# Controlling Dross in Fiber-Laser Cutting of Stainless Steel

## Confirm System Suitability Before Process Tuning

A visible line on the top surface does not demonstrate that a stainless sheet can be cut cleanly through its full thickness. Increasing power alone can widen the kerf, increase discoloration, and produce more molten material than the assist-gas jet can remove.

Before processing, verify that the machine is approved for the selected stainless grade and thickness. Through-cutting depends on the complete system, including the cutting head, optics, nozzle, height control, assist-gas system, motion system, cutting bed, enclosure, extraction, and fire-control arrangements.

A fiber marking machine is not necessarily suitable for sheet cutting. Marking equipment may lack the controlled gas delivery, stand-off control, cutting bed, and other safeguards required to manage molten material during through-cuts. Do not improvise cutting applications outside the machine manufacturer's approved use.

Confirm the actual sheet thickness, grade, finish, flatness, and condition against the applicable cutting documentation. Near a system's practical limit, the acceptable range for speed, focus, gas delivery, and consumable condition may become narrow. A one-time separation does not establish a repeatable low-dross process.

Keep the cutting area free of combustible scrap and accumulated slag, secure the workpiece, maintain required fire-response provisions, and do not leave cutting unattended. Verify that extraction operates effectively under cutting conditions. Stainless processing produces airborne contaminants that must be captured.

## Balance Heat Input and Melt Ejection

A clean cut requires both continuous melting through the kerf and effective downward removal of molten material. Dross forms when molten material freezes on the lower edge instead of being ejected.

Power and travel speed influence heat input per unit length. More power or slower movement may improve penetration when the process lacks energy, but either change can also create excessive molten material. Signs of excessive heat may include a rounded upper edge, a wide kerf, strong heat tint, and heavy lower-edge deposits.

Focus also affects melt removal. For stainless steel, the focal point may be positioned within the material rather than directly at the top surface, depending on the approved cutting procedure. Its position influences kerf shape and the path through which gas and molten metal move.

Assist-gas performance cannot be judged by pressure alone. Nozzle geometry, aperture condition, centering, stand-off, supply restrictions, and pressure stability affect the jet at the cut. Use documented settings and approved diagnostics rather than changing multiple variables at once.

Dross concentrated at corners, small contours, or lead-outs may indicate a motion-related condition rather than a poor straight-line recipe. Deceleration can increase heat input per unit length. Use approved machine functions for corners and contours where available, rather than globally weakening a stable cutting condition.

## Establish a Controlled Starting Condition

Begin with the complete manufacturer-approved cutting-chart entry for the exact machine configuration, stainless grade, thickness, gas, nozzle, focus, stand-off, pressure, speed, and pierce method. Do not combine settings from different material thicknesses or process rows.

Before evaluating speed, confirm the process foundation: correct gas for the required edge condition, stable supply under flow, specified nozzle, correct stand-off, documented focus, and acceptable condition of authorized consumables and optics. Gas selection affects edge chemistry. Nitrogen may be used where an oxide-free edge is required, while oxygen or compressed air can produce different edge conditions. The selected gas and all associated settings must follow an approved recipe.

Static regulator pressure does not confirm delivery during cutting. Supply problems can arise from leaks, restrictions, contaminated filters, unstable equipment, or partially closed valves. Gas-system inspection, purging, leak testing, and any pressure-related work must follow the machine documentation and be performed by qualified personnel using approved procedures.

Replace damaged, distorted, contaminated, or spatter-covered nozzles. A nozzle strike, unstable centering result, or suspected optical contamination should trigger an approved inspection and service process. Do not attempt unapproved alignment, optical maintenance, calibration, or repair.

## Tune and Evaluate Methodically

Use representative test coupons and change one approved variable at a time. A practical sequence is to compare nearby approved speed settings, then evaluate nearby approved focus positions, then make limited approved gas-delivery adjustments. Adjust power or pulse-related controls only within the applicable cutting documentation.

Keep other conditions unchanged during each comparison, including material condition, gas, nozzle, stand-off, pierce program, and test geometry. Record the baseline and each test condition so results can be traced.

Treat piercing separately from continuous cutting. Piercing concentrates energy at one location and can create spatter or an incomplete opening that resembles a cutting-speed problem. Repeated abnormal piercing, popping, or visible spatter near the cutting head requires a pause and inspection under authorized procedures.

Evaluate the whole cut, not only the underside. Observe separation, upper-edge rounding, spatter, lower-edge deposits, cut-face appearance, striations, kerf width, oxidation color, and consistency between repeated coupons. Repeat promising conditions at another sheet location and on representative geometry that includes corners, curves, and lead-outs.

## Respond to Instability and Preserve the Recipe

Dross is a symptom, not a complete diagnosis. Record where it occurs and whether it follows a straight cut, corner, lead-in, pierce, sheet location, or travel direction. Separate causes should not be corrected with a single global setting change.

If identical tests alternate between clean and failed results, stop tuning the recipe and investigate unstable inputs. Possible areas include gas delivery, consumable condition, authorized optical condition, height control, centering, material condition, and machine capacity. Do not bypass interlocks, alarms, pressure limits, or service limits.

When a verified baseline remains inconsistent, or when the material and configuration are not covered by approved cutting documentation, consult qualified service or application personnel. Provide the recorded recipe, material details, observed alarms, maintenance findings, and images of both cut faces.

For repeatable production, save the successful process as a complete setup record rather than only a power-and-speed value. Record material identification and thickness, gas specification, nozzle condition, focus, stand-off, speed, power-related settings, pierce method, machine and program revision, and acceptance result. Revalidate the process after changes to material, gas supply, nozzles, optics, alignment, focus references, or machine maintenance.

A low-dross result depends on a controlled relationship among heat input, focal position, travel speed, kerf geometry, and gas-driven melt removal. All adjustments, maintenance, and diagnostics should remain within the machine manual and authorized procedures.
