T

he sheet metal business has long-established production models for low-quantity, high-mix part flow, with parts moving from the laser cutting and punching department to bending, welding, and so on. Stamping covers the opposite end of the spectrum—low mix, high volume. But what about the spectrum in between low and high, both in volume and product mix? And is dedicated tooling still the only practical alternative for high-volume sheet metal work?

Here is where coil-fed operations—including laser cutting and punching—can play a role. For insight, The Fabricator spoke with Andrea Dallan, CEO of Italian company Dallan S.p.A and author of a book, "The Revolution of Efficiency," in which he takes a broad perspective on the subject. True efficiency, he argues, moves more value (completed jobs) and less waste (scrap) out the door in less time. Metrics like inches and strokes per minute matter, but they need to be put within the context of dock-to-dock part flow.

Coil-Fed Cutting Overview

Conventional cut-to-length lines cut sheets from coil and feed them to an inventory buffer held either at the service center or fabricator (usually both). A manufacturer with in-house cut-to-length capability can cut sheets to varying lengths, but that cut-sheet inventory buffer usually remains.

Coil-fed laser cutting eliminates that inventory buffer. Perhaps the best-known form of this technology is

laser blanking

, which has its roots serving automakers wanting to replace a conventional blanking press with high-speed laser cutting. The technology gives automakers and other high-volume manufacturers the flexibility to design blanks optimized for forming on a stamping press downstream. In certain setups, the lasers cut the blanks and destroy the surrounding skeleton. The blanks then are offloaded and stacked using robots or another mechanical system.

Another kind of coil-fed parts cutting stems from (among other things) the need to cut extremely long parts that won’t fit on a conventional punch or laser cutting bed. Optimal material yield is another driver. Parts can be nested to fit any sheet length, minimizing excessively large web sections. Nests can have identical parts or perhaps different parts that comprise a kit for downstream assembly.

"Products processed in these coil-fed setups are typically long and narrow, often rectangular," Dallan said. "Exceptions exist, of course. But as always, you need to find the right technology for the kind of parts you are processing and the level of flexibility you need."

Programming

Typical applications involve static nests with standard products. Dallan added, however, that many manufacturers with this kind of coil-fed cutting technology have standard products, like kitchen hoods, but with infinitely variable sizes within a specified range.

"These applications can have a dynamic programming environment where CAD software can produce a kit of parts that can be different every time," Dallan said. "From here, the machine software takes DXF files and creates a nest optimized to save as much material as possible."

In other cases, parametric programming can automate the front-end process entirely. Dallan described a door fabrication example where the company wrote a script to create a "master" program that’s adapted automatically based on the order data it receives. "This allows the machine to bypass traditional CAD/CAM programming," Dallan said. "The machine reads a job list with specific quantities and parameters, creates the drawing, and nests them automatically."

Coil-Fed Punch and Laser

Today, some inline systems perform punching and laser cutting sequentially. The coil is fed into a leveler and straightener, then into the punching work envelope, where the coil feed stops to allow punch tools to create geometries optimal for the process, like hole clusters created by cluster punch tools along with extruded holes, louvers, embosses, and other common forms.

"Material feeds 120 in. forward, then a camera finds the edges of the material and reference points. The machine starts punching, and once it finishes, it indexes another 120 in. forward," Dallan said. "If it’s cutting, say, a 40-ft.-long part, the machine might index several times to cut the entire piece." (Note that unlike a turret punch, the punch heads on this coil-fed setup move in the Y direction while the sheet below remains stationary.)

From there, a shear cuts a custom sheet length. That newly cut sheet then travels to the laser, where a camera system locates the workpiece (gauging off previously processed features, like a punched hole). It also again recognizes the sheet edge, which is especially critical if nests incorporate sheet edges as part edges. After the piece is located, the fiber laser head moves in X and Y to cut the remaining part profiles and contours.

In this setup, the laser and punch operate simultaneously; when the laser cuts, the punch module upstream processes the next group of parts. And on typical setups, the punch and laser create separate features: For instance, the punch creates the holes while the laser cuts the part profile. In certain setups, though, "the laser can continue one operation exactly in the position where the previous process [in this case, punching] stopped," Dallan said. "The camera technology allows the positioning to be extremely accurate."

To laser cut in this inline setup, the machine supports the material with a conveying slat table. Think of a typical belt conveyor, but instead of a belt, you have rows of supporting metal slats. And just like a conventional laser table, fabricators can cut their own replacement slats.

A coil-fed operation incorporates a decoiler, leveler, straightener, laser cutting, and robotic part removal.

Dallan added that how the system handles the skeleton varies depending on part geometries and on what downstream operations need. In some instances, a company might choose to keep all pieces microjoined in place within sheets of variable lengths (again, cut to optimize yield in a nest). Here, operators downstream denest the pieces and perform the next major process, like bending on a press brake.

In other cases, the skeleton is destroyed during cutting, with web sections cut into small bits and falling into bins below. And in still other cases, pick-and-place robots denest and stack parts as the remaining skeleton falls into a bin at the end of the line.

And as Dallan explained, the punch and laser can be "decoupled" to operate independently, with operators manually feeding the occasional sheet into the laser as the punch continues to process coil-fed material. When that short run of laser-cut parts ends, the punch and laser modules can be "recoupled" to cut parts that require both processes.

Dallan described recent laser cutting innovations, including a twin-head fiber laser cutting setup in which each head moves independently. "The heads are on two different gantries," he said, adding that the machine doesn’t cut faster just because it can. It cuts with two heads simultaneously to avoid a cutting bottleneck and ensure inline processing with balanced flow. "For one application, the setup gives the same production throughput that would have been achieved with stamping presses, only with a lot more flexibility."

Part Removal Options

Incorporated within that flow time is not only the laser cutting speed but also the sorting time. And as Dallan explained, when cutting very thin stock—0.03 in. up to 0.10 in.—another coil-fed processing option comes into play:

suspended laser cutting

. This uses no slats for material supports, which eliminates the need for slat cleaning and, for that matter, the possibility for material tip-ups.

Instead, the material is cut while "suspended" in air. Specifically, the material feeds forward, stops, then is pulled taut by two opposing grippers. The laser cuts interior cutouts and most of the part profiles, and the slugs and slag fall to a container far below. After this, a conveyor moves underneath and catches the part as the laser makes the final cuts to release it from the skeleton.

Perspective on Capacity

Fabricators today have some incredibly fast laser cutting at their disposal, but, of course, no operation can produce more than a plant’s operational constraint. Consider the flow in a conventional, noncoil-fed laser cutting department. Operators work to maintain or increase uptime, but most of the work those machines produce flows to forming, welding, and assembly.

Having excess laser cutting capacity can help a fabricator respond quickly to change—which, in the world of metal fabrication, is unfortunately ever present. When a machine is down unexpectedly or a customer requests a rush order, the last thing a fabricator wants is a constraint in its primary cutting process.

Some of that need for capacity, though, comes from the required handling after cutting: shake-and-break denesting and stacking, deburring, waiting for a fork truck or someone to move a cart of parts. What if all that handling could be eliminated with an inline process?

That, Dallan said, is the central efficiency behind coil-fed processing, from leveling to punching and laser cutting to label marking or printing (now integrated on some coil-fed systems) to downstream processes like roll forming and panel bending. Of course, coil-fed cutting doesn’t fit in operations that consistently change material grades and thicknesses. But if an operation has product families that use a few select materials readily available in coil, coil-fed cutting could be an option.

Working on coil-fed material, punching heads move in the Y direction to cut features before the material indexes forward.

Eleven years ago, the Fabricators and Manufacturers Association hosted a lean manufacturing event at Legrand, a light fixture and electrical infrastructure manufacturer in West Hartford, Conn. There, the group observed several lines that represented just how impactful lean thinking can be—reducing overall manufacturing time from days to just a few minutes. A coil-fed roll former with a prepunching station fed an assembly and packaging line. The line had no robots or sophisticated automation, but it did have consistent flow.

That fact, the presenters at the time said, made the plant competitive on the global market. The flow eliminated WIP and the need for finished goods inventory. An order triggered production into action immediately. What was coil in the morning was packaged and on the delivery truck by early afternoon.

Today, coil-fed cutting lines feed various forms of automation, from pick-and-place robots to autonomous mobile robots that carry parts to bending, welding, and beyond. But as Dallan explained, what makes the technology so effective isn’t just about the cutting inches per minute of the laser or strokes per minute of the punch (though they are important pieces to the puzzle).

It’s how quickly the work flows

. For the right application, coil-fed cutting can help streamline that overall flow in significant ways.

"Ultimately, we all need to pay more attention to the value being added at every step of the process," Dallan said. "You want to limit the time from when a part enters the operation to when it exits. You can see people looking very busy moving a lot of partially finished products on the floor, but all that movement doesn’t provide value. And when they move large batches of [work-in-process], they’re really just moving money that’s sitting there, doing nothing."
