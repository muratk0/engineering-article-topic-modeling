How the laser beam and assist gases interact determines the method of cutting. Andujo described several distinct methods for laser cutting metal. First is

endothermic cutting

, also known as

fusion cutting

or "

clean

" cutting. Here, the laser beam heats the metal, so it can be liquified and expelled from the kerf by a controlled coaxial flow of inert assist gas (usually nitrogen, but sometimes argon or helium) at high pressures and/or volume. The molten metal evacuates in just the right way to prevent dross and create a smooth cut edge.

Second is

exothermic cutting

, also known as

oxidation cutting

or

oxygen cutting

. Here, oxygen assist gas reacts with the laser beam to create ignition, allowing it to burn carbon steel or copper to facilitate cutting, though it also leaves an oxide film on the edge.

Another method is

plasmatic endothermic-exothermic cutting

, a phenomenon that occurs when a plasma is generated when cutting thin gauges at high speeds using an inert assist gas, such as nitrogen or high-pressure compressed air. This phenomenon also occurs in very high-powered lasers. Here, a ball of plasma causes an erosion effect that can boost cutting speeds.

High-powered fiber lasers are making endothermic cutting—using nitrogen, air, and gas mixing—a dominant method, but as Andujo explained, operators should still know the basics behind all the different laser cutting methods. Most important, they should know what’s really going on inside the kerf.

To that end, he named four factors that operators should check

before

diving into the specific cutting conditions a job might require. These include optimal assist gas flow, clean optics, a centered beam, and the base focus.

1. Keep the Assist Gas Flowing

In laser cutting, the beam and the assist gas are two sides of the same coin. Often, Andujo said, the issue resides with the flow of assist gas. If a machine requires a certain PSI level, the gas source needs 100 PSI higher to accommodate the pressure loss. A 350-PSI source of gas will not yield 350 PSI at the cutting head. To that end, it helps to think first about what pressure and flow are required at the nozzle front itself, then work back through the system to account for and correct those unavoidable pressure drops.

In fusion cutting, lasers require higher pressure and volume of assist gas—450-PSI pressure and a flow rate from 4,500 standard cubic feet per hour (SCFH) minimum to as high as 12,000 SCFH—yet the exact pressure use at the cutting front depends on the material and application.

Say the pressure required to cut material effectively is 450 PSI. A system using a 500-PSI dewar can be a red flag, since it’s only 50 PSI over the pressure required at the cutting head. That difference isn’t enough to account for the assist gas pressure and volume drop. As the pressure drops, the SCFH of gas drops as well, and cut quality suffers. Most important, this can be detrimental to the consumables, optics, the fiber cable, and even the laser source itself.

All this applies to just one laser. Adding more lasers to a single assist gas supply system and maintaining sufficient assist gas pressure and volume becomes even more of a challenge. It’s not insurmountable, of course, but systems do need to be designed with proper assist gas flow in mind. If they aren’t, operators will be fighting an uphill battle.

Gasification also factors into the equation when drawing from a liquid nitrogen source. A dewar or bulk tank needs to be able to turn the liquid nitrogen into a gas state quickly enough to maintain the required pressure and volume flow. An improperly sized dewar, without adequate vaporization, can wreak havoc on cut speeds and quality.

"If you draw too quickly, you now start drawing liquid particles that—at -320 degrees F—create a freezing effect on the gas lines," Andujo said. "You’re not allowing enough time for the liquid to convert into a gas." This again goes back to having the proper pressure, volume, and gasification at the source to account for pressure drops on the gas’s journey into the cutting head, through the nozzle, and into the metal.

A change in the nozzle orifice diameter has an effect as well. A larger orifice demands a greater SCFH of gas to maintain the flow rate, which in turn affects pressures required at the source and regulators. "If you’re using a 7- or 10-mm nozzle, you might be drawing 12,000 cubic feet per hour," Andujo said, adding that the gas delivery system—tanks, plumbing, vaporizers, and regulators—need to account for that volume. If it doesn’t, cut quality will suffer.

The right back pressure (PSI at the source) and gasification will help maintain the required SCFH flow rate, but the settings vary greatly depending on what kind of gas you’re using and what kind of cutting you’re achieving. Again, fusion cutting uses an inert assist gas to evacuate the molten metal. High-pressure compressed air as well as gas mixing systems add oxygen into the equation and, hence, change the nature of the cutting process and the dynamics surrounding it. Oxygen creates a chemical reaction in the kerf. Gas mixing combined with high laser powers allow for dross-free cutting of more materials without such high assist gas pressure.

"I was using a 30-kW fiber laser to cut 1-in. HRS plate, using less than 1% oxygen in the gas mix, and the system had just three bars of pressure," Andujo said. "The increased power creates a larger kerf area. This allows you to evacuate metal through that area a lot easier, eliminating the need for that higher pressure."

2. Inspect the Optics, Ceramics, and Nozzle

A dirty lens protector and/or focus lens affects the laser beam and the overall process, including its stability. A beam changes as it passes through traces of dirt and dust on the optics, which makes the overall cut quality unpredictable and usually poor. The optics also heat as they absorb and scatter the laser beam. In some cases, this scenario can singe the optics and even create a 3/8-in.-dia. hole in the lens protector.

Andujo used a weather analogy, with the sun representing the laser and the clouds representing dirt on the optic. "The cloudier the day, the less sunlight there is, though the sun itself never loses its energy."

Before their shift, operators working with sealed cutting heads need to inspect and clean the lens protector daily. Other machines on the market have cutting head designs where the operator can remove both the lens protector and focus lens. Andujo recommended inspecting and cleaning them daily—again, before starting any cutting for the day.

Here, shops should establish procedures to ensure lens handling doesn’t make a dirty lens even dirtier. For instance, using straight shop air—full of water and oil droplets—to blow off a lens can be detrimental. Start with the machine manufacturer’s specified procedure. On some machines, operators remove the focusing optic within a casing. On other systems, operators remove the raw optic, handling only the edges to avoid contamination. Some use special gloves.Some clean with medical-grade acetone and/or lab-grade (99.8%) isopropyl alcohol.

The idea is to develop a procedure to ensure both the lens protector and focus optics are clear and contaminant-free. Operators can inspect optical elements with an LED light shone at a 45-degree angle to the surface. The light should reflect back—not be absorbed, as that denotes a dirty surface. "A clean optic always makes the light brighter, not dull," Andujo said.

Lenses and protectors damaged with metal spatter can signify a piercing process that isn’t as fine-tuned as it could be. A pulsed and/or staged pierce "drills" the beam into the material and minimizes the spatter. While efficient, a power pierce can blast a lot of molten metal back toward the nozzle. A power pierce works well with small nozzle orifices, higher standoffs, and using just enough piercing power for the job. Just because you have 20 kW doesn’t mean you should use all of it to pierce. Just 6 kW can power-pierce through ¼-in. plate quite effectively, creating minimal spatter.

The pierce procedure should minimize the flashback and account for the spatter that remains with a proper standoff, so that little to no molten particles lodge themselves inside the nozzle orifice. Failing to stop immediately after losing the cut also can lead to excessive spatter. As Andujo explained, address these two factors—a good pierce and stopping immediately after losing the cut—can allow a lens protector to last for months.

3. Center the Beam

"Centering is absolutely critical," Andujo said. "For the gas flow to be good enough to cut in any direction, any time, you need to be dead center to always achieve coaxial gas flow around the laser beam. The smaller the nozzle, the more critical the centering becomes. It sometimes can make a difference between cutting and not cutting at all."

Operators need to inspect the nozzle orifice to ensure it’s perfectly round, with no dents, spatter, or other imperfections that can alter the orifice shape. Dings on the nozzle itself might affect the capacitive sensing used to maintain cutting nozzle gap, but they shouldn’t affect the beam itself. Nicks or buildup on the orifice circumference, however, can be detrimental. "Instead of flowing, now the gas is fluttering, which affects your cutting," Andujo said.

Certain machines today do offer automated beam centering, but many do not—and even so, it helps to know the fundamentals of beam centering. This involves the operator pressing tape onto the cutting nozzle orifice and performing a tape shot.

Andujo recommended using Scotch transparent tape. "Why? Fiber lasers have a 1-μm wavelength. That means they cannot cut a lot of nonmetals. Instead, the laser beam is scattered and/or refracted, so [if you use frosted tape], you may end up seeing a distorted beam shot, or a ‘melt shot,’ and no impression of the nozzle orifice.

"Transparent tape is also very tacky. The smaller the beam diameter, the harder it is to get an impression on the tape. Tacky transparent tape allows you to see the ring of the nozzle impression much better. You need to be sure you’re looking at the actual diameter of the nozzle orifice against the shot of the laser. You then make sure you have concentricity between the impression of the shot [from the laser beam] and the impression of the nozzle orifice. That concentricity gives you your coaxial gas flow."

Also, operators shouldn’t just look at the tape with their naked eye. Instead, they need to use a 10X magnifying glass. With an uncentered beam, the gas can’t flow coaxially and the machine will cut differently while changing the direction of cut. In fact, the machine won’t cut as well as it should in general.

4. Don’t Forget the Base Focus

To describe the base focus procedure, Andujo used a caliper analogy. Before you use a set of calipers, you open them up, clean the jaws, zero them, open them three times, and if you get the same reading every time, you’re good to go. "Laser cutting has the same requirement. You need to find the thinnest kerf. That thinnest kerf should be a 0-focus command ±0.5 mm."

The base focus sets the zero point for the gears in the cutting head that move the optics to create the desired focal position for the job. If the zero point is off on the laser, every other focal position will be off too.

On all lasers, the base focus needs to be derived at the commissioning of the laser, the servicing of resonators or fiber engines, and/or the replacement of any upper optics. Also, operators swapping out an old focus lens with an identical new focus lens (replacing, say, a 150-mm lens with another 150-mm lens) need to recalibrate the system with the derived base focus of the new optic.

Many base focus procedures involve cutting a series of slits on 20-ga. stainless or cold-rolled steel. The technician or operator might start with a -6-mm focus and adjust the focus in 0.5-mm increments for every focal point change, up to a +6-mm focus. The kerf at -6 mm might be 0.030 in.; at the zero point, the kerf could be just 0.005 in. As test-cut focal points go positive (toward and above the material surface), the kerf again widens to 0.030 in. The operator performs the slit test cuts and uses a feeler gauge (with 0.001- in. increments) to determine which kerf is the thinnest. That’s the base focus, the zero focal point for the start of the cutting process.

The exact kerf measurements and procedure will vary with the machine and material, but the theory is the same—analogous to zeroing out a caliper. Also, the procedure can be very different for some modern fiber lasers, especially those with beam shaping and adaptive optics. Setting the procedure might require a deep dive into the machine manual and good communication with the machine vendor.

Incidentally, this is why regular slat cleaning is so important. Dirty slats increase the potential for the sheet to weld to a slat tip. Additionally, a sheet sitting atop a slat peak built up with slag can change the actual thickness being cut. It’s like cutting a thicker material than the actual one being cut, thereby changing the cutting dynamics and adding an unwelcome variability to the mix.

Start With Stage One

All these factors represent what Andujo called "stage one, the laser cutting fundamentals" that set a firm foundation for process optimization. He uses an automotive analogy, comparing those who learned to drive on a stick shift versus an automatic transmission. Those who drive stick know all about what gear works best in a variety of driving conditions. Over time, the shift action comes naturally. When they drive an automatic, they get a feel for the transmission and know how to make the most out of every acceleration. The process might be automated, but they know what’s happening under the hood.

The same applies with laser cutting fundamentals. Operators who know what makes a quality cut have a strong foundation that gives them a solid footing, helping them get the most out of what remains a fab shop’s greatest capital investment.
