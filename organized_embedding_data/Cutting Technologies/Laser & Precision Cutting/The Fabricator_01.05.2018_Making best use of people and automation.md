# Making best use of people and automation

[TARİH: 01.05.2018 The Fabricator]

Andrew Mackessy, Controller (left); Tom Mackessy, President; Ometek Inc. Columbus, Ohio

A

sk Tom Mackessy how fast the laser cuts on Ometek’s new punch/laser combination machine and he’ll say it’s blazingly fast, and that it’s nice and all, but truth be told, he doesn’t really care.

For Ometek, the combo machine’s fiber laser is critical for processing copper material for several major bus bar jobs. And Mackessy really appreciates the system’s cut quality and ease of maintenance. But he doesn’t scrutinize how quickly the machine cuts. It’s plenty fast.

He’s more interested in how effectively work flows: into and out of the machine, to the press brakes, and on to downstream operations. If work doesn’t flow faster, Ometek isn’t shipping more quality products in less time—which, of course, is the metric that really matters.

Since 2013 the fabricator has adopted quick-response manufacturing (QRM), a brand of continuous improvement tailored for highproduct-mix manufacturing. Like other methods, QRM focuses on the time between value-adding operations, which together make up most of a job’s lead time.

To that end, the company has arranged much of its floor into cells. Its newest has the punch/fiber laser combo system feeding several press brakes, including one that changes tools automatically.

The Mackessys view automation as key to the company’s success, especially for its consistency and repeatability. "The greater number of times people touch a part, the more variability you will have," Andrew said.

Ometek’s newest punch/laser combo cell exemplifies their view. Sheets flow from a six-shelf tower to the machine bed, then are removed via a chute or pick-and-place automation.

The company has a highly diverse part mix, and some parts must be adapted for the pickand- place automation. No machine can defy physics, and some parts are difficult to pick in a repeatable fashion.

Once perfected, the automation allows parts to flow predictably and efficiently. Automation doesn’t call in sick or have a bad day. It just does what it’s told.

Part size and shape matter. The system has a chute for parts that, because of their size or shape, don’t fit the automation. But whenever possible, the company nests parts to make maximum use of the suction-based pick-andplace system.

For instance, for thin aluminum, the pick-andplace automation has a hard time. Part size isn’t the problem; it’s the low weight. So engineers nest these aluminum parts together and punch "snap-apart" lines that keep multiple pieces intact. Grouping parts together in this way increases their weight, so automation can retrieve them reliably. Once the automation neatly stacks the parts, operators retrieve them, snap them apart, and send them downstream.

Still, why go through all this trouble? In fact, some shops choose to forego pick-and-place automation in favor of the traditional "shakeand-break." People sometimes move parts faster than machines.

But for the Mackessys, it’s not about speed. Throughput is governed by the constraint process anyway, and cutting isn’t the constraint. The reason for automation goes back to consistency and making effective use of people.

With automation, the variability occurs when the machine is new, in programming and testing. That programming becomes easier when people learn and document best practices, to accommodate what Tom calls "those oddball situations." But once perfected, the automation allows parts to flow predictably and efficiently. Automation doesn’t call in sick or have a bad day. It just does what it’s told.

Ometek does employ people who shake and break nests in certain blanking cells. But when the task can be automated, the company takes advantage of the technology. Having people shake, break, and manually sort cut parts really doesn’t capture the strengths of shop floor personnel. Their strength is in their ideas for improvement. At the end of the day, those ideas are what makes a custom fabricator competitive.
