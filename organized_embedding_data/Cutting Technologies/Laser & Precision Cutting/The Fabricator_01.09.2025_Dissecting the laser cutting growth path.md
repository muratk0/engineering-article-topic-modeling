# Dissecting the laser cutting growth path

L

aser cutting has reached a kind of inflection point. The present market is more nuanced than ever. Cutting speed is just one piece of the puzzle, and it can be misleading if considered in isolation. Laser speed depends on the part geometries, nest layout, time between cuts, piercing strategies, and more. Zoom out, and you’ll see a puzzle that includes everything that touches a job through its routing, a fabricator’s growth plans, even its business model.

To dive deep into those nuances, The Fabricator spoke with Brett Thompson, laser technologies and sales consulting manager at TRUMPF North America. His message: There’s more than one way to grow a metal fabrication business, and in the coming years the industry could change in some profound ways—and faster than many expect.

Knowledge Base

Several market dynamics are at play. First is a dearth of experienced laser cutting operators. "Finding really good operators is a rarity these days," Thompson said. "This isn’t news. It’s been like this for the past 30 years. The nuances about how to move the focus on specific material, when to change the assist gas pressure, and other details that have historically been tribal knowledge have gradually been leaving the industry for years."

Fabricators combat these trends in two ways. First, they make best use of technology. Today’s machines have simpler controls, and they also assist the cutting process in myriad ways. The laser might monitor and regulate the cut to maintain a quality kerf, adapting to deviations in sheet thickness and surface conditions. Certain machines, if they’re connected to the internet, can make strategic use of AI to correct for quality issues. "If your cut quality is not quite there, you can use a scanner to scan the part edge, which builds a growing model database [in the cloud]," Thompson said. "The machine will sample from that learning and make corrections to cutting parameters."

Second, they invest in operator training—ideally on multiple levels. Initial training should involve the basics: inspecting the cover glass in the laser head; inspecting the concentricity of the laser nozzle; knowing the importance of slat cleaning; understanding the role of the chiller and required maintenance; ensuring the assist gas flow, laser power, and focus are what they should be.

The first level of training essentially covers what the machine needs to keep running smoothly. Subsequent levels cover

why

. "First, you need to know how to operate the machine; then you can learn how to adapt to problems," Thompson said. "Maybe you notice when you’re cutting with oxygen that you’re changing protective glass more frequently. The idea is to apply what you’re observing by identifying characteristics of the cut and learning how to improve or change them. You’re now understanding the physics behind what’s happening at the kerf."

Thompson pointed out that this two-pronged approach—leverage technology and proper training—helps keep the primary cutting operation steady, safe, and reliable, while producing optimal flow: minimizing the need for deburring and knocking off microtabs, orderly and predictable (and perhaps automated) denesting. Knowledge provides the foundation.

Market Dynamics

Laser cutting machine buyers have never been more diverse. You have legacy job shops and contract fabricators looking to upgrade to meet increased capacity demands, while juggling the challenge of an aging workforce and veteran operators heading out the door. "At the same time, you now have a lot of entrepreneurs who have products they want to make in-house, and they might not have any experience in the sheet metal world," Thompson said.

He added that many are entering the laser cutting and bending fray with open ears and a desire to learn how best to design in sheet metal for optimal manufacturability. Also, the sheer variety of laser cutting users has been driving the enormous variety of technology from laser cutting machine manufacturers, from the most basic machine without automation to fully automated cut-bend-weld lines and even coil-fed laser processing.

The Well-Worn Path

The traditional custom fabrication job shop might start with a laser, land more work from several key accounts, then buy additional lasers, brakes, panel benders, welding equipment, and coating technology, all the while progressing toward automation as quantities grow. The job mix is unique, but the 80-20 rule usually applies: A small number of customers (around 20%) provide the majority (around 80%) of revenue.

With this scenario in mind, Thompson described several laser cutting machine qualities that could help this growing custom and contract fabricator. First is

machine dynamics

—that is, how quickly and efficiently the laser moves. Second is

laser beam power

, which defines the energy density available at the cut. The two characteristics work in concert, and which one dominates depends on what an operation needs.

Say a fabricator needs a machine to serve a small group of large customers in the product mix, all of whom need large, thin, dimensionally simple parts that efficiently fill up standard sheet sizes. The machine here could have relatively low machine dynamics and low beam power and still meet downstream needs—especially when paired with automation (more on this later).

What if, however, the parts don’t fill the sheet efficiently, and, after considering alternatives (different sheet sizes, living with lower material yield, etc.), the fabricator has decided to rely on

geometrically complex

filler parts from a variety of repeat customers to optimize material yield? Here, machine dynamics becomes more important. Alternatively, what if an operation wants to run high-mix work during the day and automated production at night? This likely increases the importance of laser power, especially if the shop cuts a wide range of material thicknesses during its day shift.

Of course, laser cutting isn’t truly "complete" until it’s stacked and ready for the next major operation like forming, finishing, or packaging. Here, Thompson described how thinking about cutting "efficiency" has changed, as illustrated by the industry’s use of slug- and skeleton-destruct cutting sequences. Years ago, job shops in particular would avoid such sequences to maximize their parts-per-hour metrics. The idea was to minimize the cutting time so the machine could keep denesters in the shake-and-break area busy.

Even with today’s basic setups, modern lasers are no longer a constraint, so the time spent destroying slugs and skeletons within the laser work envelope can be time well spent—if it makes the cutting operation reliable (no part or slug tip-ups), eases denesting, and minimizes or eliminates the use of microtabs.

Thompson illustrated two scenarios. The first was a laser cutting department with a shake-and-break denest area. Parts needed microtabs so they remained stable within the skeleton throughout the cutting operation. Denesters spent time manually knocking off microtabs before sending pieces on to the staging location for forming. The laser cut incredibly quickly, yet it just fed a denesting bottleneck. Theoretically, managers could just throw more labor at the problem, but finding people isn’t easy—and let’s face it, few dream of a career denesting laser-cut parts all day every day.

The second scenario was that same nest with a longer cutting cycle time, because the laser had to destroy skeleton sections before sending parts to a conveyor. At the end of the conveyor, an operator grabbed parts and put them on a cart, which was soon wheeled to the next operation. The operator then placed the remaining scrap in adjacent bins. The program minimized microtab usage, perhaps taking advantage of nanojoints (tabs that comprise less than half the material thickness) and common-line cutting.

During an unattended second or third shift, the laser produced high-quantity work with automated part sorting. The material yield might not be incredibly high, especially if parts needed to be oriented in a specific way for the part removal automaton. And if a nest has part geometries that catch on the skeleton, then "inefficient" cut sequences like skeleton-destruct could be employed. Still, pieces have no microtabs and are stacked neatly in the morning, ready for the next operation. From this perspective, the laser system as a whole was extraordinarily productive.

A conveyor presents parts and portions of the remaining skeleton to an operator, who sorts pieces into bins. Laser cutting is "finished" because those pieces aren’t sitting on a shelf, tabbed in a skeleton. They’re in a bin, ready to be moved to the next operation.

The Entrepreneur

That holistic view, Thompson said, has been a primary driver in machine development, not least because a host of new players in the laser cutting arena share that view. This includes those entrepreneurs with a diversified business model in mind. They purchase a machine intending to laser cut a product line, the parts for which they can optimize for manufacturability. At the same time, they plan to enter the custom fabrication space and sell the excess laser cutting capacity. In this case, a laser with high power and high machine dynamics might be a good initial investment.

Alternative purchase and operational strategies, including leasing and power-by-the-hour plans (pay for high power only when you use it) might be an option. The job shop work diversifies the revenue while the company builds a flexible, automated system to accommodate a growing product line. And here, dock-to-dock throughput remains the goal. This broadens the focus. Instead of cutting speed, the shop analyzes the throughput of the entire cut-bend-weld cycle.

Ergonomics plays a role here. Thomspon described a hypothetical scenario involving a laser cutting system with in-line panel bending along with a separate robotic laser welding cell. The panel bender can’t accept every part. Pieces need a flat surface and, because of the panel bender’s throat depth, flanges can be only so high. If the piece had shorter flanges, it would need to be welded downstream.

A traditional approach might be to cut the large piece and have the machine unload it before forming, so workers can bring the job to a press brake. It might be an ergonomic nightmare, but the piece just requires two operations—cutting and bending. Considering this, bending on the brake seems to be worth the effort, especially if a piece can avoid welding.

The speed and precision of laser welding (with no postweld grinding), and the precision of upstream processes, changes the calculus. If fixturing costs are reasonable, then redesigning with a shorter flange for panel bending and then laser welding can make sense.

And in the not-too-distant future, robotic and other material handling automaton, equipped with intelligent vision, between forming and laser welding might become more commonplace. That seamless flow from cutting to bending and welding could help bring overall throughput to the next level.

The Automated OEM

Driving this growth in automation has been renewed interest at large OEMs and upper-tier suppliers. "We’re now seeing really big companies interested in laser cutting and bending, and they’ve never had in-house laser cutting before," Thompson said. "They could be in stamping in the automotive world or white goods manufacturing. The conversations with these companies immediately go to automation, with the aim of having minimal touches on every part."

This can include comprehensive lines, but it also can include coil-fed laser cutting—especially in instances where material cost is a big factor. "When you buy coil, the material becomes less expensive," Thompson said. "And it’s a single direction of flow. When you consider cutting time for flatbed lasers, you need to consider the actual cutting, but you also need to consider the pallet change and sheet measurement times. And on large-format machines, the pal-let change and sheet measurement times can be even longer. When you have that single direction of flow, you greatly increase the actual cutting time of the machine and increase efficiency of the material. You’re no longer restricted by the size of the sheet."

A panel bender can’t bend every part. Flanges can be only so high. Would redesigning a piece so it flows through both bending and laser welding be the smarter option, even though it must pass through an additional work center? The application would dictate the answer—but the first step is asking the out-of-the-box question in the first place.

Long pieces can be nested, and the system cuts blanks to the optimal length for a particular group of parts. Material yield goes up, sparse skeletons fall to a tray below as the laser-cut blanks move to pick-and-place robots for parts stacking.

Automation Gets Intelligent

Product mixes that are less predictable stop most fabricators from pushing automation’s limits—but this, Thompson said, will be changing. Vision will help robots, AMRs, and other forms of automation work well even the most high-product-mix operations. The data they collect in turn will feed to the office and allow manufacturers to make better use of capacity—both for the products they have and potential contract work.

Thinking about the industry’s future, Thompson considered types of demand, with a spectrum of variants in between. On one end, a

continuous DFM feedback loop

exists between the metal fabrication technology and the products being designed. The company works to optimize manufacturing throughput and remain as flexible as possible.

Metal fabrication capacity

is the second type, and in this arena the change could be even more dramatic. "Here, we’re thinking of shops in which you submit a request for quote, receive the price, then through an MES [manufacturing execution system] receive information about capacity, as in, ‘The laser cutting machine will have available time for your order in 30 minutes.’ We know what the machine is doing, we know what it’s going to be doing two hours from now, two days from now. And through a fully automated system, you can book that capacity automatically."

Thompson added that this kind of seamless front-office job flow isn’t a pie-in-the-sky dream. The MES technology is available to make this a reality, and progressive web shops have had online ordering available for years. Regardless, it’s likely to become more common in the years to come.

Many operations will likely employ a mix of both extremes: selling capacity and offering extensive DFM and supply chain optimization. For instance, a contract fabricator’s long-standing high-quantity customers will benefit from continual DFM and supply chain improvements. At the same time, automated ordering of piece-parts will make the long tail of low-quantity parts profitable and, not least, enhance the first-time customer experience.

Ultimately, Thompson said, the future will be about transparency, traceability, and intelligent feedback.

Is there a better way to design this part for optimal flow and improve our end product? I need a part cut this afternoon—can I go online and find someone to cut it for me right now?

One thing is for sure: We’re a long way from worrying about the cutting-inches-per-minute metric on a particular laser.
