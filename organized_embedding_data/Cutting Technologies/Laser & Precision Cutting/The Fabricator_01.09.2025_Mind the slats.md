T

he ultrahigh-powered fiber laser has started to reshuffle the competitive game in metal fabrication. What was once purely in the realm of plasma arc or oxyfuel cutting is now being cut by fiber lasers 30 and 50 kW in power, and even beyond.

What hasn’t gotten as much attention is an unfortunate side effect that comes with incredible laser cutting power: hard-to-remove slag buildup on a cutting table’s slats. Slat cleaning has become an inherent operational cost that comes with high-power laser cutting, and it really can’t be ignored.

Why Slat Condition Matters

Eric Chalmers, president, and Mark Jeans, sales manager at SlatPro, Mounds View, Minn., have heard about and even witnessed some horror stories, all spawned by neglected slats on the cutting table.

A fabricator invests in an incredibly productive automated laser cutting system. The laser cuts a nest of parts, and forks come in to lift the cut nest off the slats and transport it into a cartridge. But there’s a problem. The forks try to lift the sheet, but alas, the molten metal from the kerf welds the sheet to the slag built up on the slats. Bam! The forks lift the sheet and the slats, laser cutting suddenly halts, and the finger-pointing begins.

"High-powered lasers are in desperate need of a solution," said Chalmers. "As slag builds up on the higher-powered lasers, the automation doesn’t work. If you have automation, you need to keep the slats clean so the forks can go underneath the finished product. Otherwise, it can hang on the slag and literally pick up the slats from the table."

Several realities have made slag buildup and, hence, slat maintenance more important than ever. High-powered fiber lasers make the most economic sense when cutting thick plate extraordinarily quickly—but this also produces a lot of slag. Much of that plate cutting uses pure nitrogen or a nitrogen-oxygen mix (or ultradry shop air, which is itself a mix of nitrogen and oxygen) for an assist gas. That produces copious slag that differs from lower-powered laser cutting with oxygen assist gas.

Molten metal liquifies as it evacuates the kerf, then solidifies extremely quickly as it falls between the slats—or lands directly on them. If the latter occurs, the slag adheres to one of three places: the valley of the sawtooth, somewhere in between, spilling over on the edge (effectively making the slat thicker), or on the very tip of the tooth.

Excessive amounts of slag can land on the slat tip, which can cause positioning errors in the sheet. That said, the sawtooth design minimizes direct slag buildup on the tip. But as Jeans explained, the quick solidification makes the slag especially tough to remove. That quick "freeze" usually means it has fewer impurities. The result: Slag from the kerf is closer to being "pure" metal, and so quickly adheres and fuses to the slat teeth. The slag builds up quickly, and it’s more difficult to remove.

"Gas mixing has made it so the slag essentially welds to the slat," Jeans said, "especially after your laser beam runs over the same spot over and over, if you’re cutting a batch of identical nests in carbon steel. If you wait too long to do your cleaning, you eventually just can’t clean it and you end up having to replace your slat."

He added that slag from mixed gas cutting represents the latest in slag that has only become more difficult to clean over the years. Oxygen cutting left relatively soft slag that was easy to remove, while nitrogen made the slag harder and tougher. Nitrogen-oxygen gas mixing (with thick plate especially) has just taken the slat cleaning challenge to the next level.

Operational Strategies

If possible, incorporate slat placement into your nesting strategies. Some nesting software platforms show the grid that supports the sheet. Especially if it’s part of a high-quantity order that will run for hours, if you see a cut path that runs directly over a slat, you might be able to adjust the layout to ensure this doesn’t happen.

The adjustment might be worth it, even if you end up with a slightly lower material yield. Yes, the skeleton web sections might be thicker, and you won’t fit quite as many parts on a nest. But you need to weigh that against the time spent cleaning the slats you have, as well as ordering or cutting and installing new slats.

The slats can be moved and rearranged too. Say you have a high-quantity nest that will run overnight, one that involves several large blanks in each sheet. You notice that portions of the nest layout involve cut paths that travel directly over a slat. You also notice various slugs (cutouts) that sit directly atop an existing slat.

You could tab in those slugs, but that means they and the microtabs would need to be shaken and ground off in a downstream process. Or you could even program a slug-destruct sequence, cutting the cutouts to bits so they fall cleanly between the slats.

That sounds logical, but if the slats don’t have to be there, why keep them there at all? Yes, toolpaths still need to ensure slugs don’t get caught, perhaps with wider kerfs or a few cuts to ensure the slug contents fall cleanly downward. But if you remove the slat, you eliminate the possibility of that slat causing a tip-up issue. Even better, the slat teeth aren’t exposed to molten metal being evacuated from the laser cutting kerf. The result: Your slats last longer.

Chalmers added that parts do need to be of a certain thickness for this to work well. Thin parts and slugs can still get caught in the assist gas turbulence from the cutting head, which can make untabbed and insufficiently supported parts and cutouts behave unpredictably. Thicker material, however, is another story. "If you’re cutting large parts that are fairly heavy, just pull out the slats you don’t need. And it can help your slug cutouts fall through."

"That strategy can even help with dross and contamination on the bottom of your parts," Jeans said. The fewer slats you use, the fewer points of contact you have, and the less chance you have for slag or debris to affect cut edge quality or the surface condition of a blank.

Another strategy involves paying attention to the mix of nest layouts that the laser cuts and then rotating and moving the slats as needed, so certain portions of slats aren’t exposed to the same cut path over and over.

"Rotating slats from front to back and back to front of the table can definitely prolong your slat life," Jeans said. "Even rotating the slat 180 degrees in the same slot can help your slat life significantly."

You can swap slats from one end (or quadrant, for large-format systems) to another. Some table setups allow you (with the help of a jib crane) to lift out a section of slats as a single unit.

Slat Design Strategies

You also can try different slat materials. For instance, copper slats hold less slag and tend to release slag easier when cleaned thanks to their high thermal conductivity. "Copper extends the time for slag material to break down," Jeans said, adding that this characteristic mitigates slag buildup. "And when you do clean them, they clean easier."

The industry has played a kind of balancing act over the years when it comes to slat design. Some machines have offered cross-hatch and other specialized slat designs that have resisted slag buildup. That said, the slat grid eventually needed to be cleaned, and the cleaning could be extremely difficult.

Machine manufacturers design slats with specific intents, and the design is a balance of function, simplicity, having the right number of contact points (and sufficient slat thickness) to support the material, while also having enough space to allow molten metal to collect between cleanings. Yes, a slat design might mitigate slag buildup, but it always needs to be cleaned and eventually replaced.

"The best slat design can depend on the thickness of material you’re cutting," Jeans said. "If you cut thinner material, like 20- or 16-ga. material, you can have numerous small contact points. If you cut 0.5-in.-thick plate, you’ll need wider peaks and valleys on the slats themselves and thicker slats. Before, it was standard to see 11-ga. slats on most of the laser tables. With the higher-powered laser, we’re now starting to see those goes up to 7 ga. or even 0.25 in."

"When you laser-cut thick material especially," Chalmers said, "having deeper valleys in the slat’s sawtooth design can be beneficial." That allows for more space for slag to collect and (most important) move away from the bottom of the kerf. The last thing you want is for too much slag to collect on the side or near the tip, which again opens the door to possibly welding the slat to the plate being cut.

Beyond this comes the make-vs.-buy decision. A fabricator might choose to cut slats in-house, even if they’re made of copper or other specialty material. The approach makes sense if you have laser cutting capacity to spare. If your machines are running full-out, buying slats (including copper ones) from an outside supplier can make more sense.

Some specialty slat designs exist, including slats with a subtle side-toside "snake" pattern, that can help on large batches of sheets incorporating large blanks. This means a straight cut directly over a slat won’t throw slag constantly to the teeth surface.

"Again, you need to watch for where you’re cutting over the same area over and over," Jeans said.

There are tradeoffs here, too, of course. Specialty slats might mitigate slag buildup, but they also probably can’t be fabricated cost-effectively in-house. An easier approach, Jeans said, especially with a static nest run repeatedly throughout a shift, might be to simply move or remove slats directly under the cut line.

Develop a Slat Maintenance Routine

You might analyze your cut paths, rotate your slats, even consider new slat material or designs. How effective these practices are depends on the job mix you have and the kind of cutting you’re doing. Cutting thin sheet all day doesn’t produce the same amount of slag as a high-powered fiber laser cutting through 0.75-in.-thick plate.

Regardless, developing a regular preventive maintenance schedule is a must. Periodically, you can schedule time for slat cleaning with a mechanical tool. Older designs worked well for CO

2

and even lower-power fiber lasers, but high-powered fiber lasers have presented unique challenges—and today, unique tools are available to make slag removal less challenging than it used to be.

As Chalmers explained, heavier-duty slat cleaners are now being designed specifically to remove the slag from higher-powered lasers. "These have multiple stacked cutters, each of which have many teeth. The teeth, offset by 30 degrees from each other, rotate on both sides of each slat. This results in smoother applied power and more teeth to better remove the slag."

The more frequently you clean, the easier it will be to remove the slag. After each cleaning (or more frequently), you can spray coating that slows slag buildup.

"In our experience, shops that run two shifts a day, five days a week, clean their slats once a week," Jeans explained. "That said, some shops now are finding it easier to clean every shift, simply because they’re cutting so much more material than they were before. This is especially true when you use lasers with 15 kW or more." (He added that switching material grades is also a good time to clean the slats, just to avoid cross contamination.)

Beyond this, schedule regular slat replacement after a certain number of cleanings. Over time, slat tips wear, which in turn changes the positioning accuracy in laser cutting. Good operators adjust the focus as slats wear and the workpiece position in Z changes slightly. That said, the more adjustments a laser operator needs to make, the less repeatable laser cutting can become.

"Some shops actually add a little height to their slats, between 0.125 in. and 0.25 in.," Jeans said, explaining that the strategy "does give you quite a bit more time before the broken-down tips force a slat change-out."

Regardless, he said, if you keep slat replacement on a regular schedule, laser cutting can become more repeatable and remain extraordinarily productive—the very reasons you purchased a high-powered fiber laser in the first place.
