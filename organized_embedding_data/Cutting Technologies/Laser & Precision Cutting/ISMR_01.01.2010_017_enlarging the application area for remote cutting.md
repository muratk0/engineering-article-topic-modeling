# ENLARGING THE APPLICATION AREA FOR REMOTE CUTTING

[TARİH: 01.03.2010 ISMR]

ISMR SAYS:

“Investigations by Fraunhofer IWS Dresden in Germany have shown further application areas for remote cutting – outstanding performance can be seen for sheet thicknesses of up to 0.5mm”

Matthias Lütke1, Thomas Himmer2, Andreas Wetzig2, Eckhard Beyer1,2 1 University of Technology Dresden, 01062 Dresden, Germany 2 Fraunhofer IWS, Winterbergstr. 28, 01277 Dresden, Germany

Remote cutting is an alternative method to conventional laser cutting processes when working on thin sheet, which significantly reduces inertia. Depending upon sheet thickness, cutting speeds of approx. 100m/min. in contours are possible.

The development of high brightness lasers is rapidly progressing. Fundamental mode fiber lasers are now available with up to several kilowatts of output power, offering long focal lengths accompanied by small spot sizes. Intensities in the focal plane of up to 109 W/cm2 can be generated (even in cw mode). This opens the door to new methods of material processing.

One of these new technologies is the remote cutting of metal materials. Using the benefits of high brightness lasers, metals can be cut without cutting gas (ie no cutting nozzles) so the working distance no longer depends upon the distance between the nozzle and the work piece. The laser beam also becomes very flexible. High velocities at the surface of the work piece can be generated by moving the laser beam with mirrors. Using a 2D scanner, the laser beam can be deflected into the working area of the scanner and the recognized benefits of remote technology can be applied.

Investigations demonstrate the potential of this technique. Depending upon laser parameters as well as material properties, cutting speeds in contour of up to several hundreds of metres/min. are possible and speeds of over 60m/min. for 0.1mm thick stainless steel at a laser power level of 1000 W are achievable. Furthermore, the cutting speed in contour increases with enhanced laser power. At a laser power level of 3000 W, cutting speeds in contour of more than 200m/min. are possible.

Higher laser power enables the cutting of thicker sheets. Investigations into possible cutting depths were made and, depending upon the applied laser power, spot diameter, processing speed and number of scan repetitions, achievable cutting depths were reported. Only a certain depth can be achieved by a single scan of the cutting kerf. The typical range of cutting kerf’s depth went from 30 to 50 μm. Thus, cutting thicker material requires a re-scanning of the cutting kerf. A cutting kerf is formed layer by layer until the whole thickness of the material is cut.

Hence, the material thickness defines the necessary number of scan repetitions. However, this principle works only for a certain range of material thicknesses. The remaining molten and re-solidified material causes a reclosing of the generated cutting kerf. A separation of the cut parts is not possible. Although the laser beam completely penetrates the entire thickness of the material, the cut is incomplete. The question is: how to cut thicker materials?

Similar effects can be observed when reducing the processing speed over a wide range. Investigations show that major cutting depths can be achieved by lower processing speeds. Otherwise, any reduction of the processing speed is not possible because of limits within the process itself. This is the critical processing speed vcritical. Applying lower processing speeds than vcritical results in undesired welding effects such as the formation of humps. The cut is also incomplete.

The value of the critical processing speed depends on the applied laser parameter and material properties. For stainless steel and common focal diameters, it can be in the range of 150m/min. to 300m/min. That implies cutting rectangular corners which also requires processing speeds higher than the critical one. This leads to a second issue to be addressed: how to cut rectangular corners without reducing the cutting speed beneath the critical one?

For the investigation, a 5 kW single mode beam source was used. A 2D scanning system was also used and, due to the small inertia of such scanning system processing, speeds of up to 800m/min. were possible. The 2D scanning system was mounted on a robot, see Figure 1.
