arge-scale fabrication operations traditionally have relied on the oxyfuel or plasma torch, two thermal cutting workhorses that have dominated plate cutting for decades. Their domination isn’t likely to end any time soon, but another process has been making a significant splash: Ultrahigh-powered lasers are stepping up, and cutting into, some extremely large and extremely thick plates.

"Fabricators still need to determine the right process for their application. There’s a misunderstanding that’s spreading in the market: Because we now have so much laser power available, people think they can replace their collection of plasma and oxyfuel machines with just one laser. That really isn’t the case."

That was Carlos Rodriguez, director of business development for Tecoi USA, Longview, Texas. The company specializes in large-format machines of different thermal cutting varieties, including oxyfuel, plasma, and laser. The Fabricator spoke with Rodriguez to dive deep into plate cutting trends in the large-format arena, where cutting tables can be hundreds of feet long and sometimes span the entire factory floor.

So, what should a fabricator consider when looking to streamline large-format plate cutting? The answer boils down to three factors: edge requirements, both for the part profile as well as holes and cutouts; the range of thicknesses; and the true cost of the cutting operation, including handling and secondary ops. Combine this with knowledge of the state of the art in plate cutting, and a large-format plate cutting operation can develop a strategy for growth.

Laser Power

Fabricators have good reason to think that one powerful, fully automated laser cutting machine can replace a host of plasma cutting systems. Oxyfuel still holds its dominance in extreme plate cutting, especially for material beyond 2 in. thick. The thinking goes that it’s hard to beat the efficiency of a row of oxyfuel torches on a gantry.

Still, some operations are thinking anew, especially if they process a lot of plate between 0.5 and 1 in. Three advancements are catching fabricators’ attention, Rodriguez said. First is the advancing beam characteristics and assist gas technologies of today’s ultrapowerful 1-μm-wavelength lasers.

lasers at all," Rodriguez said. "But over the past few years, increasing laser powers are starting to change the market."

As laser power climbs past 24 kW, fabricators are seeing significant speed increases. Novel assist gas technologies, including nitrogen-oxygen mixes, have helped improve edge quality in thick plate in a significant way. Inert nitrogen flushes the molten metal out of the kerf, and just the right amount of oxygen—consistently dispersed throughout the assist gas column enveloping the beam—gives the cut that "reactive boost" to minimize dross. Some operations also make use of dried shop air—a combination of nitrogen, oxygen, and other elements. Pure nitrogen can help produce an oxide-free edge.

"Today, when you’re laser cutting thick plate, you have a lot of flexibility and a lot of options," Rodriguez said.

Combine all this with high-powered beams that can produce a wide kerf, and you create a clean-cutting, stable laser process for thick plate. In solid-state, 1-μm-wavelength lasers designed for cutting, a delivery fiber enters the laser cutting head, in which the beam travels through a collimator and focusing optics. Advances in beam shaping, delivery fiber, and manipulation technologies have helped "tune" the beam profile to cut thicker plate more effectively.

Laser cutting produces extremely accurate edges, tolerance-wise, but the edges lack the smooth surface produced by the plasma torch.

Rodriguez described another approach, offered by Tecoi, that makes use of two separate delivery fibers, a small-diameter and large-diameter one. When cutting thinner material, the system uses the smaller-diameter fiber for both the pierce and cutting cycles. When cutting thick, it uses the smaller-diameter delivery fiber to pierce and the larger-diameter fiber to cut.

"When you use that small fiber to pierce thick plate, you have better concentration of power," Rodriguez said. "Then, to begin the cutting cycle, it switches to the larger fiber optimized for thick plate cutting."

The heads themselves are now being designed to handle the harsh environment of heavy-duty plate cutting. Watch some lasers pierce and cut plate, and you’ll notice their cutting heads are longer and narrower in shape. These head designs further remove the optics from spatter in the cutting zone, a reality that has gotten only more pronounced as increasingly powerful lasers cut thicker plate.

More Beveling, Fewer Secondary Ops

The narrow head also gives clearance for another emerging laser cutting technology that once was seen mainly in plasma cutting: bevel cutting. The cutting thickness increases as the head tilts toward 50 degrees, which is why, Rodriguez said, laser bevel cutting is typically limited to 1-in. plate and thinner.

Such beveling capability can create countersunk holes, as well as bevels with lands (Y bevels) and a variety of complex bevel shapes. For instance, to produce a K bevel, with a vertical land in the middle of the material thickness, requires three passes: one for the bottom bevel, another for the land in the middle, and a final pass for the top bevel.

Because certain bevel shapes do require multiple passes, cutting throughput can fall a bit, but as Rodriguez explained, the inches-per-minute and cutting cycle time metrics don’t consider what for most is the greatest benefit—eliminating those secondary operations. Sure, if you compare plasma and high-powered laser, the laser wins the race. But for very thick plate, cutting speed increases aren’t dramatic, especially as you cut material 1 in. thick and beyond. But add in the fact that beveled plates no longer need secondary grinding or machining, and the high-powered laser wins by a mile.

Edge Requirements and Multifunction Lasers

Rodriguez emphasized that the laser benefits remain anchored to the application, including cut quality requirements along the part perimeter and the complexity and size of internal holes and cutouts. All the cutting productivity in the world doesn’t count for much if it doesn’t produce the kind of edges an application requires.

Generally speaking, lasers excel in cutting accuracy—that is, the position of the cut—while the chemical reactions induced by plasma cutting produce smoother edges. Many early adopters of high-powered lasers made the leap to eliminate secondary machining—the only process that could bring those plasma-cut edges within the required tolerances. For them, cut accuracy really mattered, but they didn’t necessarily need the smooth cut edge that plasma so adeptly produces.

"When you cut with a laser, you still get those striations on the edge," Rodriguez said. "Again, tolerance-wise, the laser is much better than plasma, but if a smooth surface finish matters more, plasma still might be the better choice."

If plasma-cut edge quality suffices for most—but not all—edge surfaces, a multifunction system might help, a table with not only thermal cutting but also drilling and tapping. Today’s high-definition plasma systems can produce extremely smooth, small-diameter holes with little taper, with 2-to-1 and even 1-to-1 diameter-to-material thickness ratios. But as Rodriguez explained, even the best plasma-cut holes have lead-in and lead-outs as well as a minute amount of taper. Here’s where integrated drilling and tapping make sense. Another use case is for extremely thick plate that pushes the envelope of plasma cutting. An operator might choose to drill a pilot hole in 2-in. plate, then edge-start the cut.

Many plate fabricators jump to laser cutting in part because of its holemaking capability. The laser beam’s extremely high energy density can pierce through material quickly, leave a less pronounced heat-affected zone, and create holes with diameter-to-material thickness ratios as small as 0.5-to-1. That said, laser cutting is still a thermal process. It cannot produce extremely small holes, nor can it tap to thread those holes.

Enter the multifunction laser cutting machine, with a drill and tap head mounted on the same gantry as the laser head. "This is typically for very small holes," Rodriguez said, adding that the extreme accuracy of the laser usually negates the need for drills larger than ⅞ in. Like their plasma and oxyfuel multifunction cousins, these multifunction laser systems can come with special slats that have pins made of soft material (aluminum, for laser systems) to prevent tool breakage.

In these setups, the machine typically drills and taps first to create the threaded holes across the entire plate, after which the laser head completes the remaining part profiles and internal contours. And because of the laser’s lower heat input, compared to plasma and (especially) oxyfuel, the head can cut the entire plate using the shortest toolpaths. With other thermal cutting technologies, the programmer might have to program the toolpath to alternate quadrants of the plate, "spreading the heat" and minimizing distortion.

More Power, More Cutting Heads

More laser cutting power sounds great. Problem is, more kilowatts give diminishing returns, at least when measured on a purely inches-per-minute basis—a reality that becomes especially true in thicker material. "This means that 24 kW of power will not cut twice as fast as 12 kW. But what if we could split that 24 kW of power into two cutting heads, each delivering its own 12-kW beam? This is how we can increase throughput," Rodriguez said.

For its dual-head setups, Tecoi uses TRUMPF disk lasers, with one laser power source feeding both heads. "In these cases, if you’re processing very thick plate, close to the machine’s maximum capacity, you can send full power through one cutting head. If you’re cutting thinner plate, you can split the power and have two 12-kW heads cut simultaneously."

Plate Cutting Automation

Say a fabricator has a dual-head laser with integrated drilling and tapping. It might seem to be a configuration decked out for maximum productivity—except when it’s just waiting for material.

In many setups, large-format cutting machines have tables spanning literally hundreds of feet. An enclosure and gantry holding the laser cutting head move along the length of the table. Once cutting is complete, the gantry moves to another plate on the table while workers use a crane to retrieve cut parts and the skeleton.

This arrangement helps maintain uptime, but what if an operation requires even higher levels of throughput with minimal labor? Here, tower systems designed for plate cutting can help. These consist of a stack of what are essentially separate cutting tables. Each table transports one heavy plate—material that can weigh thousands of pounds—into the laser cutting work envelope.

Rodriguez added that plate cutting automation technology is designed to be highly customized, and this includes automated denesting. He described a recent installation in which lifters position multiple plates onto a large cutting shuttle table. When the cutting cycle for the previous program finishes, robots retrieve parts and place them onto a rotating carousel of bins. Adjacent to this are tables to hold skeletons and raw material for manual loading—for plates too thick for the robots to handle.

Putting It All Together

Where such automation fits depends on a fabricator’s part mix, of course, including the volumes of parts it produces. A multitude of factors need to be considered for a fully automated plate cutting system to reach peak productivity. To illustrate, Rodriguez pointed to a shaker conveyor system underneath the cutting table that automatically removes the slag. Heavy-duty cutting produces a lot of it, and plate cutting systems need an efficient way to remove it all.

Such expansive setups represent the extremes of plate cutting automation—yet as Rodriguez emphasized, the process still starts with some basic questions about edge requirements, thickness ranges, and volumes. A plasma can produce a smooth edge that a laser can’t match, but a plasma can’t beat a laser’s cutting tolerances.

A laser can outpace a plasma in many cases, but not if 1.5- or 2-in. plates make up most of the product mix. In this case, plasma or oxyfuel (or both) might be the way to go. Yes, ultrahigh-powered lasers can be pushed to cut 1.5 in. or even thicker, but it’s not their sweet spot for production.

Will a fabricator or plate processor be able to replace all its plasma machines with one multifunction, multihead laser cutter? Probably not, at least for now. But that one machine could eliminate a multitude of secondary operations downstream and, in doing so, give a heavy plate shop a seriously competitive edge.
