FAQs answered about the oldest thermal cutting tech around

Having an oxyfuel torch in your shop’s repertoire offers a degree of versatility for processing very thick OXYFUEL CUTTING plate that other more expensive technologies simply can’t. AKS Cutting Systems.

O

xyfuel cutting might be the old man on the fab shop dance floor, but don’t let the proverbial gray hair fool you: When it comes to cutting thick carbon steel plate, no tech has better moves.

We sat down with Chris Phillip, director of aftermarket service and sales for Cleveland-based AKS Cutting Systems, to get his take on some frequently asked questions about oxyfuel cutting, its inherent strengths and drawbacks, and why shops should consider having at least one oxyfuel torch handy at all times.

The Welder:

What exactly is oxyfuel cutting?

Chris Phillip:

Oxyfuel cutting very simply is rapidly rusting steel. That’s literally what you’re doing. You’re heating up the steel to its kindling temperature enough that it rapidly rusts. Then you’re dumping a tremendous amount of oxygen on it, enough to eject the molten material. This jet of O2 pushes the molten metal out of the cutting path.

What are the intrinsic benefits to oxyfuel, as opposed to other technologies?

Process reliability. That’s really the big thing.

Other technologies … have lenses and water tubes, and gas flows through different designs and whatnot. Oxyfuel is a tube that you run gas out of. It’s very, very simple. It’s truly a compressed bottle of a gas that you’re choosing, and then you’re igniting the gas stream. There’s a flash arrester right there so [the flame] doesn’t travel up the tube, and that’s the tool. So, these are very inexpensive, very reliable tools because of the simplicity of their design.

What applications are especially suited to oxyfuel cutting?

There’s basically one: if you’re cutting thick steel plate—not aluminum, not stainless. You need a ferrous material or else it won’t cut with oxyfuel. Oxyfuel can cut ½ in.-plus, but if you’re cutting more than 3-in.-thick steel as a good portion of your workload … then oxyfuel becomes far and away the best choice.

What are the drawbacks?

Two big ones. Oxyfuel is slow. And two, you have a large kerf. The width of the flame is significant—typically 1 to 3 mm. And that really puts a design limitation on what you can cut.

That’s more of a strategic choice. Say you’re oxyfuel cutting, and the guy next to you is using something else that has a smaller beam width or a smaller tool radius. They have a larger market that they can approach. That’s just the truth of the matter. Precision being what it is for many applications, obviously that jumps into play right away.

What safety precautions does an operator need to maintain?

If you have a shop that is able to weld safely, you have a shop that’s able to oxyfuel-cut safely. Follow the standards and you will be well on your way.

How is that?

[Employ] standard practices like flash arresters at both the torch and regulator, soap bubble testing for leaks, and proper cylinder management.

At the end of the day you’re managing hot metal and explosive gases, right? So, you’re dealing with explosive gases. You’re dealing with explosive leads—flexible tubes that have gases running down them to deliver the gas to the torch head. And so, you must have a way of managing that by using durable piping conduits and otherwise managing the gases in a way that doesn’t expose it to a lot of risk by the nature of the tool.

If you buy oxyfuel gas leads, they’re inherently puncture-resistant. They’re heat resistant because that’s the environment that they’re designed to operate in. So, if you are buying quality materials and piping and tubing leads from the manufacturers of the oxyfuel equipment, that coupled with active safety measures and practices will create a robust solution.

I have seen people who skimp out on that, and they use other types of tubing, and then they have a fire halfway down their machine. It does happen. It’s a heck of a thing to skimp out on.

All that said, it is best to follow the standards. All gas pipes need to be actively protected against cuts and abrasion, and you should not rely on the puncture resistance of the gas leads for safety. Well-designed machines consider this and place the leads in protected structures.

What would you say to customers that could benefit from oxyfuel but maybe haven’t considered it or invested as much as they might in it?

Job shops in particular have a wonderful usage case for having one oxyfuel torch in their facility. And it’s so you can insulate your customer base—you don’t have to say no to somebody.

Let’s say you have a job shop, and people often come to you, and they say, "Hey, I’ve got this 1/4-in., ½-in. plate," and you cut it. And then one day, that same guy shows up, and he’s got in the back of his truck a 5-in.- thick piece of steel that’s 2 ft. long by 1 ft. wide. He goes, "Hey, do you have a way for me to cut this in half? I just need to just rip it in half so I can process it myself."

And now you have a choice. You can either say no, and they start looking for an alternative supplier, which may cost you the entire pool of business with that customer, or you’ve bought a $1,200 oxyfuel torch that you sit in the corner, which anybody can operate, that you pull out three times a year and it keeps your customers happy. It’s a nice tool to have, to be able to say, "I can process from 1/8 in. to 12 in. thick." Because if you don’t make that investment, you can only process from 1/8 to 3 in. thick. That’s a huge trade-off there.

Do you really need to be able to cut 12 in. every day? Absolutely not. I’ve never seen a facility that does it. But the guy who says, "Hey, I’ve got this one block of 12 in. I know I’ve been working with you for two decades. Can you help me do this?" You go, "Of course I can—I’m your guy." That’s worth a lot more than the investment in the piece of equipment.

I would argue that you can’t say that you’re a steel service center if you don’t have one oxyfuel torch. You need to be able to service your customers, or other people are going to very easily be able to take them from you.

What level of training is involved with this?

It’s kind of like playing a musical instrument, believe it or not. You can get competent on it relatively quickly. Oxyfuel is slow. You might cut a plate for four or five hours. That’s why you use so many torches— that way you can multiply your linear distance, which might be 2 or 3 in. of cutting a minute, by 10. So, now you’re cutting 20 to 30 in. a minute.

A good operator is operating three or four machines. He’s not watching each torch. He is listening, and when the gas pressure changes on one of his torches, or it’s no longer burning the same way, he can hear the difference from 50, 60, 70 ft. away.

Can you train a guy to go over and spark a torch with a little sparker and have him heat up the plate for two minutes and then press go and have it change over from preheat to cut? Yeah, you can train somebody to do that in a couple hours. Is that a competent oxyfuel operator? Absolutely not. You’ve got to learn how it sounds. You’ve got to really pay attention to it for a long time before you can walk around and not have to sit there and watch all these torches run all day.

What secondary operations are involved in oxyfuel?

I mean, obviously, you’re doing a bit of cleanup—dross, slag, anything like that you’ve got to grind on. You are burning the material, which means you’re turning it to a gas. You’re also melting it. As it melts, it will form slag. You’re going to get some nice big chunks on the bottom of your plate.

You can toss them through a sanding system, or you can chip it off. I’ve seen people that have such thick dross on alloys of steel that they’re using robots [that] come in with a plasma torch and cut off the slag.

It depends on the scope of the application. A lot of people are taking thick plate, chopping it up, cleaning it, and then putting it on a machining center and finishing it. They’re taking a big plate that’s 10 in. thick by 5 ft. wide by 10 ft. long, and that’s what they store as their stock. Then they cut a section out of it, they clean it, and then they’re putting it onto a machining center. Then they’re drilling and tapping and machining it otherwise.

I still think that oxyfuel is an old tech, but it still 100% has an application. People overlook it for new shinier stuff, but oxyfuel is an absolute workhorse. And if you’re looking to invest in a big piece of equipment, add an oxyfuel torch for the capacity and the versatility it provides you.

It shouldn’t be overlooked. There are a lot of applications for it that people miss out on, and I think protecting your customer base from having to look to somebody else because you can’t help them is one of the biggest.
