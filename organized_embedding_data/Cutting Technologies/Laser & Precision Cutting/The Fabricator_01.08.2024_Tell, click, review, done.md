apco Metal Stamping has a long history in metal fabrication. Walk the floor and you’ll see robotic press brakes and fiber laser cutting machines up to 15 kW in power, all complementing the stamping capabilities that launched the business back in 1972, when Tom and Josephine Kacmarcik founded it. Today, the Grafton, Wis.-based metal manufacturer has eight facilities and more than 600 employees.

The founders and the people working for them would have been astounded by some of the tech used today on the floor—though it wouldn’t be entirely foreign. Press brakes were in wide use in the ‘70s, after all. Laser cutting is a giant leap, especially with today’s modern fiber lasers, but the machines still cut metal parts out of sheets and plates, the same as plasma and flame cutting tables did back in the day.

What might amaze them even more is the technology now being used in the office, some of which are now just beginning to leverage the power of artificial intelligence. It’s technology Kapco’s founders couldn’t have imagined. And considering how quickly technology is advancing, the AI office revolution has only just begun.

AI in Metal Fabrication

Ross Boettcher, Kapco’s communications director, knows that AI adoption really is in its infancy, especially in the metal manufacturing arena. "Along with a lot of midsized manufacturers, we’re still in the very early stages of implementation of artificial intelligence throughout the business. We’re just now figuring out how much efficiency we can really draw from these tools."

Several years ago, when those in metal fabrication thought of AI, their minds turned to smart machines and scheduling. Could machines program and run themselves? Could AI tap into disparate data to transform the way metal fabricators operate?

That hasn’t happened yet, though some AIbased technology is starting to affect the way manufacturing software is built and sold. Coders are using AI-based technology for writing highly customized software, a feat that could have far-reaching implications in the complex world of high-product-mix manufacturing.

AI isn’t entirely absent, though. As Mike McManus, Kapco’s director of IT, and his team at Kapco are starting to find, generative AI tools—the algorithms that automate content production—could help the company streamline the way people in the office communicate and strategize. To investigate AI’s potential, the metal manufacturer has launched a cross-functional committee to share use cases and work through current challenges.

Good Processes, More Potential for AI

AI can’t magically turn chaos into order. Kapco’s existing processes in project management, new product introduction (NPI), and cross-enterprise communication set the foundation. As company sources explained, currently available AI tools could help refine and streamline present-day best practices. This starts with the initial contact with existing and potential customers. "We love to go on-site to their plants, look at the parts they process, and talk about how they align with our capabilities," McManus explained. "We then get into how we can help them on the front end. ‘Let’s take a look at your part. What kind of assembly is it in? Can we make that a different way? Are you welding something that we could fabricate as one piece?’"

He added that the experience goes deeper than just looking at a print and submitting a bid for work. Kapco’s sales team has delved into CRM (customer relationship management) platforms and related tools, tracking win rates and specific customer communications to ensure Kapco prioritizes tasks in the best way and no detail falls through the cracks.

Once the order is accepted, "it becomes a game of ERP optimization," McManus said. "How do we understand the terms of the order? How do we get the process through our factory? How do we align scheduling and purchasing?"

The company’s NPI team uses software like Trello to streamline project management, ensuring everyone sees what needs to happen to successfully launch a product—again, so nothing falls through the cracks. This gives visibility and traceability:

Who needs to do what, when, and in what sequence?

NPI teams complement this with process mapping exercises to make sure tasks are performed consistently, and identifying the internal suppliers and customers throughout the process map—that is, who needs to provide

whom

with

what

, and

when

?

Kapco Metal Stamping employees use generative AI to create the initial draft of a proposal. AI tools cannot automate content generation entirely, but they can at least help streamline the process.

Complementing all this is an analysis of customer order patterns, quantities, and demand trends. The idea is to look at the business holistically, break down silos, align production with customer demand, and not just produce everything at once to, say, achieve a better material price or stellar machine uptime. Running huge batches can benefit automated machinery uptime, but it might overwhelm downstream operations. Why move a mountain of work if it’s just going to sit in inventory?

"Related to all this, we’re seeing the best use cases for AI coming out of the front end," Mc- Manus said. "We had a lot of interest, and we made the choice to cultivate that interest. People are meeting, and they’re now starting to use tools inside and outside of work. In fact, one way to really learn about these tools is by using them outside of your day-to-day work.

"And it’s not just ChatGPT. How about Microsoft Copilot? How do we make use of multimedia tools that use video and imagery outside of producing text? We’re finding that the easiest way for us to adopt AI is to do so organically through those who are excited about it."

AI Use Cases

So, what are the use cases for AI in metal manufacturing? Some immediate potential, McManus said, is in business communication.

"AI tools today can help us produce a lot of content in little time. Say we want to justify the purchase of a new piece of equipment. How much time do we spend preparing our PowerPoint to go to our owner or executive team? I can measure that in hours. We might spend six hours on a presentation and run it by six or seven people for another hour. Well, what if I could eliminate those first six hours by having ChatGPT or Copilot produce the presentation?" The tools likely won’t eliminate that hour spent on review, refinement, and customization—but they still could take that presentation 80% of the way toward completion.

In one recent example, McManus’ team needed to prepare a presentation to teach the basics of cybersecurity. "Within seven minutes, someone on my team had a Power-Point ready, complete with speaker notes." He prompted the AI tool to build a 45-minute course, described the target audience, and sketched out basic elements of what should be included. "In less than 10 minutes, we had an engaging presentation ready for about 16 students, most of whom were from the shop floor."

Business Intelligence

"The next level will be seeing how AI gets embedded into your business intelligence systems," McManus said. "How do you let the AI engine start to make characterizations about the data it sees? It might start to lead you in new directions."

He described a scenario in which AI could monitor patterns in order activities. Say that every time the shop lands an order with a Tier 1 ag equipment supplier, a similar order comes from an OEM several weeks later. What’s the relationship between these two, and what opportunities could this present?

"As AI engines become integrated inside current BI [business intelligence] tools, such as Tableau, PowerBI, or Qlik, you could even link order activities to broader market conditions or commodity price trends," McManus said.

"We’ve already used AI in our forecasting, using certain tools for our three- to five-year business plans," Boettcher said, "helping us identify significant growth markets and collaborating between the sales and marketing team on strategies that would resonate for specific sectors. How can we communicate with those markets effectively, in a way that feels right, and ensure we’re meeting them where their challenges currently exist?"

McManus added that, when it comes to sales strategy research, how people instruct generative AI tools matters, especially when they crawl the web for information. "You never want to be too specific on where you want the generative AI to go find information. This sometimes puts you in a self-limiting box. You don’t want to say, ‘Check Bloomberg for the agriculture equipment market forecast.’ But you don’t want to underprompt the AI either. You want to say, ‘Check 10 major sources that can help provide a five-year forecast and give me the references on where you pulled it from.’"

Generative AI should never be in the driver’s seat. Sourcing, accuracy, and the human element matter. Otherwise, faulty insight from AI could drive the business in some detrimental directions.

Again, generative AI use cases are just emerging, and they’ll go well beyond producing a fancy PowerPoint. Ultimately, AI tools could uncover new business opportunities and help a metal fabricator navigate through ever-changing customer demands.

"The business intelligence aspect is going to be an interesting use case within the next 24 months," McManus said. "The technology is evolving and advancing so quickly, it really is incredible."
