The last few years have seen an explosion in new laser model launches, particularly with the recent advent of the fiber laser.

The laser can handle a variety of cutting tasks. These range from micrometre-precise cutting joints in paper-thin semiconductor chips to quality cuts in 3mm thick steel. Where the focused laser beam strikes the work piece, it heats the material so extremely that it melts or even vaporises. Once it has completely penetrated the work piece, the cutting process can start: the laser beam moves along the part contour, melting the material as it goes. Usually, a stream of gas blows the melted material downwards, out of the cut. The gap is barely wider than the focused laser beam itself.

In flame cutting, oxygen is used as the cutting gas. The oxygen is blown into the kerf at pressures of up to 6 bar. There, the heated metal reacts with the oxygen: it begins to burn and oxidises. The chemical reaction releases large amounts of energy – up to five times the laser energy – and assists the laser beam. Flame cutting makes it possible to cut at high speeds and handle jobs involving thick plates such as mild steel with thicknesses of over 30mm.

With fusion cutting, nitrogen or argon is used as the cutting gas. The gas is blown through the kerf at pressures ranging from 2 to 20 bar. Argon and nitrogen are inert gases and do not react with the molten metal in the kerf. They simply blow it out towards the bottom. Simultaneously, they shield the cut edge from the air. Cut edges are oxide-free and do not require further treatment. Nevertheless, the laser beam must supply all of the energy needed for cutting. For this reason, cutting speeds as high as those in flame cutting can be achieved only in thin sheets.

## A bird’s eye view

The last few years have seen an explosion in new laser model launches, particularly with the recent advent of the fiber laser. We chronicle some of the latest new global innovations here for you.

Amada UK recently celebrated a consolidated turnover of £51 million for the first time and launched no less than 12 products globally at the EuroBLECH exhibition in October. Amongst these were innovative laser products such as FLW series of robotic fibre laser welding systems which are fully programmable offline, the FLC 3015 AJ linear drive fibre laser cutting system and the latest addition to Amada’s punch/laser combination machine range, the ACIES 2515T.

Amada’s FOL-3015 AJ delivers axis speeds of 340m/minute and high quality laser cutting. The short wave fiber laser beam also provides an expanded process range for materials including copper, brass and titanium. Cut process monitoring continuously checks both the pierce and cutting processes in real-time. If laser problems such as plasma or material burn are detected, the system adjusts the cut data and, if necessary, will interrupt the cut. Cutting speeds of up to 100m/minute with 70 per cent electricity savings are offered.

At EuroBLECH 2012, Swiss specialist Bystronic displayed its BySprint Pro 3015 laser cutter with six kilowatts of power. Bystronic’s fiber laser cutting system, BySprint Fiber, is now available in the 4020 format.

“A 4×2m sheet allows 78 per cent more parts to be nested than a sheet with a format of three by one and a half metres. The time during which the machine can cut without interruption increases in the same ratio. Put another way: unproductive times are once again reduced, while the part output moves in a diametrically opposed direction. BySprint Fiber is also now available with both three as well as four kilowatts of power and hence approaches the sheet metal thickness range that until now was limited to CO2 laser cutting systems, and provides the user with even greater flexibility,” said the manufacturer.

Innovation number three at EuroBLECH for Bystronic was the ByAutonom. It was developed from the beginning as an autonomously working machine which, together with automation modules such as ByTrans, ByTower and Bycell, can be used for reduced-manning operations. If the type and thickness of the raw material has to be changed, the machine simply sets the applicable focal length and selects the appropriate nozzle, centres automatically and continues cutting.

Hypertherm, a manufacturer of advanced cutting systems, recently launched a new 3kW fiber laser system, the HyIntensity® Fiber Laser HFL030, with a recommended cut capacity of 20mm for mild steel, 12mm for stainless steel and 10mm for aluminium. See ISMR Product Reviews for further details.

A powerful 3kW fiber laser has been launched by global industrial laser manufacturer JK Lasers, part of the GSI Group. The new addition to the company’s Watt-to-kiloWatt fiber laser range will process thicker section metals at faster speeds and to higher standards. The JK3000FL features a consistent focused spot size and beam profile over the complete power range. It is fitted with JK Lasers’ detachable ‘plug in, pre-aligned’ (PIPA) beam delivery fibres, offering core diameters from 100µm, providing maximum versatility when processing difficult-to-access areas.

A new eX3015 4.5kW laser cutting machine from Mitsubishi Electric, supplied by laser cutting specialist World Machinery, has significantly improved manufacturing flexibility and capacity at PSS Ltd, a UK manufacturer of power steering components and other equipment for the commercial vehicle, construction and agricultural equipment markets. The company is currently using the machine to process up to three tonnes of material a day, including steel, stainless and plastic in thicknesses ranging from 1.5mm to 25mm.

“Now we are getting used to the machine, we are finding new jobs for it all the time,” said Richard Pratt of PSS. “We are increasingly using it to rough out parts like washers that might previously have been cut from bar on one of our CNC lathes. We are also finding that we can now produce various sheet components that we would previously have outsourced, which helps us to cut the number of parts we need to keep in stock, and reduces our input costs.”

Engineers have installed over 650 total Laserdyne systems, 499 multi-axis systems, and 309 systems dedicated to aerospace / defence manufacturing worldwide. In addition, systems are used for laser processing automotive, electronics, power, fine mechanics and medical devices. Laserdyne Systems provide a variety of multi-axis laser machining systems designed for demanding drilling, cutting and welding applications. Products include 2D and 3D motion systems with a range of options for the specific application and production requirements. Lasers incorporated include CO2, pulsed Nd:YAG and fiber lasers.

## New process options

LVD has introduced a process control system engineered to automatically optimise laser cutting results. The patented Adaptive Laser Cutting system (ALC) uses dynamic feedback to monitor and regulate laser power, speed and assist gas pressure in real-time during the cutting process, automatically optimising cutting parameters and helping to ensure a consistently accurate cut, high productivity and reduced scrap. Unlike other process controls systems, ALC does not rely on pre-defined parameters but measures and adapts in-process to achieve the best cutting results.

LVD also introduced Electra FL, a new highspeed fiber laser cutting system, at EuroBLECH 2012. It can process traditional sheet metal materials such as mild steel, stainless steel

Salvagnini has demonstrated cutting filmcovered sheet metal in a single step on its laser systems. The metal sheets were covered by two different protective films; both films had already been tested by a number of Salvagnini customers and demonstrated a number of interesting characteristics, particularly in terms of quality and performance. More importantly, they could also be used with both fiber and CO2 lasers.

During laser welding, particles in the metal vapour plume can cause a deviation of the laser beam. They also absorb laser power. Both can lead to inconsistent welding depth and irregular seams. It is to suppress the plume and optimise application results that TRUMPF has developed a special coaxial nozzle.

In addition to ensuring welding depth and constant surface quality, the new nozzle ensures higher precision and optimised use of laser power. Operating costs are also reduced through the increased service life of the protection glass. Trial results are impressive. The new nozzle prevents up to 22 per cent less original laser power loss and up to 40 per cent greater welding depth, dependent upon process gas and speed. It also ensures a reduction in weld spatter on material surfaces.

Also new is the TRUMPF cassette module, another laser welding option for manufacturers wishing to safeguard their TRUMPF OEM laser or TruLaser Robot system. This unit is designed to increase the lifetime of both the optics and the protection glass. It also reduces the risk of contamination by introducing an air flow to blow away any debris from optics when changing the protection glass.

TRUMPF also unveiled several innovations at Photonics West. On display were two units from the field of diode lasers, the TruDiode 151 and the TruDiode 301. These two new units, generating 150 and 300 watts, are suitable for selective soldering and for welding thin-gauge sheet metal.

At EuroBLECH 2012, TRUMPF unveiled the TruLaser Cell 3000 to the general public for the first time, where the new five-axis processing centre was seen operating in a process chain with a TruBend 7036 and a TruMark Station 7000.
