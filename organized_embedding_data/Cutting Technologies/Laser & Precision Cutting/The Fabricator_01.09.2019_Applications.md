3D laser’s speed, accessibility increase fabricator’s productivity tenfold

Situation

Kryton Engineered Metals, co-founded in 1981 as Iowa Metal Spinners Inc. by current owner Kevin Harberts, is a job shop providing such services as metal spinning, laser cutting, and fully finished metal fabricating from two locations in Cedar Falls, Iowa.

"We have increased our fabrication capacity to support the spinning process," explained Bret Clikeman, engineer. "We went from a small handspin shop to robotics and automation. We started doing 5-axis laser work to support our spinning products about six years ago to trim the parts from the spinner and also add holes and contours. Today nearly 30 percent of our products need laser work."

The company’s 5-axis laser machine was not meeting its growing needs. The laser’s manufacturer informed Kryton that it no longer would offer maintenance support, and the laser had only a single bay and was too slow to keep up with production needs.

"We grew by 20 percent last year, and we were feeling the heat for more capacity," said Clikeman. "You can only open the door and process the part so fast. When you’re opening the door and loading a part, the laser isn’t running parts. We were looking for a machine that could load a part while it was running a part.

"So we were forced to start looking for another 3D laser because we could not send out 30 percent of our parts to a subcontractor."

Resolution

After much research and 3D laser comparisons, Kryton chose the Prima Power Laser Next 2130. It offered a very fast turntable and good part accessibility—two important features to meet the company’s increasing production requirements.

"We did a huge amount of research prior to purchasing the laser," Clikeman explained. "So we could compare the different lasers side by side, we reviewed all technical aspects of the lasers, including service and training. The Laser Next was installed in November of 2018. It is an impressive piece of equipment. I really like the tremendous capacity of the turntable design. We can do an outside diameter of 63 in. It has opened up other doors for us. We now have new customers coming to us because we have this capacity … and we have this added capacity to sell."

"Comparing the older laser we had before to what we have now with the Laser Next is like going from the industrial age to the internet age," added Ian Davis, utility supervisor. "It has improved our productivity tenfold. With the Laser Next dual cutting tables, we are able to pull a part while another part is running. That is a huge improvement for us."

"We were running much cruder equipment in the past," said Clikeman. "Today we are now selling extra capacity because it gobbles up the work as fast as we can feed it.

"We are cutting aluminum now and we are using the high-pressure air cutting, and there is no burr. We don’t have to do any secondary deburring operations. That is a huge savings."

Durable part identification tags help fabricator track orders

Situation

North Alabama Fabricating Co. (NAFCO) is no stranger to large-scale fabrication. Its work involves engineering, fabricating, and installing heavy structural steel, bins, tanks, and industrial ductwork for complex projects around the world. It’s also no stranger to finishing processes that can wreak havoc on part markings. Many of the components it fabricates are galvanized after fabrication; many others are shotblasted and painted.

These processes often cover or remove the identification labels the company needs to track pieces during and after fabrication.

NAFCO had a marking system that used printed identification tags, but the company found that it needed three tags per item. Another drawback was the printer’s speed, which became a bottleneck.

Because the tags survive the entire production process, NAFCO now uses just one tag per item.

Resolution

NAFCO was familiar with KettleTag

®

PLUS, an identification system used for galvanizing developed by InfoSight Corp., and decided to try it out. NAFCO purchased several identification tags from its galvanizer and they performed well.

Based on this assessment, the company purchased a LabeLase

®

1000 printer, the tags for galvanizing, and ShotTagTM for components that would be blasted and painted. Although NAFCO had not tried the latter tag type, it also proved to be a durable option, withstanding the preparatory blasting for the paint booth. After shotblasting, the tags were intact, readable, and scannable.

Because the tags survive the entire production process, NAFCO now uses just one tag per item. The tags include bar codes, so when shop personnel pull a piece to work on, they can scan it into the system. This saves time that would be spent writing down the identifying number or keying it into the system, eliminating the risk of misreading handwriting or making a typo.
