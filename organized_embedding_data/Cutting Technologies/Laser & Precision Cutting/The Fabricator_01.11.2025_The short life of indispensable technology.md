Equipment upgrade cycles are getting shorter

ne of our fiber lasers may be obsolete. Five years ago, we bought it new from a wellknown and respected sheet metal laser manufacturer. Unbeknownst to us, a critical optical component in the laser head, manufactured by a third-party company, is no longer in production. It’s a part that sometimes needs to be replaced, and with a limited existing supply of refurbished units, there’s a good chance that it will simply become irreplaceable in the future.

To date, the machine has produced excellent value for us and our customers, and I expect that we’ll get many more years out of it. Even so, it makes me worry.

The Marvels of Modern Equipment

Modern equipment enables incredible productivity and simplicity. All of our press brakes are hyperaccurate, with extremely tight control of ram motion, backgauge placement, safety, and even automated bend measurement and correction. Combined with our in-house offline programming software, it’s extremely easy for us to crank out thousands of SKUs, even in quantities of one, every single week. Our operators are great at what they do, but on these modern machines, the learning curve is manageable. Results are repeatable without arcane black magic.

Our five lasers are similar. An operator can learn the ropes in a day and develop deep expertise in months. Operators rarely if ever interact with G-code, and automated nesting and programming software make it efficient to queue and release jobs. Visual interfaces create clarity; nozzle changers and staging decks streamline changeovers; and the lasers monitor their own cut conditions, generating warnings or errors if anything seems off. These machines are extraordinarily productive when they are running. Put together, I understand that running these modern fiber lasers is a piece of cake compared to older CO

Challenges of Modern Technology

Even so, the stack of hardware and software that makes these modern machines tick has intrinsically less permanence compared to old machines. Most components of high-powered laser cutters can’t be serviced manually, so we rely heavily on a healthy vendor relationship to keep things running. When parts are out of stock or are discontinued, there is very little recourse. Meanwhile, these machines are extremely complex, and many things can go wrong. They have a laser source, optics, cooling systems, servos and controllers, power supplies, transformers, PLCs, built-in control computers, and touchscreen monitors. Any single component can bring a machine down.

Valentina Burdina/iStock/Getty Images Plus

Gone are the days when you could buy a machine, depreciate it over 10 years, and then continue minting dollars with it for another two or three decades.

The software layer on these machines can also be a cause for concern. Our newest laser had an odd behavior where it would sometimes fail to turn the laser off during rapid moves. It was caused by some kind of software glitch, which was eventually fixed by the manufacturer with a software update.

But vendors don’t control all, or even most, of the software running on their machines. One of our lasers runs a build of Linux internally, which has proven stable so far. But some of our other lasers run Windows under the hood—a version of Windows that won’t be supported by Microsoft forever, and that may have unfixed security vulnerabilities. It’s important to keep these machines isolated, especially given the prevalence of ransomware and other attacks targeting manufacturers.

It’s breathtaking. The sum of software and hardware that powers modern manufacturing equipment is incomprehensibly complex. It’s a wonder that it works as well as it does.

In contrast, we also run an old Bridgeport manual mill. It’s already more than a decade old, and I’m certain that with proper care, it’ll last many more decades. Its lack of a CNC isn’t much of a drawback for its use-case, but even if it were, CNC retrofits are common and relatively inexpensive. Good iron keeps, and there’s something comforting about that.

Of course, old manual machines may last, but they are less capable. A machinist with decades of experience can work magic on a manual mill and lathe, but it’s hard to match the productivity and capability of a good CNC machine. This highlights another factor driving the practical lifetime of manufacturing equipment: Obsolescence. It doesn’t matter that solid iron can outlive your grandkids. If a machine can’t produce value competitively, it may as well be retired.

Machine Advancements Accelerate

Obsolescence affects modern machines at an ever-increasing rate. When we bought most of our fiber lasers, 10 kW was the peak power available, the state of the art. Now it’s possible to buy machines boasting laser powers of 20 kW and beyond. They cut extremely fast, of course, but their massive power also gives them a wider "sweet spot," a better capability to cut even thick materials predictably and with great quality. Shops with the latest equipment have an advantage: better speed, better quality, thicker materials, and more capacity per square foot.

Gone are the days when you could buy a machine, depreciate it over 10 years, and then continue minting dollars with it for another two or three decades. How long will modern machines last? It’s honestly an open question. Some equipment is just too new to have a proven track record, and effective lifetime will vary depending on the equipment, markets served, pace of technological development, and the competitive landscape.

Factor In the Upgrade Cycle

At OSH Cut, we expect to replace all of our machines every 10 years—not all at once, of course. On top of capacity growth, replacement capital expenditures are a permanent facet of our financial plan. I’ve heard of other companies replacing machines as often as every seven years.

When machines can cost millions of dollars, depreciation can become a significant (and often overlooked) drag on the bottom line. I was chatting with another shop owner who was looking at tooling up with a bunch of modern machines: a laser, press brakes, finishing equipment. I ran some napkin math with him to find that on a 10-year lifetime, he’d incur nearly $20,000 per month of depreciation expense, on top of interest payments, consumables, and maintenance and repair costs. That makes sense; these machines easily produce well beyond that in gross profit if they are running. But for this smaller operation, $20,000 per month of added expense would have put them in the red without significant new growth.

If the machines really had to be replaced after 10 years, that depreciation expense would become very painful down the road. The owner ended up buying a subset of lower-cost machines in the short term. Probably a smart move.

When exploring new manufacturing equipment, the machine lifetime and the vendor’s support infrastructure should figure heavily into the decision. Shiny, new features are cool, and they can make a huge difference. But how reliable are they? If automation breaks, can you still make parts? How many machines are in the market, and how long have they been around? How many support technicians serve your region, and how quickly can they be on-site if your equipment is down? If a machine going down stops production, you can’t cut corners. Vendor and technology maturity matter.

For this reason, we tend to buy machines that are just a little behind the leading edge. If a vendor has a machine line that has all the newest bells and whistles, we prefer to order the next-best equipment that includes features that have been around for at least five years and that have been proven by hundreds or thousands of users over the years. We feel like this often represents a good tradeoff between leading-edge performance, longevity, and reliability.

If the machine represents a single point of failure, we err even more on the side of proven technologies. These more proven machines also tend to be a little cheaper, which mitigates the reality that they will probably need to be replaced in a decade or less.

Investing in modern machines is indispensable today. They enable such amazing productivity that it can be hard to compete without them. Faster machines yield more revenue per square foot, minimizing fixed operating expenses. They leverage automation to reduce labor costs and are often easier to operate, making it easier to hire and train operators. All these wins are counterbalanced by the fact that they can have a limited lifetime, perhaps as short as seven to 10 years.

Be sure to consider all these factors, especially if you are contemplating a new equipment purchase. Believe me, they really matter.
