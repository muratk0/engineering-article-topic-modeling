# FOCUS ON PLASMA CUTTING

[TARİH: 01.05.2018 ISMR]

Plasma cutters are used to perform cutting and gouging operations, with the average handheld system capable of cutting a maximum metal thickness of about one inch. Plasma typically requires a source for compressed air and a substantial amount of electrical power. One of plasma’s greatest advantages is its ability to cut non-ferrous metals such as aluminium, stainless steel and cast iron.

Speed and precision cutting are also benefits of plasma, which typically cuts with minimal slag and can provide smooth cuts with a narrower kerf than that produced by an oxy-fuel torch. Plasma does not require the metal to be preheated before cutting, which saves time, and plasma cutters can also outperform oxy-fuel torches when cutting stacked metals. Faster speeds can be achieved on thinner metals with plasma, with minimal metal distortion. Plasma systems are also relatively simple to use compared to oxy-fuel systems, with the benefit of minimal clean-up. Plasma cutting of stainless steel is one of the most frequently used technologies in sheet metal working.

Plasma cutting systems can offer benefits for cutting thinner ferrous and non-ferrous metals, including shaped metals – angles, channels and tubes, for example. Plasma cutters are also better able to cut large volumes of thin sheet metal and offer rapid grate-cutting capability. They do not require storing, handling explosive gases or dealing with an open flame, resulting in a safer job-site.

## An eye on cost

In addition to the quality of the plasma cut, users attach high importance to the costs per cutting metre to maintain the efficient operation of their production. Factors include cutting speed, gas consumption and the lifetime of consumables (e.g. gas usage, consumable nozzles, electrodes, garnet abrasive etc.) plus utilities (electricity, water) etc.

In some cases, amortised capital equipment cost may also be included as well as labour cost (such as for loading fresh sheets or unloading cut parts and scrap). Productivity or cut speed is the biggest influence on the real cost per part.

The quality of cut surfaces when using thermal cutting processes is determined by the rectangularity and inclination tolerance and the average surface roughness. Additionally, the groove lag, the initial fusing of the upper edge and possible dross or drop formation at the bottom cutting edge of the workpiece can be taken into consideration.

In conclusion, plasma provides a strong mix of cut quality, productivity and operating costs for mild steel, stainless steel and aluminium across a range of thicknesses.

## Benefits of plasma cutting

- High flexibility when cutting all electrically conductive materials.
- Low requirements on material quality and working environment.
- High cutting speeds when cutting thin and medium-sized plates (up to ten times higher compared to oxy-fuel cutting).
- Higher cutting speeds when cutting medium-sized and thick materials compared to laser cutting.
- Materials can be cut nearly dross-free (no after-treatment required).
- Very competitive when cutting medium-sized and thick high-alloyed steel and aluminium plates.
- Suitable for cutting thin and mediumsized mild steel (up to 30mm).
- Cutting of high-tensile mild steel with low heat input.
- Very good automation capabilities.
- Underwater plasma cutting for very low heat warpage of the workpiece and low noise levels in the working environment.

## Disadvantages of plasma cutting

- Limited use for materials with a thickness from 0.5mm up to 160mm (at present).
- Can produce a lot of fumes.
- The arc is very bright.
- Can be noisy when cutting thick materials.
- Slightly broader kerf compared to laser cutting.
