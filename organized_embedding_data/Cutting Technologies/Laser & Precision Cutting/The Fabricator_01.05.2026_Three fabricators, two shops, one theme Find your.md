Even before Solomon Brenneman and Jesse Springer launched TubeLabs in Rockingham, Va., a little more than one year ago, they thought that if they could just land some huge OEM contracts—the kind that would keep their lone tube laser busy for days or weeks at a time—they’d see positive cash flow in a year’s time.

Turns out they were only half right.

GO SMALL TO GO BIG

What the duo discovered early on was that it can take weeks or even months just to find the right contact person at an OEM, let alone quote or land an actual job. As a two-person operation that had essentially bet all its chips on one machine, Springer and Brenneman didn’t have that kind of time.

So, even before they signed the paperwork—personal guarantees and all—for their used (read, inexpensive) 2-kW BLM LT5.10 tube laser, they bought some lists and started cold-calling hundreds of possible clients near their facility in the Shenandoah Valley. What they found was, instead of those OEM jobs, they just might be able to make a go of it with smaller shops that needed the one thing they had.

"Tube lasers are still not being adopted widescale," said Springer, who was Brenneman’s boss at a warehousing and logistics company before the two took the plunge into fabrication. "It’s not like tube laser technology is brandnew; we just think there’s so much room left on the adoption curve. Our thought was, ‘This will be unique, and we can maybe serve a more regional customer base.’"

Brenneman said that while the chain of command at OEMs is long (making relationships hard to establish), the right people at fab shops—estimators and purchasers and engineers—all tend to work close or even next to each other. That makes for easier inroads at business partners looking for tube work done quickly.

"There’s just a lot of fabricators out there that could make use of a tube laser, but they can’t quite afford one," said Brenneman, whose family owns an aluminum trailer company in Indiana. "A lot of shops are tight on space and big, long tubes are really difficult to maneuver.

They just want kitted parts showing up ready to weld together. "The thing that’s been great about that for us is we’re trying to make an entrance into the market really quick. We need sales fast, and we need to build relationships fast, and these fabricators are down for it. We will fill that kind of gap in the value chain that’s there at the moment."

FILLING ANOTHER GAP

More than 530 miles away from the Shenandoah Valley, another small fabricator is filling a different gap in the market—parts and repair work for the stainless pipe and tubing used by food and beverage processors.

Of course, large and established businesses already command the lion’s share of that market. But that’s where Steve Feamster and his team of five at Sanitary Welding in the Detroit suburb of Novi, Mich., come in.

"The bigger guys are doing what I’m doing, but they’re all booked," said Feamster, who has been running Sanitary Welding for the past 10 years. "The big guys locally, they’re $250 million in revenue on the small side. I have the advantage of speed. I’ve seen places with 52-week lead times. I can do it in two weeks."

Feamster said with more than a little angst that when he’s called to fix a leak at a large food processing facility, it’s quite often "a symptom of a much larger problem"—a problem that keeps business coming through his door, of course, but that should be cause for deep concern at a higher level.

"There’s bad equipment out there, bad maintenance on sanitary systems that have been making our country’s food," Feamster said, citing many personal experiences. "The old guard who took care of equipment is retiring. They’re old factories and companies trying to hang onto it. Sometimes they can’t afford to do the right thing. They almost have to build new because they can’t take care of the old."

For example, during the COVID-19 pandemic, a shortage of baby formula could be traced not to a run on the product at stores but rather to poor weld quality and preventive maintenance at one of the main facilities that makes it for the U.S. market. That maintenance—clean in place procedures—can be hard to do. But when the Food and Drug Administration is telling many of those factories to fix pipes and tanks or get shut down, it means business for people like Feamster who are willing to jump into nasty tanks and do tedious TIG welding to code in environments where others won’t go.

"They won’t change unless they’re forced to," Feamster said of some of the food processing plants he’s encountered. "Thank God for the FDA and what they do.

"It’s a moral responsibility. There’s where I am now—trying to teach my guys the same thing. I take pride in that. What we fix affects the community."

TAKING THE NEXT STEP

At a practical level, meeting the demand for ultrapure stainless weldments has come to mean more than repair work for Sanitary Welding. It means a greater push toward manufacturing products (mainly out of stainless 304 and 316) like tanks, pumps, flowmeter temperature sensors, and manifolds for fluid distribution.

Feamster is also looking at how to capture larger contracts that he wasn’t able to pursue when the business was just himself—federal contracts for cybersecurity facilities, for instance.

"We have go into the industries where the growth is," Feamster said. "We’re trying to lead where other welders are not focusing as much."

Brenneman and Springer were targeting April of this year to turn a profit, and as of February, they had already met the goal. They could have spent time celebrating, but never ones to idle long, they’re already looking at what future expansion will look like.

With both of them juggling the responsibilities of families and a startup business, they could look at a second machine right away, especially since they got such a great deal on their current one. But that’s probably not in the cards.

For one, they can do a huge number of different jobs within the 4.5-in.-square limits of their current tube laser. But second, they haven’t exactly had lots of time to establish credibility with a would-be lender.

"There’s going to be this weird noman’s land that we hit between our first and second machine where we have customers breathing down our necks for more parts or shorter lead times," Brenneman said. "We don’t have the capacity to do it, but we’re too early for a bank to go, ‘OK, you guys can afford a machine that’s four times the price of the one you have.’ That is going to be a high step to make.

"So, we’re talking about ways to be able to bridge that gap—like a night shift. One thing I like about our business model is that we have one machine. So, especially if you would take the easiest parts we have to run, the contract stuff that is just the same thing over and over again, and put it on the night shift, you can hire somebody that’s pretty new and doesn’t have a lot of experience but just likes to work nights and shows up on time. You could train them how to run those parts, then leave the difficult stuff for day shift with your better-trained guys while you’re there. So, that’s our plan for [adding] more capacity. I think that will bridge the gap between machine one and two."
