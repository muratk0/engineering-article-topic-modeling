# The match game: Choosing the right saw blades for your tube and pipe

Tube Cutting

How to pair tube materials and wall thickness with blade varieties and tooth counts

By

Lincoln Brunner

Tubes and pipes present different cutting dynamics from all other material shapes. Knowing how to select the right cutting blades is key.

L.S. Starrett Co.

F

or fabricators sawing metal tube and pipe, achieving the goals of high cut quality and long blade life comes down to a few key factors. The Tube and Pipe Journal recently talked to three industry pros who shared their insights on band saw and circular cold saw cutting, and all noted just how important tooth count, blade speed, and blade material are—and specifically why matching those characteristics to the material you’re cutting can make the difference between a good cut and a production line shutdown. Virtually all blade makers and dealers will gladly supply customers with charts and guides to help shops choose wisely, but knowing the basics by rote is still a must for operators trying to move material productively.

"The correct blade under ideal conditions—meaning good coolant, solid clamping, and proper speeds and feeds—means the difference between success and failure," said Jason Schofield, cold saw expert and customer service representative for Scotchman Metal Fabricating Solutions, a maker of saws, punches, and other fabrication equipment. "The right blade will provide a good quality cut with long blade life. The wrong blade may not even make one cut—and in the worst case, the blade will break and becomes scrap metal."

Of special note here is that a shop can have all the right blades, but mounting them poorly or placing them on an unstable or poorly maintained saw that vibrates or allows blades to wobble or drift will nullify any benefit those properly chosen blades provide.

"Blade life is heavily influenced by machine condition, setup, and blade selection, not just the blade itself," said Richard Otter, vice president of sales at Tru-Cut Saw Inc., a maker of circular saw blades. "Straightness and rigidity are very critical, along with proper coolant, correct speed and feed rate for the material being cut, tooth pitch matched to wall thickness, stable clamping, [and] avoiding excessive feed pressure during interrupted cuts."

MATCHING BLADES TO SHAPES

Because tube profiles change multiple times during fabrication, cutting them differs qualitatively from solid bar or other shapes in that they naturally produce instability during the process. Cutting tube and pipe effectively means moving chips efficiently out of the cutting zone and not allowing them to accumulate either in the kerf or on the blade itself—all while accounting for those constant changes in profile.

"Tubes and pipes present a continuously varying wall thickness throughout the cut," said Felipe Arntsen, product manager for saw blades and hand power tool accessories at The L.S. Starrett Co., a manufacturer of band saw blades for metal and many other materials. "This means the number of teeth in contact with the material changes constantly, directly affecting chip load per tooth, heat generation, and vibration risk."

Shops that cut tube and pipe must use blades that can adapt to those transitions without sacrificing stability—otherwise, they’ll see premature tooth wear and off-square cuts.

"Tube and pipe cutting is different because the blade is constantly entering and exiting material," Otter said. "That interrupted cutting action creates vibration and impact-loading on the teeth. Solid bar provides continuous engagement, which is generally more stable. Structural shapes and channels create intermittent engagement, but tube and pipe introduces even more variation because wall thickness and contact area change continuously throughout the cut."

That means shops cutting tube and pipe must choose blades with tooth geometries that reduce impact loading and tooth counts that closely match the wall thickness of the tubes being cut, Otter said. While some blade manufacturers recommend that three or four teeth be in constant contact with the workpiece, some go as high as six.

Regardless, it’s incumbent on shops to mind wall thickness carefully. Thinner walls need more, and perhaps more finely pitched, teeth to prevent tooth snagging, while thicker walls need coarser pitches that provide better chip clearance, Arntsen said.

"Wall thickness is one of the most important variables in blade selection for tube and pipe," he explained. "Variable-pitch blades are particularly effective here because they reduce vibration and distribute cutting forces more evenly as wall contact changes throughout the cut."

On thicker materials in particular, it’s important to select blades that move a lot of material out of the gullet quickly. That not only prevents the blade from gumming up, but it also makes for a cleaner cut. Bevels can complicate the equation even more, especially with thick-walled pipes. Even with saw blades, nuance plays a role.

"Cutting it at an angle makes this even worse, as the blade sees this as an even larger and thicker-wall tube," Schofield said. "The saw blade ends up being a compromise. It must be coarse enough to cut through the top and bottom so it doesn’t plug the teeth, but it must be fine enough not to hook a tooth on the wall thickness of the tube, as that can break the tip of the tooth off."

Having a power-down feed on your saw helps provide a more consistent feed rate, Schofield added.

MATCHING BLADES TO WORKPIECE MATERIAL

Blades come in a variety of compositions, from general use to those designed specifically for certain materials.

Band Saws.

On the band saw side, categories to consider are bimetal blades that can process a variety of tube materials (under the right conditions), carbide-tipped blades that handle higher temperatures than their bimetal brethren, carbon steel blades for light-duty work, and carbide-and diamond-grit blades that cut challenging exotic and abrasive materials with an extremely hard, clustered grit edge rather than conventional saw teeth.

Arntsen detailed several examples that underscored the need for band saw users to understand just what they’re cutting.

Rotary saw blades, like their bandsaw counterparts, must be maintained in a clean environment and paired properly with workpieces to ensure maximum blade life.

For example:

•

Carbon (mild) steel

is the most forgiving, so a bimetal band saw blade with a positive rake and variable pitch usually does the trick, as long as it’s paired with the right wall thickness.

•

Stainless steel

work-hardens quickly, Arntsen noted, so blades must cut aggressively without rubbing. "A positive rake angle is essential, and feed rate matters more than most shops realize—too light, and the blade skims the surface, accelerating work-hardening."

•

Hard nickel alloys

usually require carbide-tipped blades to sustain cutting performance, as opposed to high-speed steel, which wears out faster.

•

Aluminum

, which is soft and gummy comparatively, often lends itself to chip welding and gullet packing—coarser pitches, larger gullets, and high positive rakes help mitigate this.

•

Titanium

, the trickiest and least forgiving common material of the bunch, is both heat-sensitive and susceptible to work-hardening like stainless. Carbide blades are the go-to here, and constant feed and proper cutting fluids are absolute must-haves.

•

Circular Saws

. Many varieties of circular cold saw blades are available, including high-speed steel blades that provide high hardness and wear resistance, carbide- or tungsten carbide-tipped blades designed for low- and medium-alloy and nonferrous metals, cermet-tipped blades for better finishes on carbon steels, and friction-cut blades. Many standard circular cold saw blades often come with a black oxide coating.

Titanium nitride (TiN)-coated (or PVD-coated) blades are common for circular saws, said Schofield, because they keep material from sticking to the blades, thereby extending blade life. As Otter pointed out, the coating is so helpful that his company routinely offers it for customers looking for general-purpose blades.

However,

there is no such thing

as a true all-purpose blade that performs optimally in all situations, chiefly because so much of a blade’s effectiveness truly depends on the material being cut. When Otter and his team are asked for such a blade, they propose a product with standard thickness and a tooth count based on the percentages of material the shop cuts—say, 75% stainless and 25% mild steel. In that case, they simply point the customer toward a blade specifically made to cut stainless.

"We always let them know there is a trade-off," Otter said. "A true applications-specific blade will almost always outperform a general-purpose blade in either speed, finish, or life."

Schofield emphasized one key point: Shops must use blades with the proper number of teeth for the material size.

"In some situations, you can use a single blade to cut different materials, if they are a match for the blade," Schofield said. "If you are doing production cutting—many parts cut from the same sized material—one blade configuration may be all you need.

"If you have a metal fabricating shop where you have to cut several different sizes during the day, you should have a small assortment of blades on hand—usually three or four blades with different numbers of teeth will do, from fine to coarse. And the saw operator should be educated on which blade to use with each size of material."

A NOTE ABOUT MAINTENANCE

Choosing the right blade is one thing; maintaining it through its longest possible lifespan is another. Scotchman’s Schofield noted a few common mistakes or negligence-related problems in the upkeep of circular blades.

Dirty Flange.

Dirty flanges occur when operators fail to keep blades and mounting surfaces clean—specifically when they neglect to clear chips between blades and the drive spindle. With enough chip buildup, blades begin to wobble, which keeps them from running true.

Backlash.

Because the pins on a cold saw mount are smaller than the holes in blades, operators must rotate the blade up against the drive pins and hold it steady while tightening the flange bolt. If a blade is mounted improperly, backlash can occur when it inevitably catches in the material it’s cutting, causing the drive pins to slam into the pin holes and shatter the blade.

Pickup.

When material adheres to the blade, it tends to get "picked up" on both sides, making the blade thicker and causing more and more material to bond to the blade. Eventually, it causes a jerking motion while cutting—a sure signal that the blade needs sharpening.

"One of the biggest things shops overlook is that blade performance is not only based on the manufacture of the blade but the operator and machines," Otter said. "The best blade in the world cannot compensate for poor machine maintenance, weak clamping, incorrect feeds and speeds, or poor coolant.

"Another important point is that blade cost alone is misleading. Shops should evaluate total cost per cut. A premium blade that costs more upfront often delivers lower total production cost because it cuts faster, lasts longer, and reduces downstream labor."
