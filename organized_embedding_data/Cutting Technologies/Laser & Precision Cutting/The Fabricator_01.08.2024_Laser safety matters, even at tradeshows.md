# Laser safety matters, even at tradeshows

[TARİH: 01.08.2024 The Fabricator]

Safety

There’s no better way to demonstrate proper respect for fiber lasers than to hold exhibitors to industry-accepted standards

By

Dan Davis

Looks can be deceiving. Even though a fiber laser cutting machine looks to be operating as it should, is it operating with all the right safety measures in place?

Puchit/iStock/Getty Images Plus

T

he emergence of fiber laser technology in the metal fabricating industry has dramatically changed what it means to be an efficient producer of metal parts. But while most of the focus over the years has been on cutting speed, edge quality, parts sorting, and eliminating downstream bottlenecks, more attention needs to be paid to safety.

It turns out that not all laser cutting systems are created equally, and while the majority of systems have a long history of safe operation, some of the equipment now available on the market has been found to exhibit safety shortcomings. This has been especially evident at some recent tradeshows. Laser safety glass, which keeps the infrared light inside the cutting chamber, has sometimes been missing from machines. Laser cutting equipment has even operated with enclosure doors open. Close inspection of ventilation systems reveals that they aren’t even hooked up properly. These are poor practices that the organizers of FABTECH do not endorse.

The incidents have been numerous enough in the past that organizers of FABTECH, North America’s largest sheet metal fabricating tradeshow, have worked with a laser safety officer (LSO) in recent years to ensure that the risk of injury related to poor laser cutting machine design and operations is greatly minimized for both tradeshow attendees and exhibitors. The Fabricator spoke with Steven Glover, the LSO for FABTECH shows and a former leader at The Laser Institute, to learn just what is happening at the tradeshow and what metal fabricators might want to keep in mind as they continue to purchase more powerful lasers and expand their use.

The Fabricator:

Can you explain your role with FABTECH?

Steven Glover:

I’ve been doing the FABTECH shows in Canada, Mexico, and the U.S. now for a couple of years, and I’ve certainly learned a lot. I’ve also witnessed some bad practices, and that’s what we are trying to correct.

FABTECH show organizers require that exhibitors adhere to American National Standards Institute recommendations for safe use of lasers, or ANSI Z136.1. And, of course, you can get the ANSI standard through The Laser Institute, which is an amazing organization that focuses on laser safety. I encourage anyone purchasing a laser to contact LIA and figure out how they can operate it safely.

We’re trying to bring that same sense of responsibility and vigilance to FABTECH. In the past, there have been some exhibitors who either are not taking the time to be safe or just don’t seem to have the level of concern about safety that we would like. We want to change that.

As far as safety goes, we want to ensure that everything is operated within a proper enclosure. We want all attendees and exhibitors to remain safe. So when people do not adhere to the safety standards, we will require them to shut down their machines and they will remain inactive during the duration of the exhibition.

Steven Glover

Fab:

What are some of the things that you’ve seen that would be classified as unsafe practices?

Glover:

I’ve seen some manufacturers use glass that’s not suitable for laser cutting. In one instance, Plexiglass was used instead of proper shielding. I’ve actually seen where they’ve tampered with the lockout so that they can operate the laser while the doors are open. I’ve seen people climb up into the enclosure while the laser is on.

There’s been some interesting examples of unsafe laser use, but obviously we work very hard to mitigate those instances and make sure that they don’t continue to occur.

In fact, I would add that the show itself and the show partners do a fantastic job of understanding that safety is of utmost importance, and they make sure that we follow and adhere to the industry’s safety standards.

Fab:

Can you describe what might happen if one of these fiber lasers were to penetrate an unguarded human eye?

Glover:

If a laser operating at high power or an unsafe wavelength were to penetrate an unprotected eye, it can lead to permanent eye damage. Some examples of damage include visual impairment, spots, blurred vision, and floaters.

That’s why this is a serious subject. Attendees deserve to be safe, so we need to ensure that they are safe.

Fab:

Can someone easily identify an enclosure that prevents any laser beam from exiting the cutting area?

Glover:

That’s somewhat of a trick question because we do have cases where a manufacturer has copied some other manufacturer’s machine design, trying to make their systems look like they are safe, when in reality they’re not proper enclosures.

Fab:

Is there a way someone can determine whether the glass they’re looking at on a laser cutting machine is the correct glass, designed to keep the laser from escaping the cutting area?

Glover:

We do have equipment that can test the glass on these devices. We’ll be using it at FABTECH in Orlando, Fla.

With our equipment we can measure what wavelengths are allowed to escape through the glass. We will be able to determine if the glass is blocking harmful wavelengths and will not allow operation if it is determined not to be blocking effectively.

Fab:

What else can a metal fabricator do to enhance safety efforts after purchasing a laser cutting machine?

Glover:

I highly recommend someone on their staff taking courses to become a laser safety officer. An organization like The Laser Institute has a lot to offer as far as courses, classes, and even free information on their website. All of that would help get somebody ready for having a laser in their facility.

Fab:

What does having a laser safety officer on staff do for a company?

Glover:

It certainly mitigates your risk for sure, and that’s important. At the end of the day, the most important thing is keeping everyone safe.

As an employer, I’d want everyone operating the machinery correctly. In general, having a laser safety officer on-site is going help make sure that a company is going to avoid any accidents that could injure an employee and protect the company from being liable for that injury.

The laser safety officer is going to know what personal protective equipment is needed and when to replace it. The training courses also cover the different types of lasers, the different classes, and explain the hazards related to each one.

I highly encourage any company that’s buying a laser to invest in safety training, as it is a very small investment, all things considered.

Also keep in mind that this doesn’t necessarily mean that the laser safety officer can’t do other work in the facility. The laser safety officer can be someone who has an interest in it. They can take the lead in safety efforts related to the laser cutting machine and still have time for other responsibilities.

Fab:

Are you aware of injuries related to shoddy safety practices in laser cutting?

Glover:

I actually met someone who told me about how he injured his eye because of a laser. Now this wasn’t at a tradeshow, and as far as I know, no one has been injured at FABTECH. But injuries can—and do—occur involving lasers.

One of the reasons that this hasn’t happened at FABTECH is that early on, show organizers adopted the principles of keeping everybody safe. They started doing it right from the beginning, which really helped.

Safety Checklist for Those Looking to Purchase a Fiber Laser Cutting Machine

Does the laser cutting machine have a completely sealed cutting enclosure?

Does the enclosure have the proper safety glass?

If an enclosure is not part of the laser cutting machine, do you have room to operate the equipment safely in an isolated area?

Can the machine still be operated in a lockout/tagout scenario?

Does the equipment have UL or CE certification?

Is the ventilation system connected correctly?

Fab:

Are you hopeful that these kinds of efforts can make a difference in terms of safety?

Glover:

I’d like to think so, but there’s still a lot of work to do. You are dealing with a lot of people who simply don’t know what’s going on. They really don’t understand that lasers are dangerous because they really can’t see anything that looks like it might hurt them. Then others see lasers and picture the wild things they have watched in science fiction movies.

There are a lot of misconceptions about lasers. They are a very powerful tool, and as long as proper safety is implemented, they are no more dangerous than any other tool.
