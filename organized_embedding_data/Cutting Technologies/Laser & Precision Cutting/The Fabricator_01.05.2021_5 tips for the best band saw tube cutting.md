Below are frequently asked questions about common problems and answers that may help you smooth out production hiccups you come across.

1. How can I get a cleaner cut than I am getting from my current saw and blade?

Choosing the correct blade, feed, and speed is critical to cut quality. Tube diameter and material affect tooth pitch, shape, and set requirements. Generally, a thin-wall tube requires higher teeth per inch; conversely, a larger-wall tube requires fewer teeth per in.

For soft materials, a right/left set position, with the teeth individually oriented right and left off of the horizontal center line, ensures that the blade maintains material clearance during cutting. However, for most small tubing, a wavy set—three or more consecutive teeth going to the right followed by the same number of teeth going to the left, and so forth—will provide a cleaner cut and the longest blade life. This pattern of three teeth in each direction, as opposed to one, creates slightly more clearance for the blade as it travels through the material.

With tubes, this extra clearance gives the blade a tiny bit more space, reducing friction on the tube edge and thus reducing chattering (vibrations) in the material.

Rough edges can be caused by a mismatched blade speed and downfeed. Correctly aligning your blade speed to your downfeed is the first step toward cleaner cuts.

Excessive blade speed with insufficient downfeed is a common problem. Excessive speed causes heat, and insufficient downfeed causes the blade to pull across the material very shallowly, pulling off too-small chips. Heat plus small chips equals slag-small bits of melted metal that adhere to the material and blade. That slag needs to be chipped or ground off if a clean finish is important.

To prevent slippage during bundle cutting, tack weld the back end of the material, which usually is trim waste anyway.

Insufficient blade speed with excessive downfeed causes the opposite problem. The blade’s teeth are pushed deep into the material, causing too-big chips. These large chips disrupt the teeth efficacy and rip the material, resulting in a jagged edge.

2. My blades and blade teeth are breaking prematurely. How can I make my blades last longer?

This is a common problem. The first suggestion is to make sure you are using the highest-quality blade available and that it is matched to the material you are sawing. It’s a good idea to have a blade dealer visit your shop to help you match the blade to the job.

Once again, feed and speed are important factors to regulate while sawing. If you are producing slag while cutting, the slag that adheres to the blade typically sticks to individual teeth. When the tooth with the slag hits the edge of the material, the entire tooth is ripped off, as it is too wide to fit into the channel created by all of the other teeth.

The next thing to check is that your blade is properly supported during the cut. It’s important for a band saw to have rigid vertical arms fitted with rollers for twisting the blade into the correct orientation. The arms should be location-adjustable so that the operator can reduce the cutting envelope, thus increasing the support ratio. Carbide guides matched to the support arm rollers also can help with blade alignment and support.

3. What’s the best way to orient a typical tube profile?

It is important to have the smallest cutting envelope possible. For example, rectangular tubes should be positioned so that their profile is tall and narrow. This allows for the blade guides to be placed closer together, resulting in less vibration in the cut and a better edge quality.

4. My tube cut quality is fine, but I need to increase production output to meet demand. How can I do that?

One simple way to increase output and decrease waste in the process is by using a fully automatic band saw.

A fully automatic band saw is the fastest way to achieve production goals. With an automatic saw, after you clamp the material into the vise, advance the material to the desired length, and enter the number of cuts needed. Then you can walk away and leave the work to the machine. The saw makes a cut, raises the head while advancing the material, and repeats until it makes the required number of cuts.

Another way to increase your production output is to bundle your material and cut through multiple pieces with each stroke. Bundling increases the duration for each cut, but that time is quickly recovered by the reduction in the total amount of time spent advancing the material.

5. I want to cut tube in bundles to accelerate production. How do I do that without causing problems?

When you are bundle cutting, you need to watch for a few different problems. One of the most common is slippage of the inner pieces, which results in poor cut quality and inconsistent length. This is caused by chatter, the result of vibrations in the material. Slippage itself can cause additional chatter in the cut.

When you prepare material for bundle cutting, tack weld the back end of the material, which typically is trim waste. Banding or strapping the material also helps. It’s most effective to do this before and after each cut location. This can be tedious, but it offers the most assurance for a smooth, clean cut.

Once your materials are prepped, use a band saw with a hydraulic shuttle clamp. This will be the most effective way to move the material through the cut envelope. Adding a hydraulic top clamp at the cutting envelope will decrease the amount of tack, band, or strap needed to secure the materials.
