Cover Story

Punches and lasers without slats fill a growing need

By

Daniel Pfisterer

and

Nick DeSoto

O

ver the years, the rows of metal slats lining the beds of flat sheet laser cutting machines have become a staple in many shops. Slats are simple, inexpensive, and able to support heavy plate.

But the industry is evolving. The data center boom in particular is changing the game and increasing the focus on high throughput and maximum productivity in manufacturing. The demand for more power, longer unattended runs of high-mix production, reduced part damage, and greater reliability paired with lower maintenance is pushing fabricators to ask a question:

Could a slat-free technology be better for my application?

This isn’t just about the latest technology. It’s about the real, measurable friction in a production day. Slats collect slag, get gunked up, require periodic replacement and regular cleaning, and create tip-up and denesting problems that chew into uptime and add unpredictable manual work. Alternatives, like the brush and ball tables of punching or punch/laser combination machines and sheet-moving laser cutting machines, don’t simply change the bed on which material or parts lie. They change how parts are supported, removed, and handed over to the next stage in production.

For many shops, that’s a direct path to higher throughput and fewer surprises. And slatless options align well with make-to-order and justin-time models seeking to reduce changeover and manual work for manufacturers. And they also offer some hidden benefits to companies with an increasing number of data center jobs.

RETHINKING SLATS

Two dynamics collide here. First, laser sources have become more powerful. While high-wattage lasers seem to offer unlimited speed, their motion systems, optics, part removal, and cutting strategies have limits. If the initial and downstream production processes cannot keep up, that speed is wasted.

In addition, higher-wattage lasers can create harder slag that is more difficult to remove and increasingly likely to cause problems. Slag from high-power lasers can require aggressive removal techniques such as grinding, chiseling, or using dedicated slat cleaning machines.

Second, shops are under pressure to scale up with less labor: longer unattended shifts, predictable part presentation for automation, and fewer downstream touches. The data center industry, with its demands for precision, quality parts in high quantities and short delivery times, compounds these factors.

To meet new throughput requirements, it is no longer enough to measure cutting speeds. Shops must evaluate how fast parts move, from the time raw sheet enters the building until finished parts sit stacked and ready to ship.

Slats can complicate that picture. The traversing cutting head of flatbed lasers spreads slag across many support points and requires regular interruptions for maintenance. Slats introduce tip-up risk because small parts can pivot where they are unsupported.

Slats can break some of the assumptions that automated production depends on. This increases the potential for work stoppages, emergency maintenance, and variability that is hard to price into a job quote.

WHAT SLATLESS REALLY MEANS

Slatless is an umbrella term. The engineering behind each bed type matters.

Brush tables

use dense polymer bristles to support the sheet being processed. Rather than metal on metal, contact is plastic on metal, so the chance for scratching sensitive finishes drops significantly, reducing rework.

Brush tables are quiet, modular, and easily maintained. Many systems add pop-up roller aids to lower manual loading efforts. Brush tables shine when surface finish matters. You can’t have a brush table directly under a laser cutting head, but new machines engineer around these limitations.

Ball tables

make sliding and manual handling easy. They are common where sheets must be moved by hand or indexed quickly. Tables with roller balls are particularly useful in shops punching nonmetal sheets. Ball tables facilitate smooth sheet travel and are straightforward to keep clean. They’re not as gentle as brush tables for delicate finishes, but they’re efficient for many common shop tasks.

Sheet-moving systems

flip the paradigm. Instead of the laser cutting head flying over a stationary sheet, the sheet moves under a fixed or semifixed head. In some systems, the head and bed move in complementary axes. When compared to machines with slats, one practical advantage of sheet-moving systems is their concentrated slag management. All expelled, molten material is delivered to a controlled collection zone and suction system rather than being sprayed across a field of slats.

Think of it like mowing with a bagger: You collect the clippings where you can easily empty them, instead of raking clippings into clumps scattered across the entire lawn. These systems also enable live, automated part removals. Smart gates, flaps, suction pickoffs, or chutes allow parts to fall into bins or be picked up immediately as they’re finished. Skeletons can be removed predictably and stacked for easy transport. Because parts are removed as soon as they are free, the manual guesswork of tabbed parts and potential tip-ups that kill lights-out reliability are eliminated.

PUNCHING AND COMBINATION MACHINES

If most of your parts are in material less than 5/16 in. thick, or if you are doing work for the data center industry, then punching and punch/laser combination machines deserve your serious consideration. Punching isn’t simply a holemaking process. Modern machines control tool position, rotation, and force with great precision. The descending die on advanced combination punch/laser machines prevents scratches on component surfaces, which improves product quality and functionality.

Data center requirements emphasize precision, accuracy, and automation. Modern punching and combination machines easily produce the high-quality cut edges necessary for large data center components, such as server racks or switch cabinets, and are well suited to handle a wide range of sheet metal components.

Independent control of the upper tool, stripping plate, and lower tool opens the door for unique tools and adaptable processes. These include punching, embossing, stamping, offsets, louvers, countersinks, cluster hits, roll formed beads, formed threads, and even extruded features that replace rivet nuts, clinch nuts, and weld nuts.

Sheet metal parts with elements such as flanges, threads, or louvers are integral to data center work. Performing these operations during the punching process ensures high precision and required stability after assembly. It also prevents time loss and potential errors (and scrapped parts) created during secondary operations.

Secondary operations matter because each step requires equipment time and operators. Tap a formed thread with the punching machine instead of sending the part to a separate tapping station and you remove a machine, an operator, and a manual handoff. When countersinking, marking, tapping, and stiffening can all happen on one machine, you reduce touches, improve tolerances, and shrink overall lead time.

A combination punch/laser machine multiplies the benefit: Do the forming and high-speed repetitive features first, then let the laser finish contours and complex internal cutouts. For many jobs, that means fewer programs, fewer setups, and a single source route from blank to finished part.

AUTOMATION AND PART HANDLING

One of the biggest practical advantages of punching and punch/laser combination machines is how naturally those machines map into automated workflows. Modern punching technology makes it possible to remove parts—from the size of a dime to as large as a dining table—out of the sheet reliably and without human intervention.

Basic punching machines have built-in part removal capacity via chutes and flaps; midrange systems add compact sorters and automated sheet handling/loading and skeleton unloading automation. High-end machine automation handles the loading of multiple sheet types as well as full part unloading to multiple locations.

Top-tier cells tie into storage towers with full-sheet and part handling that enables continuous shifts of automated cutting and bending. Precision part stacking and palletizing simplify robot pickup routines, vision requirements, and downstream feeding.

Combination punch/laser and slatless laser cutting machines bring similar gains. When small parts are dropped into controlled bins via a gate or lifted via suction to a belt or stacker, part presentation is predictable. Tip-ups disappear because parts are removed as they finish and are not left tabbed into a skeleton, where air currents or unstable sheets can flip them up and stop production. Slatless laser cutting systems with integrated storage help shops run overnight or weekend shifts with minimal on-site staff.

MAINTENANCE AND DOWNTIME TRADEOFFS

Slats require routine cleaning and replacement. If slats are unmaintained, parts can end up unintentionally welded to the table, denesting becomes a chore, and downtime spikes. Brush tables and sheet-moving systems centralize slag into bins. Emptying a bin is far less disruptive to operation than scraping, removing slag, or replacing slats across a large bed.

Brush tables are engineered for longevity. Carefully chosen bristle material and diameter ensure support for a wide range of sheet types and thicknesses. Modular sections keep maintenance predictable and easily manageable. Brush tables are easily removed via quarter-turn fasteners, and brush clearing can be carried out with an air gun. Meanwhile, ball table maintenance involves making sure the table skins are clean and free of gouges. This includes checking rollerballs for dents, confirming smooth rotation, and cleaning and lightly oiling each roller.

WHERE SLATS STILL WIN

Flatbed laser cutting systems with slats remain valuable in fabrication. These machines still make the most sense where heavy plate is the norm, or where you must cover a wide material range with one machine. Stationary slats support heavy plates that exceed a sheet-moving machine’s capacity.

Flying-optic systems on fixed sheets can deliver superior cut speeds, as the optics aren’t encumbered by sheet material mass. Kilowatt for kilowatt, a flatbed laser will often outpace a combination machine. If a shop wants the greatest flexibility for economical cutting across everything from light gauge to very heavy plate, a flatbed laser remains a logical choice.

SOLUTIONS FOR REAL PROBLEMS

Slatless systems reduce tip-ups and prevent parts from falling between supports because parts are removed quickly or supported continuously on a flat, predictable surface. Centralized slag collection reduces maintenance and the risk of accidentally welded parts.

Brush beds protect finishes on stainless and plated parts. Integration-ready part sorters and stackers eliminate ergonomic risks and free workers from repetitive tasks. The net result for many shops is fewer interruptions, faster cycle times, and dependable presentation for downstream robots and cells.

METRICS TO CONSIDER

If most of your work is thinner than 5/16 in. and includes tapping, countersinking, embossing, or other forming, a punch or combination punch/laser machine will likely be the most economical. Investigate forming capabilities and the speed that can be gained by combining operations. If you routinely cut thicker plate, a laser cutting machine with slats remains necessary.

Don’t buy on cut speed alone. Consider your overall throughput and the role of the machine in the context of a production day. Look at unattended run-time, parts per hour, part removal time per sheet, percent of downstream operations eliminated, and total cost of ownership, including consumable and labor costs for maintenance and cleanup.

A gripper removes a cut part from a punch/laser combination machine. Unlike conventional flying-optic lasers that use slats, combination systems are sheet-moving machines with brush tables.

Find out what options are available to expedite and automate tool changes for quicker changeover in high-mix environments. Modularity also matters, so determine whether machines can be connected to storage towers or other machines and automation. Interlinked processes allow different cycle times to be buffered and unproductive downtimes to be avoided. This is a necessity for manufacturers handling data center work.

Regardless of industry, the larger trend is connectivity and transparency. Machines that report condition status, integrate with ERP and storage, create service tickets automatically, and permit remote intervention will let shops push further into lights-out production. Condition monitoring lets service teams pre-order parts and spares to reduce recovery time.

Compare maintenance intervals and consumable lifecycles. Ask about bristle life and how frequently brush tables need replacement; contrast this with slat replacement and cleaning labor costs over a year. Confirm crash recovery procedures. If a part tips, how long does it take for an operator at the machine to clear and restart the program?

A CUTTING PATH FORWARD

The choice between slats and slatless isn’t binary. It’s a strategic decision about what you need your cutting operations to deliver.

Choose punching and combination machines when processing material less than 0.31 in. thick, and if your parts require typical secondary operations. Punches and combo machines are also suitable for manufacturers that have lower volume and high-mix production but want to reduce changeovers and the need for manual work. An automated laser cutting center with a sheet-moving design is best for processing steel up to 0.5 in. thick as well as nonferous materials up to 0.25 in. thick. This sheet-moving approach provides stable support and reliable automated sorting.

Choose slatless and sheet-moving designs where surface finish, tip-up risk, small part evacuation, and material handling are business priorities, particularly if long, unattended run-time is a goal. Keep flatbed lasers with slats if you need flexible, economical cutting across a broad material range that includes thick plate.

Doing more data center work? Look for a slatless machine that ensures high-quality parts, enables multiple work steps in a single pass, reduces changeover times, and boosts productivity.

Shops that measure production flow instead of just inches per minute will get ahead. Reducing manual denesting, avoiding welded slat tips, and delivering predictable parts to automation will unlock more capacity than chasing increases in straight-line cut speed.

Slats will be with us for the conceivable future, especially since there are some jobs they simply handle better. But alternatives give fabricators practical ways to increase throughput, improve quality, and scale with fewer surprises.
