# Great laser cutting beam delivery relies on control, rigidity, minimizing moving parts

[TARİH: 01.06.2026 The Fabricator]

Laser Beam Delivery

Not all systems are created equal— software and stability make the difference

By

Lincoln Brunner

So many factors play into effective laser beam delivery that it pays to find a system that both offers the best software-driven control and the fewest moving parts (all of which present additional risk of failure).

BLM Group USA

W

hen it comes to innovations in laser cutting, the conversation always seems to come back to kilowatts. Even five years ago, 20 was the vanguard. Now it’s 30, 40, 50, and even beyond.

But as a famous superhero’s mentor once said, "with great power comes great responsibility." And in the case of lasers, whether for 2D or 3D cutting, that means solid, stable beam delivery systems that not only focus and modulate the cutting beam reliably but also help users, whether through software or training, to tailor that cutting beam to the wide variety of materials and thicknesses that a shop encounters on any given day.

"Power is nothing without control," said BLM Solutions Center Applications Manager Kenny Criger. "You can pump all the power you want into anything and hope for a good cut, but that’s just not the reality of the situation."

Grant Fergusson, product manager for 2D fiber optic lasers for TRUMPF, noted that when people focus solely on power, they tend to overlook the other components of the machine that also need bolstering—a mistake that carriers more risk the higher one moves up the wattage ladder.

"All of these components create an ecosystem," Fergusson said. "When you increase power levels, you need your machine to be more rigid, the frame to be more stable. When you’re cutting at those higher powers, it’s not just about the laser. So many people make the engine more powerful, but they forget to make the brakes, the shock absorbers, all of those components more powerful too."

With the dearth of experienced machine operators, it’s all the more important to invest in a system that delivers the beam in a way that is predictable, safe, and consistent. Criger noted the danger posed by operators veering from the straight and narrow path (literally) with increasingly powerful machines.

"As power levels go up, so does the importance of having very robust and engineered parameters," Criger said. "The best laser manufacturers out there are going to make sure that those actually come equipped on your machine direct from the factory [and] from install. You should not be driving those yourself … because if you’re talking 20K, 30K, 40K worth of power, you have the realistic possibility of a novice operator chewing up your machine, especially a flat sheet laser."

A WORD ABOUT SOFTWARE

As every machine-maker would attest, the key to dialing up the optimum settings for all those factors is a robust software package that leaves as little to chance as possible.

"We use this software combined with the machine’s technology … so that the operators themselves aren’t trying to micromanage the process," Fergusson said. "The operators are not trying to do too much at the machine; it’s really been all decided for them."

MINDING THE DETAILS

But which parameters need to be minded, exactly? In terms of the beam itself, you have focus, beam angle, spot size, beam thickness, and collimation (focusing divergent laser light into a cogent path through the torch). Some of those variables change, mainly according to material type (copper vs. stainless steel, for example), material type (in the case of aluminum vs. mild steel), and material thickness, though desired cutting speed factors into the equation as well. In addition, alongside beam delivery are variables like gas mixture, gas pressure, and cutting speed.

With solid-state fiber lasers having superseded CO

2

machines as the default technology for laser cutting, collimation is a completely different ballgame now that the mirrors and lenses and moving parts endemic to CO

2

technology are but a fading memory for metal fabricators.

One key advantage that CO

2

lasers offer compared to fiber is edge quality, largely because of the wider beam they use (10 μm vs. 1 μm in fiber lasers). That points to one important decision that comes into play daily—beam spot size and shape.

Specifically, the choice is whether to employ a larger fiber-optic cable (say, 400 μm wide, in the case of TRUMPF machines) for thicker material or a smaller (perhaps 100-μm) cable for thinner-gauge material. The wider cable produces a doughnut-shaped beam that concentrates power on the perimeter and yields a wider kerf, while the thinner cable delivers a center-focused beam and narrower kerf.

TRUMPF, for one, has integrated adaptive and fully adaptive collimation and zoom optics into its fiber laser cutting heads and control software, leaving very few if any adjustments to operators—all with the goal of creating near-CO

2

edge quality with fiber lasers while maximizing run speeds on whatever is being cut.

It truly is all about focus: With the smaller cable, the idea is to use the collimating optics to maintain a short focus with large numerical aperture angles to create smaller spot sizes (and shallower depth) while using smaller numerical aperture angles on beams with larger spot sizes and deeper focus for thicker materials."

"We don’t just focus on one specific material; we want to be able to cut all materials, and we can drive this with a software-driven spot size," Fergusson said. "The actual spot size changes are being delivered by the machine; the software is just telling it what to do."

2D VS. 3D CUTTING

BLM’s Criger emphasized that the manner in which the beam is collimated— whether mechanically or statically—matters a great deal because it directly influences spot size. Also worth consideration is whether a shop is cutting flat sheet or tubes, which makes a difference not only for the wattage of the machine (3D lasers don’t range as high as 2D) but also for how those cutting beams are focused.

In addition, beam size and focus cannot be addressed without considering the assist gas being used, whether pure oxygen for thicker materials or high-pressure ambient air or perhaps nitrogen for thinner gauges. While users should have a great deal of control over spot sizes on flat lasers, BLM has taken a different tack for 3D cutting by using a fixed collimator on its tube lasers, changing the beam delivery directly from the resonator rather than mechanically further into the process.

"We’re adjusting [the beam] in the resonator to minimize the mechanical portions of the optics so you don’t have all of these moving pieces," Criger said. "One of the biggest shames is to have a perfectly good optic fail just because a mechanical component of it was no longer working.

To mitigate that, the company has moved to using a nitrogen-infused mirror that can be expanded or flattened to make it more convex or concave, depending on the focus depth needed.

And for tubes in particular, BLM uses an adjustable-mode approach that switches typically between a higher-density, 50-μm beam with a centralized core coupled with air or nitrogen for thinner material and a 150-μm ring-shaped beam coupled with oxygen for thicker gauges. Whether with 2D or 3D, the oxygen yields a cleaner cut by working with the wider beam to create a wider kerf, generating a better thermogenic reaction and pushing more material out of the cut.

"We eliminated a mechanical optic and replaced it with an optic that has no mechanical movement in it," Criger said. "Minimizing or completely eliminating the need for mechanical movement within your optics is just eliminating a variable and potential for failure."

"A high-quality beam delivery system typically requires years of development and refinement," Fergusso said. "Overall performance is generally superior when the entire optical chain is engineered as an integrated solution rather than assembled from ad hoc, separately purchased components."
