# A SHARP FOCUS

[TARİH: 01.01.2015 ISMR]

What are the advantages of CO2, fibre and new diode lasers for sheet metal cutting? We chronicle the benefits of each process for different applications.

How do you determine the best laser cutting process for your operations – CO2, fibre or diode laser? The answer to this question is not always simple and does not always involve just one of these processes. In reality, each of these processes has its niche area that may involve a metal thickness range, cut part tolerance advantage, metallurgical benefit, cost or outright speed/productivity advantage.

Selecting the best cutting process depends upon the specific application, your business needs and the areas that are most critical such as capital equipment cost, cut quality, productivity, operating costs and flexibility. A sheet metal user needs to consider the various benefits of each laser cutting technology, as well as all these additional factors, before coming to a decision.

In this article, we spotlight the various benefits of each technology – CO2, fibre and the new diode range – to help focus your decision-making in this area.

## Attributes of the cutting process

The attributes of a cutting process that are most often important from a metal fabricator or job shop perspective are capital equipment cost; cutting cost per part or per metre (including consumables and utilities); ease of use; productivity; cut part accuracy; edge quality and metallurgical effects plus service/maintenance requirements.

Laser provides excellent cut quality and productivity on material of less than 6mm and can be used on material of up to 25mm. Metal cutting with laser has a variety of benefits compared to other cutting methods on the market. For instance, higher levels of accuracy when cutting thin sheet, rectangular cutting edges and strong automation possibilities. It can also be used to cut very small holes, narrow webs, acute-angled geometric shapes and very complex contours.

As a process which boasts very low heat input, materials are not warped and are minimally affected by heat during the cut. It also features a very small cutting kerf (0.2mm to 0.4mm) and high cutting speeds can be reached when cutting thin sheet (and now medium thickness) sheet.

However, investment and operating costs can be higher, when compared to other processes (such as punching), and it may not always be an economical process for small batch sizes. Precise height control between the torch and the work-piece is also necessary. However, lasers add significantly to manufacturing flexibility for materials with thickness ranges of up to 25mm.

## The CO2 laser

CO2 lasers are suitable as robust all-purpose tools for stainless steel, aluminium and mild steel in all sheet thicknesses. If stainless steel and thick sheet metal are the priority, CO2 lasers are strong tools. When processing electrical steel sheet, for example, CO2 lasers are better suited than solid state lasers to cutting thin sheets of these specially-coated soft magnetic materials.

Smart Collision Prevention is now available for the new TruLaser 5030 Fiber as well as all latest machines in the TruLaser Series 3000 and 5000.

8kW CO2 laser cutting solutions are now offered as alternatives to plasma or waterjet cutting – they are particularly suited to the production of laser-cut blanks. It is much more cost-effective than milling the part from bar material, a process that is very wasteful by comparison. The ability to use thick sheet instead also introduces the potential for nesting – saving material, machining time and therefore cost.

Important CO2 laser characteristics are high unsaturated gain, high power output and good efficiency. Traditionally, the cut quality of a CO2 laser remains constant throughout all ranges of material thickness whereas a fibre laser, whilst being a superior cutter in the thin material range, previously started to lose cut quality and required more power than a CO2 once a certain material thickness was exceeded. However, in the last year, rapid developments in fibre laser technology for processing medium sheet thicknesses have largely negated this.

CO2 technology has therefore been somewhat overtaken by these rapid developments in fibre laser machine development, over the past year, but it remains a strong workhorse for thicker material processing and stainless steel work. CO2 lasers also have the capability of cutting wood and acrylics that fibre lasers cannot, providing greater flexibility to niche industries where the need to cut these materials is a necessity.

“In the future, CO2 will enjoy a niche market of thicker aluminium and stainless steel materials to cut. If the customer’s day-to-day business demands good quality, we still feel that, for the next few years at least, CO2 technology will still be in demand,” Japanese manufacturer Amada told ISMR.

## Fibre laser in focus

A relatively new innovation in laser cutting systems for machining thin, and now medium thickness, sheet metal has been the introduction of the fibre laser which can cut not just steel materials but also nonferrous materials such as copper, brass and titanium. Linear drives enable rapid positioning and cutting speeds to be achieved.

The use of fibres eliminates the need for elaborate and costly optics for beam guidance and the space requirement can be downsized. Fibre lasers can be comfortably used for the efficient processing of gauge and fractional materials and fine feature cutting.

Fibre lasers operate at a light wavelength that allows them to deliver the beam to the cutting head using a flexible (fibre optic) cable, compared to the mirror and tube delivery system used by CO2 lasers. This provides a simpler complete system layout which requires far less maintenance compared to the older technology. The laser uses a properly focused, high energy laser to melt a small spot on the material being cut, and an assist gas (typically oxygen for cutting steel) to remove the molten metal. A 3kW fibre laser can cut with similar speeds and power to a 4 to 5kW CO2 laser, indicating its better efficiency. Cut capability on steel is from thin gauge to about 20mm.

Fibre laser light is created by banks of diodes. The light is channelled and amplified through fibre optic cable similar to that used for data transfer. The amplified light, on exiting the fibre cable, is collimated or straightened and then focused by a lens onto the material to be cut. Not only is creation of the light 200 per cent more efficient than via a traditional CO2 laser, but delivery is also far simpler, with no expensive optical mirrors. The focusing lens is, unlike on a conventional CO2 laser machine, sealed in the cutting head and thus not a consumable item.

“Fibre laser cutting systems have several advantages over CO2 laser systems as they require virtually no maintenance, are more energy-efficient and take up less space. The power supply is much smaller than that of a CO2 laser, yet fibre delivery enables the beam to travel greater distances allowing for installation on larger tables,” explained fibre laser machine manufacturer, Hypertherm, which includes in its product portfolio the HyIntensity™ Fiber Laser.

A range of global manufacturers such as Amada, BLM Group, Baykal, Ermaksan, Bystronic, Kjellberg Finsterwalde, LVD, Prima Power, Salvagnini, Thalmann and TRUMPF have invested heavily in fibre laser offerings to the global market. Subcontractors and job shops have quickly found that the fibre laser’s extended cutting capabilities have extended their reach into new markets and new customers.

“We are cutting up to 50 per cent quicker when using the Bystronic fibre laser on stainless steel, which is on the machine for about 70 per cent of the time, 24 hours per day. The material is ideal for extracting maximum benefit from fibre lasers, so the machine will pay for itself quickly. For the other 30 per cent of its time, the BySprint laser machine is available for cutting reflective metals that we could not previously quote on. The fibre laser is already bringing in new business of this type, which we had to turn down before," commented Gareth Taylor of subcontractor WEC, which is now cutting aircraft parts on its fibre laser.

“The over-riding reason for purchasing a fibre laser, according to our customers, was the speed of cutting thin (1 to 2mm and up to 3mm) materials. An important secondary reason was the lower running cost and the lower power consumption, which was particularly important in companies at the top end of the electrical supply limit in their workshops,” Bystronic told ISMR.

Bystronic UK's experience at the beginning of the year was that the companies most likely to adopt the new fibre technology were either sub-contractors which specialise in cutting thin metal, such as for point-of-sale sheet metal work and white goods, or subcontractors using a fibre laser machine's strengths to complement CO laser machines cutting thicker grades of plate. However, the capability of fibre lasers has jumped in the space of a year and they are now able to cut medium thickness grades of sheet comfortably.

For example, German manufacturer TRUMPF extended fibre laser’s suitability for thin sheet cutting to thicker sheet processing. From mild steel to stainless steel through to highly reflective materials such as copper and aluminium; from 1mm to 25mm sheet thickness – all components can now be produced on one single laser machine. Its TruLaser 5030 Fiber with BrightLine fibre function enables manufacturers to cut thin sheet with exactly the same attributes for thick sheet.

“This combination achieves quality and productivity in material between 15 and 25mm. Even stainless steel and aluminium, up to 25mm thick, can be cut cleanly and very fine holes and contours applied; previously, they could only be drilled,” TRUMPF told ISMR.

Bystronic UK does not see the death of CO laser cutting machines as a result of the introduction of fibre laser technology, as was predicted. The two systems both have their advantages and disadvantages, it told ISMR, and Bystronic works with customers to ensure that the best machine is proposed for individual requirements.

“We were the first to develop and market a laser using both a fibre optic resonator and fibre optic beam transmission to achieve superior results vis a vis CO lasers. Costs per part are slashed by more than 50% and energy consumption is reduced by over 70%. Running costs are only one third that of a 4kW CO laser with chiller. There is virtually zero maintenance,” Italian manufacturer Salvagnini told ISMR.

“On our ENSIS laser cutting machine, operators can cut 20/25mm mild steel on the fibre laser – people just don’t believe it or believe that the machine is only 2kW,” Amada told ISMR at last year’s EuroBLECH exhibition. “Traditionally, 20mm mild steel is cut on a 4kW CO machine. The ENSIS just uses 25% of the power of a 4kW CO machine. That has really opened a lot of customers’ eyes.”

## Direct Diode Laser (DDL) technology

One great surprise for the sheet metal industry at the EuroBLECH exhibition in October last year was the unveiling of Amada’s new EXC laser technology for sheet metal cutting – its EXC direct diode laser system. The DDL machine’s efficiency is upwards of 40%, compared to 32/33% for a fibre laser and 10% for a CO laser cutting system. The Japanese machine tool specialist is eventually aiming to produce 4kW and 6kW models. Amada is the first company in the world to exhibit a direct diode sheet metal cutting laser.

“EXC gives a cut surface finish, particularly on mild steel, equal to or better than the best CO machines but with fibre laser running costs. The beam quality is not so different from a CO machine but, because of the stability of the oscillator, you can get a very high quality finish, faster than CO but not as fast as fibre. So there will be a very clear distinction between fibre and DDL (Direct Diode Laser) technology,” explained Matt Wood, Senior Product Manager, Marketing, Amada. “If you are cutting 3mm or 4mm mild steel, fibre is the choice because it will always be faster. However, if you need to cover the whole material range from 1-20mm mild steel or 1- 12/15mm aluminium or stainless steel, the DDL technology will cover that range faster than a CO laser with fibre laser running costs.”

Traditionally, diode lasers have always been used for welding and hardening but the problem has always been harnessing the power of the high quality beam to cut sheet metal. Amada has found a way to harness the diode light directly, increase the brightness and feed it straight to a fibre cable to the cutting head. Other companies make diode lasers for welding applications but no other company, to date, is making this for sheet metal cutting yet.

“I believe that fibre laser will always cut faster in thin material because its beam quality is better than DDL (Direct Diode Laser) or CO so, if a customer processes material of 3mm or 4mm and below across the board, fibre laser will be suitable. However, the advantage with DDL technology is that, with 2kW of power here, operators can already cut up to 16mm mild steel already and DDL is in the fairly early stages of development…With a 4kW DDL model, you could maybe process 20mm mild steel cut and the finish will be like a CO laser – so the machine cuts like a CO machine but with fibre laser running costs. The DDL technology is 40% efficient and CO is 10%,” concluded Matt Wood of Amada. ■

WHERE PRODUCTIVITY MEETS FLEXIBILITY

The 25th TIMTOS (Taipei International Machine Tool Show) will feature around 1000 exhibitors on approximately 5,400 booths

The 25TH TIMTOS 2015, co-organized by TAMI and TAITRA, will be held from 3-8 March 2015 at the Taipei World Trade Centre in Taiwan. More than 1,000 exhibitors, on approximately 5,400 booths, will support the event. Taiwanese leading machinery manufacturers will attend the show and 7,000 overseas buyers are expected to generate business opportunities of up to US$ 2 billion. Seventeen countries/regions will be represented at the event and approximately 53,000 visitors are expected to attend. It is Asia’s second largest machine tool show.

The Taiwanese machine tool industry is one of the most competitive industries worldwide, ranking as the fourth largest machine tools exporter in the global market place, with exports amounted to US$ 3.5 billion in 2013. The export value of Taiwan's machine tools from January-August 2014 reached US$ 2.4 billion, a 5.2% rise compared to the same period in 2013. Export growth in emerging markets is also promising, showing a growth of 11.1% for Malaysia, 17.8% for Vietnam and 19.3% for Turkey. According to the latest statistics, the total export value of Taiwan’s machine tool industry from January to September in 2014 edged up by reaching US$ 2.8 billion, a 6.6% increase on the same period in the previous year.

As well as the exhibition, other events at TIMTOS include over 200 one-on-one procurement meetings, the "Taiwan Machine Tool Industry Awards for Excellence in Research & Innovation" and 20 seminars and new product presentations.
