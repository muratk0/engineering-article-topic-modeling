(They called them teenage sex comedies for a reason!) But that same exclamation came to mind as I was watching a video from a tradeshow in Southeast Asia where a company was operating both a fiber laser cutting machine and a manual fiber laser welding power source outside of any sort of an enclosure.

In fact, a tradeshow attendee in the video took the laser welding gun and nonchalantly laid a bead. He had no protective eyewear. He had no gloves and only a long-sleeved T-shirt to protect his arms. He had no idea what he was doing.

This isn’t too much of a surprise for those in the metal fabricating industry who have had a chance to travel around the world attending tradeshows and visiting foreign metal fabricating companies. In other parts of the world, laser safety is not as stressed as in the U.S.

It’s somewhat understandable that people have become comfortable around lasers. They’ve been used to cut metal for more than 30 years. Laser pointers have been around long enough that a lot of younger people probably think of them as cat toys instead of a teaching aid.

But even with the laser pointers, most people have the common sense to not point them at someone’s eyes. Now imagine operating a fiber laser, which has a wavelength of 1.06 μm, making it near-infrared and invisible to the human eye, without proper safety precautions. If a fiber laser enters the eye, either directly or reflecting off a piece of metal, it causes serious and irreparable damage. The fiber laser’s small but powerful beam pierces the lens and focuses onto the retina. When this happens, the best-case scenario might be permanent spots that obscure the vision. The worst case is loss of sight.

That was the injury risk at that foreign tradeshow. Luckily, it’s a situation that attendees at FABTECH won’t have to worry about.

In "Laser safety matters, even at tradeshows" (p. 62), Steven Glover, FABTECH’s laser safety officer and former COO of The Laser Institute, explains why it’s important for companies to be vigilant about laser safety—both in their own shops and at tradeshows. The risk is too great to ignore.

But the risk becomes much greater as more metal fabricators adopt laser-based metal fabricating technologies and have no real experience operating them. When they are working with machine tool companies that have a lackadaisical approaches to safety, the risks only increase.

Most fabricating technology companies have been very good about laser safety over the years. But some new entrants to the North American manufacturing market might not be approaching it with the same level of commitment.

Earlier this summer, I attended an open house event at Miller Electric’s Appleton, Wis., headquarters for the debut of its OptX 2-kW handheld laser welding machine, developed in collaboration with IPG Photonics. Manual laser welding is getting a lot of buzz in the industry, and it should because it’s an incredibly efficient tool, especially when compared to gas tungsten arc welding. It’s also a dangerous one if not operated correctly.

Miller Electric officials recognized this and spent a good portion of the presentation talking about safety. For instance, a code is needed to activate the machine, and a key is required to start it. The laser power source can’t be engaged until external door interlocks detect that the welding enclosure is shut, which ensures that no one can just walk into the welding area.

The company goes as far as requiring any potential purchasers of the OptX to have someone trained as a laser safety officer, per ANSI Standard Z136.1. A metal fabricating shop might not know about the inherent dangers of lasers before it purchases the OptX, but it certainly will after sitting through this credentialing process.

A metal fabricating shop floor is an exciting place to be, and it’s not a place where workers can afford to have their heads in the clouds. Lift trucks are moving material and products. Press brake rams are coming down with tons of pressure. Storage and retrieval systems are quickly moving sheet metal from pallets to staging. All of the previously mentioned activities carry the risk of doing major damage to a human operator in the wrong place at the wrong time.

Fibers lasers can do the same thing. Metal fabricators need to ensure that their employees are protected from unsafe operational practices and substandard machine designs. Ask questions, talk to other metal fabricators, and keep your eyes on what matters most: your employees’ health and well-being.
