# The laser/punch combination machine’s soft touch

[TARİH: 01.05.2025 The Fabricator]

Shop Management

Under the right circumstances, metal fabricators don’t have to worry about processing surface-sensitive material

By

Dan Davis

Metal fabricators like laser/punch combination machines because multiple processes can be done on one piece of equipment. They should feel confident that they can enjoy similar benefits when processing surface-sensitive materials on these same machines.

On some laser/punch combination machines, brushes can be raised during material movement to avoid possible scratches that occur when the material is dragged over a die.

A chute and a conveyor belt can be configured to drop parts so that they fall in a flat fashion as opposed to leading with an edge, which can damage parts already in the bin.

ob Stiels is the manager of Prima Power’s Training and Technology Center outside of Chicago. He gets a lot of customer requests and often helps those metal fabricators find out what can and can’t be done on the company’s machines. So when he gets a question like "Can prepainted material be processed on a combination machine and still maintain an acceptable-looking finish?" he replies diplomatically, "Let’s find out."

In this particular case, the metal fabricator sent him the prepainted material, and testing on the combination machine showed no scratches from moving the material around the brush table and no burned top edges from laser cutting.

"Sometimes working with prepaint can be terrible, but sometimes it can be very good," he said.

That’s the nature of processing surface-sensitive material on a laser/punch combination machine. It can work, when the variables make it possible.

A Natural Progression

Laser cutting has emerged as the dominant blanking technology for metal fabricating companies over the last two decades. Toolless holemaking, unlimited shape cutting, and fast processing make the laser cutting machine hard to beat.

But in the age of demanding customers and ever-tightening lead times, high-speed blanking is not the only thing that matters. Total processing time matters more. A part that is laser-cut in a matter of minutes that then sits on a pallet for hours waiting to be formed on a press brake really isn’t maximizing the full potential of the laser cutting machine. But if you could create a form on that same laser-cut part, then you’ve removed material handling time and eliminated a fabricating step. Thus, the laser/punch combination machine is attractive for those shops looking to tackle forming, laser cutting, and possibly other activities, such as tapping, on one machine.

Those who have been in metal fabricating for a long time or who work in a shop with an older combination machine might, however, question whether this type of equipment is suitable for surface-sensitive material. After all, with sheet metal being dragged across a metal table with ball bearings, how could its surface not be marred after the job was complete?

That was then, and this is now. Stainless steel, aluminum, and other sheet metal that needs to remain mar free can be processed on today’s combination machines.

Stiels offered up this warning though: "What is a scratch? What is damage? It’s all very subjective. You cannot measure this. Sometimes I say a part looks good, but then the customer holds the part in the light and says, ‘I see some small scratches.’"

If a metal fabricator wants to find out if a surface-sensitive part can be processed on a combination machine, it needs to know what is acceptable and what is not.

Plastic Film and Brushes

The film attached to metal to protect it from scratches and any other marring is something familiar to most people, even outside of metal fabricating. It’s found on all types of consumer goods, like appliances and consumer electronics, and needs to be removed upon delivery to the home.

This type of protective film has been around quite a while, and it’s improved over the years. Stiels recalled the early generations of the material and how they posed a danger to the CO

2

laser cutting heads of the time.

"When you started piercing, it blew up the plastic, like a balloon. Then when the laser started moving, the plastic wrapped around the cutting head," he said.

Now the protective film sticks much better. It’s also more resilient.

The emergence of fiber lasers proved a challenge to the film being used on the material. It exacerbated the burrs that appeared on the bottom of the material as the fiber laser cutting head did its work.

Today, however, new protective film designed for use with fiber lasers not only protects the surface, it also doesn’t react in the same way that led to excessive burrs, according to Stiels. In fact, he said Prima Power stresses the importance of this type of protective film as it generally allows for good cutting results.

The protective film often will remain on both sides of the material as it is being processed on a combination machine to protect the surface not only from the effects of the laser, but also from the punch mark of the stripper.

The other part of the mix when it comes to keeping surface-sensitive material free from marring is the brush table, a longtime staple of punching and combination machines and a marked improvement over the ball-type tables of the past.

The brushes provide the stability that is needed to support the sheet metal and ultimately the skeleton, once the parts are removed. It also minimizes any potential for deep marking on the underside of the metal.

Stiels said that his company found that brush tables with a pattern of straight rows of brushes did not provide the needed support of skeletons, particularly as they lost structural integrity as automation was used to remove parts. The skeletons were more prone to pop up and potentially cause a collision.

Instead, Stiels recommended a pattern of diagonally staggered brushes, providing more support for skeletons. With the amount that a company invests in automated parts removal, he said that it’s important to maintain uptime.

He added that shorter brush lengths work better than longer brush lengths. A brush fiber that is likely to bend or wobble opens the door for potential scratching and generally doesn’t help for the part’s ease of movement across the table.

To protect the brushes during laser cutting, Stiels said that metal fabricators that use oxygen regularly as a laser cutting assist gas should look at brass brushes around the laser cutting area. The regular nylon brushes can’t stand up to the repeated heat exposure, particularly during piercing.

This has become less of a problem in recent years as more metal fabricators choose to run nitrogen during laser cutting. Also, Stiels said that metal fabricators are encouraged to prepunch holes, instead of laser piercing, which eliminates the intense heat affecting the brushes and reduces wear on the laser cutting head.

Moving the Brushes Up

Most laser/punch combination machines maintain die heights at the same level as the top of the brushes. This helps with forming. If the die is not at the same level, the sheet metal might sag, which affects the quality of the form.

With the die being at that same height as the brushes, it can leave small scratches on unguarded material as the sheet is moved during processing. The damage is not extensive, but it might be noticeable if the underside is going to be visible.

That has led to some combination machines including an option where the brushes are elevated during material movement. The die stays at the same height, but the material is raised to avoid potential scratching issues.

Minimizing Marking During Clamping

Clamping is a necessity when it comes to processing sheet metal on a laser/punch machine. That, however, doesn’t mean that it has to be marking the material.

When clamps are used, pins drive into the material to secure the grasp. Those pins can leave marks.

Clamp shoes can reduce the visible impact that those pins leave on the material and act as a barrier between the clamps and the surface.

"Many times, if film is also present, you won’t see anything" from the clamps, Stiels said.

Dropping the Parts Out of the Chute

Metal fabricators that are fans of punching and combination machines especially like the chutes that allow them to collect parts in a bin without the need of a robot or someone breaking microjoints to collect parts out of a skeleton. In a way, this type of material collection option was ahead of its time.

Having said that, it can pose a danger to the final finish of a part, depending on how it slides down a chute and lands on top of other parts. If a sharp edge is the first thing that hits another metal part in a bin, it likely will mar any part it lands on.

Stiels said that rollers can be added to a chute to allow for parts to avoid such an awkward, and potentially harmful, drop. The rollers help to position metal parts so that they land more often in a flat position, instead of leading with an edge.

"It’s up to the customer to decide what to do," Stiels said. "The other options are to have microjoints or have a robot pick up the parts and stack them."

Indeed, it is the customer’s decision. At the very least, a metal fabricator can feel confident in knowing that a laser/punch combination machine is a suitable choice for processing surface-sensitive materials. It’s just a matter of determining what can be done to maximize the chances that the sheet metal surface of the final parts meet final expectations.
