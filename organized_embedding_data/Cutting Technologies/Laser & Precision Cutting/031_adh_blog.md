# Historical Milestones in the Development of Industrial Laser Cutting

Laser cutting developed through several distinct advances rather than a single invention date. A useful historical account separates the underlying physics, the creation of working laser sources, and the engineering of machines able to process materials in industrial settings.

## Theoretical foundation

In 1917, Albert Einstein described stimulated emission in work on the quantum theory of radiation. Stimulated emission is the process in which an excited atom or molecule, when influenced by an incoming photon, emits another photon with corresponding characteristics. This concept became a foundational principle of laser operation.

The theoretical principle alone did not provide a cutting tool. Practical laser systems required a suitable active medium, an energy source, optical components that support amplification, and methods for directing the resulting beam to a workpiece.

## Early working laser systems

A major hardware milestone occurred in 1960, when Theodore Maiman demonstrated a ruby laser. The system used a ruby crystal energized by a flashlamp and mirrors at the ends of the crystal to amplify light. Its output was pulsed rather than continuously sustained.

The article describes this early ruby laser as important proof that a coherent laser beam could be generated, while also noting limits for continuous industrial thermal processing. A pulsed source and a continuous source serve different process needs. Historical descriptions should therefore avoid treating the first working laser as automatically equivalent to the first production-ready laser cutting machine.

## Continuous-output CO2 lasers

The article identifies the development of the carbon-dioxide laser in 1963 as another key milestone. A CO2 laser uses a gas medium and produces infrared radiation. The article states that its beam is not visible to the human eye and that the system provided continuous output suitable for heating and melting materials.

In the described gas-laser arrangement, nitrogen transfers energy to carbon-dioxide molecules, supporting laser emission. The article presents this as an important route toward higher-power continuous operation compared with the earlier pulsed ruby system.

Laser radiation, including infrared radiation that may not be visible, presents serious hazards. Operation requires suitable engineered safeguards, enclosures, interlocks, beam-control measures, and trained personnel. Specific safety procedures, protective equipment, and machine operation must follow the applicable machine manual and site safety requirements.

## From laser source to industrial machine

A laser source alone is not an industrial cutting system. Industrial use also depends on workholding, motion control, beam delivery, process monitoring, guarding, and methods to manage material removed from the cut zone.

The article describes a 1965 Western Electric application in which a laser-based system was used to drill small holes in diamond wire dies. This application is best understood as an industrial laser-material-processing milestone rather than as identical to continuous sheet cutting. It illustrates that laser processing could be integrated with machine structures, handling arrangements, monitoring, and protective enclosures.

## Material removal and assist gas

For thermal cutting, molten material can remain in the kerf and resolidify. The article describes the historical introduction of a nozzle that directs assist gas along the beam path as a way to help expel molten material from the cut zone.

It specifically discusses oxygen-assisted processing in connection with steel and notes that oxygen can contribute an exothermic reaction in that context. This should not be generalized to every material, laser type, or cutting application. Assist-gas selection and process behavior depend on the material, thickness, laser source, nozzle arrangement, and other process conditions. Any setup, adjustment, or intervention involving laser optics, gas supply, machine parameters, or safety systems must be performed according to the machine documentation by qualified personnel.

## Interpreting invention dates

Several dates can be relevant depending on the question being asked:

- **1917:** theoretical description of stimulated emission.
- **1960:** demonstration of an early working ruby laser.
- **1963:** development of the CO2 laser described in the article as a continuous, higher-power source.
- **1965:** industrial laser drilling of diamond dies, as described in the article.
- **Mid-1960s:** development of gas-assisted laser cutting concepts for removing molten material from a cut.

Accordingly, the question “when was the laser cutter invented?” has no single answer without defining the milestone: laser theory, the first operating laser, industrial drilling, or continuous thermal cutting. This distinction is useful when comparing technical histories because it avoids conflating a scientific principle, a laser source, and a complete manufacturing machine.
