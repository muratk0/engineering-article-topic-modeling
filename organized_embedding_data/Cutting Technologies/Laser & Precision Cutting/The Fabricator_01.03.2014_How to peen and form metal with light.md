# How to peen and form metal with light

[TARİH: 01.03.2014 The Fabricator]

TECHNOLOGY SPOTLIGHT

How intense pulses of laser light can prolong component life

By Tim Heston, Senior Editor

Laser peening uses a powerful pulsed laser to induce a high-pressure wave into the workpiece.

A technician sets up a laser peening operation, with a robot directing the laser processing head over the workpiece.

The laser has evolved to become the most flexible tool in manufacturing’s history. It can cut, weld, clad, and heat-treat. And now lasers are being used to peen and form workpieces with unprecedented accuracy. If you’ve flown in a Boeing 747-8 recently, you might be interested to know that its wing—or, more specifically, its precise curvature—was formed using a laser.

If you took physics in high school, you may remember the ideal gas law, which relates volume, pressure, and temperature. One side of the equation multiplies pressure by volume. The other side multiplies the amount of substance (basically, the amount of molecules) by temperature, along with a universal gas constant. If you increase the temperature to about 28,000 degrees F within a fewhundred-picosecond (yes, picosecond) time period over a 1 cm

2

area, you get immense pressure.

So how do you heat such an area to such an extreme temperature so quickly? Try a pulsed neodymium glass laser with a peak power of 1,000 megawatts. That extraordinary peak power lasts only 25 nanoseconds, but it’s enough to create a shockwave that penetrates deep into the workpiece.

That deep penetration induces a powerful, precise peening action that can prolong component life, and in many cases allow engineers to design components with stress corrosion cracking and erosion wear in mind. An entire assembly may not need to be made out of an expensive wear-resistant material. Instead, a laser can peen specific areas, effectively designing stress and corrosion resistance into material precisely where it’s needed.

Compared to cutting and welding, laser peening is utterly different. “In laser welding or cutting, the laser heats the metal,” said Lloyd Hackel, vice president for advanced technologies at Paramus, N.J.-based Metal Improvement Co., a business unit of Curtiss-Wright Surface Technologies. “Laser peening doesn’t heat the metal. Instead, it uses a very short pulse with very high energy, and this creates a plasma. This plasma rapidly tries to expand, and that creates a pressure wave that runs into the metal, plastically compressing the metal and resulting in residual compressive stress.”

So how exactly is this plasma created? Hackel, an MIT graduate with a doctorate in physics as well as laser and optical engineering, put it this way: “If you think of light entering your eye, it’s an electromagnetic field. And it’s this electric field of this light, when the light is at an intensity of 10

9

watts, that actually creates this plasma, ripping the electrons off the surface of the metal. Those electrons absorb the laser light and create that plasma, which is actually a sea of electrons and metal ions.”

To set up such an operation, a technician places a sacrificial layer of aluminum tape over the work to be treated. A curtain of water, called a tamping layer, is directed over the workpiece surface, and the pulsed laser engages. During perceptible periods of time, the water isn’t that heavy. But when that time is brief enough, the situation changes. Thanks to its inertia, water just can’t get out of the way in a matter of nanoseconds (20 to 100 nanoseconds, depending on the laser pulse length). That’s how the water helps confine the laser-induced pressure wave, sending it through the metal surface.

From here the wave pushes down, and the metallic molecules in that area under the laser push outward against other molecules that haven’t been affected by the laser. This outward push by the affected metal, together with the pushback from the surrounding metal, creates compression.

“And cracks just don’t start in areas that are under compression,” Hackel said. “They start in areas that are being stretched and tensioned.”

Unlike shot peening, the peening action from a laser can be precisely controlled, and the waves of compressive stress penetrate deeply. “Think of this compressive stress as a pair of pliers with a lever arm,” Hackel explained. A longer lever arm—created by laser peening’s powerful pulse—allows those pliers to pinch deeper and with more force. “The longer lever arm we have, the more intensely we can pinch off the crack from starting or growing. So deeper stress typically means more benefit for lifetime enhancement [of the workpiece].”

Hackel and his team have performed extensive work with finite element analysis to help designers calculate areas in a design that over time could lead to cracking. The FEA shows where laser peening can add compressive stresses to stop or prevent cracking.

Many workpieces that can benefit from this process—airplane engine components, nuclear power plant systems, and the like—aren’t the easiest to transport. So the company developed a mobile unit that allows the laser peening to be brought to the work. It all fits inside a trailer, temperature-controlled to keep the Nd:glass laser and ancillary equipment in good operating order. When the truck arrives on-site, technicians remove a robot from the trailer and take it to the workpiece to be treated. The robot carries the laser processing head, and a fiber-optic cable connects to the laser equipment back in the trailer.

The company has deployed these units to perform numerous laser peening applications, including the repair of airplane engine blades. It has also deployed the units to perform a related process: laser peen forming, and this includes the wing panels of the Boeing 747-8.

The peening action from the laser, because it can be so precisely controlled, allows engineers to tweak the parameters so that it induces a specific amount of stress and strain. It goes back to metallurgy—specifically, Young’s modulus, which describes the inherent connection between stress and strain.

Stress and strain coexist in all materials. As Hackel explained, when the laser peening action induces compressive

stress

around a hole, the complementary

strain

causes the hole to close up ever so slightly. Engineers take this interaction into account, ensuring that the peening action causes the optimal amount of stress to create the required compression, while ensuring the strain causes minimal movement.

But the strain doesn’t need to be so small. Because engineers can control the pulse wave so precisely, they can adjust it so that it induces enough strain to move and form material with extreme accuracy. In one test, the company adjusted the curvature of the bulkhead of a single-aisle airliner by 1 cm, and the forming was controlled to less than 1 mm of deviation. “And we could have formed it much tighter than that,” Hackel said. “But that was what the application was set up to measure.”

Such forming is what occurs with the 747-8 wing panels. To take weight out of the wings, engineers previously had no choice but to use costly manufacturing methods. They took the wing’s press-formed skin and placed it in a bath to perform a not-so-environmentally-friendly chemical milling process, which created pockets to remove material and reduce weight. This skin then would be placed over the rib skeleton and riveted in place. Not only was this costly, but every rivet created a potential source for fatigue and corrosion problems.

Today, thanks to both advanced machining and laser peen forming, the manufacturing process is much simpler. The company starts with an aluminum ingot and machines out pockets, ribs, and the skin, but in a flat state. “With a laser, you can form that to the shape you need,” Hackel said. “And you don’t have rivets. It’s now a stronger design created with a two-step process. You machine it, and then you form it.”
