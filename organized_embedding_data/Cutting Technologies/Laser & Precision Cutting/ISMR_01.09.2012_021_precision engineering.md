Batch sizes, said Amada, have also dropped significantly which have also affected technology developments on Amada’s machines. Average batch size now is 50 or 100, as opposed to 400 a few years ago.

“On the press brake side, if you are doing a lot of tool changing because the batch sizes are smaller, then automatic tool change is very useful. On the lasers, being able to react more quickly and process more material through the machine, changing between different types of materials, is now very important. Flexibility and versatility have become crucial,” added Wood.

“A lot of punch-press challenges are toolingrelated,” he continued. “Customers want to be able to punch more and different types of shapes, extra processes that can be incorporated into the punch programme i.e. joggling (small folds into components), tapping, burring etc. The use of QR codes, for example, on punch press tooling caters for inexperienced operators. This seems to be where punch pressing is going now.”

Williams agreed. “Five years ago, punch presses appeared to be on the downward slide as laser technology advanced. However, if you have square panel work with holes, punch presses will still be more cost effective than lasers. Lasers come into their own with their versatility and range of thickness, as well as complexity of shape for parts. Punch presses can be solid cost effective workhorses,” he explained.

Amada’s press brake tooling is throughhardened and combined with Amada’s patented Amanit process for surface hardness, lubricity and grinding to maximise tool performance, tool durability and component accuracy. Its punch press tooling is designed to complement its latest all-electric punching machines.

The mentality right now amongst customers, added Wood, is that lasers tend to be used for material that is more than 3mm thick. This is driven by the quality required for a part – punching can often leave burring and overlap marks as opposed to clean laser finishes. The key, said both men, is to juggle between laser and punch technologies to achieve the most efficient process for the job required. They are often asked to advise customers which technology would be most applicable for a job or a process so that customers can work more efficiently.

“It is quite surprising how often you see someone who has bought a new machine still work exactly the same way as they used their old machine, so we try to help customers get the best from their new machines perhaps by working more efficiently. It is a process of education,” said Williams.

## New technology launches

At Amada UK’s recent June show, it launched four new products – the ATC tool changer, a 4m HD press brake (220 tons), a new style of automation for flatbed lasers called the MPF (halfway between a shuttle table and full automated tower) which loads single pallets and a large format flatbed laser (FO Mark 2 4222) – a 4.2 x 2.2 cutting bed that can accept larger cutting jobs.

About a year ago it launched the ACIES, a punch/laser combination machine. One of the main differences is that the lower turret is hidden and covered by brushes for operators that process materials that have to be scratchfree. It also incorporates a new tooling management system and a small buffer turret to add capacity to the tool change operation.

“While it is completing a laser operation, it can also be changing the punch press tools in the turret ready for the new punching operation,” added Wood.

He also believes that the trend towards automation will also benefit its range of robotic folders.

“However, I’m happy to say that we are seeing an increase in the number of apprenticeships that are being offered in the UK. We got involved with the World Skills event last October and will be doing the same for the UK World Skills competition in November. Getting young people interested in sheet metal is key – we often host school and college visits and we believe it is important to educate the teachers not just the children.”

Amada is making panels and bracketry in Kidderminster for the Bloodhound supersonic car project. It has cut and folded titanium parts with an Amada fiber laser. As well as aiming to break the 1000 mph land-speed record, the Bloodhound project’s main target is to promote science and engineering amongst school-age children.

“One of our clients, Hampson Aerospace, is making the back half of the car, with the rocket engine,” explained Williams. “He asked us to get involved in the project and we have made the first and second batch of parts (supports for the engine, outer skins for the lower half of the car, side supports etc.).” www.amada.co.uk for more details.
