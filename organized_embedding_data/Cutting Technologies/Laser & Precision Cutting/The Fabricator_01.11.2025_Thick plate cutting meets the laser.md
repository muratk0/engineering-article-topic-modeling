# Thick plate cutting meets the laser

6 considerations for plate fabricators new to laser cutting

By

Tim Heston

A 30-kW fiber laser initiates a plate cutting job. High-powered lasers cut plate quickly, but the real benefits come from eliminating secondary operations.

Images: Steel Warehouse

I

f you walked the halls of FABTECH in September, you might have noticed some extraordinary plate cutting going on—not with oxyfuel or plasma or even the waterjet, but with the ultrahigh-powered fiber laser. As powers reach 20, 30, 50 kW and beyond, fiber lasers have the potential to transform the plate cutting market.

Imagine you’re managing a shop that’s specialized in heavy plate for decades, relying on a collection of oxyfuel and plasma cutting machines—and now, you’ve installed your first high-powered fiber laser. What can you expect?

To walk through the process, The Fabricator spoke with South Bend, Ind.-based Steve Pugh, director of laser engineering at Steel Warehouse. For more than 20 years, Pugh has worked to push the laser’s limits, testing the quality of the service center’s material, including its signature hot-rolled pickled-and-oiled stock (which the high-powered fiber laser beam cuts very well). He’s worked with metal fabricators to make the most out of their operations and has managed Steel Warehouse’s in-house thermal cutting capabilities. Reflecting on all this experience, Pugh identified six factors that can help plasma cutting veterans transition to the ultrahigh-powered fiber laser.

1. Mind the Economics

"I’ve seen burn tables with plasma cutting heads that literally have been hit by the heavy magnet lifters hanging from a crane," Pugh said, adding that "most plasma tables are rack-and-pinion systems. The techs just put them back together, and the burn table was back in commission. You’re not going to do that with a fiber laser. If you hit things, you start destroying things."

Fiber lasers have enclosures surrounding the fiber laser head, of course, and on the largest machines, the enclosure travels with the gantry as it moves along the long cutting bed. Regardless, Pugh told the story to make a point: Treating a fiber laser as just another plate cutting machine can run counter to why a plate fabricator invests in the new technology in the first place. Fiber lasers have precision positioning systems. Anything that alters the workpiece position relative to the cutting head nozzle can throw carefully crafted cutting parameters out the window.

A plate cutting operation really has one core reason to invest in a fiber laser: to produce more high-quality cuts in less time using less floorspace. Translation: One high-powered fiber laser can replace several plasma cutting tables. It’s why, unlike in the sheet metal world, talk of cutting inches per minute (IPM) still pervades the plate cutting arena.

"We’re cutting 2-in.-thick material at 40 IPM," Pugh said. "On a powerful plasma system, cutting in that same material would be around 27 IPM, so you’re gaining 13 IPM."

The kerf quality also plays a role in the cutting economics. A plasma arc can produce a smooth, almost shiny edge, while a clean laser-cut edge still will have noticeable striations. The plasma arc does produce a tapered edge, however, and in 2-in.-thick material, that taper will be quite noticeable. The laser kerf, on the other hand, "will be absolutely straight," Pugh said, adding that this, among other reasons, is why the laser wins on cutting accuracy.

Now that high-powered lasers have cutting heads capable of beveling, welding operations are benefiting, especially those with Y preps involving narrow lands. "On a plasma, it’s easy to cut the land right off," he said. "It’s very difficult to hold a tight tolerance right at the edge of the material. This kind of bevel cutting becomes much easier with the high-powered fiber laser. If you have your settings right, you might have to do a little cleanup, but that’s about it."

Imagine a precision-cut plate being removed from the laser and fixtured directly into a robotic welding system, where the beveled cuts and precision lands mate perfectly to create what looks to be a zero-gap V groove—making life easy for the welding department. That quick flow, from cutting directly to welding, epitomizes high-powered laser cutting economics.

"We don’t like secondary operations," Pugh said.

Exceptions exist, of course. Some OEMs these days require such precise flatness—again, think of dropping a plate into a robotic weld fixture without any adjustment—that you’ll need secondary part leveling after cutting. For the most part, however, that direct flow from laser cutting to the next major operation remains the ideal.

When fiber lasers cut, they produce parts extraordinarily quickly. But when they’re down, they remove an enormous amount of cutting capacity in an instant. They’re precision pieces of equipment and, as such, demand unique attention.

2. Mind the Material Handling

On a large-format plasma table, once the torch stops, operators retrieve parts as the plasma starts cutting again on another area of the table.

These edges show the smooth, almost "shiny" quality that plasma cutting can achieve. Plasma-cut edges do have tapers, however, which can cause issues for applications with tight dimensional accuracy.

Laser-cut parts are stacked and ready for the next operation.

"You’re able to do this because of the plasma’s slow run-times," Pugh explained. Lifting parts out manually takes time, but the plasma cutting cycle can take even longer, especially if it’s cutting a large nest of small parts.

High-powered fiber lasers change this. They cut even the most intricate nests very quickly, and most manual denesting operations just can’t keep up. When the beam isn’t on, that investment isn’t making as much money as it could be—hence the need for a good material handling strategy.

"To keep the machine fed, you need to ensure parts are off the table," Pugh said. "When that machine isn’t cutting, it’s a cost."

Instead of operators lifting parts out with magnets or hand tools on the cutting table itself, Pugh recommended taking the entire plate—the parts plus the skeleton—off the cutting table and transporting them to an offload station. Once there, operators can remove pieces from the skeleton.

As plate cutting volumes rise, Pugh said, more material handling automation begins to make sense. He added, however, that material handling systems should consider the idiosyncrasies of plate, especially those in the secondary material market.

"If you’re not buying prime material, you need to pay attention to the variances in plate width," Pugh said. "If you’re buying 80-in.-wide material, you might get 82-in.-wide plate. And guess what? Your automation might not be able to handle that."

3. Mind the Material Yield

When moving to laser cutting, managers might see that taper-free cut in heavy plate, then immediately think of the material utilization possibilities. They picture dynamic nests that make full use of common-line cuts; not only is the laser cutting faster, but it’s also cutting two parts at once.

Pugh added that, yes, the potential for common-line cuts abounds, as long as cutting parameters are dialed in and the material quality is high. And dynamically nesting various jobs together can leave a sparse skeleton and send material yield—and job profitability—through the roof.

However, as laser cutting moves into extreme thicknesses, the customer mix that uses such heavy plate dwindles as well. "When you cut 0.75 in. and thicker, you might have only a handful of customers that use such material," Pugh said. This can mean using customer-specific, static nests when cutting heavy plate, which can drive down the resulting material yield.

Pugh clarified that the situation varies depending on a fabricator’s customer mix as well as the part shapes and sizes customers need. Regardless, when a shop can identify several large customers that use the same material grade and thickness, opportunity for increased material yield rises. When only one or a handful of customers use, say, 1.5-in.-thick material, the potential for extremely high material yield goes down. Programmers simply don’t have as many parts to draw from when puzzling together a nest layout.

4. Mind the Material Quality

Pugh recalled working with a manufacturer that was cutting 3/8-in.-thick hot-rolled plate. "For demonstration purposes, we started cutting this material, and as we did, the camber was so bad, it was blowing parts out of the skeleton. It looked like it was wanting to wrap back into a coil, yet it didn’t come from a coil. It was rolled straight from the slab into plate. It was some of the worst material I’d ever seen, and it could have caused some major damage to the laser head."

Material can often be the largest component of costs of goods sold (COGS), especially in heavy fabrication, so of course it’s seen as a potential area for cost reduction. But as Pugh explained, cheap material can be very, very expensive—especially when laser cutting.

To identify quality material, Pugh listed three areas to watch. First is the residual stress and potential distortion. Material might be roller, temper-pass, or stretcher leveled—but whatever the method, the need for material to remain flat during and after cutting never goes away. If the material moves during cutting, the process overall becomes less predictable—and a head crash on an extraordinarily productive machine can put a serious chokehold on capacity.

"If you have poor-quality material, it’s going to move after laser cutting," Pugh said. "There’s no getting around it."

The second element to watch for is the plate’s top surface quality. Some of the best plate material on the market offers a surface quality that facilitates a clean cut. This includes the scale jacket on hot-rolled material. "If you have a nice, tight scale jacket, the high-powered fiber lasers love it. And they love pickled-and-oiled material too. But if you run hot roll with a flaky surface, you’ll have trouble, so you’ll need to clean that surface."

Pugh described how Steel Warehouse has instituted a precleaning process for hot-rolled material with flaky surfaces. Operators place the heavy plate on the floor and use a device that looks like a walk-behind floor burnisher. "We use large pads with a heavy grit," adding that "this tends to break up the loose scale."

The third material quality factor to watch for is the chemical consistency. "The chemical composition of each material seems to dictate how we set up the optimal cut," he said.

Unlike the smooth edges from plasma cutting, laser-cut edges do have striations, but they also aren’t tapered and offer high dimensional accuracy.

Pugh added that material with higher silicon content (like 0.8% or higher, though the exact composition depends on the grade and application) tends to affect cut quality. Specifically, the silicon tends to "ball up" during the cut and cause heavy striations and blowouts at the bottom of the kerf. To accommodate, operators might tweak the cutting parameters to control heat—perhaps use less oxygen in the nitrogen-oxygen mix, increase the cutting speed, or turn the power down a bit.

Skilled operators who know laser cutting fundamentals (more on these later) can make the appropriate adjustments. But if the chemical makeup is extremely inconsistent, then all those adjustments (in cutting but also in bending and beyond) start making the laser less productive than it could be.

5. Mind the Slats

"If someone is having to change their Z axis position [of the cutting head] because of slat buildup," Pugh said, "they have unbelievably poor slat maintenance."

As a process, plasma throws plenty of slag onto the slats, but as Pugh explained, slag buildup on a laser machine can throw a wrench into the cutting process’s economics. Too much slag hurls another uncontrolled variable into the mix, which can hinder productivity.

Copper slats can help. Less slag adheres to them, and they require less effort to clean. "But if you’re processing heavy material, the copper slat tips tend to wear quicker," Pugh said. "Under the weight of heavy plate, they can bend." This changes the height of the plate and, hence, affects cut quality.

The best long-term solution is to integrate slat maintenance into the operator’s routine—be it once a week or even once a shift. Make the task as easy as possible and allow time for it in the production schedule.

"We’ve actually designed our own manual slat cleaners," Pugh said, adding that the team talked with operators about what would make the process as easy as possible. They wanted a light, simple tool that could be drug across the slats on a regular basis. So, they got to work and developed one, with teeth that chip away at the slag as operators pull the unit toward them.

6. Mind the Cutting Process

As Pugh explained, training plasma cutting operators on high-powered laser cutting usually isn’t difficult. The nature of the job is similar. Good plasma operators inspect the electrode and other elements to look for wear. Good laser operators look for variations in the nozzles, ensuring the orifice is free of debris. They both perform system calibrations, and they both observe the plume under the cut and watch out for variations.

For those with previous thermal cutting experience, laser cutting basics should sound familiar. The big differences are in process sensitivity. "A plasma will cut through pretty much anything, as long as you maintain the arc," Pugh said, "and anything that grounds will maintain the arc."

The idea in laser cutting is to maintain that laminar flow of assist gas surrounding a properly focused beam. Cutting carbon steel with oxygen assist gas relies on an exothermic reaction to drive the cut, and the pressure and flow are relatively low. When running with straight nitrogen assist gas (fusion cutting), the energy from the laser melts the material and the nitrogen assist gas evacuates it from the kerf—a feat that requires lots of pressure and high flow rates.

"When we’re laser cutting 2-in. plate with oxygen [assist gas], we’re running at just about one bar of pressure, or about 14.5 PSI," Pugh said. "On the other hand, when we’re cutting 20-mm material with straight nitrogen, we run between 8 and 10 bar, or around 140 PSI."

The assist gas strategy can change the optimal beam focus. These days, high-powered machines cutting thick carbon steel or aluminum with a mixed gas often use a negative focus—that is, a focus positioned below the material surface. The same applies to straight nitrogen cutting. In both cases, the energy concentration within the cut tends to help drive molten metal cleanly away.

Meanwhile, cutting extremely thick carbon steel with oxygen assist gas often benefits from a positive focus, above the material, a position that can optimize the exothermic reaction that drives the cutting action.

"When you get to laser cutting 2 in. and above, it’s almost as if you’re flame cutting," Pugh said. "It’s a much more concentrated and controlled flame, and a much faster cut, but that exothermic reaction is in the driver’s seat."

Machinery tech tables offer a solid foundation, and they vary with the specific machine. Pugh also described how operators might tweak the settings to optimize the cut. It boils down to controlling the heat and allowing time for molten metal to evacuate the kerf (and not "freeze" to the bottom as dross). To make this happen requires a well-focused beam and assist gas with sufficient flow and pressure. If, say, multiple lasers draw from bulk nitrogen all at once, the assist gas delivery system must ensure pressure stays steady. If not, operators risk losing the cut.

As a process, laser cutting offers plenty of "knobs" to tweak, and nearly every one of them is application-dependent (Pugh qualified every laser cutting parameter suggestion with "usually" or "often," never making a blanket statement). Operators might increase or decrease the speed, change the assist gas pressure and flow, perhaps even change the assist gas mixture to get more or less "boost" from the oxygen content.

"Surprisingly enough, when we run a mixed gas, we might go with a smaller nozzle, since that helps direct the flow and offer a better cut quality," Pugh said.

He added, however, that tweaking cut parameters can become a bit quixotic, with operators spending their days chasing those metaphorical windmills. They might be fighting poor material quality; poorly maintained equipment; a cutting nozzle with an out-of-round orifice; poorly designed plumbing and insufficient assist gas pressure; "gunky" slats; and not least, an overarching business strategy that doesn’t make the most out of the laser cutting process.

This is partly why Pugh likes to think of the economics of thick plate laser cutting first:

to cut more parts in less time using less floor space, no secondary process required

. The faster quality parts flow out the door to the customer, the more inventory turns occur, and the more successful a plate cutting fabricator can be.
