# FIBRE, CO2 OR DDL?

[TARİH: 01.01.2017 ISMR]

LASER (Light Amplification by Stimulated Emission of Radiation) has been a watchword for efficiency and quality in materials processing since its advent in the sixties. When used for cutting materials, the focused laser beam is directed at the material, which then either melts, burns, vaporizes away or is blown away by a jet of gas leaving an edge with a high-quality surface finish. Industrial laser cutters are used to cut flat sheet material as well as structural and piping materials.

The benefits of laser cutting over mechanical cutting include easier workholding and reduced contamination of workpieces. Precision may be better, since the laser beam does not wear during the process. There is also a reduced chance of warping the material that is cut, as laser systems have a small heat-affected zone. However, the laser cutting process can also involve high power consumption (heat input). The power consumption and efficiency of any particular laser will vary, depending upon output power and operating parameters.

For metal cutting, CO₂, fibre and Nd:YAG/ Nd:YVO (crystal)laser technologies are often used. Fibre and Disk lasers are the more efficient, more powerful big brothers of the early Nd:YAG machines. Each individual laser type is suitable for different requirements and applications, and each has its own advantages and disadvantages. The fibre laser is a relatively new innovation in laser cutting systems for machining thin, and now thicker, sheet metal (including non-ferrous materials such as copper, brass and titanium). A 'new kid on the block' for metal cutting is Direct Diode Laser (DDL) technology, which we also examine in this article.

The fibre laser is a relatively new innovation in laser cutting systems for machining thin, and now thicker, sheet metal.

We examine the benefits and drivers of different laser cutting technologies for sheet metal manufacturers.

## CO₂ laser cutting systems

CO₂ lasers are still the most widely used cutting systems on the market today. Their reputation has been built over many years and, while the leaps in process technology have substantially benefited fibre lasers, there have also been important enhancements in CO₂ laser cutting systems. CO₂ lasers have a relatively high efficiency rate and generally good beam quality. These laser cutting machines have been the main workhorse of the laser cutting world since the 1970s.

For example, high voltage and RF power supplies now used in CO₂resonators have evolved from vacuum tubes to solid-state devices and gas turbines have exchanged hard-bearing contact for non-contact magnetic bearings. These advances caused the related maintenance aspects of CO2 resonators to largely disappear.

New CO₂ resonators are also able to shift into power-saving modes that reduce energy consumption by as much as 80 per cent when idling. Rapid-piercing technologies can significantly reduce - by up to 80 per cent - the time needed to pierce through materials, then monitor when the pierce is completed to immediately start the cutting process.

CO₂ lasers are still the most widely used cutting systems on the market today.

Another strength of CO2 laser technology has also historically been high cutting quality when processing thicker materials, but this advantage has been gradually eroded by leaps in fibre laser process technology.

## An eye on fibre

Research shows that approximately 70 per cent of all cutting applications in the flat sheet cutting market are in the thin-material range (under 1/4-

inch thick). This constitutes a large portion of the market segment for flat sheet cutting and thus represents significant market potential. Over a relatively short period of time, we have seen exponential advances in fibre laser technology used for flat sheet metal cutting.

The primary advantages of cutting flat sheet metal with this laser technology are derived from its monolithic, fibre-to-fibre, compact solid state design configuration that is almost maintenance-free and provides a lower cost of operation than can generally be achieved with comparable CO₂ lasers. The higher absorption of the fibre wavelength and the higher power density created by the focused beam combine to achieve a 300 per cent increase in cutting speed in thin materials that are less than 1/8 in thick.

Prima Power launched its Laser Genius 5030 fibre laser cutting system at EuroBLECH 2016.

Approximately 70 per cent of all cutting applications in the flat sheet cutting market are in the thin-material range.

Systems can certainly cut thicker and faster with higher fibre laser power, but the "sweet spot" where the most valuable benefits are achieved remains in the '1/8 inch and under' range when making comparisons with CO₂ systems.

The use of fibres eliminates the need for elaborate and costly optics for beam guidance and the space requirement can be downsized. This also makes fibre lasers very stable and easy to use. The beam quality is high and continuously becoming more powerful. Fibre lasers are also very efficient and easy to cool, converting around 70-80 % of the power from the pump source. This is better for power consumption, but also means that less power is being converted to heat.

A range of manufacturers have invested heavily in fibre laser offerings to the global market while subcontractors and job shops have quickly found that the fibre laser's extended cutting capabilities have extended their reach into new markets and new customers. Fibre lasers can be comfortably used for the efficient processing of gauge and fractional materials and fine feature cutting. Cut capability on steel is from thin gauge to approximately 20mm.

## Direct diode laser cutting

Interest in using direct diode lasers, rather than fibre or CO₂ lasers, is largely enabled by improvements in performance that now allow high-speed diode laser cutting of all relevant industrial metals. The combination of higher wall plug efficiency, together with improved reliability and ease of servicing, have made the technology attractive to manufacturers.

Companies such as TeraDiode and Laserline have showcased direct diode laser-based production equipment for metal cutting. Japanese manufacturer, Amada, also showcased a prototype DDL (Direct Diode Laser) machine for cutting sheet metal at EuroBLECH in 2014. Yamazaki Mazak most recently launched a variety of new laser and automation systems at EuroBLECH 2016 in Hanover last October, including a new Direct Diode Laser (DDL) cutting system.

"We are revolutionising laser cutting with the global debut of our first ever Direct Diode Laser (DDL) to be developed for flat sheet, at EuroBLECH 2016," explained Gaetano Lo Guzzo, Director, Laser Business Europe at Yamazaki Mazak. "Direct Diode Laser is an important step forwards in laser. The application is similar to fibre, but the performance is much better (20% faster than fibre). This is because the wavelength is smaller for better material penetration and absorption. Energy-efficiency for the DDL is also around 50%."

The new OPTIPLEX 3015 DDL 4kW, using Direct Diode Laser technology, is the next generation of solid state laser for industrial laser applications, offering high efficiency for laser cutting operations. The machine, which complements Mazak's existing CO₂ and Fiber OPTIPLEX machines, uses a Mazak-manufactured Direct Diode Laser and has been positioned to offer high cutting performance.

"The OPTIPLEX DDL series can cut thin material 20 per cent faster than fibre lasers and thick materials with unsurpassed surface quality. The machine has an axis acceleration of 1.8G and benefits from rapid traverse rates of 120m/min. Positioning accuracy is maintained to within ±0.05mm/500mm in the X- and Y-axes and to within ±0.01mm/100mm in the Z-axis. The machine also offers exceptional repeatability accuracy of ±0.03 in the X-, Y- and Z-axes," explained Lo Guzzo.

"The OPTIPLEX DDL is also capable of a wall plug efficiency of 40-50 per cent compared to 10 per cent for a CO₂ resonator; 15-20 per cent for a disc resonator and 30-40 per cent with a fibre resonator. It also features our new CNC control, MAZATROL PreviewG."

## Making a choice

The question of which technology is best really boils down to your operation. How well does the system fit your particular application?

How much faster and how much more cost-effectively are your parts going to be produced? A user will need to make a careful analysis of all of the pertinent data including the application range, operating costs, throughput, cost of ownership and, of course, investment costs.

The other key aspect in determining which technology to choose is differentiation. How important is it to distinguish your operation from that of your competition? Will a high speed 6kW CO₂ laser with a larger table size provide a significant competitive advantage, or will a fibre laser give you a better competitive advantage?

Whatever the final conclusion, don't forget about the impact of this investment on your downstream processes. What effect does adding this new laser cutting system have on the flow of operations performed on the parts after they are cut?

## A new shelving line concept

IRON's punching and bending line is a flexible modular line that incorporates a punch press (with thick turret tooling), a part-turning device (after punching), positive and negative bends on the punched parts and can handle part dimensions ranging from 650mm width x 1500mm length to 1500mm width x 3600mm in length. It is now part of an innovative new shelving concept, conceived by IRON, for different markets and applications.

"We have supplied many punching/bending lines for customers who produce supermarket display shelving systems in countries like Russia. We have also sold lines to customers for producing electrical enclosures," explained Dalla Pace.

"When I started travelling in India, I saw that supermarkets and shops needed to display retail goods on shelving and racking. Many consumers in India prefer to browse through goods on shelves rather than burrow through 'cash and carry' racking. I have therefore proposed a new 'Shelvy' customised line concept for the Indian market for shelving production - instead of just supplying the punching/bending line to manufacturers, they can also produce customised shelving units onsite from coil."

The concept is intriguing - users produce the shelving drawings that they want, split into different part numbers (e.g. specifying the number of uprights, supports etc.). IRON software will then modify the drawings to their requirements. IRON's punching and bending from coil line can produce the customised shelving on a Just in Time basis to suit user requirements. The line can laser cut coil to different lengths and thicknesses.

"The punching and bending line is very flexible and very cost-effective, compared with the investment needed in roll forming machines to produce individual shelving. If customers have this flexible line, they can make their own shelving on-site. You don't need a large workshop for the line (punching and bending, laser cutting, painting and perhaps some welding support). I estimate that 10,000 square metres will suffice. The laser can cut perforations in the tube needed for the shelving units. The entire line can process one million panels on average per year (depending upon the type and size of shelf). The workshop can then produce up to 55,000 freestanding shelving systems, with full flexibility (just one shelving unit can be manufactured, if that is all that is needed)," Massimo Dalla Pace told ISMR.

The same line can also be used to produce electrical enclosures. The concept is therefore portable and can be used to manufacturer a variety of products in different industries.

IRON's coil-fed laser cutting line, dubbed TEMNOS, has been installed as a demonstration line in the US by RDI. The coil-fed laser line can handle material that is 2m wide and up to 3.2mm thick. IRON USA's office can test and process samples.

"The entry feed line (6/10-tonne decoiler/ levelling) uses 19 rolls with 6-high backup rolls, then loop compensation," Massimo Dalla Pace told ISMR. "The main advantages of using coil over sheet is that you don't need to cut to length, there is no need to nest (hence less scrap) and no downtime as you do not have to wait for blanks to be loaded onto the machine. There are substantial time and cost advantages to using coil over blanks with less material used overall. It is a more efficient process and certainly less costly.

"The customer can manufacture, for example, metal furniture parts (a cupboard's side panels, door, back panel etc.) using just one coil width," he added. "The line can punch and cut to length as required. A full range of parts can therefore be produced from just one coil - a very flexible process. This reduces scrap levels and increases productivity and efficiency on the line."
