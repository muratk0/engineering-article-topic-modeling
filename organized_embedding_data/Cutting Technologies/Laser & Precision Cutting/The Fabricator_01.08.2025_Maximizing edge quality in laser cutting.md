Laser Cutting Basics

A laser is a source of intense heat that is used to cut metal, but how does it work?

The laser, whether CO

2

or fiber, generates a beam of light delivered by the cutting head, where the beam is controlled precisely to a specific speed and power. When the concentrated laser beam interacts with the metal, it generates intense heat that is absorbed by the material, causing it to melt. Since the laser beam cannot cut through material on its own, it requires an assist gas such as oxygen or nitrogen to help push the molten metal out of the cut.

This process integrates optics, CAD programs, and computer-controlled lasers to create highly precise and intricate cuts across a variety of materials, including stainless steel, aluminum, copper, brass, and carbon steel.

When material thickness increases, it becomes more difficult for the laser to cut a perfect edge, but it can be achieved with practice and a thorough understanding of the laser cutting process.

Different types of lasers can cut different thicknesses. Some are able to cut through more material than others:

• A low-powered laser might only cut thin sheet metal (a few millimeters).

• A mid-range laser (around 3 kW to 4 kW) might cut carbon steel up to 1 in. thick and stainless steel up to 0.75 in.

• A high-powered laser (6 kW or higher) can cut carbon steel up to 2 in. thick and stainless steel up to 1.5 in.

Achieving a Quality Edge

So what makes that clean, consistent, uniform edge? It’s a combination of correctly set parameters, such as power, frequency, duty cycle, feed rate, assist gas, laser beam alignment, and focus adjustment, as well as regular machine maintenance.

Each of these factors influences the cut in a different way, and the parameters may vary depending on the material type. Understanding how they work together is key to achieving the highest-quality cuts.

Power.

Measured in watts, power refers to the amount of energy delivered by the laser to the material to melt it. The power required depends on the material’s melting point, so the first step in setting power is understanding the characteristics and properties of the material.

Higher power means more energy consumption, with faster and more efficient cuts through various materials. Higher power is needed for thicker materials, while lower power is more suitable for intricate jobs that require high tolerances and precision.

Frequency.

Frequency is the rate at which the laser beam pulses, measured in pulses per second, or hertz. Typically, lasers operate at frequencies from 500 to 5,000 pulses per second.

Duty Cycle.

Duty cycle works closely with frequency, controlling how long the laser beam is on during each pulse. Duty cycle allows for cooling in between pulses. A lower duty cycle allows for more cooling time, while a higher duty cycle adds heat and does not allow as much cooling.

For fiber lasers, the assist gas used depends on the material type. For example, the reflective properties of stainless steel can make it difficult for a laser to cut cleanly through it. Therefore, the fiber laser requires an assist gas such as nitrogen or air to cut through reflective materials. For aluminum, copper, and brass, which are reflective and soft, and nickel alloy, which is soft but not reflective, nitrogen yields a cleaner cut than compressed air, because it does not cause the edges to oxidize. Nitrogen also provides a better finish, giving a smoother, cleaner cut with less slag. Oxidation might occur when there is not enough gas pressure (see Figure 2).

Cold-rolled steel, carbon steel, and galvanized steel are harder and dirtier materials than aluminum, copper, brass, and nickel alloy, so they are cut with oxygen as the assist gas, which provides more heat.

Alignment and Focus Adjustment.

Exact centering of the nozzle is a crucial step to maintain the tightest tolerances and achieve precise cuts.

The nozzle tip is located at the bottom of the torch. As the beam passes through the lens, it focuses into a very small spot which then passes through the nozzle tip. The beam must pass through the very center of the nozzle tip to produce the highest–quality cut. Centering misalignment can be caused by several issues, including an overheated nozzle, directional cutting errors, piece blowouts, and spotting on the lens.

In addition, the nozzle must be positioned correctly in relation to the material. A closer position results in higher-quality cuts, but if it’s too close, the nozzle can become dirty with buildup or even sustain damage (see

Understanding the relationships the parameters have with each other is crucial. Setting the laser at maximum power is a good way to create a baseline. Adjustment of the other parameters then depends on the shapes being cut and the material’s characteristics. Making sample cuts will help you dial in the right feed rate, frequency, duty cycle, and other parameters to ensure a fast, successful cut.

Laser Cutting Machine Maintenance

Also essential for achieving high-quality cuts consistently is proper machine maintenance, and it all starts with the cutting head, which houses the focus lens and nozzle tip.

As mentioned previously, the beam needs to go right through the center of the nozzle tip to produce a quality cut. For this reason, the lens, mirror, and nozzle components must remain free of debris and buildup, as contamination can change the location of the focus tip. Accumulation of particles, smoke residue, and dirt can not only reduce cut quality but also increase component wear and the potential for machine failure.

The quantity and type of materials being cut determine how often the components need to be cleaned. In high-quantity runs, for instance, the laser nozzle must be cleaned frequently. A clogged nozzle can reduce the beam’s effectiveness, create inconsistent cutting, and damage the machine. Be sure to inspect and clean the nozzle as often as necessary to ensure optimum performance and highest possible cut quality.

Some laser cutting machines are equipped with self-cleaning technology, while others may need to be cleaned manually. Using a soft brush or compressed air is often most effective for removing residue from the nozzle. Additionally, regular inspection of nozzle wear is essential, as nozzle degradation can impact cutting precision over time.

Laser cutting machines are very dependent on assist gases, so also be sure to check the gas system and gas supply. Regular system inspections for leaks or signs of wear help ensure gas hoses and connections remain secure. Also be sure to maintain proper lubrication for all moving parts to help prevent operational issues and extend machine longevity.

Finally, preserve the machine’s cooling system by regularly checking coolant levels, keeping the cooling fan free from particles and dust, and keeping a fresh filter. Proper cooling maintenance helps sustain high-quality production runs while maximizing the laser’s reliability and longevity.
