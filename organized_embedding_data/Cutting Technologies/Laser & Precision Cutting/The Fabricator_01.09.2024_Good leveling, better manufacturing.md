# Good leveling, better manufacturing

Leveling

How internal part stress wreaks havoc

By

Denis Weinfurtner

Distorted laser-cut parts (left) can wreak havoc on downstream operations.

L

et’s not beat around the bush here: In the sheet metal and plate fabrication world, leveling is a process everyone loves to hate. Fabricators of course want to receive laser-flat sheets that can be cut into burrfree, dead-flat parts that head right to the next downstream process. That’s the ideal, but we don’t live in an ideal world. Every action has a reaction. When you work material, you change its stress characteristics. It’s physics.

Leveling is often looked upon as a non-value-adding process. In truth, though, when you look at the entire metal fabrication value stream, including downstream processes like bending and (especially) welding, you see just how much value a well-tuned leveling operation adds. Jobs flow without issue—no struggles fixturing distorted parts, no frustratingly long setups at the press brake, no paint coverage issues due to poor upstream processing. Sending a pile of warped blanks down the value stream is like throwing a jagged rock into calm waters. The ripple effects run far and wide.

Wherever it happens—before thermal cutting (sheet leveling) or after (part leveling)—the process remains a critical step in the metal fabrication value chain. Used wisely and correctly, it can increase quality, smooth part flow, and, ultimately, make a metal fabricator more competitive.

Manage Your (Material) Stress

No technology can remove all residual stress from sheet metal or plate, though the latest leveling systems get very close. The idea is to remove as much residual stress as you can so that the metal behaves predictably downstream. Also note that manually manipulating parts after cutting—say, with a roll bender or other device—back to a flat state does

not

relieve the internal stress. Such manual, uncontrolled cold-working can in fact induce

more

stress into certain areas and, hence, wreak havoc on downstream processes.

Warping issues can be obvious, especially for fabricators manufacturing precision products to a specified, very tight flatness tolerance. Many times, however, the problem isn’t so obvious. Cut parts look flat when they’re stacked. Yet downstream, the welding department supervisor struggles with a robot teach pendant. Try as he might, he can’t get the robot to create a quality weld. The fit-up just isn’t consistent, and that’s due to high internal stresses within the parts that showcase during welding.

You dig a little deeper. It turns out, your manual welders knew this weldment presented issues. They’d adjust their approach and tweak the fixturing to "make it work."

Just part of the job

, they thought.

Just because a part is flat doesn’t mean it’s stress free. As welders apply heat, the stress manifests itself as the workpiece starts to bow. Rework ensues—more clamps, tweaked welding technique, new clamp placement, more tweaking. So much for trying to automate.

The Basics of Roller Leveling

Roller leveling remains the most common way to remove residual stress from sheet and plate. It’s proven and simple. Upper and lower leveling rolls, with backup rollers behind them, are set with a gap in between them. The gap at the entry rolls starts at a percentage (usually around 50%) of the material thickness, then ends up equal to the material thickness at the exit rolls (see

Figure 1

).

Pressure from the rolls repeatedly brings the material past its yield point as the material serpentines through the machine. The material then is straightened and worked less and less as it approaches the exit rolls. The bottom rolls remain stationary while the top roll-set tilts to create the increasing roll gap between the entry and exit rolls. The strong bends at the entry rolls remove that residual stress, while the rolls near the exit return the material to a flat state. The material that emerges not only is visibly flat but also has most of its residual stress removed. (Again, note the word "most." Leveling can remove nearly all residual stress, but it doesn’t remove all of it.)

Challenging leveling work, like applications involving high-strength material, can call for specific leveler designs. In many cases, a six-high leveler can be a good choice (see

Figure 2

). These have three rows of rolls above and below the workpiece: the support rolls, the intermediate rolls, and the leveling rolls that contact the workpiece. Specialty systems have motorized, adjustable "backups" underneath the leveling rollers that apply pressure in specific areas of the work—say, to remove edge wave on the sides of a sheet (see

Figure 3

).

The options abound, but the goal remains simple: to address defects like edge wave, center buckle and coil set, and, arguably most important, the residual stress. For some defects like edge wave or center buckle, roller levelers use adjustable backup rollers to precisely apply more pressure onto the material at specific spots. This allows the leveling rollers to deflect in specific areas across the sheet, addressing defects like edge wave and center buckle. Some machines also tilt the upper rollers in such a way that eliminates specific shape defects (see

Figure 4

).

Critical Factors

Choosing a leveler requires focusing on three principal material factors: the thickness range, yield strength, and flatness tolerances. Two grades might be the same thickness, but if their yield points are dramatically different, they will have different gap settings on a leveler.

Cutouts are another factor. Two materials might be the same grade with the same specified yield point and even the same thickness, yet one has cutouts and the other does not. Test parts will likely show the material with cutouts behaves differently.

Heat treatment can also enter the equation. If cut blanks undergo heat treating and then require leveling, letting those hot blanks cool completely might be a problem. The material yield rises after cooling. Sometimes it’s so hard and brittle that the leveler induces cracking and can potentially break the parts.

To prevent this, operators in certain cases choose to heat-treat and then, as the blanks are cooling and still malleable, send them through the leveler, after which they let the parts cool completely. This isn’t common in general fabrication, but for certain sectors, like knife and saw blade manufacturing, the strategy works extremely well.

Material Grades, Surface Condition, and Burrs

Stainless steel and aluminum can be particularly susceptible to warping, but it can happen to any material, including thick carbon steel. A fabricator might get what seems to be perfectly flat carbon steel plate from the mill, yet it discovers the material distorts when processed downstream. The plate might be flat, but the residual stress is still hiding within. So, it sends the plate through a high-quality leveler, after which the pieces flow downstream without issue.

Surface conditions also can factor into the equation, depending on the fabrication requirements. In some cases, operators send hot-rolled plate through the leveling machine, scale and all. The rollers themselves are hardened, so they aren’t affected by scale and debris. That said, they do need to be cleaned, especially if a shop produces surface-critical parts along with scaly carbon steel. In many cases, operators might run scaly hot-rolled in the morning, pull out the roller cassettes to clean the main and backup rollers, then run surface-critical material in the afternoon.

The same would apply for hot-rolled pickled and oiled (HRP&O) or any other material with an oily surface. Yes, that oil does get on the rollers, but as long as the leveler cassettes are cleaned regularly, the machine should operate without issue. The oil doesn’t affect machine operation at all. Operators simply need to clean the rolls to ensure oil isn’t transferred onto other parts.

Blasted surfaces also have an effect, though most of the issues emerge indirectly—through burrs. Cutting those blasted surfaces, particularly with a laser, can create some significant dross that requires deburring, and those burrs can be problematic for a leveler. The leveler works as intended, but it also can "press" those burrs onto the plate edges. This makes a poor edge even worse and, if unaddressed, can cause issues downstream, especially if the edge will be painted. To rectify it, an operator needs to not only grind it but also perform some edge rounding—a feat that takes time and finishing talent.

Burrs in general can create problems, which is why deburring and part leveling are often used as complementary processes. Say a cutting process creates a very small burr. It could drop off neatly after the leveler’s rolls make contact, but that’s not guaranteed to happen. The burr might embed itself into the edge. And tiny as that burr might be, it still can stick out like a sore thumb after painting.

The ideal approach is to cut first, then deburr, then level, then prepare the surface for painting (via blasting or cleaning and pretreatment). Even so, this process order might not be possible for parts that are distorted. A deburring machine is not a part leveling machine. Its brushes work well when dealing with slightly or even moderately warped parts. But they aren’t designed to deburr severely warped material. At best, the brushes will grind heavily in some areas but leave other areas untouched. In these cases, a fabricator might have no choice but to level that

severely

distorted workpiece first.

Ideally, a fabricator can scrutinize the leveling that occurs

before

cutting, so the part doesn’t distort so severely on the cutting table in the first place, as well as the cutting process itself, including the cutting parameters, toolpath sequence, and overall nesting strategy. After all, a bowed part increases the chance for head crashes and makes the primary cutting process less predictable overall (see

Figure 5

).

Sizing a Leveler

Investing in a leveler isn’t like investing in, say, a press brake or laser cutting machine. A highproduct-mix job shop might choose the largest cutting table, the highest fiber laser cutting power, and the longest brake bed it can afford to handle the largest variety of parts, both in size and thickness.

Leveling doesn’t work like that. Today’s most powerful machines can level material that’s more than 2 in. thick. Still, those machines are not designed to handle the full range of material thicknesses and yield strengths.

FIGURE 5 A thick aluminum part bows upward after laser cutting, while parts in preleveled aluminum plate remain flat in the skeleton.

Roll diameter and the spacing between them are all designed to handle a specific thickness range. If you try to level very thin sheet on a roller leveler designed for thick plate, the pitch between the rolls will be too large to level and remove that residual stress properly. Conversely, if you try to level thick plate on a roller level designed for thin sheet—with small roll diameters pitched closely together—the machine won’t work as intended, and you can end up damaging the material.

The best approach is to choose a leveler that fits most of your product mix. You then can rely on outside service providers for the rest. This of course comes with a cost, but depending on your material supply situation, it might be the best option.

Part Geometry Factors

There’s no getting around the fact that a fabricator’s primary cutting operation induces stress, and both the cut path and part geometry play a role in how that stress makes itself known on the cutting table.

Long parts are particularly susceptible to distortion, as are parts with large cutouts. Depending on the technology, thermal cutting all those cutouts can put a lot of heat into the material—an environment ripe for more distortion and more internal stress.

Levelers also can have issues with microtabs holding multiple parts or internal cutouts in a cut sheet. Leaving cutouts in place within a laser-cut part might be a good solution for material integrity when, say, you’re bending a large workpiece on a press brake; after all, large, thin workpieces with large cutouts can be difficult to handle. But in part leveling, those microtabs can sometimes break as rollers bring the material past its yield point.

Circular parts present unique challenges. For circular parts that need to be absolutely flat, an operator often sends the piece through a part leveler once, then rotates it by 90 degrees and sends it through again. This eliminates distortion in both directions and creates a dead-flat blank. Leveling circles twice does take a little time—but again, doing so can eliminate so many headaches downstream.

Leveling in Context

To drive overall costs down and make your manufacturing operation predictable, residual stress reduction must happen

somewhere

in the supply chain. If cheap material hasn’t been leveled properly, its

true

cost can be a lot higher.

As a process, leveling needs to be put in context. Imagine you have sheet in which only 30% of the residual stress has been removed before laser cutting. That means you have 70% of stress remaining. Laser cutting adds another 20%, making a stressful situation (so to speak) even worse.

How much leveling is required depends on the application. Some operations—especially those that focus on high-precision work like aerospace and medical—might have sheet leveling before laser cutting and part leveling after. Again, it depends on flatness requirements and how material stress affects downstream operations.

Regardless, the better those blanks are prepared for downstream processing, the more predictable the entire metal fabrication value stream can be. Pieces are deburred and edges are rounded and then leveled to a perfect flatness. That offers a level of precision where even highly automated downstream processes can thrive.
