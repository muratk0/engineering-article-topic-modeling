# Knowing the ins and outs of plasma cutting tube

C

an using plasma to cut your tube and pipe be a better option for your operation than a saw or laser? Quite possibly, but using plasma cutting technology well means knowing what the guardrails are and the latest measures being taken to mitigate one of plasma’s inherent trouble spots.

THE BUYING DECISION

The decision goes further than sticker price. While the initial cost of a typical tube plasma cutting machine is one-tenth of even a lower-end tube laser, shops also need to start the process of finding a plasma cutter by evaluating some basics, including what type of cutting they’re doing, what material sizes and types they’re typically cutting, and how much detail work they’re doing.

"The first question is always, ‘What’s your current process?’" said Kyle Merry, director of sales and business development for Bend-Tech, Osceola, Wis., maker of the Dragon A400 and A250 tube plasma cutting machines. "That drives a lot of other questions, but understanding their current processes and their current struggles really helps navigate that purchasing decision—whether they’re outsourcing, using hand tools or hand plasma, or anything like that."

The great benefits of lasers are speed and the unparalleled precision of their cuts. The choice begs the question, though: Just how precise do you need to be? While lasers can deliver tolerances of a few thousandths of an inch, that level of accuracy—and the cost to achieve it—may be more than most shops need to satisfy their customers.

"The majority of our customer base don’t need laser quality," Merry said. "With plasma and how we’re utilizing it with our machine and our software, we’re achieving ±0.01 in., which for general fabrication and the majority of things being built in this world is plenty good."

GUIDING THE LIGHT

Software abounds for all cutting machines today, and many shops are even writing their own. The key is interoperability—the software’s ability to accept different vendors’ STEP files and talk directly with the software on which customers are designing their parts.

Bend-Tech has long been offering its own software package, which includes CAD and CAM. It is designed to communicate in particular with three platforms popular among metal shops: SDS2, Fusion, and Tekla.

"We put a Bend-Tech button right on their software screen," Merry noted. "It’ll only bring over the materials that the machine is capable of doing. You don’t have to worry about importing all the major detail work, nuts and bolts. It’s only going to bring over the material that our machine’s going to cut.

"If you’re a startup and you’re used to drawing things on napkins with a pen, we have you covered as far as being able to jump in, start designing, and start cutting."

SUPPORT, COOLING, AND GETTING AHEAD OF THE KERF

Of course, cutting tubes with any method comes with inherent drawbacks. With blades, you often have to deburr the finished piece, and lasers almost always produce some level of slag (melted metal). One of the most prominent arguments against cutting tube with plasma is the amount of dross (impurities) that gets sprayed into the ID of the tube (as opposed to simply blowing into the table bed underneath the cutting surface while processing flat sheet).

One option offered by Bend-Tech is a coolant support system, introduced three years ago, that runs coolant through the tube to displace the dross and slag blown into the tube’s ID by the plasma jet as well as harmful exhaust fumes. The system also includes a mechanism to catch coolant underneath the workpiece so that it can recirculate it.

"It makes the material a lot safer to handle right off the machine versus being weld-hot," Merry said. "It allows it to be essentially weld-ready right off the machine, so you have a lot less cleanup time and a lot more accuracy as far as the fit-up goes.

"But then also … reducing that exhaust, especially when you’re talking stainless steel and galvanized steel," he added. "They have some pretty nasty fumes to them."

Another possible drawback to plasma cutting, especially when cutting pipes with thicker-than-average walls, is increased kerf width at the bottom of the cut. But that, too, can be mitigated.

"As your amperage goes up, you need more of a beam to blast through that material," Merry said. "What we’re doing is actually adjusting for the kerf.

"If you’re cutting thicker material, you’ll have a higher kerf. We’re adjusting that line so that your parts are coming out correct," he said. "There’s significantly more dross being sprayed through the material. There’s more material being taken away because there’s more material to cut. We’re grabbing all of that and flushing it out before it has a chance to weld to the inside of the tube."

SPEAKING OF THICKER MATERIAL …

One of the risks of plasma cutting is trying to run ahead of physics— in other words, not minding the bell curve that governs the concurrent decrease in cutting speed as material thickness increases at any given amperage. It’s a common temptation, but rules are rules.

"The thickness [question] really has to do with the plasma system itself, the amperage of the system," Merry said. "We can go all the way up to 1 in. if we really wanted to, but typically the material is going to be sub-¾ in. The majority of our customers are running 0.060- to ¼-in. wall—that’s the normal range—with some ⅝-in. in there when it comes to pipe.

"A very common question I get is, ‘Well, can I just get a 125-amp plasma and run it at 700 IPM?’ Well, no, there is a bell curve there as far as having the right speeds for cutting for that material thickness."

And that curve isn’t trivial. Merry noted that speeds can decrease by about one-third. For example, for tube with a 0.060-in. wall thickness, a plasma torch can cut at a rate of 140 IPM. But bump that thickness up to, say, ¼ in., and suddenly that drops to around 80 or 90 IPM. After that, it drops even more.

"You can get into the 60s—that’s pretty much the floor, that 60 IPM," Merry said.
