I

magine you have an immense blanking operation, complete with multiple towers for the laser and automated part removal in punching—everything fine-tuned for unattended production. All seems right with the world, until you get an alarm. The forks that lift the cut skeleton jam under a long, skinny piece that bowed downward. Or the punch stops because of a tangled skeleton that the clamps are trying to move out of the work envelope.

Or maybe the problem resides at denesting. Standing next to immense towers representing the latest and greatest in laser cutting automation, a few workers wield hammers and other tools to pry parts out of sheet skeletons. The scene exudes some serious irony.

Blanking experts at Dalsin Industries have worked for decades to avoid such irony. The Bloomington, Minn., fabricator has a collection of TRUMPF punch presses, three with automated part removal and stacking, as well as automated laser cutting with towers and parts offloading systems connected to several Mitsubishi lasers, including a 10-kW fiber laser installed this year. The fabricator’s engineers consider myriad factors to ensure optimal part and skeletal stability, from the first cut to final part removal. Central to this is the microtab. Used wisely, they really hold a blanking operation together, both figuratively and literally.

Diving Deep Into the Microtab

Matt Dalsin, maintenance and facilities manager at Dalsin Industries, remembers receiving "colorful" feedback from those at the denesting station, especially before investing in automated part removal and stacking in punching. To ensure nest rigidity as the table clamps moved the sheet throughout the punch program, cut parts remained securely tabbed in place. Getting pieces out of the nest, though, wasn’t always easy.

At Dalsin, facilitating feedback among operators and denesters always has been critical, he said. Otherwise, programmers would fall prey to overtabbing. "We call it the shake-out operation for a reason. It’s not a pound-out station. If we put too many tabs in there, they ended up hitting the sheet with a hammer."

Overtabbing is understandable. No one wants unexpected downtime in blanking, and tabbing too little would seem to be worse than tabbing too much. In punching, insufficient microtabbing could tip or even shake loose a part mid-process.

"Even when we were doing traditional punching with manual shake-out, we never defined our tabs too early in the code," said Brian Tennessen, engineer. "As the sheet moved around, parts could rattle and break free. So, we’d always define those tabs as late in the code as we possibly could."

Tabbing issues in automated laser cutting have even more dire consequences. A part or slug can tip, which is bad enough; but tabbing incorrectly can also leave parts sagging below the skeleton farther than they should. Pieces catch on the forks moving in to remove the sheet, bringing a large automated system to a screeching halt.

As Kurt Dalsin, engineer, explained, "Long, skinny parts are the most challenging here with fork [tines] going over or under a part or a skeleton web section. The right tabbing strategy keeps your parts flat."

Still, microtabbing too much has consequences too. Just ask anyone at the offload table, with a deburr tool, or those sending blanks through a flat-part deburring machine. The key is finding the optimal balance between securing that blank and removing it easily after cutting.

Nested onto a laser-cut sheet, parts are held in place by a single microtab. That makes them easy to remove and sufficiently stable for blanking automation.

From the Manual to the Automated Punch

To this end, the fabricator’s microtabbing strategy has evolved with the cutting technology. For instance, Dalsin traditionally set microtabs kitty-corner on the short edges of rectangular blanks. Holding the piece diagonally kept everything secure as the clamps moved the sheet through punching.

Today, tabbing like this would be overkill. Because of that continual feedback from denesters, Dalsin programmers know that tabbing kitty-corner makes part removal a bear. It used to be just a necessary evil in traditional punching; after all, a challenge in denesting was better than crashing a punch press.

Today, the shop’s automated punching technology rarely requires microtabbing. Parts are punched with (usually) internal features and forms first, then the part perimeter, before the final punch parts-out the blank just before it falls down a chute or is retrieved by the grippers.

Sometimes, piece perimeters are cut partially before certain features and forms, simply for access and collision issues. For instance, an emboss close to a cut line could be partially flattened if formed before that perimeter cut is punched. In these cases, the punch cuts the perimeter part of the way—enough to eliminate the tooling interference issue but not so much as to affect part stability in the skeleton. Once the form is made, the punch makes the final cuts before the part is removed. In either case, the last hit that parts the blank from the skeleton can be significant. "We often use a slotting tool, and that last hit can be about 3 in.," Tennessen said. "This means that the part is very secure in the nest until that last hit."

Automated part removal not only eliminates the need for denesting and deburring tabs, but it also makes new nesting strategies more practical—including common-line cutting. A group of common-cut parts is probably not going to be stable enough to remain secure as the clamps move the sheet from cut to cut. But this isn’t the case if parts are punched along a common line and then immediately sent down a chute or removed by grippers.

That said, the remaining skeleton still needs to be rigid enough not to warp and potentially collide with machine components late in the program. Many common-cut sheets end up looking like big picture frames, thick enough to remain stable as they’re transported out of the work envelope for disposal.

"In some cases, all that’s left of a skeleton is the strip that’s held by the clamps," Tennessen explained, adding that the final stages of the punch program "eat away" at the remaining web sections. This of course adds cycle time and increases the number of punch hits, "but it also makes the automation run very reliably." There’s absolutely no chance for elements of the skeleton to curl and collide as it’s being evacuated because, well, no skeleton remains after the punching cycle.

As Matt Dalsin explained, "When you have one entangled skeleton, all the gains with automated punching go out the window as you stop everything to clear the machine."

The Microtab and the Automated Laser

Though exact tabbing strategies on the laser depend on the part and specific nest, rectangular parts are often microtabbed in the middle of their short edges. To ensure part stability, parts are nested across the slats whenever possible. Depending on the part geometry, nesting skinny parts with the longest edges parallel to the slats might lead to some instability. (Nesting long parts parallel to the slats also can lead to slag buildup, especially if a cut line sits directly over a slat—more on this later.)

At Dalsin, engineers might assign permanent microtab locations to parts (especially if the tab location affects downstream operations), but after that, the laser operators actually perform the nest programming on the floor. Their proximity to the denesting station is a big reason for this.

"The sorting department relays the message to the laser operator, who’s on the floor," Kurt Dalsin said, "getting direct feedback from his internal customer downstream."

It’s a continual give and take. To keep parts secure during cutting—mitigating potential distortion, bowing, and sagging between the slats (which could cause collisions with the forks removing the sheet)—operators account for the worst-case scenario and microtab all four sides of the rectangle.

Still, how many tabs are

really

needed here? High-quality, laser-flat material might not bow after cutting, so one tab on each short edge might be overkill. What if the piece is always nested across the slats? In this case, the side tabs might be overkill too. Here, one small tab, placed in the center of a short edge, might suffice. That way, denesters easily lift the piece out with little trouble.

Of course, if a part is large enough, it might not need any tabbing at all. That’s the ideal, but where to draw the line? Which parts need at least one tab? Dalsin has a general rule that says any part smaller than 6 by 6 in. needs a tab. Why? The slats are 3 in. apart.

A cosmetically critical part is formed at the brake in a V die with a hemming station. This piece has no microtabs. On some parts, however, a tab that isn’t exposed might not be deburred. If that’s the case, programmers make sure the tab doesn’t cause issues downstream, like rocking against a press brake backgauge finger.

"We need parts to sit on at least three slats in order to keep them from tipping during shuttling," Matt Dalsin said. "We rely heavily on lights-out, unattended cutting, and we often run unattended over the weekend. For us, smart microtabbing is critical to keep the automation running."

Part orientation plays a role here. Say that a part requires tabbing simply because of its orientation to the slats. "In these cases, operators might see that and rotate the piece 45 degrees, or some other amount," Kurt Dalsin said, "to ensure better support and eliminate the need for a tab."

Weight plays a role as well. High-pressure assist gas can jostle very thin material. Historically, this has especially been true for slugs. The laser cuts a hole, and back-pressure forces the slug to tip or flip and land on top of the sheet.

"If we’re cutting, say, a 12-in. square with a 3-in. circle in it, that internal slug would tip from the high-pressure nitrogen," Matt Dalsin said. "The height sensor would then detect that, and the machine would stop. We need to avoid that."

In these cases, Dalsin programmers have usually tabbed the slug in place. Yes, that spurs more deburring, but it’s better than dealing with a crashed laser head. More recently, however, they’ve relied on slug-destruct sequences, with the head cutting the slug into bits small enough to fall reliably between the slats. In the past, they avoided slug-destruct sequences because of the increased cut times—the theory being that extra deburring time was a small price to pay for more cutting capacity at the laser. Today, though, especially with the immense capacity made available with the high-powered fiber laser, slug-destruct sequences make much more sense.

About the Slats

All that cutting, of course, still produces slag that can stick to the slats, and when built up enough, it changes the sheet’s position (in Z) slightly—enough to affect how pieces cut and behave in the sheet, whether they’re tabbed or not. Dalsin operators ensure slats remain clean and worn slats are replaced as needed. "When your slats start wearing down, you have undulating slat heights, and your automation [including the forks removing the cut sheet] is set for a certain sheet position," Matt Dalsin said.

If the sheet isn’t exactly where it’s supposed to be, the microtabs holding a piece might not prevent tipping as originally planned. Or, a large piece that isn’t tabbed might be in an unexpected orientation. All of this might be enough to bring the automation to a screeching halt.

Being a high-product-mix custom fabricator, programmers nest statically only for long runs, and many of those go to the punches with automated part removal and stacking. For laser cutting, programmers usually rely on dynamic nesting, and every sheet can be a different nest layout with different parts. Considering this, it’s impossible to tweak every nest to ensure cut lines don’t run right on top of slats, so from a nest layout perspective, minimizing slag buildup is difficult.

That said, Dalsin’s Mitsubishi laser does have slats with about a ½ in. of "wave" about every foot. This ensures a cut line that runs parallel and directly over a slat won’t spray molten metal over every slat tooth. Also, after a cut sheet is removed, automated brushes enter the work envelope to sweep away any small slugs or loose material that might be lodged at the bottom of the V in the slats. Even small pieces can force a sheet to sit differently on the bed and throw cutting automation off kilter.

The idea is to keep that sheet at exactly the right position so the laser cuts cleanly and the microtabs do what they’re designed to do—keep pieces steady as they’re transported to the offload table. Looking at all the potential causes of part instability is key. Again, adding a microtab to an unstable part can make life more difficult in denesting. In some cases, though, a part might not need another tab, just cleaner slats.

Tab Designs

In traditional punching with part shake-out, Dalsin’s programmers would often punch up to 0.015 in. from the corner (depending on material grade and thickness) to leave a tab. Alternatively, they would use a trapezoid punch to create a triangular microtab, with the wide side of the tab at the skeleton (at about 0.020 in. wide, depending on the sheet thickness) and the thin side at the part, securing the part while allowing it to break away easily.

In laser cutting, programmers used to rely on straight tabs, providing a narrow "bridge" between the skeleton and sheet. More recently, they’ve instituted programming features that cut triangle-shaped tabs, to ease part removal.

The tab geometry will vary depending on the grade and thickness of the sheet, the part geometry, and the assist gas being used. Compared with nitrogen and ultradry air cutting, oxygen cutting creates a wider kerf and, hence, demands a slightly thicker tab to bridge the gap. A thicker tab often requires new tabbing strategies when it comes to their shape, number, and location.

"We’re now trending more toward all nitrogen and air cutting, which allows us to standardize our microtabbing practices," Kurt Dalsin said.

Two lead-ins are adjacent to two microtabs on a laser-cut nest. Emerging microtab techniques at the laser are emulating the tapered tabs created by a trapezoid punch. The tab shapes provide enough stability for laser cutting automation yet can make part removal much easier.

Downstream Implications

Programmers consider the needs of downstream operations. Consider forming at the press brake for a part in which the edges won’t be exposed. So, the job routing skips deburring and goes right from blanking to bending. But what are the gauging requirements? Where and how will the backgauge fingers contact the edges, and will a side gauge be required? If so, a part with a microtab remnant might rock on a gauging surface and throw off the entire bend program.

Sometimes, Dalsin programmers simply move the tab to fix the problem. Say a tab on the edge rocks against a side gauge the operator needs to form the piece. Instead of deburring that single tab, why not move it so that it’s on the bend line? The bend itself is never a gauging surface, and the elongation that happens at the bend radius can actually draw that microtab into the edge itself.

Part edge requirements factor in as well. "If you have a critical feature in which the customer demands the piece fits exactly into an assembly, it really can’t have a microtab," Tennessen said. "That’s because if you try to deburr it, you find there’s a very fine line between sanding a tab flush versus sanding it into the parent part."

In these cases, specific edges that can’t have tabs govern how the flat part is nested. The part could be nested in such a way (across the slats, etc.) so it doesn’t require tabbing, or it could be punched and removed with the shop’s part removal and stacking automation.

Sometimes, tabbing strategies require some counterintuitive thinking. Consider a large panel with an internal window—so large that other pieces could be nested inside of it, a no-brainer for a programmer looking to maximize material yield in laser cutting.

But what about the forming process? Dalsin has plenty of press brakes, but it also has a Salvagnini panel bender. Forming such a large, flimsy piece on a brake can pose some challenges. Two operators need to support it, and they can have trouble holding it steadily and repeatedly against the backgauge. Using the shop’s Salvagnini panel bender can make much more sense—but the part manipulator that holds the blank between bends needs something to grasp.

At Dalsin, processing the piece on the punch press and keeping that large internal window tabbed in place proved to be the best strategy. It then is sent to the panel bender, with the part manipulator grasping the piece between bends. Yes, that internal cutout needs to be removed and the edges deburred after forming, but that excess deburring time ended up being a small price to pay for the efficiency and repeatability of panel bending. In fact, the shop also produces similar parts that are shipped with that internal cutout tabbed in place, just because it makes the piece easier to ship and handle at the customer’s factory. When the cutout is finally removed, the triangular microtabs (narrow at the part side) make break-out easy.

These pieces represent the few remaining blanks cut on Dalsin’s automated TRUMPF punch presses that emerge with microtabs—proof that there really are no hard-and-fast rules in blanking, especially at a custom fabricator running thousands of different jobs. Employees must weigh an incredible number of details—tab position, slat conditions on the laser, material distortion after cutting and punching, downstream forming and welding. They then dial in the process. The more dialed in the operation becomes, the more competitive it can be.
