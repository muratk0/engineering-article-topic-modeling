# New innovations in laser cutting include fibre lasers for thin sheet cutting, new punch/laser combinations and digital photonic production

[TARİH: 01.08.2013 ISMR]

Laser cutting has become an industrially accepted production process mainly because of the flexibility of the technique and the high quality cuts which can be made. Solid state lasers can swiftly and efficiently cut thin sheet metal and mild steel, while they can also process non-ferrous metals.

In contrast, CO2 lasers are suitable as a robust all-purpose tool for stainless steel, aluminium and mild steel in all sheet thicknesses. If stainless steel and thick sheet metal are the priority, CO2 lasers are more suitable. When processing electrical steel sheet, for example, CO2 lasers are better suited than solid state lasers to cutting thin sheets of these specially-coated soft magnetic materials.

8kW CO2 laser cutting solutions are now offered as cost effective alternatives to plasma or waterjet cutting – they are particularly suited to the production of laser cut blanks. It is much more cost-effective than milling the part from bar material, a process that is very wasteful by comparison. The ability to use thick sheet instead also introduces the potential for nesting; saving material, machining time and therefore cost.

Laser systems have also been used to cut from coil. The concept is not new – specialist manufacturers such as IRON SpA in Italy have employed this concept for years and Schuler recently introduced a new laser cutting line that combines coil feeding technology with innovative laser cutting technology for continuous cutting from coil. The ability to individually program several laser cutting heads working in tandem ensures maximum flexibility and offers new perspectives for the tailored cutting of blanks. This will play an increasingly important role in press hardening.

Exciting innovations are also now being made in pulse laser technology and new ‘digital photonic production’ technologies for next generation products.

It is not just the main laser cutting machine tool manufacturers that have introduced fiber laser models into the market – new market entrants have been smaller players from around the world with their own brand of fiber lasers. The market has consequently seen an explosion of fiber lasers launched by a huge number of market entrants.

For a TRUMPF customer, John Feijen of Maarheeze (the Netherlands), productivity was the deciding factor for his purchase of a fiber laser. Since the end of 2010, he has been using the TruLaser 5030 fiber primarily to cut thin stainless steel and mild steel sheets that make up about 50 per cent of his portfolio. He cuts thicker sheets on two TruLaser 5030 classic machines with CO2 lasers. This is the ideal combination for Feijen.

“I use the new machine for metal sheets of up to four millimetres thick. For everything else, I use my CO2 machines,” he said. He was particularly excited about the speed of the machine that came with the TruDisk 3001 fiber-directed disk laser. “Particularly when cutting with nitrogen, the TruLaser 5030 fiber is considerably faster than a comparable CO2 machine. The quality of the cut edges is also good,” he reported. Now he can also submit new bids and has already received enquiries for cutting non-ferrous metals.

## An eye on fiber

A recent innovation in laser cutting systems for machining thin sheet metal has been the introduction of the fiber laser which can cut not just steel materials but also non-ferrous materials such as copper, brass and titanium. Linear drives enable rapid positioning and cutting speeds to be achieved. The use of fibers eliminates the need for elaborate and costly optics for beam guidance and the space requirement can be downsized.

“Companies with capacity for more machines should invest in higher productivity special-purpose units. With almost double the axis speeds, the TruLaser 5030 fiber increases productivity for thin sheet metal by around 15 per cent at the same laser power,” explained TRUMPF. “The machine is also available with a higher-power 5-kilowatt laser. This allows it to achieve lower cost per part at high utilisation rates, making it best suited to users who cut a lot of thin sheet metal and non-ferrous metal.”

Fiber lasers can comfortably be used for marking, efficient processing of gauge and fractional materials and fine feature cutting.

“They have several advantages over CO2 laser systems as they require virtually no maintenance, are more energy-efficient and take up less space. The power supply is much smaller than that of a CO2 laser, yet fiber delivery enables the beam to travel greater distances allowing for installation on larger tables,” explained fiber laser machine manufacturer, Hypertherm.

Some manufacturers, such as Prima Power, have also introduced punch-fiber laser combination machines. Prima’s Combi range of integrated manufacturing cells is a new generation of punching-fiber laser cutting. Its series LPe6f with high-end punching technology and a 2 or 3kW fiber laser source can run complex production orders with versatility and speed. Sheet positioning speed is 150m/min. In versatile servo-electric punching, performance values such as punching speed up to 1,000hpm/1mm, punching force of up to 30 tons and simultaneous tooling capacity up to 384 tools with Multi-Tool® technology can be reached. Automation can be arranged flexibly with a wide range of modules.

Amada launched no less than 12 products globally at the EuroBLECH exhibition in October last year. Amongst these were innovative laser products such as the FLC 3015 AJ linear drive fibre laser cutting system and the latest addition to Amada’s punch/laser combination machines range, the ACIES 2515T.

BLM Group’s LT9 is designed for steel sheet cutting (mild steel up to 20mm thick, copper and brass up to 5mm thick with high speed cutting of aluminium and stainless steel) using linear motors and can be equipped with either CO2 or new fiber sources. Its modular structure allows it to be configured in the most convenient and profitable manner for a manufacturer. It can also process large format steel sheets.

## Finger on the pulse

At the LASER World of PHOTONICS in Munich recently, TRUMPF unveiled its first femtosecond laser – the TruMicro 5050 Femto Edition. Like its picosecond lasers, the ultrashort pulse laser in the femtosecond range is suitable for industrial use and operates around the clock without any interruptions or variations in the processing results. First orders for this model have already been placed.

TRUMPF, the world’s largest manufacturer of lasers for industrial use, recently expanded its primary solid-state laser development facility by constructing a new building in the town of Schramberg-Sulgen (Germany). Investment for this new project came to 13.5 million euros while construction took 17 months.

The TruMicro 5050 Femto Edition is based on the laser concept used in its TruMicro series 5000 which employs solid picosecond lasers. Here again, the stability of both the pulses and the power level is achieved by separating pulse generation and pulse output. Controls monitor each individual pulse and keep the power and pulse energy at exactly the required levels, regardless of external influences. This is done using a patented double feedback loop, guaranteeing that the exactly specified power level comes to bear on the work piece. The beam quality and pulse duration remain constant.

The short pulse duration is advantageous, especially when processing temperature-sensitive substances such as composites and polymers.

The use of fibers eliminates the need for elaborate and costly optics for beam guidance and the space requirement can be downsized.

Ultra-short pulse laser systems are achieving new records almost daily regarding laser power and pulse rates.

“We are hoping that the femtosecond laser will cause an expansion of applications in cold processing. In the past we could work especially sensitive materials only with considerable quality sacrifices, or not at all. Now we have built the ideal tool for this purpose. The shorter pulse duration means that the material absorbs less thermal energy. Thus the power, in the high mid-range, can be better utilised on the piece being processed,” explained TRUMPF.

Initial trials with nitinol – a shape-memory alloy used, for example, in medical technology to manufacture stents – have resulted in superior cutting without any need for reworking. This is due in large part to the superior beam quality (M² < 1.3).

“Ultra-short pulse laser systems are achieving new records almost daily regarding laser power and pulse rates. And yet in the power range of 50 to 1,000 watts, only a small part of the energy they have can be used effectively for many applications. If the laser power is too high when it is coupled into the work piece at the processing point, bulges from the melting effects occur due to the high thermal load, leading to poor processing results,” explained experts from Fraunhofer ILT.

Researchers at Fraunhofer ILT are looking at making the high power of USP lasers useful for material processing: for example, by dividing the laser beam into several individual beams which then can process the component simultaneously. This multi-beam technique is used, amongst others, to generate periodic microstructures.

At the LASER World of Photonics, Fraunhofer ILT presented five examples of successful cooperation with laser partners. This included simulation of the laser processes for cutting display glass with ultra-short pulse lasers for TRUMPF Laser Technology, water-guided laser.

## Digital photonic production

Lasers are not just used for cutting – their functionality includes welding, brazing, etching, melting, deposition etc. A new and exciting initiative for laser uses in manufacturing highlights this. The “Digital Photonic Production” research campus in Aachen offers local industry and science a skilled and responsive instrument with which to shape the future of production technology. Support comes from the German Federal Ministry of Education and Research BMBF, which has provided financial backing of up to 30 million euros through its “Research Campus – Public-Private Partnerships for Innovation” funding initiative.

The Chair of Laser Technology LLT at RWTH Aachen University emerged from the national competition as one of ten winners, having coordinated a proposals consortium made up of industry partners and further RWTH institutes. A new German national centre for digital photonic production is now being set up in Aachen, in collaboration with the Fraunhofer Institute for Laser Technology ILT, one of the leading facilities for laser technology in Europe, and the initiatives and networks already existing in this field. This intensive cooperation between research and industry also means the chances are good for new spin-off companies.

The aim of the BMBF’s nationwide German funding initiative “Research Campus – Public-Private Partnerships for Innovation” is to offer long-term support, at an early stage, to encourage universities, research institutes and businesses to work together in ten economically and socially relevant technology fields. Over the course of the next 15 years, the research cluster will have up to an additional 2 million euros at its disposal each year, on top of its own contributions, with which to systematically drive this pioneering issue forward.

“From bits to photons to atoms” – this phrase sums up the technological potential of digital photonic production. On the basis of numerical data, light can be harnessed as a tool for manufacturing customised components of almost any complexity cost-effectively in small batches. Photon-based production methods for rapid manufacturing have been a research and development topic in Aachen for many years now. One of the research team’s major achievements was the development of the first process for the fabrication of metallic dental prostheses using selective laser melting which subsequently led to the creation of new business models in dental technology. Professor Poprawe and his team received the NRW Innovation Award 2011 for developing the SLM additive manufacturing process, now widely deployed in many sectors of industry.

Automotive and aerospace companies are currently testing the potential of such production methods to optimize component functionality and the consumption of resources. The next step involves linking the laser-based production processes to the upstream design and construction plans and the downstream processes and logistic issues. The aims of the industrial partners include channelling tailormade components in small quantities into construction plants designed for mass production, involving customers in the design of individual components and systematically reducing existing spare parts stores.

Digital photonic production is not limited to additive manufacturing processes. Further production strategies being systematically pursued within the scope of the BMBF funding initiative include the generation of nanometrethin structures via abrasion using ultra-shortpulse lasers, laser polishing of metals, glass and plastic, and the generation of threedimensional microfluidic systems using selective laser etching.

The 15-year funding period for the research cluster is also intended to be used to establish the fundamental requirements that will make digital photonic production marketable in many sectors of manufacturing industry. The actual challenge is linking planning, construction, design, material selection, production and logistics in such a way that flexible series production using additive, abrasive and functionalising laser processes is economically viable, irrespective of batch size. Digital photonic production opens up new business models and services for the manufacturing industry, such as co-creation and mass customization. Important future fields of application include mobility, energy, health and information and communication technology.
