n response to metal fabricators’ needs for ever more efficient production, laser cutting machinery continues to evolve and to push the limits on cutting speeds. New challenges have arisen as a result in terms of simply keeping machinery fed and being able to process parts throughout the whole operation quickly enough—without errors.

Some of those challenges related to laser cutting automation include tip-ups, unstable tabbing and part positioning, crashes, and misaligned commonline cutting.

1. How is laser cutting automation getting "smarter" to help meet these challenges?

Automation has become much smarter in terms of feature avoidance, or the software’s ability to recognize features such as small parts or cutouts over which the laser cutting head shouldn’t travel once they have been cut.

Gains have also been made in cutting-path quality with dynamic lead-in placement. This entails adding lead-ins and lead-outs to CNC programs as parts are being nested. Dynamic lead-in placement produces efficient cutting paths and reduces the potential for collisions with tipped components.

In that same vein, advanced technology designed to partially raise cutting heads at times when they are most likely to collide with wayward or unstable components has further eliminated the likelihood of collisions.

Scrap utilization, processing, and handling can be vital to efficiency and safety, and so it can only be good news for fabricators that both CNC software and machinery have come a long way in terms of the ability to process scrap. Machinery is now fast enough to destruct scrap to avoid collisions before it becomes a real threat to productivity and safety.

2. What are some current and critical developments?

Using scheduling information to create nest projects—which entails integrating management resource planning (MRP) or manufacturing execution system (MES) software into existing processes—is an ideal way to take advantage of automation while avoiding problems related to tabbing, common-line cutting, and tip-ups. With a robust system, fabricators can use and customize automation while generating safe, efficient programs that avoid collisions and maximize machine capabilities.

3. What is new with nesting?

Nothing beats nesting for efficiency gains, so the quest for the best possible nesting algorithm and utilization continues.

In the arena of batch, or continuous, nesting, more jobs can be nested more quickly because fabricators can look at entire schedules of parts and nest entire jobs continuously.

Traditionally, nesting software can generate the first nest, the second nest, and so on, but then go back as conditions change and automatically modify earlier nests if better utilization can be achieved for the overall series of nests. Batch nesting is a great tool for using automation to constantly readjust for better optimization.

In addition, parts can be added to nesting projects for hot jobs, so jobs can be nested just ahead of what the machine is cutting to accommodate last-minute orders. Fabricators often express a significant need for flexibility. They get the best of both worlds with the ability to dynamically nest parts on demand while retaining the control required to create and save kits of parts as needed.

That need for flexibility extends to needing control over how parts are cut. Rigid kits or mini-nests allow for common-line cutting and more control over the cutting process. The kits can then be added into the next project for the best efficiency.

4. How are stacking and retrieving advanced with automation?

When it comes to handling, stacking, and retrieving finished parts, there is greater support for the automation of part picking and the nesting of finished parts in unload areas. QR codes etched into parts for identification assist with downstream operations, and the automatic application of labels with information from MRP systems is ideal for downstream sorting and routing.

5. How is technology helping sync laser-cut parts with automation?

Connectivity begins with approaches that link the virtual with the real seamlessly, beginning with the MRP or MES software that drives processes from start to finish. Better connectivity allows fabricators to eliminate errors that begin at the data-entry stage and plague projects downstream. This is the best way to speed up processes without sacrificing quality.
