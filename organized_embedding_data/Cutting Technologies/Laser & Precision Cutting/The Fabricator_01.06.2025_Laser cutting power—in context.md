How much wattage do you really need?

hat was once a simple checkers match for more laser cutting power has evolved into a strategic game of chess. What should a metal fabricator’s next laser cutting investment be? That depends on a host of factors that go far beyond the traditional inches-per-minute (IPM) cutting metric.

For a dive deep into the subject, The Fabricator spoke with Matt Milazzo, laser product manager at AMADA AMERICA, about putting laser power in the right context. Yes, the industry today boasts some extraordinary fiber laser cutting power (see

Figure 1

), but what does an operation truly need? That depends on a fabricator’s business model and production needs. No two metal fabricators are identical, but these days, all share a common trait: They’re no longer running a simple horse race for more kilowatts of cutting power.

Fewer Isn’t Always Better

Years ago, large jets had four engines; today, they can get by with just two, mainly because of their efficiency, power, and high reliability. Would the same analogy apply to lasers?

As Milazzo explained, not always. Years ago, many fabricators—especially those processing gauge material—did end up replacing, say, two or three old CO

2

lasers with just one modern fiber laser. The differences in cutting speed, maintenance requirements, the ability to produce more with less shop floor space, and the lower costs of operation were just too great to ignore.

Today is different. "Shops that already have embraced fiber lasers usually aren’t replacing lower-powered machines," Milazzo said. "They’re adding capacity. They automate to get more capacity out of what they already have, or they add another laser cell."

In gauge material especially, cutting speed isn’t as important as it once was, mainly because the laser beam itself is no longer a speed constraint. Milazzo described a nest of parts with intricate contours in 18-ga. carbon steel, placed on a 12-kW fiber laser. "The limitation now is in the motion system," he said. "The 12-kW system could be set to 2,000 in. per minute, but it’s never going to get there."

Beyond Inches Per Minute

The IPM metric can become significant as material gets thicker. Today’s ultrahigh-powered systems can cut through 0.5-in.-thick plate at some eye-popping speeds. That said, IPM is still just one factor to consider. Another is nest density and the number of pierces in each program. The pure IPM difference between a lower- and higher-powered fiber laser might not be substantial, but the piercing efficiencies might be dramatic.

"That 3-kW fiber laser will need a few seconds to get through 0.25-in. material, while a 9- or 12-kW system will basically give you an instantaneous pierce," Milazzo said. "If you have 600 or 700 pierces in a nest, we can take a substantial amount of time out of the program just through those rapid piercing commands."

New laser cutting technologies show just how critical the pierce has become. Spatter-free, or oil-shot, piercing helps control debris, preventing it from landing on a nozzle tip and affecting cut quality. Auto-columniation techniques in higher-powered fiber lasers change the focus as the system transitions from piercing to cutting. In typical setups, the focus remains high in the material thickness for piercing, then shifts lower in the kerf for cutting, where it can work in concert with the assist gas (usually nitrogen or a gas mix) to evacuate molten material as effectively as possible.

Material quality and utilization also step into the picture, especially when oxygen cutting 0.75 in. and thicker. Here, Milazzo described a water-assist cutting technology that can help mitigate distortion effects and allow for denser nest layouts with thinner webs in the skeleton (see

Figure 2

).

"The idea is to keep the surface of the material cool during cutting," Milazzo said. "This minimizes warpage, and it helps maximize material utilization in plate processing. You can nest thick parts closer together."

The warpage mitigation opens the door for more efficient toolpaths. To mitigate the effects of heat, operators traditionally would cut a plate in different quadrants, which meant the cutting head spent a lot of time traversing between cuts. Now, water-assist cutting can allow the machine to cut in a conventional toolpath.

High Wattages, Big Cutting Tables

A shop that’s cutting thick is often cutting large workpieces, which is why high-powered fiber lasers can often be found on large-format cutting tables. But as Milazzo explained, large tables have several benefits that apply to parts of any size, small or large.

Picture a high-powered fiber laser cutting on a conventional bed size. In some cases, beam-on time can be extremely low. Some high-powered lasers have gotten so fast, even automation can’t load and unload material quickly enough. Now picture a high-powered laser cutting a nest on a large sheet placed on a large bed. The cutting is still extremely fast, but the cycle time is longer, allowing more time to feed and remove material, manually or through automation. The result: Beam-on time rises.

Milazzo added that such a setup can work especially well in operations that nest both make-to-order pieces as well as repeatedly ordered filler parts (to fill out the nest) that can be made to stock. Of course, all this needs to be put in context of the overall operation, such as part flow and existing technology on the floor—including automation.

Automation Strategies

Years ago, a fabricator might have chosen a centralized approach to laser automation, with one tower or automated storage and retrieval system feeding several lasers. Now, Milazzo sees a trend toward localized automation, with towers and part removal and sorting stations dedicated to a single laser cutting machine.

He clarified that a centralized automation approach still has its place. Determining which approach works depends on the kind of blanks, material nests, the sheet thicknesses the machines are processing, and—not least—the overall needs of part flow in the plant. But looking at the laser process in isolation, several technology trends are "pushing the pendulum" toward localized automation.

First is the sheer speed of the modern fiber laser. "Can a single automation system keep up with three or four fiber lasers these days? In many instances, the answer is no," Milazzo said.

This leads to a fabricator investing in a number of individual automated cells, with each tower and parts picker dedicated to a specific laser.

Second is the wide range of thicknesses modern fiber lasers are cutting, especially with the ultrahigh-powered fiber laser. Overall, automation is much more common within sheet metal operations. Traditional material handling technology in laser cutting can accommodate material thicknesses of 1 in. and below (see

Figure 3

).

As Milazzo put it, "Now the question becomes, what automation technologies can work well as the laser cutting wattages grow?" As thicknesses grow, the approach to material handling automation changes. Sheet metal laser cutting operations use grippers to pick and transport sheets from tower cartridges, then sort parts after cutting. When operations start cutting 1- and 1.5-in. plate that could be 8,000 lbs. or even heavier, the cartridge approach just isn’t practical.

Instead of relying on a cartridge tower and suction-cup grippers, material handling automation for heavy plate can entail a tower of individual slat tables, or pallets. The pallet moves from the tower directly into the laser cutting work envelope, no material transfer required.

Such an approach also extends the amount of time between slat cleanings—a big benefit, considering the amount of molten material that can accumulate in heavy-duty laser cutting. Compared to a traditional shuttle setup with two slat tables, each slat table in a tower spends less time in the cutting work envelope. A multitude of slat tables also means slat cleaning and maintenance can happen while the laser continues to cut.

Milazzo added that it’s important to put all these automation technologies in context. When it comes to material handling, most plasma, waterjet, and oxyfuel cutting taking place in North America isn’t highly automated. When operations make the jump to laser cutting, they see dramatic gains in the elimination of secondary operations like grinding (see

Figure 4

). Whether automation makes sense depends on the available budget, production goals, and the overall competitive landscape.

Consider the Business Model

When you look across the metal fabrication market, three types of operations have become ultrahigh-powered laser’s early adopters. The first group comprises high-volume, thick-material-cutting operations within large OEMs serving the agriculture, military, mining, trucking, and heavy machinery sectors. "They play in the world of 0.5 in. and above all day," Milazzo said. "The parts are big, thick, and they need to get through them quickly. They really need the laser cutting power."

Second are plasma and waterjet operations that now need to adopt laser cutting simply to remain competitive. "These operations might now be geographically surrounded by fiber lasers in their local market," Milazzo said. "Here, it’s often about quality. The OEMs they serve are demanding the higher-quality results that a fiber laser can provide. To be sure, when processing extreme thicknesses, plasma has its advantages.

But with the advent of 26 kW and beyond, the fiber laser now has become a viable alternative to plasma and waterjet, resulting in better edge quality and tight tolerances."

The third group to adopt ultrahigh-powered lasers includes job shops and contract fabricators aiming to offer services that competitors simply can’t. For job shops, an ultrahigh-powered fiber laser is another tool in their Swiss army knife of cutting capabilities. "These are the early adopters," Milazzo said, adding that their ability to push the technological envelope is a key selling point. Embracing the bleeding edge helps their businesses grow.

Edge Quality, Downstream Requirements, and Wattage

Imagine an operation that invests in a high-power laser to nitrogen-cut medium-thick plate. The machine produces parts with pristine edges that can flow right to the next major manufacturing step.

That sounds ideal, until Milazzo asked a poignant question: "Do you really need those pristine edges? What are your quality requirements? What parts coming off the laser really need to look like often determines whether or not wattage plays a role. Are parts painted or powder coated? What kind of postprocessing is required? Also, do you care if you need to do secondary processing?" He clarified that in most cases, eliminating a secondary operation does make a world of difference. Still, the answer can vary depending on the context, including the production methodology and where the constraint processes are in the value stream.

Milazzo described an outdoor trash bin manufacturer that laser-cut 3/16-in. carbon steel. Tolerances weren’t tight, because downstream processes didn’t require a clean, precise edge. The company simply needed a basic, consistent blanking process. "They wanted to get the part off the machine as inexpensively as possible, and they didn’t care what the edge looked like."

The company theoretically could have purchased a high-powered laser to nitrogen-cut blanks as fast as possible, but that wasn’t the lowest-cost option. Oxygen cutting with a lower-wattage fiber laser with some basic load/unload automation proved to be the best approach. In this case, money spent on automation was far more valuable than money spent on laser power and nitrogen assist gas.

Automation, Laser Power, and Part Flow

In fact, even in cases requiring higher levels of edge quality, a relatively lower-powered fiber laser paired with automation (see

Figure 5

) can lead to greater throughput than a higher-powered fiber laser without automation.

"A lower-powered laser could give an operation a productive first shift and an unattended second shift," Milazzo said. "This helps you increase throughput without focusing strictly on wattage." He added that this approach might work well for a contract fabricator that mostly cuts sheet metal and medium-thickness plate, while processing the occasional job that is 1 in. thick or more. The automated processing of thin and medium thicknesses more than makes up for the occasional thick-plate job with lengthy pierce and cutting times.

Move to agricultural equipment manufacturers with high throughput and edge quality requirements, and the story changes completely. They likely will nitrogen-cut every part, not just for the speed but also to be able to skip downstream secondary edge-prep processes before welding and powder coating. Here, Milazzo said, higher-powered lasers will probably be the best fit. "And frankly, in these instances, automation becomes essential. Here’s where automated parts sorting comes into play. And we have those cutting pallet systems where you can access parts immediately."

Years ago, schedulers relied on large batches of parts that fed significant work-in-process buffers, all to ensure downstream operations were never starved of the blanks they needed. Now, with blanking systems more productive and reliable than ever, "many OEMs are relying on kitbased flow," Milazzo said.

Here, quick response is paramount. Lasers produce on demand, and parts flow downstream immediately. Regarding specific part offloading strategies, the nature of the kit matters. If kits comprise of parts cut from the same material type and thickness, parts can be offloaded to a common pallet, which then can be moved directly downstream.

If the kits comprise of, say, five different material types, then offloading and sorting from a tower might make the most sense. Different cut nests could be placed in the same or different cartridges, from which they can be sorted to common kit pallets. "This allows you to group unlike material onto the same pallet," Milazzo said.

Wattage in Context

Milazzo recalled representatives from one OEM who initially thought a 15-kW fiber laser was the way to go. Then they thought a 12-kW might suffice. Upon further analysis, they chose a 9-kW system. "They saw that, looking at their part mix, a higher-power laser would get through material so quickly, it would back up their part sorting." Again, he clarified that the power consideration would have changed if the OEM processed a different range of material thicknesses and had different processing needs downstream. Ultimately, the optimal laser cutting power depends on the needs of the operation. After all, customers don’t pay for fast cutting speeds; they pay for quality products delivered on time.
