# Laser cutting changes shop floor dynamics for EXP Fabrication

[TARİH: 01.05.2026 The Fabricator]

Laser Cutting

Speed and accuracy elevate shop’s fabricating capabilities

By

Dan Davis

Parts cut on EXP Fabrication’s laser cutting machine fit snugly together. No postprocessing is needed.

Images: EXP Fabrication

V

isit EXP Fabrication and you’ll notice an automated welding setup that suggests these folks aren’t totally reliant on outsiders for technological support.

In the 25,000-sq.-ft. shop in Justin, Texas, a welding robot sits on a 60-ft. track, with eight uniquely configured weld stations set up in a linear fashion. All of the stations are designed to accommodate a variety of products depending on their geometry. Also, some stations accommodate specific products because those parts best fit that station’s capabilities.

For example, one station that is more than 10 ft. long with air locks that hold the weldment down helps minimize heat distortion on long weldments. Another station rotates for very wide assemblies that exceed the robot’s reach; after the robot welds half the assembly, the table spins so it can weld the other half.

EXP Fabrication also has two other welding cells that are more automated. One cell has a robot that can weld parts as they are rotated. Both cells have rotating tables to improve throughput.

"Whenever you start to get into 1,000- or 2,000-part orders, depending on how complicated the assemblies are, of course, that’s where we really fit in. It’s where we can help our customers get their lead times and costs down," said Ethan Blankenship, a project manager at EXP Fabrication. "We also can help our customers with repeatability because we have the robots do a lot of that work."

That experience with welding robots is no surprise. The company’s founders, John Hart and Les Porter, originally started the business to serve as a robotics integrator for other manufacturers. After years of traveling to keep those robot installations running and getting calls at all hours of the day, the duo decided to apply their robotics knowledge for their own metal fabricating operation.

For a couple of guys with "get-it-done-anyway-you-can" backgrounds, as Blankenship described them, the transition from automation consultants to a job shop with in-house robotics knowledge was pretty seamless. They could figure out ways for robots to tackle difficult-to-automate welding jobs that others simply couldn’t replicate.

The challenge, however, was that robots like accurate parts and fixtures. Blankenship said that because the plasma table routinely produced inconsistent fixtures, the design team had to intentionally oversize the jigs, which then made repeatability a problem. The CNC mills and lathes could have been used to produce more accurate fixtures, but they usually were busy producing parts.

That improved over the years as new equipment was purchased. But jobs with really tight tolerances still had to be farmed out, which added to lead times and increased costs for customers.

EXP Fabrication eventually saw the light on how to obtain higher tolerances on the blanking side of the business. It happened to be the light of a laser cutting machine.

FOCUS ON PROCESS, NOT CUTTING TIME

"We had been running a high-definition plasma table for about 10 years, because that was affordable and could cut what we needed all day long," Blankenship said. "It wasn’t until about three years ago that we seriously started talking about laser cutting."

At first, the team thought it was too expensive, especially compared to new plasma cutting systems. But then it considered the potential cost savings that would come with laser cutting.

Blankenship said that the company took on jobs that involved laser cutting, but all of that work had to be outsourced. Obviously, bringing the work in-house provided a good financial foundation for an investment in new blanking technology, but it wasn’t the sole motivator.

When it came down to it, the elimination of secondary processes really helped justify the laser cutting machine purchase. Parts would get out the door faster, and EXP Fabrication would get paid sooner.

Consider how the shop floor had to handle postcutting duties for one plasma job. Because this job required that the clean side of the panel be facing up when it entered the press brake, each large panel, ranging in size from 80 to 110 lbs., had to be removed from the table after plasma cutting, flipped to the dirty side to clean the dross (about four to eight hours of work), and then flipped back so that it would be in the correct orientation for the press brake. The side that dross accumulated on, even when cleaned, was not as appealing as the clean side of the panel, according to Blankenship.

The laser delivers much more precise cuts than the plasma cutting system could, allowing for more precise fit-up in downstream processes.

"Our guys hated turning dozens of heavy panels over just to turn them right back over to start breaking out the parts," Blankenship said. With the focus on reducing production costs and tightening up lead times, EXP Fabrication was ready to improve its blanking capabilities.

That coincided with Hart Machine Tool, a distributor of Cutlite laser cutting machines, reaching out to check on the company’s fabricating operations.

THE RIGHT FIT FOR THE SHOP FLOOR

Blankenship said the conversations with the Hart Machine Tool representatives focused on the realities of EXP Fabrication’s operations. For a company representing Cutlite, a brand known for demonstrating a 50-kW machine at FABTECH 2025, that might surprise some.

"We wanted to at least meet what we were capable of cutting on our plasma unit, and we could comfortably cut 1 in. with that," Blankenship said. "There also were times that we could cut on 1.5 in., but towards the end of its life, the plasma system didn’t do the best job with that thickness."

A 30-kW machine sounds exciting, whipping through 1-in. material like it was thin-gauge sheet, but that extra power added to the machine’s price tag. EXP Fabrication didn’t need that much power since it was only cutting really thick stuff, like 2-in. material, a couple of times per year.

The fab shop eventually purchased a 12-kW Cutlite with a 2,000-mm (roughly 6.5-ft.) by 4,000-mm (just over 13-ft.) cutting table, perfect for processing 6- by 12-ft. sheets. Installed in mid-2025, it’s proven to be the right fit for the shop.

"Footprint was a big thing that we were looking at, because our shop is not ginormous," Blankenship said. "We couldn’t afford to give up too much space."

A 60-ft. table with a moving gantry for the laser cutting activity wasn’t going to work. That’s why the shop decided to add a shuttle table with the laser cutting machine; it would unload parts from the skeleton that sat on one shuttle table while parts were being cut on the other shuttle table.

LEARNING LASER CUTTING

With the machine in place, Blankenship and the team had to learn how to get the machine to run and produce quality parts. They were trained at the Cutlite America facility, but that covered the basics, such as the introduction to the controls and maintenance tips. EXP Fabrication was embarking on a new journey, and learning on the job was part of the curriculum.

"We still have a plasma table, a little bit smaller than the one the laser replaced, and I could teach somebody how to use that in probably 20 minutes. After that, they’ll be off to the races cutting parts," Blankenship said. "It might take somebody a week and a half to get a new operator on a laser to be somewhat proficient with it."

Blankenship added that as operators learn more about the science of laser cutting, they’ll become key contributors in helping to maintain quality and increasing throughput.

"Having a strong understanding of the physics behind kerf formation, focus position, gas behavior, and heat transfer is critical for diagnosing cut quality issues," he said. "Without that foundational understanding, an operator may recognize that a cut is poor but struggle to identify which variables need adjustment to correct the problem."

After several months with the machine, the team at EXP Fabrication is experiencing some very positive benefits:

•

Throughput has increased

– Laser cutting has not only eliminated the need to remove dross from cut parts; it’s also delivered a level of tolerance on cuts, like holes, that eliminates postmachining processes. Blankenship estimates that approximately 75% of the jobs that used to require some machining after plasma cutting now can be cut on the laser without the need for additional processing. Now, parts go straight from the cutting bed to forming, powder coating, welding, or even shipping, saving hours in overall lead time.

•

Laser-cut parts are good for fixturing

– When fixtures for robots were cut with plasma, the components had to be ground down to the right dimension and then machined so that they could hold the workpiece in an accurate position for the welder. The laser-cut components avoid that extra processing, often ready for fixturing after leaving the laser. Also, keys and keyholes can be cut accurately to assist with fixture setup. "It makes a huge difference to be able to send all of those parts to the welder and just tell him, ‘Hey, just slide this down in that little slot, let the key find the keyhole, and get the right fit.’ That’s been extremely helpful for us," Blankenship said.

•

Etching simplifies multiple-part jobs

– It might take the laser cutting machine a little extra time to etch numbers on parts, but the payoff comes in welding, especially with jobs that have a lot of similar-looking parts. The welder is no longer walking around wondering if he’s found the right part and asking the supervisor for confirmation. Laser-etched part numbers take the guesswork out of these large assembly tasks, keeping the focus on completing the job.

LASER SETS THE FOUNDATION

The arrival of the laser cutting machine at EXP Fabrication has already had an impact, and now the company thinks it might be capable of more work outside of the original 10-ga. to 1.25-in.-thick material.

"We now know we can cut 16, 18, or 20 ga. really accurately, and we can do electrical cabinets and things like that, which previously we wouldn’t have been able to do," Blankenship said.

That’s now put the focus on bending. EXP Fabrication’s two press brakes, 200 and 250 tons, are useful for the heavy fab work, but they’re not the best tools for high-precision bends in smaller fabrications made of thin-gauge metal.

When it came down to it, the elimination of secondary processes really helped justify the laser cutting machine purchase. Parts would get out the door faster, and EXP Fabrication would get paid sooner.

Laser cutting has increased throughput at EXP Fabrication. Parts don’t need to be ground to remove dross, which would normally happen after plasma cutting, and the laser-cut holes are precise enough that they don’t need to be sent to machining.

So the evolution continues for EXP Fabrication. Fortunately, the company already has the automation part of the journey nailed down.
