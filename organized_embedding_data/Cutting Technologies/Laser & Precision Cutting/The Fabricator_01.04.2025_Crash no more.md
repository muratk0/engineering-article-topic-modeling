Collision-avoidance strategies in laser cutting

M

odern fiber lasers cut extraordinarily quickly, but is their output stable and predictable? The process creates a lot of kinetic energy, which in turn creates the potential for pieces to tip or fly up and interfere with the fast-moving cutting head—one of the most sensitive parts of the entire machine. If a head crashes because a slug from a previously cut internal feature tipped or flipped on top of the sheet, the process isn’t stable.

A stable and predictable process needs a smart toolpath strategy and a nest that considers material yield; incorporates part orientation requirements (grain direction for cosmetics or bending); and makes judicious use of microtabs to satisfy clean edge requirements for downstream processes, ease denesting, and minimize the need for deburring.

The Need for Dynamic Nesting

Most metal fabrication in the U.S. occurs in high-product-mix environments. The most optimal nest for part flow can often include a wide variety of parts for numerous jobs. Most important: the job mix is always changing. A programmer can spend serious time on a static nest, optimizing material yield and cutting toolpaths—but that time isn’t free.

Considering the nature of customer demand, how often will that nest be run on the laser? Some jobs might run quite often, but what if the nest involves a collection of low-quantity orders that might never be seen again?

For this reason, many programmers today rely heavily on dynamic nests automatically generated by software. They identify constraints, like a specific part orientation so that the blank has the required grain direction. And they specify the maximum distance that a head can traverse without moving upward (in Z) and out of harm’s way.

They also review microtab use and placement. A long, skinny piece might need to be microtabbed in place, lest it tip up between the slats. But what if that same piece extended across several slats? This could eliminate the need for microtabs, ease denesting, and eliminate deburring.

These all represent basic features of nesting software. Software technology has come a long way in recent years, though. Veteran laser programmers probably recall nests where a small slug tipped up and brought a cutting operation to a screeching halt. (Such a small piece of metal has a big consequence.) Today, software can predict where this will occur and prevent those slugs from "going rogue" and wreaking havoc.

Smart Toolpaths, Smart Collision Avoidance

Nesting has numerous subtilties, and this includes collision avoidance. Slug pieces that most often tip up or fly uncontrollably are the ones most expect to fall neatly between the slats. A critical moment occurs just as the slugs are completely released from stock. If they wobble, tilt, or sail up rather than fall between the slats, the laser head or even the machine’s glass enclosure could be damaged.

A laser that simply cuts internal features sequentially could be operating the fastest way it can, assuming the slugs from those features fall cleanly away. Of course, when they become lodged, tip, or fly upward, they create an unstable scenario—a head crash waiting to happen (see

Figure 1

). Today, software makes use of automated collision avoidance algorithms that, when activated, analyze total sheet integrity, then suggest specific strategies to ensure laser cutting stability.

Imagine a part with several internal features, each irregular polygons comprising both sharp and rounded (radiused) edges. Software identifies areas along the internal feature profiles that could cause the slug to stick and not fall cleanly through the slats.

Once the software identifies those risks, it adjusts accordingly. Specifically, it instructs the laser head to cut an interior feature’s perimeter

almost entirely

, leaving an uncut section that keeps the slug secure (see

Figure 2

). Because it’s secure, the cutting head needn’t lift to make a rapid traverse between cuts. That said, the cutting head

never

traverses over previously cut sections. Instead, it finds the safest path over sections that haven’t been cut.

Later in the cut cycle, the head returns to those internal features to make the final cut, allowing the slug to drop cleanly through the slats. Here still, the cutting head doesn’t traverse over the hole it just cut, but instead finds the safest path to the next pierce.

This is where programmers can customize the approach. For instance, they can specify the order in which those last uncut sections are cut and where those uncut sections should be on the internal feature profile. As they customize this, the software identifies any uncut section that might need to be lengthened, just to add strength and ensure the slug remains stable. This allows the cutting head to return and make the final cut, after which the piece falls safely through the slats.

FIGURE 2 Here, the laser has completed the cutting for an internal feature, but it leaves a short uncut section that holds the slug in place. Later in the program, the cutting head returns to cut this final section, allowing the slug to fall cleanly downward—no tipping or flying uncontrollably.

The Laser Isn’t the Constraint

In some cases, a slug-destruct sequence could be in order. Here, the laser can move in a zigzag or even spiral motion, "chopping up" the slug into small pieces that fall reliably between the slats. This works especially well with very small slugs in light material, which have the potential of flying uncontrollably after being cut.

Such approaches have become more popular as laser cutting has become less of a constraint. Years ago, programmers would try to optimize the laser cutting toolpath for maximum efficiency. All the additional cycle time just to chop up a slug (or skeleton section, for that matter) didn’t seem practical. Today, however, the laser cuts so quickly, the added cutting cycle time usually isn’t an issue.

Similar thinking goes when cutting with "minimum heat." Here, software divides the sheet into zones and instructs the head to alternate between them. This head traverse does add to the overall cutting time. However, cutting with minimum heat dissipation helps with cut geometries that could cause heat to build up in a small area of the sheet, leading to part distortion, which might lead to the final cut part not meeting the tolerances specified.

The same logic applies to collision avoidance for part slugs. The cutting cycle time might be longer—partially cutting all internal features, then finally cutting the remaining uncut sections—but in most cases, the added cycle time doesn’t amount to much. Again, the laser isn’t the constraint … until, that is, a slug tips up and crashes the head.

The Road to Laser Cutting Stability

Strategies boil down to using smart toolpaths to ensure parts remain stable and small slugs fall cleanly in a predictable manner. Think of it like a maintenance fee. We might grumble about it (

the laser isn’t taking the quickest possible path!

), but when we need and don’t have it (

the laser head just crashed!

), we have a problem.

Collision avoidance in laser cutting isn’t exciting, but it’s absolutely critical. If you end up damaging a cutting head, you’re dead in the water while you wait for the laser to come back online. If this happens regularly, you end up doing more damage to a business than anything else could.

It’s easy to invest in the latest, fastest machine. The best operations, though, also utilize software and cutting strategies that help make the most out of what remains the most valuable capital investment on the shop floor.
