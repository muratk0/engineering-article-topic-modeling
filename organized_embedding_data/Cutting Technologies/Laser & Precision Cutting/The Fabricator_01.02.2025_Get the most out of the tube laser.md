Tube Cutting

Strategic design and software go hand in hand

T

ube lasers sometimes help fabricators make the complex much simpler. Innovative designs reduce the number of pieces in an assembly and, through tab-and-slot self-fixturing strategies, even make the assembling easier. Walk the FABTECH show floor and you can see sample assemblies illustrating just how far "designing in tube" can go. What looks like a complex product can have fewer than a half dozen pieces.

Tube lasers might eliminate some secondary processes like drilling and even tapping. Threaded inserts might even be installed within the tube laser’s work envelope. Still, comprehensive product redesigns "around tube" aren’t as common as they could be.

The reasons vary. Some simply lack the knowledge, but a tube laser isn’t a new technology anymore, so this isn’t the problem it once was. Some engineers struggle with tedious, time-consuming design tools. Features aren’t easy to draw in conventional 3D CAD. Designing "in tube" can be quite nuanced too. A missed detail can make a seemingly simple snap-together assembly impossible to put together. Rework costs can add up, especially in environments with frequent design changes.

"Some of the roadblocks include understanding how to minimize your costs, how to minimize your setup, and having the tools you need to really achieve those cost reductions."

That was Kenny Criger, applications manager at BLM GROUP USA, Novi, Mich., who described some of the industry’s new approaches to laser tube cutting. This includes creative thinking that, when combined with some advanced software, gives fabricators intuitive ways to make the most out of the process (see

Figure 1

).

Tab-and-Slot and Cut-Bend

Criger described several design for manufacturability (DFM) building blocks designers use for the tube laser. First is the

tab-and-slot

or

hook-in-slot

strategy, allowing an assembler to place the hook of one part into the slot of the other. In many cases, the tab and slot positions allow pieces to go together only one way, error-proofing the assembly.

Another strategy, the

cut-bend

, involves cutting a V-shaped pocket out of a tube—often rectangular, but it works for round as well. Different-sized V cutouts allow assemblers to handbend a piece to different angles. The strategy basically brings the old-school V-notch technique for sheet metal into the tube arena.

Used together, the hook-in-slot and cut-bend techniques can help designers simplify designs in dramatic ways, not only reducing the number of parts but also simplifying and error-proofing their fixturing, welding, and assembly.

Nuances of the Self-Fixturing Assembly

Self-fixturing assemblies can have extraordinary benefits, but the headaches can be extraordinary too, especially when overlooked details at the design station snowball into major rework issues on the shop floor. Even the tabs and slots—the classic elements of a self-fixturing assembly—are more complicated than they look. Together they create what’s known as a

compound feature

. The tab is on one piece and the slot is on another, and changes in one part affect the other (see

Figures 2

and

3

).

Continual changes can lead to some tedious, time-consuming tasks for the CAD technician. As Criger explained, new software is helping to streamline the process, making design changes less prone to error. If a designer changes a tab location, the slot location on the connecting part changes automatically and vice versa, complete with the proper offsets and other details to ensure two pieces fit together as they should.

"I can have my preferences [in software] set up that show me what will work in the real world," Criger said. "They give me tight fixturing, but they also allow me to put things together easily."

He added that as self-fixturing assemblies get more complicated, they have more of a chance of "locking out." That is, no matter what sequence the product is assembled, at least one piece can’t connect the way it should because previously assembled components are in the way. Designers can spot these issues with simple designs, but as the number of pieces grows, identifying the issues becomes more difficult.

"When you have a five-piece assembly, it’s usually pretty easy to look at it to make sure you have no locked-out pieces," he said. "But when you have 300-piece assemblies, that’s another story."

Some creative thinking can help, including designs that can hook into a wedge-shaped slot in the mating tube’s top face (see

Figure 4

). This is just one strategy that, depending on the situaton, can help a designer from "locking out" an assembly. Historically, designers learned this through months and sometimes years of experience. Today, software applies measures to help prevent designers from locking themselves out of a part.

About Miters

A multiaxis tube laser can manipulate the cutting head to miter in multiple dimensions. In laser tube cutting, this is called a

3D miter

. The laser cuts like a band saw when creating a miter that mates to a flat surface. This also can involve a complex cut to fit coped ends or cutouts in round tube. The result is a tight fit-up with zero gap, which can suit certain downstream processes perfectly, including robotic welding (see

Figure 5

).

But what about manual welding? Sometimes manual welders actually benefit from having more of a gap to fill. This calls for some subtle DFM at the tube laser. Instead of cutting a 3D miter, the tube laser can cut a

2D miter

, with its head always positioned normal (perpendicular) to the tube surface. In certain circumstances, the 2D miter helps give welders the gap they need for better weld quality.

"For robotic welding, though, a 2D miter might not be ideal," Criger said, "so in those cases, you’ll institute 3D miters to get that tight gap. But many shops are manually welding these assemblies, so it’s good to have the 2D miter option."

About Tolerances

A tube wall might have a nominal thickness of 0.125 in., but assembly designs need to account for the acceptable wall thickness tolerance as specified by the tube supplier. Small tolerance variations can stack up to a significant error, especially in a large assembly, and designers need to account for that. This includes just giving enough "play" for tabs and hooks to insert into slots securely and with little resistance. Designs should account not only for material tolerances but also the tolerances that downstream processes demand.

Designers also need to account for issues with ovality, which is especially critical for coped features. Copes need to be oversized to fit onto tubes that might be slightly out of round. The tab-and-slot design within that coped connection needs enough play to account for these variations too.

Today, software preferences can be set up to find the optimal balance between fit-up requirements and ease of assembly. Again, in the past, a design change called for tedious, time-consuming tweaks in CAD, and if anything was missed, assemblers struggled on the floor to make ends meet (in the literal sense). Now, software can solve these problems preemptively using built-in offsets.

Software also can help set the datum points needed. "When designing parts, we can datum off the edge, off the center, and even use an average datum," Criger said, describing a rectangular tube assembly creating a square frame, with pinholes on the top and bottom members that need to line up. "What if your square is slightly askew? If I center those pinholes perfectly on the tube face, those holes won’t align because the tube is askew. So in this case, we can take an average of the deviation so we can line the holes up with each other."

Nontubular Parts

Tube costs more than flat sheet, right? Not necessarily. Sure, the price-per-pound might be higher for tube, but the true cost might actually be lower, especially considering the labor and scrap that goes into processing flat sheet.

"Consider a part formed on a press brake," Criger said. "You can actually nest that within a tube."

To illustrate, he described a bracket with a 90-degree bend (see

Figure 6

). Typically, you’d nest the bracket on a sheet or plate, laser-cut it, then form it on a press brake. But with a tube laser, you can cut a bracket, fully formed, out of rectangular tube. The tube is more expensive than flat sheet, on a per-pound basis at least. But think of the labor savings. No need to denest, stack blanks, deal with the sheet metal skeleton, then transport the blanks to the forming department, where highly skilled press brake operators (well paid, and rightly so) form bracket after monotonous bracket.

Also, think of the scrap savings. As Criger explained, brackets can be "nested" onto a length of tube with minimal material waste. Contrast that with blanks nested on flat sheet. At best, you might have some common-line cut nests with minimal scrap, but operators or denesters still need to lift the remaining skeleton and move it to the scrap bin.

What about the processing of strips? As Criger explained, when you look at the straightforward cost of certain material, like 5-in.-wide by 0.25-in.-thick strip, the per-pound cost is less than standard plate material that would be sent to a 2D laser cutting table. And like the brackets cut from rectangular tube, the flat pieces cut from strip don’t involve standard denesting and skeleton disposal. Cut parts fall down a chute and into an offload bin, where they’re ready to be taken to the next operation.

"You don’t have the skeleton to worry about," Criger said. "And especially if you’re working with rectangular parts, you’re buying, say, a 24-ft. strip of 5-in.-wide, ¼-in.-thick steel, even the outright cost for the material is less if you buy it by the strip. And for very long parts, you don’t have to worry about part length."

Radiused Tube, No Bending Required

Say you have a tube that needs to be formed to a specific radius. You could send it through a rotary draw bender, if you have the appropriate tooling. The setup itself might require some tryout bends or final tweaks. Alternatively, you could try a

multiple cut-bend

. A new approach with the tube laser, a multiple cut-bend incorporates the same concepts as a traditional cutbend, but instead of just one V-shaped cutout, the technique uses a multitude of them.

"Basically, we now can create multiple cutbends that will allow you to ‘radius out’ an edge," Criger said. "So, whether you’re designing for cosmetics or the added strength, you can add a radius bend using the cut-bend technique."

Think of it in terms of the incremental or bump bend on a press brake, with multiple bend lines creating a wide-radius bend. How smooth it is depends on the pitch, or distance between bend lines. The smaller the distance, the smoother the radius bend.

Now, carry this same concept over to rectangular or round tubing. The laser cuts narrow wedges that, when removed, allow a tube to be hand-formed into a specific radius (see

Figure 7

). To ensure stability during laser cutting, some wedges might require microtabbing. In these cases, a few tabs are placed near the bottom of the kerf, on the tube’s inside diameter. When the slug is removed, the bump from the microtab remains on the inside.

FIGURE 7

Cut-bend strategies on the tube laser, both in round and rectangular tubing, can create a radius form without a tube bending operation downstream.

To create a multiple cut-bend, the tube laser cuts in a way that allows a piece to be hand-formed to a certain radius. Specifically, it produces wedge-shaped slugs that, when removed, allow operators or assemblers to hand-form the tube to the intended radius.

The cut geometry ensures a tight fit when the wedge slug is removed and the tube is hand-formed to the intended radius. As the microtab is on the ID, the tab doesn’t show on the outside diameter and the tube "bends" to the radius with no space between the cut-bend lines.

The multiple cut-bend concept might not work in every situation, of course. Sometimes, a design requires the strength or other attributes of a bent tubular section. Sometimes, the multiple cut-bend doesn’t work because of material constraints. It works perfectly well with a variety of carbon and stainless steels, including stainless 304 and 316. For aluminum, grade 5052-H32 works very well, yet highly tempered aluminums—especially 6061-T6—can be difficult to hand-form without cracking. (This applies to other tube cutting strategies involving subsequent hand-forming.)

"6061-T6 is brutal," Criger said. "It will likely snap if you fold it. But 5052-H32 aluminum is absolutely fantastic for cut-bend applications."

He added that in the past, the multiple cut-bend concept could work from a purely design standpoint, but the engineering work in 3D CAD is complex, tedious, and extremely time-consuming. Each wedge’s cutting toolpath has specific requirements for the concept to work: the right thickness and shape of microtab in the right place, the amount of uncut section that will form the bend’s outside radius (it’s not a straightforward decision, especially for round tube), and the exact cut geometry required to ensure each cut-bend mates tightly once hand-formed by the machine operator or assembly technician. Here again, modern software rectifies the issue by guiding the designer through a streamlined process.

Tube Cutting Flexibility

For years, a bottleneck in engineering has limited how far "thinking in tube" could be taken. Consider again the cut-bend and multiple cut-bend strategies. The cut-bend approach can reduce the number of parts in assembly.

Meanwhile, changeover at the tube laser can be extremely quick. It can typically take only two or three minutes to change over from one tube (or strip or structural shape) to another. As Criger put it, "The tube laser can make small-batch-quantity, single-kit, or even single-piece-based flow a reality."

All of these benefits would have minimal impact in an operation with a significant engineering bottleneck. Now, software is just starting to free that engineering constraint.
