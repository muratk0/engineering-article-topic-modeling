# Light, compact, multikilowatt CO2 laser fits on a gantry

TECHNOLOGY SPOTLIGHT

Company takes novel approach to the gas laser

By Tim Heston, Senior Editor

Brothers Yuri (left) and Alexander Krasnov launched Alkras in 2010 to commercialize a new CO

2

laser (center), one that’s small enough to fit on a gantry of a cutting table.

In the 1990s and early 2000s, Alexander Krasnov received several patents in the U.S., Russia, and Europe on a new CO

2

laser. The patents describe a new approach to gas excitation, and in 2010 he, along with his brother Yuri, launched a company in Milford, Mass., to commercialize it. The laser has a broad range of applications, but in the sheet metal cutting arena, the Krasnovs see big potential.

Alexander named his company Alkras, and at the FABTECH® show in November the company introduced its new-generation CO

2

lasers with powers from 700 to 3,000 W. The 1,500-W laser weighs 65 pounds, and the 3,000-W laser weighs less than 120 lbs.—small and light enough to fit directly on the gantry of a cutting table. As the company reports, this reduces the need for complicated beam delivery optics and eases maintenance, overcoming some of the traditional challenges of a gas laser.

The company reports that high energy at the beam’s center helps penetrate the material quickly, while the high energy near the edge of the beam diameter helps produce a clean cut.

The small size also makes it practical to retrofit onto an existing cutting table. The company reports that the unit is built to withstand up to 1.0-g acceleration with the operation.

Alexander is Alkras’ CEO, Yuri is vice president, and as the two explained, the laser can cut thick material quickly and with relatively low power. At FABTECH, the company showed carbon steel samples up to 0.5 inch thick, cut with a 1.5-kW laser. According to company literature, its 2-kW laser has cut 1-mm-thick carbon steel at more than 14 meters per minute, and 3-mm-thick material at more than 5 MPM. Being of the CO

2

variety, it can also handle many nonmetals.

As Alexander explained, the technology uses a new approach to laser kinetics, which led to a different design of the cavity inside the transverse-flow optical resonator, as well as different thinking behind exactly where excitation of the gas molecules (in this case, radio frequency excitation) occurs. The approach, he said, allows these CO lasers to be com-2 pact and efficient, with an optical efficiency of 16 to 17 percent.

Another factor is the beam mode characteristics. The Alkras system uses an “A” mode beam profile. In this, the beam uses a high amount of energy in the center. Moving out toward the edge, the energy quickly decreases and near the edge of the beam diameter increases again dramatically before rapidly falling toward zero.

According to the company, the laser technology takes a new approach to resonator design, including where exactly excitation of the gas molecules occurs.

In A mode, the laser energy peaks in the center, declines, then jumps up again near the edge of the beam diameter.

The high energy at the beam’s center helps penetrate the material quickly, while the high energy near the edge of the beam diameter helps produce a clean cut. “As a result of the laser in ‘A’ mode, 95 percent of the beam’s energy goes directly to the cutting kerf,” said Krasnov.

At present Krasnov and his team are concentrating on the technology’s potential in various sectors, including the retrofit market. Say a shop manager wants to retire an old plasma machine, or perhaps an old laser system. Quite often it’s the cutting source—be it a laser, plasma, or anything else—that’s out-of-date. The cutting table may be perfectly capable of accepting another cutting power source. And as Krasnov sees it, there’s no reason why that couldn’t be a laser.
