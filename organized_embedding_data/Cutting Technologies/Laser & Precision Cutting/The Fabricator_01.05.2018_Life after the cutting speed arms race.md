# Life after the cutting speed arms race

[TARİH: 01.05.2018 The Fabricator]

nyone who has been in metal fabrication for more than a decade remembers the laser power arms race, first with CO

2

and then with fiber: 2 kilowatts, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12. Concurrently came the inches per minute (IPM) race. How fast can a laser cut on 20-gauge? Quarter inch? How quickly can it move around contours? How about the rapid traverse between cuts?

These questions emerged from the earliest days of laser cutting, and for good reason. Compared to today’s speed demons, those early lasers were tortoises, and any boost in speed correlated directly with a boost in cutting throughput.

People seem to be asking not only "How fast can my laser cut?" but also "How fast can my laser respond to a change in demand?"

Today’s machines are so fast that the industry’s speed-and-power arms race has reached a kind of détente, and this survey of U.S. metal fabricators reveals the reasons behind it. Most said their laser cutting capacity increased within the past five years. That’s no surprise, considering how the fiber laser is dominating new machine sales. Fabricators overall still have more CO

2

lasers—no surprise, it being the legacy technology—but fiber lasers are catching up fast. Among all the laser cutting machines reported in this survey, 23 percent were fiber lasers. That’s a big amount, considering the technology hasn’t been around for decades. Fabricators aren’t throwing away their CO

2

s, but they’re adopting fiber lasers swiftly.

With more blanking productivity comes automation. About 80 percent reported having at least some type of material handling automation, while a third reported having at least one multishelf tower.

Most telling is this: Within the past five years, though most report having more laser cutting capacity, not everyone reported having more laser cutting machines. Nearly half report having the same number or

even fewer

machines, and more than half report employing the same number or even fewer operators. Fewer machines and people are producing more.

Throughput seems to be key. As shown by survey results as well as the operations profiled in this report, shop managers are focusing on shipping more orders in less time. Material handling is part of this, but so is reducing rework, and so is scheduling.

Although 60 percent said that they schedule jobs manually, nearly 40 percent said they let software schedule their lasers. A little more than 20 percent said scheduling is so smooth, hot jobs are sent to the cutting department with no manual intervention. Slightly more than half also said that they run only what’s needed immediately, changing over material and laser settings as required.

People seem to be asking not only "How fast can my laser cut?" but also "How fast can my laser respond to a change in demand?" The faster a laser can adapt, the faster jobs can flow, and (ideally) the more profitable a company can be.

The industry is in a new arms race, but not about laser cutting power or IPMs. The arms race now is about quick response.
