Tube And Pipe Cutting

Fabricators Should Fix Process Flaws, Then Find The Right Equipment

By

Lincoln Brunner

A

ny fabricator that’s purchased a new cutting machine is thinking one thing: Did I make the right decision?

If a shop is weighing the choice among sawing, laser cutting, plasma cutting, and waterjet cutting machines, the differences are vast and sometimes subtle. Finding your shop’s current bottlenecks is only the start. A business also must consider its part mix, the kinds of materials it fabricates, cost and availability of labor, opportunities (and need) for automation, and, of course, total cost of ownership.

So, before you dive in, ask yourself the important questions that reveal if that machine will fit you shop’s needs now and the near future.

WHERE ARE THE BOTTLENECKS?

Kevin Kilgallen, president of Harrisburg, Pa.- based machine distributor Mid Atlantic Machinery, said his conversations with customers inevitably begin by finding the pain points in their operations. Whether a shop is primarily fabricating tubes and pipes or more flat stock, when those bottlenecks are identified, good things can start to happen.

"It’s different every time," Kilgallen said of that pain-point conversation. "In pipe cutting, it could be pipe bending, it could be in deburring."

Paul Vragel, owner of 4aBetterBusiness, a business productivity consultancy, noted that there are perceived bottlenecks and real bottlenecks, and that 90% of the issues that waste time and money are embedded in a shop’s process and interactions.

Change usually ignites fears that someone will be blamed for whatever is being fixed. Finding the real sticking points, Vragel said, requires putting those fears to rest by going to the shop floor, getting the scoop from the experts there, and making the process about finding solutions as a team rather than finding fault with certain teammates.

Once the fear of becoming a scapegoat is gone, people talk—and that gets you the real information you need to fix the bottlenecks and other systemic problems hampering production.

"First, let’s get rid of the fear—the agenda is productivity and growth for the whole company," Vragel said. "We collect all that information, visually lay it out … and as we do that, all the things that need work are going to show up.

"One of the real advantages to understanding the real process is that you can do real justification," Vragel added.

DO I NEED TO AUTOMATE?

But what if you can’t find the people to run the new (or old) machinery?

In fact, many of Kilgallen’s customers are hoping to remove manual labor from many aspects, in particular material loading and unloading. What’s pushing this? A concurrence of the ubiquitous labor shortage faced by fabricators, a desire for greater efficiency, a desire to reduce tedium for workers, and the need to shore up safety.

"They want to eliminate the three or four people they have constantly loading sheets onto lasers," Kilgallen said. "We’re trying to reduce the number of human beings that are loading the machines and then unloading those parts.

"Sometimes, you just see stacks of metal everywhere," he said. "With tubes, you could have a vertical storage system to manage that. That would free up additional floor space, and with the sheet stock, also."

WHAT IS THE TOTAL COST OF OWNERSHIP?

The smart buyer considers total cost of ownership before signing on the dotted line—particularly when talking about the stark differences among lasers, plasma cutting machines, waterjets, and saws. In addition to the initial cost of the machine and service contracts, each carries its own distinctive consumable costs, including electricity, gases, nozzles, blades, lubrication, slats, grinding discs (where necessary), electrodes, and even cutting abrasives (for waterjets).

Woven into cost of ownership is the cost of not investing in technology when either the marketplace or your individual business needs call for it—what some would call "opportunity costs."

"What happens if you don’t invest in the automation? What if you don’t invest in this new technology, whether it’s a tube laser or a saw?" Kilgallen said. "Because whatever it is you’re doing, you’re not doing it as efficiently as you could or as efficiently as somebody else is doing it."

For example, for simply cutting pieces of tube to length or maybe adding a miter to the end, you really can’t beat a saw’s speed, productivity, and cost.

"There’s a lot of value in that [sawing] process, but what if I need to drill a hole? What if I have to mill a cope on the end or mark a part? Then a saw isn’t as efficient, and that would lead into other technologies," Kilgallen said. "But, if you have an automatic band saw, and then you have three other operations that are requiring direct labor, that could possibly open up opportunities for either a tube laser or even a plasma cutting pipe machine."

Whether laser, plasma, or saws, finding the right cutting tool for your tubing requires an in-depth look at your shop’s bottlenecks and customer needs—not just prices.

WHAT ABOUT FUTURE CUSTOMERS?

If you’re forward-thinking with your business, you will want to crack that next market. For example, tube lasers have caught a lot of fabricators’ attention the past few years because of their ability to reduce part counts and costs. For many assemblies, parts that currently use pieces that are fabricated from flat sheet and then welded together often can be designed with tubes that are joined together with tabs and slots or are cut and bent with no further joining necessary.

"If I’m a job shop, and I can go to my customer and say, ‘Hey, I’ve got a tube laser; now I have a different way of helping you that’s going to reduce the cost of the part,’ that’s part of my job," Kilgallen said. "I have to go [convince] the customer that this is a better way to do it. But if I don’t have the ability to process tubes, I can’t do that work. I’m missing out on opportunities."
