# Richards Sheet Metal Works

[TARİH: 01.12.2018 The Fabricator]

The beam-on time at Richards Sheet Metal Works is a sight to see. A loader sets a sheet onto the shuttle table. The previous job finishes and an unloading device removes the nest to a pallet. The shuttle tables switch out, and the laser keeps on cutting—all in under 90 seconds from beam-off to beam-on. A forklift driver removes the pallets of cut nests from the laser and moves them to a de-nesting station, around which are three individuals who seem to be the key to the company’s laser cutting throughput: Three de-nesters work full time to remove parts as quickly as possible, sort them, and send them downstream to the constraint operation: bending.

In one sense, the entire cutting operation is centered around one goal: Never starve the press brakes. After all, if you starve the constraint operation, overall throughput falls, and the company isn’t being as productive and profitable as it could be. This is one reason that Richards tends to feed only thinner gauges to its 8-kW solid-state laser. The laser can cut much thicker, of course, but on thin stock, it cuts extremely quickly, meeting Richards’ goal of optimal cutting throughput.

Throughput is why those three de-nesters play a critical role, picking out parts in minutes, scrapping the skeleton and storing any remnants. To speed de-nesting even more, the shop minimizes its use of tabbing. It tabs parts when cutting lights-out, of course, but during attended shifts, the company uses tabs minimally and only where absolutely needed to ensure part quality. Correct tabbing is critical, but tabbing too much can slow the de-nesting, create the need for deburring, and hinder part flow to bending.

Richards employs material load and unload automation, although one machine has an adjacent lifting crane to ensure material can be loaded and unloaded manually, in the event that the automation is unavailable. Richards is currently preparing to install a large automated storage and retrieval system for raw material and cut nests. This will make inventory tracking automatic and real-time, and even further reduce the time it takes for a nest to be released from programming and scheduled on the laser. The less time it takes Richards to release nests to the shop, the better—thanks to the unpredictable demand any job shop owner knows all too well. What if a few small orders get released from the ERP system just after a nesting job is sent to the laser? Those small orders could be squeezed into an existing nest to save time and material, but that requires flexibility and quick reactions.

Of course the automation would mean more productive unattended operation, especially advantageous if the company can increase sales of its "cutting only" work—which could be cut overnight, stored, then removed and shipped.

Today, the shop material manager plays a critical role. He receives all the work orders for all the cutting machines, retrieves material, and delivers it to the machines. He ensures operators record remnants properly, makes sure they’re tracked in inventory software, and works to ensure those remnants get utilized.

In the office, two individuals control the release of orders to a cross-functional engineering team. When orders are released in the ERP, they are pushed automatically to the nesting engine, where the programmer sees the jobs instantaneously—often before the paper traveler arrives at his desk.

Jobs are nested by gauge and due date, and today the company has a volume of work that allows for some sophisticated nesting practices. One nesting job (just to provide an example) includes 10 sheets with 40 jobs from 12 different customers incorporating over 500 part numbers.

This makes careful control of raw material and, especially, proper de-nesting and sorting all the more critical. What good is all the cutting horsepower and nesting sophistication if parts are lost and have to be recut, and people downstream in bending and welding have to wait?
