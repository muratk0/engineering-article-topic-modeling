# Technology isn't a bathrobe

[TARİH: 01.08.2024 The Fabricator]

Tech Talk

One size doesn’ fit all

Caleb Chamberlain

Dzmitrock87/iStock/Getty Images Plus

A

recent episode of The Fabricator Podcast featured Matt Brunner from Brunner Fabrication. I was impressed by how they’ve built an extremely lean and highly productive shop using cutting-edge laser technology: a 24-kW fiber laser that produces blanks as fast as you can blink. This made me reflect on how different Brunner Fabrication is compared to my own on-demand fabrication company, OSH Cut. While both our models are driven by technology, the similarities end there.

These differences are a microcosm of the manufacturing industry as a whole. Thousands of companies are out there, each leveraging technology in distinct ways to support their unique business models, from high-volume production at low costs to bespoke architecture.

A Volume-Based Model

Brunner Fabrication has optimized its entire operation to support high volumes. In Matt’s words, "You come in and you want 500 pieces, I probably won’t talk to you. You know, not being a jerk or whatever. That’s what we’re good at. We’re good at pushing through the most volume we can."

How does it make that happen? Even high-volume shops often take smaller jobs to keep sales funnels rolling; sometimes cultivating low-volume relationships can pay dividends down the road. For Brunner Fabrication, the tactics are beautifully simple: keep overheads low; buy the fastest, most productive machines; and leverage economies of scale to drive prices down and win big jobs. By leaning into this formula, it produces parts faster and at a lower cost than its OEM customers could manage in-house.

With just six employees, Brunner Fabrication’s leadership structure is necessarily flat. There are very few if any people who aren’t contributing directly to value-adding processes. They don’t have a dedicated sales team, engineering team, purchasing department, or HR staff. If that weren’t enough, the shop is only 12,000 sq. ft., significantly reducing energy costs and rent or loan payments.

Additionally, Brunner Fabrication participates in a group purchasing arrangement, benefiting from high-volume OEM material pricing. The company takes those low material prices and absorbs already low overheads across perhaps millions of parts using the fastest laser cutting machine on the market. It’s clear why Brunner’s prices might be hard to match.

Despite its small square footage and headcount, the company expects to generate $8 million to $10 million in revenue this year. That’s some serious productivity, but Brunner has ambitions to grow enough to land on The Fabricator’s FAB 40 list. A four-times increase in revenue might get it there. I believe Brunner will succeed if it can maintain low overhead, technological edge, favorable purchasing arrangements, and solid customer relationships.

High-Mix Fabrication

Compare Brunner’s model to OSH Cut, a fabrication company I co-founded six years ago. Our model is also enabled by technology, but all similarities end there. For starters, we don’t touch high-volume production. Instead, we’ve optimized around high-mix/low-quantity, on-demand sheet metal production. According to Matt, Brunner Fabrication might often produce only seven unique part numbers per week, albeit in very high quantities. In contrast, OSH Cut regularly produces more than 2,000 part numbers per week, most of which we’ve never seen before and likely will never see again.

Our model isn’t better, but it’s different. Since we aim to tackle as many unique parts as possible for jobs of any size, our company spends extraordinary time and resources streamlining processes. We automated quoting and the identification of manufacturability issues, and exposed it to customers through our website, enabling us to quote thousands of jobs per week economically. We built internal software for inventory management and purchasing automation, designed software tools to keep parts flowing in production, and streamlined shipping processes to ensure orders go out the door efficiently.

All of that software development has cost us millions of dollars, and we aren’t finished. We will maintain an in-house software development team indefinitely, an expensive and (for us) necessary cost that drives our overheads way up compared to an operation like Brunner’s. Additionally, we have more layers of leadership in our 50-person company, driven by the need to manage the complexity of a high-mix operation with a vertically integrated tech stack.

Even if we were to purchase a 24-kW laser, we might struggle to compete on high-volume jobs due to our higher overheads. On the flip side, we’ve scaled by collecting work from tens of thousands of companies, fulfilling orders quickly and consistently, and running small-batch production that wouldn’t interest high-volume, low-mix operations. We can’t easily do their kind of work, but they can’t do ours, either.

Bespoke Construction

A few months ago, I met the owners and operators of Flight Fabrication at a machine sales event in California. I was immediately drawn to the mantra printed on their shirts: "Forged in Quality; Fabrication and Iron." I love the combination of words and ideas conveyed there—the grounded, industrial, solid feel of a forge producing iron. Based out of Colorado, Flight Fabrication provides bespoke design, engineering, and fabrication services for high-end construction. Think of homes worth $6 million or more, and you’ll start to understand its market.

On its website, Flight Fabrication highlights its unique offering: "Old School" craftsmanship combined with "New School" CNC technology, delivering "timeless designs with amazing accuracy." Aided by realistic 3D renderings, Flight’s customers are encouraged to dream. The final product encompasses not just the product—like a custom steel staircase, for example—but the experience of it and the feelings it evokes. Modern fabrication technology broadens the canvas of artistic possibility.

Flight Fabrication’s product, customers, and value chain are entirely distinct from both OSH Cut and Brunner Fabrication. For Flight, it’s all about connections, artistic talent, and the ability to fabricate a beautiful, unique finished product that fits into someone’s dream home. In contrast, Brunner is focused on being an efficient, low-cost component of large OEMs’ value chain. Meanwhile, OSH Cut streamlines prototyping and early-stage and low-volume recurring production.

The future of manufacturing will be shaped by those who can adapt and evolve, using technology to reduce costs, improve quality, expand into new markets, or perhaps invent new markets altogether.

Hundreds of Verticals

I meet an ever-increasing number of manufacturing businesses, and they are all unique. One company makes machines that automatically wind up pool covers. Another makes desks and chairs for new schools. Yet another major manufacturer focuses on aerospace, with all the added overhead—and high margins—involved in that industry. Across all these industries, fabricators are applying manufacturing technology in different ways to service their unique markets.

As we’ve seen, it’s not just the manufacturing that’s different. Sales models range from relationship-based, interactive, and custom (like Flight Fabrication) to mostly transactional (like OSH Cut). Business models and economics vary as well. Brunner’s entire company is structured to provide a very particular kind of service—extremely low unit costs at scale for large OEMs.

OSH Cut’s business is structured to provide rather the opposite—a simple, fast experience at low volumes for many customers, at a fair price. And Flight Fabrication is all about completely unique, luxury designs that are individually very profitable. Not only are our markets, sales pipelines, technologies, and skills different, but our business models are incompatible.

A week ago, I talked with another multifacility operation based out of California, serving the construction industry. Its average lead time is four to six weeks. I’ve been so immersed in the rapid response world that I’m often surprised to hear that shops still have lead times that long, and I told him as much. But the fact is that while rapid response is becoming increasingly normal, it’s also not the end-all. Different markets care about different things. Optimizing to serve a particular niche might mean you can’t operate well in another, and that’s OK. In fact, it’s not just OK—it’s important to remember, because expanding too far outside your sweet spot could undermine your business model.

Applying Technology to Your Business

Market diversity means that everyone will apply new technologies differently. Brunner Fabrication uses the latest and greatest machines to reduce unit costs. OSH Cut leverages custom software to simplify and streamline high-mix production. Flight Fabrication employs modern CAD and rendering to help customers imagine and dream.

There is no one-size-fits-all approach. Could modern equipment improve your unit economics or quality? Could new software reduce confusion on the shop floor, or reduce quoting overhead? The future of manufacturing will be shaped by those who can adapt and evolve, using technology to reduce costs, improve quality, expand into new markets, or perhaps invent new markets altogether. How will you apply technology in your business?
