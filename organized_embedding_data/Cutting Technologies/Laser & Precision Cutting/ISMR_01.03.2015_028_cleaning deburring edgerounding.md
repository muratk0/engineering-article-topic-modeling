## Grinding fluid

Q8OILS has launched Q8 Brunel, its latest development in water-soluble machining and grinding fluids. It is formulated to be fully compliant with the latest European industry legislation providing a range of products that are free from boron, boric acid, chlorinated paraffin, secondary amine, formaldehyde-releasing biocides and nitrites.

Q8Oils product line manager, Stuart Duff, explained that, subject to customer requirements, significant improvements to tool life and surface finish are achievable.

“All Q8 Brunel grades comply with TRGS 611 and are suitable for use in hard and soft water where they display low foaming properties. They are ideal for high speed machining applications and the demanding fluid delivery pressures found on modern machine tools; and it is here where significant tool life improvements are achievable. In addition, all grades are suitable for use in hard or soft water with ultra-low foaming properties,” added the company.

## Underwater laser cutting

UNDERWATER CONSTRUCTION on offshore wind farms, bridges or locks must presently be done by scuba divers. For maintenance and repair of metal constructions, a number of processes are available but these are time-consuming and difficult for divers. Engineers at the Laser Zentrum Hannover e.V. (LZH) and the Leibniz Universität Hannover (LUH) are therefore developing a process for automated, underwater laser cutting.

“Currently, mainly light arc oxygen cutting is being used for underwater cutting. The electrodes are handguided. Depending upon the material thickness, divers need a work day to cut 20m of material. For a diving period of five hours, this means a cutting speed of only 7cm per minute. With a laser-based automated process, the cutting speed could be increased by a factor of seven,” LZH told ISMR.

“During pilot tests, we have already achieved a cutting speed of 0.5m per minute for 10mm thick steel,” said Dr.-Ing. Jörg Hermsdorf, head of the machines and controls group at the LZH. “With this process, underwater metal working could be considerably faster and thus less expensive. Our goal is to make the work of the divers safer and more efficient.”

“The process is being developed for dismantling sheet pilings in water of 2-6m. For this, it is crucial that the pilings, including the interlocks, are reliably cut as post processing is time-consuming and expensive. Since metal parts corrode under water and are subject to overgrowth, the process is designed to function even for varying material thicknesses and pollution levels,” concluded the LZH.

The finished process could also be used for the maintenance and repair of offshore structures and other hydraulic structures. The dismantling of nuclear power plants is a further field of application. The “Laser cutting under water for higher productivity – LuWaPro” project is supported by the German Federation of Industrial Research Associations "Otto von Guericke" e.V. (AiF). For further details, see www.lzh.de ■

The KASTOuniline automated storage system for sheet metal and other stock

KASTO exhibited products from its sawing and storage portfolio at the international sheet processing technology show, EuroBLECH (21-25 October 2014 in Hanover).

## Automated bending for efficient production

THE ABILITY TO combine multiple processes with a single system solution is a concept that Amada is exploiting with its latest automated bending systems. At the EuroBLECH exhibition in Hanover, Amada demonstrated three fully automated bending cells where the use of robots brought benefits in flexibility, along with time savings, even when processing small batches.

The first of these, which is designed for small component bending, is the Amada EG6013AR, a robotic bending system that features a 1300mm, 600kN servo-electric press brake mounted by a front-traversing, six-axis robot. The compact cell layout is optimised for system simplification and space reduction, while the dedicated robot features a new gripper design for handling small and complex parts. Both workpiece manipulation and tool-changing are facilitated by the same robot. Components measuring up to 300 x 300mm, and weighing up to 1.6kg, can be accommodated. It features tactile sensor systems in the backgauge which are used to detect the position of the workpiece in both X and Y axes before bending and initiate corrective measures if departures from tolerances are identified. Other benefits include a new servo drive system for higher performance and lower power consumption, and the use of Amada’s new AMNC3i control.

Another automated bending cell offered by Amada for larger parts is the HG1003ARs. The cell features a HG1003ATC (auto tool change) servo-hydraulic press brake which, for short run, high-mix production, can be used by an operator in manual mode, or by an HG robot mounted on a separate track for higher volumes. Able to handle sheets measuring up to 1000 x 800mm, and up to 6mm thick, the HG1003ARs features automatic gripper change functionality, while angle adjustment and high accuracy positioning are also fully automated.

The third automated bending cell is Amada’s HG1303Rm. Based on an Amada HG1303 press brake, the cell features an innovative bending system (special gripper and motorised repositioning device) for long parts – sheets of up to 2500mm can be accommodated by the HG robot. See www.amada.co.uk ■
