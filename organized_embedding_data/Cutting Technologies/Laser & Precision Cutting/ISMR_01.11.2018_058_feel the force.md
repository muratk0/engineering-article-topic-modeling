# FEEL THE FORCE

[TARİH: 01.11.2018 ISMR]

Eagle introduced a new 15kW fibre laser cutting system, with automated loading and unloading unit, as well as a new 3D printer at EuroBLECH 2018.

Polish fibre laser specialist, Eagle, introduced two world premieres at this October's EuroBLECH exhibition in Germany – a new 15kW fibre laser cutting system, with automated loading and unloading unit, as well as a new 3D printer.

EAGLE has taken great care developing its lightweight eVa cutting head. It has developed and patented the eVa to handle ultra-high power, up to 15kW, in busy production environments. According to the Polish manufacturer, the dual-body design within the cutting head transfers the power, up to 15kW, without risk of damage. Its light weight enables high G manoeuvrability. It features super-fast linear motor lift and drop. eVa features few optics and a protective glass design to protect against contamination.

Magdalena Klimaszewska, Eagle's head of marketing, told ISMR what this new development will mean for the productivity levels of sheet metal manufacturers.

MK: This year at EuroBLECH 2018, Eagle introduced two world premieres. The first one was, I believe, the highest power laser cutting system ever made. Our new laser cutter, with a 15000W (15kW) fibre laser source, gives us even greater efficiency than before and enables better cutting quality at a lower cutting cost. This innovative machine was showcased at EuroBLECH with automation - a CraneMasterStore system, with loading and unloading unit combined with MonoTower automated storage.

Our first 15kW fibre laser features 6G acceleration and 350m/min. rapid traverse speed. Eagle was one of the first to release and harness 6kW, 8kW, 12kW and now 15kW laser. In my view, it cuts thick sheet and plate faster than any other laser. Our patented eVa cutting head also solves the problems of ultra-high-power laser cutting, delivering reliable operation at these power levels.

The second premiere at our stand was our new 3D metal printer - one that you have never seen before. With this, we present an alternative solution to the standard printing method.

ISMR: What differentiates the EAGLE cutting head from others in the market?

MK: We believe that the eVa cutting head is unique in the world of ultra-high-power fibre lasers. There are very few moving parts in the path of the laser beam which improves reliability. It provides full beam control for all applications, is very simple and lightweight in design (enabling high machine acceleration), is easily maintained in minutes by the operator and offers rapid hole-piercing capability.

Ultra-high-power lasers require special lens consideration. The eVa is equipped with just two fixed lenses, half that of most cutting heads. Despite this, the cutting head can change the focal diameter and the angle between the beam and the material, so that all material thicknesses can be cut with any desired laser power. The glass that protects the optics is far enough away from the cutting nozzle to reduce the chance of spark damage and make the eVa much more resistant to contamination during the cutting process. As a result, replacement of the protective glass occurs ten times less frequently than with other fibre laser cutting heads. In addition, the eVa incorporates a collimator protective glass mounted over the collimating lens to further enhance durability and uptime.

eVa cutting head performance is distinguished not just by advanced design, but also from manufacturing excellence. We are in the middle of a large expansion project that involves two stages. The first stage (the new hall we built last summer), includes an R&D area; conference centre; CNC/PLC programming; construction; mechanical workshops and a clean room. The clean room is a home to the eVa cutting head and other key components. It is equipped with modern assembly equipment, laminar chambers and high-quality washing facilities.

Commissioning of the new clean room not only gives us pristine assembly conditions, but also enhances the quality control of all aspects of sub component handling (e.g. optics and assemblies, such as the eVa head).

ISMR: What is your approach to Industry 4.0 as a company?

MK: We have designed our entire machine platform around Industry 4.0 philosophies. There are two key benefits of our system design philosophy, as detailed here:

■ Common control system for the entire work cell. Our fibre laser, machine pallet changer and even the material storage towers are all controlled by the Eagle Laser's Beckhoff control system. This approach not only means that the operator, programmer or shop manager has one main interface for operating the entire system, but also means that the data from the Eagle fibre laser and material inventory are under one system and easily transferred via Industry 4.0 protocols to the manufacturing office, and even back to Eagle Laser.

■ Run at peak performance. Due to our common control philosophy for the Eagle fibre laser, machine pallet changer and material storage towers, we have greatly enhanced the tracking of productivity and troubleshooting levels. We can report which shifts are productive and which are not, and we can solve over 60% of all issues either before they happen or when they happen, without wasting time sending in a technician. We can see the data and solve problems remotely, keeping our customers at peak performance.

ISMR: What are your views on the current business climate for sheet metal professionals?

MK: Our sheet metal fabricator customers constantly need to produce parts faster and cheaper, with lower labour interaction. If you can install one high-power fiber laser machine that will produce as much as two or three older CO₂ or low-wattage fibre lasers could output, then you win on two fronts: your parts are cheaper, and you need just one operator.

Laser power has a huge impact on the efficiency of the laser cutting machine. Proof of this was the stacks of parts we presented over the four days of the EuroBLECH exhibition. The stacks showed the number of parts that could be cut in one hour at various laser powers – the highest stack was over two metres high and consisted of 277 parts.

The number of cut parts translates directly into profit. Assuming the market price of the cut-out detail is two euros, the profit in relation to the cost of producing a detail is as follows:

The production of one part with a 4kW laser costs 1.56 euros. Thus, the profit at the assumed market price of two euros is 0.44 euros. According to our assumptions, 4kW lasers "earn" us 28.16 euros while the laser with a capacity of 15kW will bring 407.19 euros profit, which is fourteen times more. It is easy to imagine a profit increase with each subsequent hour, day and month.

ISMR: Is there anything else that you would like to discuss?

MK: Yes, I would like to highlight the relationship between power and acceleration for thick and thin parts. Machine acceleration is not a specification provided by most laser manufacturers, but it is often more important than maximum machine speed. If you double the acceleration of a machine, say from 2G to 4G, the maths shows that you will halve the distance and time it takes to get up to the desired speed. When cutting thin parts, where cut speed is high, acceleration is extremely important. But remember, even when cutting very thick parts, you will still have rapid traverse moves between cut paths where the machine will move very quickly, and acceleration/deceleration will come into play.

Power is another misunderstood parameter. When fibre laser cutting systems first came out, they were operated at 1kW and 2kW levels. When Eagle released a 6kW fibre laser, it was said that the added power was not needed. The same was said when we released 10kW, 12kW and 15 kW versions. In fact, power levels have not yet reached their maximum for sheet metal and plate metal cutting. We will continue to push power levels, but only in conjunction with acceleration enhancements. Increasing power while holding acceleration at 1G or 2G will enable you to cut thicker material but will not increase productivity on thinner material.

Acceleration and power must develop hand-in-hand. Advancing acceleration performance requires advanced machine and controls engineering knowledge. Advancing power levels require advanced optics and software knowledge. Only the fibre laser builders, who are willing to pursue the goal of the 'ideal' machine, will be able to maximise both factors and consequently lower production costs for fabricators.
