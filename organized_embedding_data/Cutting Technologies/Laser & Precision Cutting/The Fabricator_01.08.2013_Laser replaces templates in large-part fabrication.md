# Laser replaces templates in large-part fabrication

[TARİH: 01.08.2013 The Fabricator]

Technology Spotlight

Photogrammetry-based laser system designed to eliminate jigs, templates

By Tim Heston, Senior Editor

Manufacturing has grown increasingly digital over the years. You no longer see rows of drafting tables, blueprint rolls stacked everywhere. Now the design happens in a virtual space. Information from the design file is postprocessed and used for nesting and bend programming. And if a fabricator has modern machinery and the right software, the information flows entirely in the digital sphere, from the front office to the machine control.

But then a welder lugs out his jigs and templates.

For large fabrications, these templates can be massive and unwieldy. But they’re necessary, because they locate parts in a large and sometimes very complex final assembly. The link between the digital model and the shop floor part breaks during welding and assembly, until the workpiece reaches the quality area, which checks the subassembly with hard gauges and, nowadays, CMMs and laser scanners that again link back to data from the digital design file. Still, that “digital connection gap” during assembly remains.

This may be changing, though, thanks in part to a development that has its origins in composites manufacturing. A completely different field, the composites sector does share one common element with heavy fabrication: precise location requirements over large areas. To make a composite panel requires the exact placement of carbon-fiber layers, or plies, into a large mold that shapes the composite into the desired geometry, be it a wind turbine blade or airplane wing. The placement of each layer is critical, and for years lasers have been used to guide the deposits by projecting an outline of the digital model onto the mold.

“Using a laser that runs off a series of galvanometers, we’re essentially steering the laser beam in a precise manner, down to thousandths-of-an-inch precision, at distances between 15 and 30 feet, onto the tool. We’re projecting the outline of where that ply is to be placed in the tool.”

So said Todd Rhodes, vice president and general manager at Virtek Vision International, an Ontario, Canada-based business of Gerber Technology, Tolland, Conn.

The name is familiar to fabricators; the company’s first-article inspection machine uses a laser to scan part dimensions. Until recently the projection side of Virtek’s business has focused on other industries, mainly because of one primary technological hurdle: Laser projection has required location points to be placed in the work area. For instance, in composites manufacturing, predetermined location points are machined into the mold. Those points give the system a frame of reference. If the points move slightly during manufacturing, the system detects the motion and adjusts its laser projection.

These location points presented a problem in heavy fabrication. To make composites is an additive process, with one layer following the next. This means that in most cases the laser projection system can see the necessary location points for the next carbon-fiber layer.

Assembly in large fabrications doesn’t work this way, of course. It’s not at all a layer-by-layer process, and a location point could be blocked by various elements—a bracket, cylinder, or anything else—as the assembly progresses.

In the inspection side of its business, Virtek engineers work with a technology called photogrammetry to reference locations not off specific location points, but actual part features, like an edge or a hole. It’s here that the company saw potential for its projection technology.

“It was the perfect storm, really,” Rhodes said. “We had a specific demand from a large OEM in the heavy equipment space, and we had our research department working on integrating photogrammetry with our laser projection technology.”

Whence came the Iris™ Spatial Positioning System in December 2012. The system is designed to eliminate the need for job-specific templates for locating assembly components. It uses a laser beam sent through a series of galvanometers to project marks—be it a single point, circle, square, or any other 3-D shape—that show exactly where a component should go for assembly.

It uses photogrammetric triangulation that entails a locator, laser, and workpiece all working together. The locator uses two sensors spaced apart (see figure) to measure feature positions. This allows the system to detect when the spatial locator, laser, or the workpiece itself has been moved, either individually or in any combination. It then goes back to digital data based on the CAD file and adjusts the projection so all elements in the projection accurately reflect the part design.

“Rather than using targets, it locates an item in three-dimensional space,” Rhodes said, adding that certain workpiece features, determined by the mechanical engineer before assembly, now can act as location points. And those location points can be changed at different stages of assembly. If, say, a bracket covers up a feature that was used as a location point, the engineer can instruct the system to use different location points at the next stage of assembly.

The system also works in three dimensions. Say an assembly requires a 6-in.-high cylinder welded onto a flat surface. The cylinder may have several critical dimensions, including the inside and outside diameters of the bottom flange and a small hole on top, 6 in. above the base plate. To project these location points, the system considers the location not only in the X and Y direction, but also Z—the depth. So when a technician places a cylinder onto the right location, the laser outlines should line up precisely, both at the base plate and at the hole 6 in. above it. If it doesn’t, something is amiss.

Traditional jigs and templates perform the same function, of course. But if a design changes, then new jigs or templates may need to be fabricated. This can be a lengthy process, especially for large workpieces. Using the laser-based projection system, engineers can import the new design data from the CAD file to the laser’s software and, within a few minutes, update the projection onto the workpiece.

Rhodes conceded that the system can’t replace templates and jigs entirely. The principal constraint involves line-of-sight problems between the laser and workpiece surface. If a welder needs to install a component inside an enclosed space—not an uncommon task in heavy fabrication—he probably still will need a jig or template to locate the part correctly.

But he added that, a few exceptions aside, the system can eliminate templates during welding and assembly.

“The designs can change all the time,” Rhodes said, “but the tools in manufacturing are usually slow to catch up to the design changes. And sometimes those changes aren’t made, so the ‘as designed’ doesn’t match the ‘as built.’ This technology aims to eliminate that problem.”
