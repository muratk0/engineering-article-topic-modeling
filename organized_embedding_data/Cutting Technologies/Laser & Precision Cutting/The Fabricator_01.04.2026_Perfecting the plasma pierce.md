Plasma Cutting

For improved cutting and longer consumable life

H

igh-definition plasma cutting remains a plate fabrication workhorse, especially in thick material. In this world, cutting-inches per minute can have a real impact on overall throughput. Even so, optimizing the actual cutting is only a piece of the puzzle. Another major piece is the pierce.

"When you pierce a plate, the molten material has to go somewhere, and usually it rises like a volcano. If the setup and consumables aren’t designed properly, you’re going to degrade consumable life."

That was Jorge Santana, senior product manager at Hypertherm Associates. The Fabricator spoke with Santana for the other half of the picture, the pierce—how to dial it in and what it means for overall plasma cutting throughput.

"Many fabricators really concentrate on the cutting travel speed," he said. "They ask, ‘How fast can I cut this part?’ But in measuring this, many people overlook the pierce. Every pierce takes time."

BASICS OF THE PLASMA PIERCE

Plasma cutting power supplies use open-circuit voltage (OCV), with the difference in electrical potential between the electrode (with a negative charge) and the nozzle and workpiece (positive charge). The OCV creates that high initial potential to ionize the gas and strike the pilot arc inside the torch body, before the arc transfers to the workpiece. Once the arc has transferred to grounded metal, the pilot circuit is shut off and the system commences cutting.

"Ideally, power sources have a lot of voltage so they can sustain the arc as far away from the plate as possible," Santana said. "The farther you are, the less molten metal splashes up toward the consumables. This is why optimizing your pierce really matters."

Modern power sources can transfer the plasma arc from the

pierce height

, which has helped streamline stationary piercing. Traditionally, the torch would descend to the transfer height, where it would initiate the arc and then immediately retract to a specified pierce height. Today, with voltage capable of transferring the arc from a higher point, the torch simply descends to detect the material, then retracts to the pierce height to initiate the arc transfer. Once the piercing time (often called pierce delay) is complete, the torch descends to the standard cut height (see

and

).

"To develop piercing protocols, we test for at least 300 pierces occurring continuously, penetrating through the maximum material thickness [rated for the power source]," Santana said, adding that every 100 cuts, technicians look at the kerf angularity and the condition of the torch consumables.

MINIMIZING THE PIERCE

Some operators might rely on

chain cutting

, a technique that keeps the plasma arc in cutting mode for most of a nest. Once the torch finishes one part profile, it slices through the skeleton web section and starts on the next part.

"This technique helps you reduce the number of pierces you need to do on a nest," Santana explained, adding that the strategy has its challenges. "To be honest, I haven’t seen chain cutting used very often for plasma cutting."

There are several reasons for this. Chain cutting doesn’t account for holes, slots, or other internal features. And even if a nest has parts with no internal features, chain cutting limits the program’s toolpath options. "And really, so many parts have internal features. It’s extremely common. You will need to pierce at some point." He added that instead of focusing on ways to avoid piercing, those using and developing plasma cutting technology have focused on perfecting the pierce itself.

EDGE START, PREPIERCE, AND DOUBLE PIERCE

Initiating the plasma arc at the plate edge has plenty of advantages. The plasma arc can cut thicker materials, and because you’re not piercing a hole and forcing molten metal upward toward the torch, consumables last longer. To perform an edge start, the torch is positioned near the material edge. As soon as the arc transfers, it "pulls" toward the plate edge (the electrical ground). Once established, the cutting mode can commence.

FIGURE 2 A plasma torch initiates a pierce in thick aluminum plate. Note the significant pierce height, protecting the consumables from molten material.

While common in manual plasma cutting, performing an edge start on a plasma cutting table can present some challenges. The plate edge isn’t a fixed point but instead varies slightly from plate to plate.

"Because the plate isn’t at a fixed point every single time, you need an operator with enough experience, or you need to invest in specific peripheral equipment, to ensure you find the edge and start in the same place every time. When you think about the setup time it requires, edge starting can really hinder your productivity. And for most jobs, you have multiple parts on a nest that will require an internal pierce anyway."

For this reason, edge starting usually works best when an operator pushes the thickness limits for a particular plasma cutting power source and torch consumables. It also works in conjunction with prepiercing. Sometimes called a

predrilled start

, it’s essentially an edge start in a predrilled hole. The operator drills (using, say, a mag drill, or a drilling station on a multifunction machine) pilot holes throughout the nest. The plasma torch then performs a series of edge starts (on the plate edge) and prepierces in the drilled holes.

Again, the operator needs to determine the precise torch position and ensure the arc fires directly adjacent to that drilled hole edge. The physics of the plasma plume, including its tendency to "hug" the material edge when the torch is positioned in just the right way, takes time to learn (see

Figure 3

). Such piercing strategies aren’t for the uninitiated.

When edge starting isn’t practical, some operators might rely on the double pierce, especially when extreme plate thickness leaves no other option (see

Figure 4

). As its name implies, the technique involves piercing once partway through, removing slag, and piercing again to penetrate through completely. Like edge starting, this technique also requires finesse. For instance, an operator might move the torch to the edge of the partially pierced hole before initiating the second pierce, just to help direct molten metal away from the torch consumables.

MOVING PIERCE

In a moving pierce, the torch starts to move soon after the arc is transferred (see

Figure 5

). "Because the torch is moving, the molten metal splashes up in a trail behind the torch," Santana said. "At the same time, the material thickness [below the plasma arc] decreases until you eventually penetrate through the entire plate. Many have found success with this method, but it does require some technique and operator finesse."

Operators need to dial in the travel speed, and they also need to plan for slightly longer leadins. In some nest layouts, this might require slightly larger web sections, which can decrease material yield. The distance the pierce travels is key here. The last thing they want is for the torch to transition from piercing to cutting mode too soon. This, Santana said, is where that finesse comes into play.

The technique has similar drawbacks as edge starting, prepiercing, and double piercing. A conventional moving pierce can save consumables, but it can add significantly to setup time and, hence, decrease overall throughput. It also requires specialized skills and finesse that take time to learn and perfect.

FIGURE 4 Like edge starting, double piercing calls for some finesse. This shows the torch repositioning after the initial pierce. Positioning the torch near the wall of the previous pierce helps evacuate more molten metal away from the torch.

PIERCE WITH ARGON SHIELDING

Even so, the moving pierce does have serious benefits, especially when it comes to consumable life. Today, some operations might choose a controlled moving pierce when tackling material that pushes a system’s thickness limits—and it’s a good alternative to edge starts that, again, can change depending on a plate’s dimensional tolerances and the material’s exact position on the table.

And as Santana explained, this is where technologies that help control the pierce come into play. This is where

argon-assisted piercing

steps into the picture.

This Hypertherm technology essentially uses argon as a kind of shielding gas. "It allows us to transfer the arc at a higher distance," Santana said. "By doing that, we’re preventing the material from splashing up and damaging the consumables. You’re preserving the consumable life, and you also don’t have to use specialized techniques like the moving pierce or prepierce."

FIGURE 5 In a moving pierce, the plasma torch initiates the arc transfer and begins moving, ejecting molten metal behind the torch body.

On a 460-amp system (XPR model), the torch can perform a stationary pierce in 2.5-in.-thick mild steel at a pierce height of 1.25 in. off the surface of the material, while the pierce delay is 4.5 seconds, after which the torch descends to a 0.5-in. cut height. The pierce height for 2-in. material is 0.850 in., and the pierce delay is 2 seconds, after which the torch descends to 0.4 in. above the material.

Material 2.75 in. and thicker usually requires edge starts, even with argon shielding. In fact, edge starts remain the preferred method for extreme material thicknesses. Although stationary piercing isn’t recommended in these materials, argon does play a role when cutting commences. As Santana explained, argon-assisted cutting effectively increases plasma cutting’s thickness capacity—up to 4 in. for carbon steel and 5 in. for stainless (using the 460-amp XPR power source).

"The argon, an inert gas, essentially helps us sustain the arc over a longer distance, which allows us to cut thicker."

LIQUID-COOLED SHIELDING

Santana added that argon-assisted technology effectively allows plasma operations to expand the use of the tried and true stationary pierce into thicker materials. And again, when delving into extreme thickness, argon helps create a controlled moving pierce.

In some circumstances, Santana might recommend another tool—used alone or in conjunction with the argon assist—that can help prolong consumable life:

liquid-cooled shielding

. In this process, which Hypertherm calls PowerPierce, water cools the nozzle and prevents molten metal from sticking, repelling spatter. In certain systems, the technology can enhance the moving pierce, avoiding the need for edge starts.

ABOUT REPEATABILITY

Santana clarified that there’s nothing wrong with edge starts. They remain the best way to start a cut in extremely thick plate. But they do add another variable— plate position—into the equation.

Zoom out and look at the product flow at many heavy fab shops, and you likely won’t see a constraint in plasma cutting. Even in some cut-only operations, though, cutting faster isn’t a sustainable improvement if the operation doesn’t repeatedly produce quality parts in a predictable timeframe. If machines are down and rework abounds (like excessive edge grinding and re-cuts) due to premature consumable wear, the inconsistent throughput that results can throw a serious wrench into production planning.

Like any metal fabrication technology, plasma cutting is about managing the tradeoffs to produce the greatest throughput of parts that meet or exceed quality requirements. To do this, no one variable can be viewed in isolation. The total cycle time comprises some combination of cutting and piercing (and for thick material, edge starting). Overlook the piercing and you miss an important part of the total processing time. It’s also why a streamlined, controlled pierce can make a world of difference.
