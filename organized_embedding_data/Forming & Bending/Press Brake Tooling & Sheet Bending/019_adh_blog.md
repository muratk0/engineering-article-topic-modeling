# Estimating and Verifying Inside Bend Radius in Air Bending

## Radius charts and process planning

Material bend-radius charts should not be treated as guarantees of the radius that will be produced on a press brake. In the article’s examples, such charts are presented primarily as limits associated with cracking risk for a specified material condition. The achievable radius also depends on the forming method, die opening, material properties, bend orientation relative to rolling direction, punch geometry, and the actual behavior of the material batch.

A drawing radius, a chart value, and the formed radius may therefore differ. Before production, the selected tooling and process should be checked against the material specification, the machine and tooling documentation, and the required part dimensions.

## Air bending and the role of the V-die

In air bending, the sheet is supported on the shoulders of a V-die while the punch drives it into the die opening. The sheet does not necessarily conform to the punch nose or contact the bottom of the die. Consequently, the V-die opening is a major influence on the resulting inside radius.

The article describes a commonly used estimate for mild steel in air bending: an inside radius near one-sixth of the V-die opening. It also notes that this is an approximation, not a universal result. Softer or more ductile materials may form more tightly, while stronger materials may produce a larger radius because of their resistance to deformation and springback.

Punch radius becomes more directly influential in processes that intentionally force material into the die, such as bottoming or coining. Those processes can require substantially greater force than air bending and should only be planned within verified machine, tooling, and material limits.

## Material condition affects bendability

Material thickness alone is not sufficient for selecting a bend setup. The article identifies several variables that can change the acceptable radius and final bend result:

- Material grade and strength
- Aluminum alloy and temper
- Stainless or high-strength material condition
- Rolling direction and bend direction
- Springback behavior
- Actual die opening and punch geometry

Minimum-radius guidance must therefore match the specific material designation and condition. A setup suitable for one alloy, temper, or grade may not be suitable for another material of the same thickness. The article also warns that bending parallel to the rolling direction can increase cracking risk compared with bending across it. Production planning should account for the material’s documented rolling direction where relevant.

## Die width, flange support, and forming load

A die opening must support the part geometry as well as provide an appropriate bend radius. Short flanges may require a smaller die opening to remain supported during the bend. However, reducing die width can increase required forming load and can also reduce the natural radius produced in air bending.

The article presents the familiar “eight times material thickness” die-opening practice as a starting point for standard mild-steel air-bending work, while emphasizing that it is not a universal rule. Thin material, thick plate, high-strength material, limited flange length, required radius, and available tooling may require a different choice.

Because forming load rises as die openings become narrower, die selection must be checked against rated machine capacity, tooling limits, bend length, material condition, and the manufacturer’s tonnage information. Do not use narrow dies or force a process merely to match an assumed drawing radius.

## Avoiding assumptions about sharp bends

The article cautions that a punch with a very small tip radius can alter the bend behavior, particularly when used with thicker material. Rather than producing a predictable air-bent radius, concentrated loading may create a sharp crease or otherwise change material flow at the bend line. Such behavior can make flat-pattern calculations less reliable and may increase the risk of defects.

Punch selection should therefore be evaluated together with material thickness, required radius, die opening, and the intended bending method. Any determination of whether a bend is “sharp” should follow the applicable tooling supplier guidance, material data, and qualified process validation rather than a single generalized threshold.

## A controlled validation workflow

A practical workflow is to:

1. Identify the exact material grade, thickness, condition, and relevant rolling direction.
2. Review the specified radius and compare it with documented bendability limits for that material condition.
3. Select candidate tooling based on thickness, flange geometry, required bend angle, and machine/tooling capacity.
4. Estimate the expected air-bent radius from the selected die opening, recognizing that the estimate must be verified.
5. Confirm that the predicted setup is compatible with material bendability and approved tonnage limits.
6. Form and inspect representative test parts using approved operating procedures.
7. Record verified inside radius, bend deduction or bend allowance, springback compensation, tooling combination, material identification, and relevant part results.

A shop-specific record of validated combinations can be more useful than relying on generic charts alone. It should be maintained through controlled trials and inspection, with machine programming, setup, and any tooling intervention performed by qualified personnel in accordance with the machine manual and established safety procedures.
