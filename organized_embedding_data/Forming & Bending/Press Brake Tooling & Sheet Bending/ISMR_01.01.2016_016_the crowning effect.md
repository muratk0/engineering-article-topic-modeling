# THE CROWNING EFFECT

[TARİH: 01.01.2016 ISMR]

By Martino Barbon, Marketing Manager, Gasparini Industries

There are four characteristics of sheet metal, says Gasparini, that press brake operators need to know

One of the unavoidable side effects of using press brakes is frame deformation. Bending steel with steel causes deflection. The ram, in particular, will bow in the centre. At that point, the punch will be further away from the die and the resulting angle will be wider. The bent profile will therefore have a boat-like shape. Even a small difference can turn metalforming into a process full of pitfalls.......

This deformation is compensated for with crowning - the table is pushed upwards to keep the die at a constant distance from the ram. There are two types of crowning - the first uses a series of wedges to raise the die mechanically and the second uses short-stroke hydraulic cylinders embedded into the bench.

But how much crowning is just right? Most manufacturers rely upon tables that have been calculated based upon the press brake structure and the sheet metal characteristics as confirmed by the foundry. However, we believe that this method does not work because sheet metal’s behaviour is unpredictable. It has high variability that depends upon a series of factors. Knowing them and, when possible, compensating for them is essential so as not to waste time and material in tests and samples. As batches today are so much smaller, getting the angle right first time becomes fundamental.

## Not all steels are created equal

What does A36 steel stand for? Sheet metal is labelled according to its yield strength. This value is highly variable and depends upon impurities in the casting and imperfections in production techniques. To avoid the risk of building weak structures, regulations require manufacturers to declare the minimum strength. So, any steel with a yield strength higher than 36,000psi will be labelled as A36.

As a result, 41,000-psi strong steel will still be sold as A36, despite being 13% harder. The increased resistance will require a higher force on the press brake, which in turn will cause the ram to bow more. For instance, switching from a 36,000- to a 41,000psi steel causes a deformation of roughly 0.002” in depth. Yet, this small curve causes a difference of almost 1° a 0.3” V-die.

## You can't win them ‘hole’

Labelling data on the steel that we buy, which may or may not be reliable, becomes completely worthless when we modify the sheet. Holes change strength along the bending line when thermally cutting, drilling or milling. And if, on the same piece, there are some bends on full parts and others on drilled parts, predictions will not help. Punched holes, on the other hand, also introduce internal stresses that make it even more difficult to consider crowning as a fixed level of deformation.

## Conditions matter

Steel is a living thing. Rolling directions create fibres in the structure, so bending across or along the grain will affect both the required bending force and the springback.

A recently pickled sheet is harder than an aged or oxidised one, even after just a few weeks. Moreover, bending a hot plate is not the same thing as bending an ice-cold one. Temperature has an influence on forming conditions and may lead to different results.

Even dimensional figures are extremely variable, especially thicknesses. For example, the EN 10051:1991+A1:1997 regulation divides sheet metal into five groups. Let’s consider steel of roughly between 37,000 and 49,000psi (260÷340MPa), up to 0.1” (2mm), between 4 and 5 feet wide (1201÷1500mm):

## Measure twice, cut once

While shearing alters the grain in sheet metal, plasma, laser and oxyfuel cutting all generate localised thermal shocks on sheet edges and hole perimeters that cannot be ignored.

For these reasons, a good press brake operator cannot and must not rely upon any type of database. We also believe that there is no algorithm able to precisely predict sheet metal reactions. Steel does not behave as a perfect and unchangeable material. If sheet metal forming is not perfect, welding becomes more difficult, painting gets harder and joining becomes a tough job, especially when tolerances are close. Material waste and working hours skyrocket.

The four factors above can drastically reduce company margins if they are ignored instead of faced. Press brake software and operators must fully understand sheet metal characteristics and be able to react to changes and adapt to them to guarantee the best bending results.

We believe that the only way to manage crowning is to use a system that measures the actual deformation and corrects it in real-time. With this technology, an operator can be sure that, regardless of the material characteristics, the results will be those that the job requires ■
