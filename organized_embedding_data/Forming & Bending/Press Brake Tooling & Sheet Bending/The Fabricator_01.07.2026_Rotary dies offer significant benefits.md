# Rotary dies offer significant benefits

Bending Basics

But they can’t defy physics

STEVE BENSON

T

here is a moment in rotary bending that most operators never see. Up to a point, the winged die behaves like a well-tuned mechanism. The material rolls into the bend. Contact moves. Friction drops. The process feels smoother than conventional forming (often quieter too), as if the tooling is helping the metal along during bending rather than forcing it to submit.

Then, something changes. The motion slows. The rotation fades. And without any obvious warning, the system transitions from rolling assistance to something much more familiar: force-driven bending.

That moment matters because that is where many misunderstand rotary tooling (see

Figure 1

). They know what it does early in the bend. They appreciate the reduced marking, the cleaner surfaces, and the smoother material flow. But they often assume that what is true at the beginning of the stroke stays true all the way through the bend, and that is incorrect.

At some point, rotary forming stops helping as much, and the punch begins to take over. Once you understand where that happens and why, you stop treating the process like magic and start treating it like mechanics.

TRANSITION DURING FORMING

Rotary dies are often described as tools that "roll the bend into place." That description is accurate, but only for part of the process. Early in the bend, the wings rotate freely. The contact point moves with the material, friction is reduced, and surface finish improves. The material is not dragged across a fixed shoulder, as it would be in a standard V die (see

Figure 2

). Instead, the rolling action guides the material through the forming zone. That is where rotary tooling shines.

But that motion is not constant, and it is not unlimited. It depends on geometry, and geometry changes with every degree of bend angle. As the bend progresses, the material wraps deeper around the punch, and the tangent points shift. The effective leverage acting on the wings changes, and the mechanical advantage begins to fade. The wings may continue to move, but their influence over the bend diminishes as the punch takes more control of the forming action.

Eventually, the system reaches a point at which the rotation contributes very little to the final shape. At that stage, the center of rotation (CoR) remains fixed at the wing pivots, but the radius center (RC) is now governed primarily by the punch and the material geometry wrapped around it. In practice, the bend has shifted from rotary-assisted bending to punch-dominated forming. This is not a tooling failure. It is simply the limit of what rotation can do (see

Figures 3

and

4

).

Operators do not usually see that transition directly, but they often feel it through the machine. As the bend begins to stiffen, the smooth, almost easy feel of the early stroke changes. Tonnage begins to climb more rapidly, and springback may become more noticeable. Surface pressure rises as contact becomes more localized. This is where a wrong assumption can send an operator or programmer in the wrong direction.

If the operator expects the bend to remain in that low-friction, low-resistance rotary mode throughout the stroke, the later increase in force feels like a problem. The tool may get blamed; the setup may get second-guessed. Someone may decide whether the material changed or the brake is acting up. Sometimes the machine is fine, the material is fine, and the tooling is doing exactly what it should. The process has simply crossed the point where rotation is no longer carrying as much of the load.

That distinction matters because rotary dies reduce friction—but they do not eliminate the need for force. Early in the bend, force is distributed along a moving contact zone, lowering friction and making material flow easier. Later in the bend, as contact becomes more localized and pressure rises, the material resists deformation more strongly. Once the wings lose enough mechanical advantage, the punch becomes the dominant forming element, and the bend begins to behave much more like a conventional forming operation.

I need to stress something here: This is one of several tonnage calculation formulas that I use. It is not the only method in the industry, and it is not presented as the one and only official way to estimate forming load. Different machine builders, tooling suppliers, and shops use variations with different constants and different correction factors. This version is simply a reliable and practical baseline that works well for estimating and for comparison.

For the baseline material, I use A36 mild steel. It is common, familiar to most shops, and serves well as a practical reference material. Is every piece of A36 identical? Of course not. Real material varies. But for estimating purposes, A36 gives us a baseline that most understand.

Using that baseline, the constant 575 provides a useful starting point for conventional air-forming tonnage calculations. Tonnage rises with the square of material thickness and decreases as the die opening gets larger. That relationship alone explains why a small change in thickness can dramatically increase the required load.

The material multiplier adjusts for differences in strength and formability. Typical shop multipliers might include A36 mild steel at 1.00, hot-rolled mild steel in the 0.90 to 1.00 range, 304 stainless at about 1.50, 301 full-hard stainless roughly between 2.00 and 2.50, 5052-H32 aluminum at about 0.50, and 6061-T6 aluminum around 1.00.

Those values are estimates, not commandments carved in stone. Actual tonnage will still vary with tensile strength, yield strength, temper, grain direction, bend length, tool condition, punch radius, machine deflection, speed, and a handful of other variables that never seem to send a holiday card before they show up and complicate your day. That is why the multiplier table is a guide, not a guarantee. When you need more precise data, you can turn to a resource such as

MatWeb.com

. It’s an excellent resource for material data, including tensile strength, yield strength, and other mechanical properties.

Now, let’s compare that standard V-die approach to rotary tooling. Rotary dies change how force is applied, but they do not repeal the laws of plastic deformation. Early in the bend, rolling contact reduces friction and can make the bend feel easier. The apparent load may be lower, and the machine may sound different. The tooling may mark the material less. All of that is real.

But as the bend progresses and the wings lose mechanical advantage, the benefit of rotation decreases. The punch begins to dominate the forming action. Once that happens, the required tonnage trends back toward conventional air-forming levels.

That is the point many people miss. They calculate or estimate the load based on what the tool feels like at the beginning of the bend rather than what the process requires at the end of it. That can lead to undersized assumptions, poor planning, or confusion when the machine suddenly seems to be working harder late in the stroke.

The safest practical rule is straightforward: For machine sizing and job planning, calculate the bend using standard air-forming tonnage as your baseline unless the tooling manufacturer provides validated data showing otherwise. In other words, use the conventional formula conservatively. Let rotary tooling be a benefit, not a gamble.

Here is a simple example. Suppose you are forming 0.125-in.-thick A36 using a 1.000-in. effective die-opening equivalent. Using the formula above, the tonnage estimate works out to about 8.98 tons per foot before any special corrections. In a rotary setup, the early portion of the bend may feel easier because friction is reduced, but the machine still needs enough capacity to finish the bend when the punch becomes dominant. If you size the job as if the low-friction feel of the first part of the stroke applies all the way through, you are setting yourself up for trouble.

The same logic applies to stainless, only with less forgiveness. Increase the multiplier, and the cost of a bad assumption rises fast. This is one reason why rotary dies should not be thought of as miracle tooling. They are excellent tools that reduce marking, improve surface finish, and help material flow. They can handle short flanges and sensitive surfaces with advantages a standard V die often cannot match. But they are still tools operating inside the same physical universe as every other forming operation in the shop. Once the geometry shifts beyond the point where rotation is helping, the bend is governed by the same basic mechanics you deal with everywhere else: material strength, geometry, contact pressure, and force.

MORE TO COME

Understanding where that transition occurs changes how you approach setup, planning, troubleshooting, and expectations. It tells you why the bend feels different late in the stroke. It tells you why the machine must still be sized conservatively. It tells you why the rolling action at the beginning of the bend does not grant immunity from tonnage requirements later on. Most important, it separates controlled forming from guesswork. Once you know where rotation stops helping and force takes over, you know when the real work begins.
