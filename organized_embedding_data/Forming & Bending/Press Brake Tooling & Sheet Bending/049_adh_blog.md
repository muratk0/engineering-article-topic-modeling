## Purpose and scope

Panel benders can combine automated clamping, positioning, handling, and folding movements. Their specific architecture varies by machine, but the safety principle is consistent: personal protective equipment (PPE) may help reduce exposure to hazards from sheet material, such as sharp edges or small dropped items, but it does not protect a person from crushing, trapping, or impact hazards created by powered machine motion.

Operators should follow the machine manual, site risk assessment, applicable safety procedures, and instructions from qualified personnel.

## Recognize the moving work envelope

During an automatic cycle, components such as clamps, folding tools, backgauges, manipulators, material holders, and associated handling equipment may move. A workpiece can also move unexpectedly as it is clamped, rotated, folded, released, or distorted. Large or partially formed sheets may sag, swing, buckle, or whip as the process changes their shape.

The hazardous area therefore includes more than the most obvious tooling pinch point. It can include the path of the sheet, moving axes, material-handling devices, and nearby linked equipment. Remain outside the designated work envelope while the machine is capable of automatic motion. Do not reach in to retrieve scrap, film, parts, or tools during a cycle.

Safeguards such as guards, interlocked doors, safety scanners, mats, and light curtains are important protective measures, but they are not a substitute for safe access control. Their coverage and behavior depend on the machine design and installation. Any damaged, obstructed, unreliable, or suspected-bypassed safeguard should be treated as a reason to stop operation and escalate the issue.

## Distinguish a stopped machine from an isolated machine

A pause, feed hold, normal stop, or emergency stop may halt motion, but it does not by itself establish that a machine is safe for entry or intervention. Controls can leave the machine in a state where it can resume after a reset, a changed sensor condition, or another command. Stored or residual energy may also remain after visible motion stops.

Potential energy sources can include electrical supplies, pneumatic pressure, hydraulic pressure, gravity, servo-held loads, vacuum systems, connected automation, and force stored in a bent or jammed sheet. The relevant sources depend on the equipment and cell configuration.

Before anyone enters a hazardous area, clears a jam, performs maintenance, or carries out another task requiring exposure to hazardous motion, use the energy-control procedure specified by the machine manual, site procedures, and local requirements. This may require lockout/tagout and verification that hazardous energy has been controlled. Only trained and authorized personnel should carry out energy isolation and verification.

Where several people or connected machines are involved, energy control must account for everyone exposed and for all relevant linked equipment. A lockout applied only to one machine may not control hazards from material loaders, robots, conveyors, or other integrated systems.

## Verify setup before automatic production

Machine programs and setup data affect physical motion. Before production, confirm that the selected program and its revision correspond to the intended job, and compare relevant setup information with the actual material, tooling, and machine configuration. Follow the manufacturer-approved process for proving out new, revised, or recovered programs.

Use only the available machine functions and procedures authorized for the task. A staged check may include reviewing the programmed sequence, observing approved simulation or test functions, and conducting an authorized first-run process. Stop if tool clearance, workpiece path, motion sequence, sounds, vibration, or other behavior differs from expectations.

Do not assume that a program copied from another job remains suitable after changes to material, dimensions, tooling, bend order, or setup conditions. A mismatch between digital instructions and physical conditions can create collision, ejection, distortion, or trapping hazards.

## Respond safely to faults and jams

A fault, collision, bad bend, buckled sheet, or jam can create an abnormal hazard state. The machine may still hold position, retain energy, or contain a stressed workpiece even if it appears quiet or displays an alarm.

Do not attempt to pull, pry, reposition, or otherwise remove jammed material while hazardous energy or motion remains possible. Do not bypass guards, interlocks, sensors, or safety functions to restore production. Recovery actions must follow the approved machine-specific procedure and may require qualified maintenance personnel.

After a fault or collision, do not assume the next cycle is safe. The condition of guarding, tooling, clamps, sensors, reference positions, workpiece location, and program state may have changed. Inspect and restore the equipment only through authorized procedures. If abnormal noise, vibration, inconsistent motion, damaged guarding, unreliable sensing, or other unexplained behavior is present, keep the equipment out of service and report it.

## Define operator limits and communicate conditions

Operators should perform only adjustments and tasks explicitly authorized by their training and the machine documentation. Electrical, hydraulic, pneumatic, mechanical, sensor, control-system, or safeguarding repairs require qualified personnel and approved procedures.

At shift changes and after faults, communicate the machine condition clearly. Record relevant alarms, observed symptoms, program context, and any approved setup changes. This helps prevent the next operator from restarting equipment with an unresolved hazard.

## Routine safety principles

Before use, review handover information; check that the area is clear; verify guards and access controls are in their intended condition; and consider hazards from connected equipment. During production, remain outside the work envelope and stop for abnormal behavior. Before any intervention requiring access to hazardous areas, use the required energy-control process rather than relying on a temporary stop or software state.

A safe operating approach prioritizes verification, controlled access, prompt stopping when conditions change, and escalation when the cause of a problem is not understood.
