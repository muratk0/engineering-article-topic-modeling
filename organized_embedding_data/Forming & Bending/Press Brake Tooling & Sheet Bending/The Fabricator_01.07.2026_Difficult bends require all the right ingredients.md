# Difficult bends require all the right ingredients

[TARİH: 01.07.2026 The Fabricator]

Tube Bending

Like a great recipe, the right machines, materials, tooling, and lubricants all must converge at the same time

By

Lincoln Brunner

Bends are increasingly becoming more complex with tighter bend radii, thanks to a surge in aerospace customer demands.

AMOB

T

hink of your favorite dish.

Maybe it’s that can’t-miss chicken parmesan from that little Italian place downtown. They make it so well every time, it’s easy to forget that if any one piece goes wrong, the whole thing’s ruined. The oven, the temperature, following the old recipe, all the main ingredients, the spices added in just the right amounts—all those elements have to come together at just the right time for that chicken parm to arrive at your table just the way you remember it.

The same holds true for fabricating tubes with difficult bend radii, especially when you’re talking about exotic materials bent at radii of 1.5 times the OD (1.5D) or even less. Those parts almost always require some kind of mandrel. (More on that in a bit.)

Not so long ago, bend angles of 1.5D to 2D were considered difficult. Nowadays, 1.5D is so common, 1D bends have become the new bar for that category.

In fact, most tube bends with anything from 1.5D to 6D can be achieved with just rotary draw bending, said Marius Horsch of Germany-based tube bending machine manufacturer Schwarze-Robitec.

"Material is important, but also the ratio between the outside diameter, wall thickness, and the respective bending radius," Horsch said. "For easier ratios, we can just bend it without a mandrel. For more critical ones, we [use] a spoon mandrel, and for [yet] more critical ones, we have ball mandrels, and the number of balls is then dependent on the ratio."

The new norms in large part have been brought about by the recent sharp increase in aerospace applications that require tubes—typically titanium or difficult-to-form alloys such as INCONEL—to be bent into increasingly difficult shapes to accommodate ever-tighter physical envelopes.

The new demands on benders and the material restrictions put all the more onus on fabricators to have the right tools and tool setup, machine rigidity, lubricants, and mandrels (again, ball mandrels for extremely tight radii). Fabricators also need to know the idiosyncrasies of difficult materials and bending scenarios and how to combine all the ingredients successfully on a tight deadline.

"To bend materials like titanium at the tight radius of just 1xD, they have to remember basically everything," said Manuel António Barros, part owner and operations manager of Portugal-based tube bending machine manufacturer AMOB, who used the cooking analogy to describe the process. "They [fabricators] have to have the best machines and tools and lubricants at their disposal, because let’s just face it—you will not be able to bend an exotic material at a really tight radius unless you have everything together.

"Sometimes these tubes are very, very thin-walled, which complicates things even more. You either have the best of everything, or if you are missing just one of these specific requirements, you won’t be able to have it at all."

KNOW YOUR MATERIAL

Assembling all the right ingredients for a good recipe also requires knowing what basic ingredients you’re working with. In the case of tubing, that means knowing the heat lot (or number) of the material as it came out of the mill. That allows you to find the correct material test report, which will tell you what you need to know about the physical properties of the actual tubes you are bending.

Bending parts that don’t end up in the scrap bin means "looking at the actual material spec by heat lot of your material, which is really commonly missed, even with non-exotic materials," said Jay Robinson, a columnist for TPJ who runs his own tube bending machine sales and repair business, RbSA Industrial. "You’ve got a purchasing guy that goes and buys the cheapest material, and then you find out that the material won’t stretch, it won’t form, because they’ve bought something different from what their norm is.

"In the material spec by heat lot, especially in exotics, there’s typically going to be an elongation test that tells you how much you can expect the material to stretch as a percentage. And then you can do the math in the bends to figure out what the expected amount of required stretch is, and if those two numbers don’t jibe, then you’re probably going to have a problem."

Those numbers also will tell you if you can get away with compensating for a discrepancy in the bending process itself by, say, pushing more material into the outside radius of a tube with a follower pressure die.

Robinson’s rule of thumb is to live within 15% of the spec listed for a particular material. For example, if a certain tube has a 20% elongation value, a shop could probably be safe to assume that it could get 15% of that value in addition to the 20%—or 23% total elongation—before breakage occurs.

But don’t push it.

"If you’re talking about titanium, you’re talking about very brittle material," Robinson said. "So, as an operator or an engineer, you might throw away that extra 15%. Generally, you want the elongation that’s required for your bend to be the same or less than what the material spec is, whether it’s aluminum or titanium or INCONEL. It doesn’t matter. There should be elongation specs from the mill. And it should be by heat lot, especially in those exotics, because those are going to come with manufacturing certificates and everything."

Speed is another important factor.

Barros noted that stainless steel actually likes speed because it heats up during fast bending, allowing it to stretch a bit more than it would otherwise. Others, especially titanium, play by different rules.

In fact, one customer asked one of Barros’ employees at a trade show years ago how slowly an AMOB machine could bend his titanium tubes. While some material is so soft that it can bend 30 degrees per second, this particular customer—a maker of exhaust systems for cars—said he wanted to keep the bending speed below 3 to 4 degrees per second for his titanium tubes.

"Titanium has a really, really low percentage of elongation—it will snap. It doesn’t like to be stretched," Barros said. "So, when you are bending titanium, you need a lot of torsional rigidity on the bending edge of the machine, and you actually need to bend it very slow. If you try to bend it with speed, you will not give the material time to allow its fibers to stretch, and you will break it."

MANDRELS ARE THE MAINSTAY

Virtually all difficult-radius bends require mandrels to support the ID of the tube in the bend zone—either a spoon or a ball variety, depending on the severity of the bend.

With 1.5D to 2D bends, a spoon mandrel (shaped like a bar with connected slices) placed within the tube at the front end of the bend is adequate. However, with bends that are 1.5D and below, ball mandrels have become increasingly common because they can support the tube throughout the entire length of the bend.

The balls are connected by links, which must be strong enough to hold the mandrel together while undergoing tremendous torque from the bend tooling but also flexible enough to provide support throughout the bend.

Barros said he has started to see four, five, or even six balls used regularly to make 1D radius bends on thin-walled tubing.

"The mandrel is probably the single most important part of the tool set when it comes to achieving these very tight bending radii on exotic materials," Barros said. "The machine is important, the other parts of the toolset, like the bend die, clamp, pressure die, wiper die—everything is important. But mandrels in combination with the proper wiper die are probably two of the most important."

A WORD ABOUT LUBRICATION

The time to apply mandrels is negligible, Horsch noted. What’s far more important for operators to understand is how to properly lubricate the tubing on their machines.

Tubing used in food processing, for example, requires lubricants that are approved by the Food and Drug Administration. Certain strictures can impact bend quality, making it all the more important to understand how to conform to regulations and still produce quality bends.

"There are different suppliers, different kinds of lubrication, depending on the material—aluminum, steel, stainless," Horsch said. "There are probably hundreds of different lubrication types."

With some materials (some grades of stainless, for example), operators want friction that allows tools to hold material in place to achieve a bend, Robinson pointed out. For many others, however, preventing the tube from tearing or breaking requires that the proper lubricant for the material be applied to both the ID and the mandrel. Finding out what fits that material sometimes requires a bit of trial and error.

"If you’re struggling with lubricant, try it without; if you’re struggling without, try it with lubricant," Robinson said. "It’s not an easy answer. Some materials, as they slide against each other, will gall. The makeup of them will cause them to create problems. In some instances, we want that wiper to have a lot of friction because it causes the inside of the radius to stretch some and keep a wrinkle out of it."
