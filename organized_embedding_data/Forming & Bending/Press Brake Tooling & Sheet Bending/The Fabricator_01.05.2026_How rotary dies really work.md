# How rotary dies really work

[TARİH: 01.05.2026 The Fabricator]

Bending Basics

The center of rotation and the virtual die opening

L

ast month’s column introduced rotary die tooling, including the Rolla-V style winged dies. These tools behave very differently from the conventional V dies most press brake operators learned on and still use.

When operators use rotary dies, they need to consider two different yet similar-sounding factors: the

radius center

(RC) and the

center of rotation

(CoR). They both involve circular motion, but they describe completely different things. One belongs to the geometry of the finished part; the other belongs to the tooling mechanism.

Understanding the difference between these references is essential when working with rotary dies. Once you understand the geometry, you’ll appreciate the advantages winged dies offer, including reduced tonnage, improved surface finish, and the ability to form extremely short flanges.

To find the radius center (RC), draw (or imagine) a circle with a portion of the circumference matching the arc of the bend. The center of that circle is the RC.

RC: A PART GEOMETRY REFERENCE

Draw or imagine a circle with a portion of the circumference matching the arc of the bend’s inside surface. You’ll find the RC at the center of that circle (see

Figure 1

).

The RC exists only as a geometric reference for describing the shape of the finished bend. This reference becomes important when calculating the bend allowance and bend deduction, developing your flat lengths, and determining the neutral axis location. No matter what tooling or method of forming—air forming, bottoming, coining—the inside bend radius will still have a radius center that can be located geometrically.

The RC describes the geometry of the finished bend, but it does not tell us anything about how the bend was actually formed. To understand rotary die bending, we must instead look at the geometry of the tooling itself.

CENTER OF ROTATION: A TOOL GEOMETRY REFERENCE

Winged rotary dies introduce a geometric reference that does not exist in conventional V dies: the CoR. Each rotor inside a winged die pivots around a fixed axis within the die body. That pivot axis is the CoR (see

Figure 2

). Unlike the RC, which is only a geometric construct associated with the finished bend, the CoR is a physical feature of the tool itself. It is the point around which the rotor rotates during forming.

FIGURE 2

The center of rotation (CoR) is the point around which the rotor rotates during forming.

FIGURE 3

The virtual V opening (VVO) equals the distance between the two centers of rotation.

When the press brake begins the forming stroke, the punch moves downward and the rotors begin to rotate outward about their centers. As this rotation occurs, the sheet metal remains supported by the rotor surfaces.

Because the rotor surfaces move with the sheet rather than resisting it, the sheet experiences rolling contact rather than sliding friction. This is the key mechanical difference between rotary dies and conventional V dies. Rolling contact reduces friction between tool and material; lowers forming tonnage; reduces marking of the material surface; and provides greater stability when bending very short flanges.

One of the easiest ways to keep the RC and CoR terms straight is to think about where they "live." The RC lives in the part. It exists whether the bend was made in a V die, a rotary die, or even by hand. If the finished bend has a radius, that radius has a center point, and that point is the RC. It is always tied to the geometry of the part itself. The CoR lives in the tool. It is built into the die and defines how the tooling moves during forming. Keeping this distinction clear eliminates confusion and saves a great deal of time when tackling forming challenges.

THE VIRTUAL V OPENING

Press brake controllers are designed to work with tooling that has a defined V-opening width. That dimension is used by the controller when calculating bend depth, tonnage, bend allowance, and minimum flange warnings.

Winged rotary dies do not have a traditional V opening. Instead, they use what is known as a

virtual V opening

, or VVO (see

Figure 3

). This is the distance between the two CoRs of the rotors when the die is in its home position. In the home position, the rotor flats face upward and the sheet rests directly on those flats; the punch nose touches the material, but no bending has occurred. The distance between the two rotor pivot axes at this moment becomes the equivalent opening used by the press brake controller.

HOME POSITION GEOMETRY

Understanding the die’s home position geometry explains why rotary dies behave differently from conventional V dies. With a conventional V die, the sheet must bridge a fixed opening before bending can begin. If the flange is too short to span the die shoulders, the sheet simply falls into the opening rather than forming a bend.

FIGURE 4

The bend cycle starts in the home position (top). The rotors rotate as the punch descends, maintaining continuous workpiece contact.

Winged dies operate differently. Because the sheet rests directly on the rotor flats in the home position, it is fully supported before the punch begins forming the bend (see

Figure 4

). The sheet does not bridge a wide gap. Instead, as the punch moves downward, the rotors begin rotating outward around their CoRs. As the rotors rotate, they carry the sheet along their surfaces while the punch forms the bend.

The sheet remains in contact with both the punch nose and the rotor surfaces during bending. As the punch descends, the rotors rotate outward, the sheet wraps around the punch radius, and the contact points move across the rotor surfaces. At no point during the forming cycle does the sheet lose contact with the tooling. The material is continuously supported during the forming cycle. This eliminates the sliding motion that occurs when a sheet drags across the shoulders of a conventional V die.

EFFECTIVE GEOMETRY DURING THE BEND

Although the programmed VVO remains constant, the effective support geometry changes continuously as the rotors rotate during the forming stroke. As the bend angle increases, the rotor surfaces move outward, the contact points shift along the rotor faces, and the sheet progressively wraps around the punch radius.

From a geometric standpoint, the opening, relative to the sheet, evolves during the forming process. However, the press brake controller still relies on the fixed VVO defined by the rotor spacing in the home position. Understanding this distinction helps explain why rotary dies behave differently from the assumptions built into conventional press brake calculations.

MINIMUM FLANGE CAPABILITY

All this leads to one of the most important advantages of rotary dies: They can form extremely short flanges. The sheet doesn’t span over a die opening, which means you don’t need to worry about material for a very short flange resting on the inside edge of a V die’s shoulder, then snapping uncontrollably down into the die space once the punch applies pressure.

Instead, the sheet is supported directly on the rotor flats in the home position. The material can therefore be supported very close to the rotor’s pivot axis. This makes it possible to form flanges far shorter than those possible with conventional V dies.

Applications for which this capability becomes especially valuable include narrow brackets, electronic enclosures, parts with bends located near holes or slots, and cosmetic stainless or aluminum components. In many of these cases, a rotary die can form parts that would otherwise need to be redesigned.

The advantage of winged dies lies in the relationship between the RC, the CoR, and the VVO. Together, these three references explain how rotary dies bend while maintaining continuous material support.
