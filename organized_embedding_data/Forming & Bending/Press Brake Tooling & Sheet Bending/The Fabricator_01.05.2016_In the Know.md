Plymouth Tube Receives the 2016 Rusty Demeules Award

Plymouth Tube of Streator, Ill., has been named the winner of the 2016 Rusty Demeules Award for Safety Excellence. The award was created by FMA’s Safety Council to recognize a company with a demonstrated safety culture. The award is named in honor of Rusty Demeules, a significant volunteer contributor to FMA and chair of the FMA Safety Committee from its inception in 1989 until 1996.

Plymouth Tube’s commitment to safety includes its Safety Engagement Program, which focuses on safety tours, meetings, observations, and safety suggestions from employees, and helps to generate improvements throughout the facility. Employees are also required to participate in their daily Stretch Program to help ensure employees’ minds and bodies are ready for a safe day at work. Congratulations to Plymouth Tube for meeting this standard of excellence for safety.

ALAW

– Advanced Laser Applications Workshop June 6-8

ALAW returns June 6-8 for its 23rd annual event at the Inn at St. John’s in Plymouth, Mich. This world-class industry event encompasses state-of-the-art laser processes for manufacturers, job shops, and suppliers.

Designers and product engineers will gain familiarity with the properties, advantages, and applicability of the laser welding process, and how to design components, assemblies, and systems for successful laser welding. The choice of laser type, component material selection, weld joint design, component part preparation, and part fixturing are all critical to successful implementation of the laser welding process. Numerous practical examples and case studies will help attendees build the foundation for sound laser cutting and welding design, including process implementation.

Is the World Shrinking?

For the past several years the manufacturer has heard nothing but a steady litany asserting the age of globalization had emerged to change all the assumptions. If a company was suddenly being put out of business by a foreign competitor, it was due to globalization. A desire to move operations to some low-productioncost locale? Globalization. Markets were global, competition was global, and that was all there was to it. The U.S. would become a nation that no longer made anything.

Today a shift of sorts is underway. Not that global business will reverse or globalization is over, but the relentless pace is slowing. Since the recession the U.S. has regained over a million manufacturing jobs as companies reverse the trends of the past and shift operations back to the U.S. The nearshoring movement that was only hinted at in the past is now a solid reality.

It is important to remember the U.S. never stopped being a manufacturing country. The sector still accounts for 12.1 percent of the U.S. GDP – up from11.8 percent in the depths of the recession and not all that far from the 13.1 percent prior to the recession. It is the second-largest contributor to GDP growth behind only health care. Manufacturing’s share of U.S. GDP is $2.1 trillion, and that makes U.S. manufacturing alone worth more than the GDPs of South Korea or Canada or Saudi Arabia. The peak of U.S. manufacturing as a percentage of GDP was in the 1950s when it was close to 28 percent, but today manufacturing is about 16 percent of total world GDP.

As the U.S. seems to be reversing the recent globalization trends, there are three prime motivators. The first is transportation costs. All things being equal, it is cheaper to produce in the U.S. than somewhere else and then pay to transport. The key is figuring total landed cost. The second motivator is technology, as this has allowed the U.S. manufacturer to compete with countries that have lower production costs. Finally, there is the cost of managing in unfamiliar areas with different expectations and skill levels. It is simply easier to manage in one’s own culture.

Dr. Chris Kuehl
