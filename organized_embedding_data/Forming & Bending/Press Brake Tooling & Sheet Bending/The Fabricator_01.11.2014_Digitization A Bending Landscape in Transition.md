# Digitization: A Bending Landscape in Transition

[TARİH: 01.11.2014 The Fabricator]

How press brake innovations are leading to excellence in sheet metal forming

Executive Summary

P

ress brake operation is in one sense the heart of metal fabrication, affecting the flat blank production upstream and welding downstream. It stands apart as one of the most complex, nuanced operations in metal fabrication. If you get bending right, there’s a better chance that everything else will fall into place. This study provides a glimpse of how some shops are accomplishing the feat.

Standalone press brakes still dominate the industry. However, change may be imminent as many operations make the move toward digitization and automation. The change gives manufacturers increased flexibility within the bending process, including offline bend programming, simulation, robotized bending cells, and automated tool changes.

Considering the advancements made in laser cutting, you might think the bending bottleneck is growing, hence the need for more bending throughput. But according to this study, most shops do not get bending bottlenecks from extraordinarily productive blanking machines.

In many cases, shops are moving toward digitization to tame the information beast. Technicians misinterpret a drawing, or engineers specify bends that are difficult or impossible to make with available tooling—and a bottleneck ensues. This isn’t surprising, considering the amount of information that goes into a well-executed bend program.

Thirty percent of respondents said their highest-volume forming job has fewer than 100 pieces, with the typical job mix having a significant amount of operational variability. Most shops indicated that they perform a mix of new jobs, repeat orders, and one-offs.

At an overwhelming majority of shops, technicians program new jobs at the press brake itself, but the business is changing. Almost half said their newest press brake is less than 5 years old. The setups are getting more complex, too. Many said they perform staged bending at their newest press brakes. Instead of using two or more machines to finish a part, technicians can get a job done at one machine. Although the majority do not program and simulate press brake operations offline, many have plans to adopt the technology moving forward.

Almost a quarter of respondents said their most senior press brake operator had more than 20 years of experience—no surprises there. But one answer regarding experience may be this study’s most significant: 30 percent said their most seasoned operators had

less than 10 years

on the job. This shows an industry in transition. The less experienced may have caught the leading edge of a gradual change toward digitization.

Also noteworthy is the fact that nearly a third of a shop’s least experienced workers have less than a year of bending under their belt. Further, they may not have learned the trade from operators with decades of experience.

Digitization has become a powerful tool for experienced bending technicians. At the same time, such technology has allowed press brake operators with less experience to function at a higher level than ever before.

Still both a science and craft

Danny Brown

Head Programmer

S&B Machine, Mobile, Ala.

Alabama native Danny Brown recalls his early days on the job at his family’s sheet metal shop, Mobile Sheet Metal, starting in the early 1990s sweeping floors. He remembers working with old press brakes, performing flat layout manually, and writing out bend calculations with pencil in hand. “I’ve run everything from old mechanicals to precision hydraulics,” Brown said.

He learned to visualize everything, how one bend after another contributed to a formed component. It was part craft, part science. With the right bend deductions and the right die opening, he air-bent a component to spec on the first try. That was the science. He had to tweak and adjust, thanks to ever-so-slight material strength and thickness variations, not to mention the fact that the available die opening wasn’t always perfect for the job. That was the craft.

In January Brown’s family business merged with S&B Machine, a Mobile machine shop that happened to be one of the Browns’ largest customers. The merged company has 35 employees, and Brown is head programmer. He still operates machines, but now much of the process is automated. Brown unfolds the 3-D CAD file and runs it through simulation software, which offers various bend sequence options. He then saves the programs, which then can be downloaded to the machines.

Changeovers, from the last good part of the previous job to the first good part of the next, take just minutes. Several years ago a job may have required setups on two separate brakes; today offline simulation makes it practical for technicians to use one complicated staged-bending arrangement that can form a complex part in one setup, freeing up another brake for other jobs. That’s the science.

“With offline programming and simulation, we know beforehand, during quoting, whether or not we can do a job with available tooling,” Brown said. “We run the job through the simulation.”

So where’s the craft? It’s still there, Brown said, but it entails fitting enhanced bending with overall part flow. Bend technicians still visualize, and as Brown explained, being able to visualize the unbent flat still has tremendous value, especially when double-checking blank sizes.

But with so much of the actual bend calculations and setups automated, technicians have the luxury of optimizing the whole of fabrication: blanking to bending to welding. How will this change in bending affect welding? How about inserting tabs into a part to create a self-fixturing subassembly? Better fixturing has in turn increased the company’s use of robotic welding.

“When I started as an operator, it was all about the angle in front of you,” Brown said. “I never would have dreamed that I could work with a part and see it all the way through the process. I now get to focus on developing a better product.”

The craft remains; the perspective is just broader, and the possibilities are greater.

Training future industry leaders

Kenny Holley

Engineering Project Manager

Precision Machine Fabrication,

Raleigh, N.C.

Kenny Holley attended technical college to become a tool and die maker, but when in the early 1980s he landed a job at Precision Machine Fabrication (PMF), he saw the flexible-tool revolution firsthand. Instead of spending money for a hard stamping press tool, more manufacturers were turning to the turret punch and press brake. “Flex-fab is what people called it back then,” he said.

As a young operator just getting into flex-fab, Holley spent a lot of time bending the same part over and over and over. “I used to hate production work, just standing there, bending two-bend parts all day. It just didn’t interest me at all. Back then, we’d get a job to run one part for a customer, and we’d run thousands of them.”

Now customers want a little at a time, but very quickly. And modern controls and offline programming have helped the shop meet that diverse demand, forming a little bit of everything all the time. Holley chuckled. “We kid now and say that one piece is a prototype job and two pieces are a production job.”

After leaving for a few years in the1980s to work at a local startup, Holley returned to PMF as a project manager, and there he has remained for more than 25 years. Today, as engineering project manager, he shepherds jobs through the shop, assisting with new product launches. An experienced brake technician, Bending Specialist Dwayne McKnight performs offline bend programming and simulation.

New press brake operators still need mechanical aptitude, Holley said. “If a person has the mechanical aptitude to understand how to bend parts, to understand the elongation and compression, the bend allowances and bend deductions, then they can do anything in a metal fab shop.”

When training newbies, Holley can sense if someone has that aptitude, because they can see the logic behind bending.

“They see the simulation at their controller, and they see how the bending sequences come together,” Holley said. “You can really tell the ones who get it. It’s still about visualizing the operation. The ones who visualize anticipate what will happen next. They get involved in choosing their own tooling, and they talk with Dwayne about different strategies.”

When the 3-D simulation on the controller shows the operator how to rotate a part between bends, those with mechanical aptitude almost intuitively rotate the part as shown, without trouble, then proceed to the next bend. That simple spark may be the first of many in a long career in metal fabrication.

Holley conceded that this spark isn’t common these days. Still, technology has made those few with technical aptitude even more productive. “Dwayne can visualize what happens, he sees what happens in the simulation, and he knows how to make the correct tooling choices,” Holley said. “He basically does the setup ahead of time, before the operator does anything at the machine, and that has made the floor much more efficient.”

Technology has not eliminated the need for the experienced bending professional, but it has made these individuals more valuable than ever. They spend less time just standing and bending part after identical part, and more time doing and thinking, seeing dozens of different bend sequences a day. Some work their way up the ladder to manage and simulate the bend programs themselves.

That’s not a bad way to train a future industry leader.

From creative thinking springs opportunity

Son Le

Director of Operations

L&T Precision, Poway, Calif.

When Son Le immigrated to the U.S. from Vietnam in 1989, he had never seen a press brake. He attended accounting school and worked at a gas station and convenience store. He also knew another Vietnam native, R. L. Nguyen, who had launched a small sheet metal operation five years before.

Previously Nguyen was a sheet metal department supervisor at a transformer manufacturer, and when the plant moved, he left to found his own fab shop, L&T Precision, which today employs more than 100 people in its 48,000-square-foot plant. Le started in 1990 as a part-timer, learning to shear, deburr, punch—essentially every fabrication operation on what was then a small shop floor.

Before L&T, Le had no knowledge of sheet metal; he was attending accounting school during the day, after all. But early on his managers saw talent. They taught him the basics of press brake setup, including punch and die placement and alignment, then the programming.

Le probably would be in a very different place if his supervisors at L&T hadn’t introduced him to the metal fabrication craft, especially bending. Indeed, the skilled-labor problem in this country might look a lot different if more youth had a chance to at least see and perhaps try metal fabrication, or for that matter any other manufacturing process. Le could have spent his life in a cubicle in a corporate accounting department somewhere, but instead he built a life making things of value.

Today Le is director of operations over a shop floor full of eye-catching technology, including lasers and punches as well as fully automated bending cells, but the act of bending—of forming a flat blank into a useful object—remains his favorite aspect of fabrication, and much of that has to do not with mechanical wizardry and automation. It has to do with something that’s much more human.

“It requires a lot of creative thinking,” Le said. “You need to figure out which flange to bend first. You need to foresee the priority of the bends, and you need to keep thinking how you can bend it faster and easier. You need to think about how to cut [the blank], and how easy the form will be to weld.”

Such thinking probably helped Le get to where he is today.
