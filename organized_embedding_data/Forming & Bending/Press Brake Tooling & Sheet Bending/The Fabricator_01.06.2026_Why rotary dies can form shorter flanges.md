MINIMUM FLANGE LIMITS IN CONVENTIONAL V-DIES

In a typical air bending setup, the sheet metal rests on two die shoulders separated by a fixed V opening. As the punch descends, the sheet is forced downward between those shoulders, and the bend begins to form. Before the bend can occur, however, the sheet must bridge the opening between the die shoulders. If the flange is too short to span that opening, the sheet simply falls into the die rather than forming a bend.

This geometry requires a minimum flange length. A commonly used shop rule is:

Minimum flange = V opening/2.

The required flange length may be slightly larger depending on the inside bend radius and material thickness. Regardless, the fundamental limitation remains the same: The sheet must be able to span the die opening before bending can begin. This restriction often forces engineers to redesign parts when bends are located near edges, holes, or narrow tabs.

SUPPORT GEOMETRY IN ROTARY DIES

Rotary dies eliminate that limitation because they operate using a completely different support geometry. Unlike a conventional die, rotary dies do not require material to span a wide gap before the forming stroke begins.

Instead of resting on two fixed die shoulders, the sheet metal sits on two rotating rotor surfaces. When the die is in its home position, the rotor flats face upward and the sheet rests directly on those flats. When the punch nose touches the material, the sheet is fully supported, yet bending hasn’t commenced quite yet. This simple difference dramatically changes the flange limitation (see Figures 2 and 3).

THE ROLE OF THE CENTER OF ROTATION

Each rotor pivots about the CoR. When the punch begins the forming stroke, the punch pushes downward on the sheet metal, and the rotors begin to rotate outward around their centers. During this motion, the sheet remains supported by the rotor surfaces.

Instead of sliding across stationary shoulders, the sheet is effectively carried by the rotating surfaces as the bend forms. Because the sheet remains supported throughout the forming process, it can be located much closer to the rotor’s pivot point than with conventional tooling. This is the fundamental reason rotary dies allow extremely short flanges.

CONTINUOUS CONTACT DURING FORMING

When using a rotary die, you achieve continuous contact between the sheet metal and the tooling. During the forming stroke, the sheet remains in contact with the punch nose and the rotor surfaces. As the punch moves downward, the rotors rotate outward, and the sheet wraps around the punch radius. At no point does the sheet lose support.

This continuous contact eliminates the sliding motion that occurs in conventional air bending. When a sheet slides across the die shoulders in a conventional setup, friction increases, and the material may momentarily lose stable support. This instability becomes especially problematic when attempting to form short flanges. Rotary dies replace the sliding motion with rolling contact, greatly improving stability during bending.

THE IMPORTANCE OF SUPPORT

Because the sheet begins fully supported on the rotor flats, the forming process starts from a stable condition. As the punch descends, the rotors rotate, carrying the material with them. The sheet does not need to "find" support during the bend. It already has it. This difference may seem subtle, but it has a direct impact on what is possible in production.

Short flanges are not just about clearance. They are a matter of stability. When the material remains supported throughout the forming cycle, the bend can be initiated and controlled much closer to the edge of the part. This is why rotary dies can often form features that would otherwise require redesign in conventional tooling. The geometry does not violate any bending rules. It simply changes the way the material is supported while those rules are applied.

MINIMUM FLANGE CAPABILITY

Because the sheet rests directly on the rotor flats before bending begins, the effective support point is located much closer to the bend line. As a result, rotary dies can form flanges far shorter than those possible with conventional V dies. You can consult your tooling supplier for data that applies to the rotary dies you’re using.

Short-flange capability becomes particularly valuable in several common fabrication situations. When a bend occurs close to a hole or slot, the surrounding material can distort during forming. Because rotary dies support the sheet throughout the bending process, hole deformation is often reduced.

You can find similar benefits when forming narrow structural tabs. Mounting tabs and small brackets frequently require bends close to the edge of the part. Using conventional dies may require redesigning the part to increase flange length. Rotary dies can often form these features without modification.

COSMETICS, FRICTION REDUCTION, AND TONNAGE

Many industries require bending polished stainless steel or prefinished aluminum panels. The rolling contact produced by rotary dies reduces sliding friction and helps prevent surface marking. The rolling contact also reduces the forming force. In conventional air bending, a portion of the press brake’s tonnage is consumed by friction as the sheet slides across the die shoulders. When sliding friction is replaced by rolling contact, this resistance decreases.

Many shops report tonnage reductions in the range of 15% to 25%, depending on material properties and tooling geometry. Lower forming forces can extend tool life and reduce wear on the press brake.

PROGRAMMING CONSIDERATIONS

Even though rotary dies operate differently during forming, press brake controllers still require a die opening value when calculating bend depth and tonnage. This is where the VVO becomes important. The VVO is the distance between the two CoRs when the die is in its home position.

The controller treats the VVO exactly as if it were the opening of a conventional V die. Although the rotors move during forming, the programmed VVO remains constant.
