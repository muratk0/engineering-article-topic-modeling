# A deep dive into the rotary die

[TARİH: 01.04.2026 The Fabricator]

Bending Basics

DIt’s not just a kinder, gentler V die

R

otary dies are often described as

nonmarking

V dies. That description is convenient and wrong. A rotary die is not simply a kinder, gentler version of a conventional V die. It is a fundamentally different forming system built around motion rather than friction.

If you approach a rotary die with the mindset you use for a standard V die, you will misunderstand what it is doing, why it works, and where its limits actually lie.

SLIDING VERSUS ROTATING

A conventional V die bends by forcing the sheet to slide across two fixed shoulders (see

Figure 1

). As the punch descends, the material is dragged under high compressive load across the die radii. Sliding under pressure creates friction. Friction requires tonnage. Tonnage combined with sliding produces those familiar shiny streaks along the bend line. For years, many operators accepted that as simply part of the process.

FIGURE 1 When forming using a conventional V die, the part is dragged over the two fixed shoulders, resulting in significant friction and higher tonnage requirements to bend the metal.

tdhster/iStock/Getty Images Plus

Instead of two stationary shoulders, a rotary support die contains two hardened inserts mounted on pivot shafts. As the punch drives the sheet downward, the inserts rotate inward. The sheet remains in rolling contact with the insert surface throughout the forming stroke (see

Figure 2

).

FIGURE 2 Rotary dies have two rotary inserts that roll during forming, leaving no area of the bend unsupported.

ZhakYaroslavPhoto/iStock/GettyImages Plus

That rolling contact changes everything. When the sheet rolls instead of sliding, friction is dramatically reduced. That reduction alone can improve the surface finish on stainless steel, painted sheet, or decorative parts. However, finish quality is only the visible result.

GEOMETRIC SUPPORT

A conventional V die leaves a void beneath the bend line. When a hole or slot is positioned near the bend line, the material between the feature and the die shoulder is only partially supported. As the sheet elongates during forming, that unsupported region tends to collapse into the opening. The results are elongated holes, distorted slots, or visible thinning.

With a rotary die, the sheet is supported almost all the way to the tangent point of the bend. Because the inserts rotate with the material, the support footprint is wider and more consistent. The sheet does not drop into a V-shaped cavity. As a result, the hole distortion near the bend line is significantly reduced.

The same principle applies to perforated sheet and diamond tread plate. Perforated material behaves like a weakened lattice. In a static V die, the unsupported span can allow localized collapse along the weakest axis. In other words, the bend line will always shift to the weakest point (see

Figure 3

).

FIGURE 3 The bend line will always shift to the weakest point using a conventional die.

Rotary support dies distribute contact over a broader surface area, stabilizing the sheet as it bends. Diamond or tread plate, a variable-thickness material, also benefits from that distributed support. The inserts adapt to surface irregularities rather than forcing the material to bridge over a fixed shoulder.

ABOUT MINIMUM FLANGE LENGTH

It is true that rotary dies allow shorter flanges than conventional V dies of similar opening size. That does not mean you can form arbitrarily short flanges. The flange must still extend far enough to reach the insert’s center of rotation. If it does not, the insert cannot rotate properly, and the forming process becomes unstable.

Put another way, the material must "engage" the insert at its rotational center. If the edge stops short of that point, the die begins to resist rotation rather than support it. Shorter flanges are possible, but only within the insert design’s geometric limits.

Understanding this distinction is critical. Because the inserts rotate, the effective support location is not static. As the bend progresses, the contact point shifts along the insert’s arc. This movement is small, but it means the die behaves as a motion-based system rather than a fixed-geometry system.

STRICTLY FOR AIR BENDING

This also explains why rotary dies are strictly air bending tools. Bottoming and coining with them are out of the question. A static V die can tolerate bottoming or even coining because the load path travels directly from the punch into the die shoulders and then into the die body.

A rotary die redirects that force into pivot shafts and internal components. If you attempt to bottom the punch into the insert, the inserts cannot rotate. Load spikes, internal stress rises rapidly, and damage occurs inside the assembly, not necessarily at the insert surface.

RADIUS LIMITS

Another geometric limit appears when forming very large outside radii. As the outside bend radius increases, the sheet’s tangent points move outward along the insert surface. When the tangent point aligns with the insert’s center of rotation, rotation effectively stops. The die is no longer operating in rolling mode. Instead, outwardly spreading forces begin to act on the die body (see

Figure 4

).

FIGURE 4 The rotation stops when the radius is too large and instead starts to apply force to the rotational inserts, which can damage the tool internally.

This condition defines the practical upper limit of the supported outside radius. Before that point, the inserts rotate freely and the load flows tangentially into the pivot system. Beyond that point, the die begins to resist motion, and stresses redirect outward rather than inward.

CONTROLLED MOTION

The real mistake isn’t assuming rotary dies reduce marking. The mistake is assuming that’s their primary advantage. Surface finish is simply the most visible result of something deeper: controlled motion. Once you understand that these tools are governed by rotation and geometry rather than sliding friction, the rest falls into place. Minimum flange limits make sense. Hole stability makes sense. Even their failure conditions make sense.

Rotary dies don’t remove the rules of air bending; they change which rules matter most. And that shift begins with understanding how the material moves during the bend. Everything about rotary dies is governed by rotation, not by static shoulder contact. The load path, support footprint, minimum flange, and maximum radius are all defined by the geometry of rotating inserts.

That is why programming rotary dies requires a different mindset. The dimension entered into the controller is not a simple V opening in the traditional sense. It corresponds to the spacing between the insert pivot centers, a fixed mechanical dimension, though the effective opening changes slightly as the inserts rotate. This is the virtual V opening, a concept I’ll discuss in detail in future columns.

ADVANTAGES AND LIMITS

Rotary dies reduce surface marking and improve stability near holes and cutouts. They offer shorter permissible flange lengths and more consistent support on irregular surfaces.

But they are not indestructible tools that remove all geometric constraints. They simply shift the forming mechanics from sliding friction to controlled rotation. If you remember only one thing, remember this: A rotary die is not a premium V die. It is a motion-based forming system that must be respected as such.

Rotational dies can bring immense value to your forming operations if, and I stress if, you see them through a new set of eyes. Using them may not seem all that different than forming with traditional dies, but those differences exist, and you must learn to see them. If you don’t, you will quickly turn that expensive tool into a boat anchor.
