# Press Brake Die Selection: Radius, Force, Clearance, and Bend Sequence

## Die selection is a process decision

Press-brake tooling should not be selected solely because a sheet fits within a V-opening. The punch–die combination affects the formed inside radius, required forming force, surface contact, flange support, collision risk, and the feasibility of later bends. Material thickness, strength, ductility, rolling direction, specified bend radius, part geometry, and the bending method must all be considered together.

The article presents the commonly used “eight times material thickness” V-opening guideline as a starting point for some mild-steel air-bending work, not as a universal rule. Its suitability can change with material grade, thickness, required radius, and machine/tooling limits. Material supplier data, tooling documentation, and the machine manual should govern final selection.

## Air bending and bottoming require different considerations

In air bending, the sheet is supported on the die shoulders and does not normally contact the bottom of the V-opening. The resulting bend radius is influenced by the die opening, punch geometry, and material behavior. This means that choosing a die opening also influences the resulting bend geometry and force requirement.

Bottoming uses closer conformity between the workpiece and tooling. The article describes it as requiring substantially more force than air bending and as reducing some springback variation through closer tooling contact. Because force demand and tooling engagement differ by process, the bending method must be identified before verifying machine capacity or selecting a punch and die.

Only qualified personnel should establish or alter bending processes. Confirm allowable tonnage, load distribution, tooling ratings, and machine operating limits using approved documentation.

## Match punch radius and die geometry to the material

A very sharp punch nose can concentrate strain at the inside of a bend. When the punch radius is too small for the material and required bend condition, the workpiece may crack or the operation may shift away from a normal air-bending condition toward more severe local deformation. Punch radius should therefore be selected with reference to the material’s bending limits and the required finished inside radius.

Die shoulders also matter. During bending, the outside surface of the sheet moves across the shoulder areas. Narrow openings and high contact pressure can increase the chance of marking, galling, or damage to the workpiece and tooling. Surface-sensitive parts may require an alternative forming approach or suitable protective measures approved for the application.

Material behavior is not uniform across all sheet products. Strength, elongation, work hardening, thickness variation, and rolling direction can affect cracking and springback. Bend orientation relative to the rolling direction should be assessed where material behavior or part requirements make it relevant. Tooling hardness alone should not be treated as a solution to difficult forming conditions; toughness, load rating, material compatibility, and service condition must be considered.

## Select tooling for part geometry and clearance

A standard V-die and straight punch may not provide clearance for return flanges, deep channels, or bends beyond 90 degrees. Previously formed features can swing into the tooling path during subsequent bends, creating a collision hazard.

Relieved punch shapes, including gooseneck styles, may provide clearance where the part geometry requires it. Acute tooling may be used where overbending and later springback are part of the approved process. Such choices require a documented interference check for the full part geometry, tooling envelope, and planned bend sequence.

Channel and U-shaped tooling can provide more sidewall guidance than a conventional V-die for parts that require controlled channel geometry. In exchange, this tooling may be less flexible across varying angles or part forms. Hemming operations also need material-specific review because flattening a pre-bent edge can impose high strain and may promote cracking in some materials. Use approved tooling and process data for the specific hem design rather than assuming that one flattening method is suitable for all materials.

## Verify force, flange support, and bend order

A smaller V-opening can be used to pursue a tighter bend condition, but it can also increase required force. Before setup, calculate or obtain the required force from approved references for the actual material, thickness, bend length, die opening, and bending method. Compare that result with machine capacity, tooling capacity, and permitted load distribution across the bed.

Short flanges may not be adequately supported by a wide V-opening. A die opening selected to reduce force on a heavy bend can therefore make another feature difficult or unsafe to form. The entire part should be reviewed before tooling is chosen:

- Required finished inside radius and permitted tolerances
- Material type, condition, thickness, and rolling direction where relevant
- Bending method and expected springback behavior
- Force demand and distributed-load limits
- Minimum supported flange geometry
- Clearance for returns, channels, and previously formed features
- Tooling compatibility and condition
- Bend sequence and part handling requirements

A bend sequence should be planned as an integrated process, not as independent individual bends. In some parts, forming a short or obstructed flange before a larger bend may avoid loss of support or later tooling interference. The final sequence must be validated through approved setup procedures and qualified review.

## Maintain job-specific process records

A shop-specific selection record can help reduce reliance on generic rules of thumb. For recurring material families and part types, document approved material conditions, punch and die combinations, bending methods, verified force requirements, clearance limitations, and observed springback. These records should be reviewed when material, thickness, tooling, part geometry, or machine conditions change.

The objective is controlled bending: select tooling and sequence operations so that the required geometry can be produced within documented material, tooling, and machine limits.
