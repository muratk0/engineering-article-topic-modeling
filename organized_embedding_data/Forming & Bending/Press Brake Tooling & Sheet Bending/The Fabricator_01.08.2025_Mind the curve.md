W

hen you first look at a curve folding machine, the movie "Stargate" comes to mind. A cobot feeds a laser-cut blank into a circular, portal-like frame. Three arms, each positioned around an inside track, start to fold the workpiece in unique ways. The bend lines aren’t straight. They’re curved.

"And yes, we do tend to call the machine the ‘Stargate.’"

That was Jonas Nyvang, CEO and co-founder of StilFold AB, a Swedish startup that has introduced a process it calls

toolless industrial origami

. Also called

curve folding

, the process can fold sheet metal in ways that can make each component far stronger than the conventionally formed alternative. Earlier this year, the company unveiled a machine, called StilWorks, that can turn those folds from concept to reality (see

Figure 1

). It’s the latest chapter in a story that began when Nyvang and co-founder Tue Beijer, chief technology officer, began experimenting with origami-like folding of thick cardboard.

"When you start folding over a curve," Nyvang said, "good things start to happen."

Industrial Origami

Consider the traditional sheet metal forming methods that don’t require dedicated tooling, like a stamping or hydroforming press. Press brakes, panel benders, folders—they all create straight bend lines made in flat sheet metal.

When StilFold’s founders began experimenting with cardboard cutouts, they weren’t thinking about conventional manufacturing techniques. They were inspired by something far older: origami. They thought of how someone adept at origami folds a creation. They fold straight in certain places, but in others, they fold carefully over the curves and contours, almost "sculpting" the paper creation into its final form. Where the origami artist places those folds really matters. A fold along a curve creates a kind of "interlock" that can increase strength. And, of course, traditional origami uses just one sheet of paper.

These insights spurred Nyvang and Beijer to ask: What if a metal folding method could reduce the number of parts a product required and, hence, help simplify manufacturing? Also, could folding in just the right way increase strength and, therefore, allow the use of thinner sheet metal—reducing the product’s overall weight? In other words, could a metal folding method make a product simpler, lighter, and easier to produce?

From this came the development of curve folding, a process in which the geometry of the folds and their exact placement can add significant strength. According to a StilFold white paper, "Unlike flat surfaces or straight folds, curved geometries resist deformation more efficiently when subjected to bending or compressive forces. This is primarily due to the enhanced geometric stiffness of curved surfaces. … [Also], curved folds create a 3D geometry that distributes loads more uniformly. This uniform distribution prevents localized stress points common in straight-folded structures, thereby enhancing the overall durability and lifespan of the material."

Starting With a Scooter

During a Zoom call in late 2022, Beijer turned around and lifted a scooter chassis off a nearby table—with little noticeable effort. "This is stainless steel, so it’s not the lightest material, but it’s still only 8.9 kg [less than 20 lbs.]."

That chassis today is part of the company’s Stil-Ride scooter, a project that began as a proof of-concept for curve folding five years ago. It’s now on the market and has since won several international design awards. The initial design had 15 sheet metal parts; today it’s down to less than half that (see

Figures 2

and

3

).

CAD, CAM, and a Machine

Curve folding starts with software. The company’s StilWare CAD platform helps engineers design folds that increase component strength and improve overall design performance (see

Figure 4

). The company’s CAM platform, Stil-Tool, translates the data into instructions—including the folding sequence that works best for the blank being folded.

"The StilWare platform is important," Nyvang said, "because it allows you to find those curved geometries that are foldable. If you don’t match the geometry with the material properties you’re working with, the curve folding process really doesn’t work." For instance, a curve geometry might be optimal for a stainless part and yet wouldn’t work for tempered aluminum. And, of course, material thickness also plays a role.

Early on, the company focused on the software, then relied on a custom robotic setup for folding. Last year, it was time to build a standard machine optimized for the curve folding process.

"As we discussed the design, we knew we needed to keep it as flexible as possible," Nyvang said. "The circle gave us that flexibility."

The machine’s "Stargate" circle has three arms extending inward, each with angle forming tools that can both fold and compress the material. Specifically, the top arm holds the crease wheel while the two bottom arms hold the forming rollers. When the tool arms rotate, the folding angle changes.

The tools themselves can be changed out to accommodate different application requirements. Like in roll forming and press brake bending—and any metal forming process, really—curve folding tools need to account for material yield, tensile, and its susceptibility to cracking.

"Some materials require sharper toolsets," Nyvang said, "while other material can have issues when the crease wheels are too sharp. Also, we’re developing different rollers that can access material to produce specific angles."

The rollers have been tested on material up to 4 mm thick. Tested grades include carbon steel, stainless, aluminum, and even titanium. "Stainless works extremely well, no problems," Nyvang said, "but some grades of aluminum are a challenge. You need enough plasticity in the material."

Even so, the curve folding process can fold material that would crack with conventional forming. "The forming is incremental and very gentle," Nyvang explained. "It can take on really high-strength material. We actually tested steels with tensile strengths up to 1,700 MPa."

The current machine can handle workpieces up to 1.25 m wide, but the length hinges on the payload capacity of the robot. The company also is testing a new machine, dubbed the Transformer, designed to accommodate larger sheets.

The machine works with software that simulates the curve folding cycle. When the robot grasps the blank, sensors detect where it is, and the system compares it to its digital twin. From there, the folding cycle begins.

This year, the company has been using the machine to test the limits of the process, including how fast the material can be fed through the rolls to create each fold. Such testing delves into new territory, and the devil is in the details. The folds themselves aren’t always straight lines, and folding in one area can affect other areas of the blank. Again, think of an origami artist molding different parts of the paper—a squeeze here, a tuck there.

In developing the technology, one of the first challenges was setting the angles of the wheels that feed the workpiece through. Folding speed also was a factor; tests have pushed the limit to determine how quickly the machine can feed and fold the material.

That said, speed is not the most critical factor. As Nyvang explained, curve folding might be slower than conventional forming processes, but if it simplifies the entire product and accelerates overall manufacturing, from the first cut to the final shipment, the folding method starts to make a world of economic sense. Plus, curve folding requires no dedicated tooling. "When you don’t require a dedicated toolset, you can cut so much time from idea to prototype and from prototype to production, especially in high-variety production where tooling investments are high and inflexible."

Thinking in Curved Folds

The machine now is being installed in several custom fabrication operations in Europe. "This year, they will be running tests and prototypes for us," Nyvang said. "By 2026, we aim to have machines available for purchase from anywhere in the world."

At this point, Nyvang sees development of two types of machines, one that’s standardized and offered to custom sheet metal operations for prototype and low-quantity work, and another that could be customized for OEMs and high-volume production. These machines will have special work envelopes and tooling tailored for a specific product line.

Such machines would never outpace a stamping press, of course, but again, if they simplify production throughout the value stream—with fewer parts, less material, less weight, and easier assembly—curve folding could take hold, especially as a new generation of engineers start to take notice.

Earlier this year, a group of engineering students from Georgia Tech visited StilFold’s operation in Sweden to learn about the process and see the latest curve folding machine in action. Also, in a lab at Georgia Tech with an early prototype of a curve folding machine, students have been studying the process and learning how to think in curved folds.

"We also have education program in Europe for engineers and designers as well," Nyvang said. "At the same time, we’re working with 10 different clients across various industries, including automotive, special vehicles, aerospace, architecture, and consumer products."

Conversations have focused on process flexibility and speed, but the most critical insights have been rooted in big-picture concepts, including the positive ripple effects curve folding could send throughout the supply chain.

"The truth is," Nyvang said, "we’re introducing everyone to a new way of thinking."
