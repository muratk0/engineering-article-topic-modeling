n recent months, I’ve been diving deep into a less common method of forming: bending on the press brake with urethane tools. Such tools have some unique considerations. You need to determine the right hardness, or durometer. And if you’re using a soft urethane pad—a solid hydraulic—you need to ensure its volume within the retainer box is at least 10 times the volume of the penetrating punch and material.

We have a few more topics to discuss—specifically, how the punch behaves, how urethane durometer can affect process parameters, and how the punch and bend angle affects the way urethane behaves during the forming cycle.

The Punch Rebounds

Punch float

refers to the unintended vertical movement, or slight rebound, of the punch during forming. It’s caused by insufficient resistance from the material or surface being formed. Essentially, the punch doesn’t seat firmly into its intended position because the forming medium pushes back or deflects, absorbing and then releasing force.

The phenomenon is most common when forming into a urethane pad. While less common in air forming, punch float can still occur if the die has excessive clearance or the material springs back more than expected. In these cases, the punch may not fully embed. This is more noticeable when using wide die openings, soft material, or excessively sharp punches.

Punch float matters because it causes inconsistent forming depth, which throws off bend angle accuracy, especially on small flanges, as it makes springback less predictable. Over time, punch float also contributes to wear or uneven pressure loading on tooling.

When does punch float occur, and how do you identify it? Urethane is elastic in nature; it stores energy under compression. As the punch bottoms into the pad, the urethane pushes back with stored force. This can cause the punch to rise slightly or fail to stay fully seated, especially with larger nose radii or thicker urethane.

While punch float is detrimental to most forming applications, there are exceptions. If you have been following my columns, you have likely heard me discuss

multibreakage

, the separation of material from the punch nose in large-radius bends. You can correct that issue by placing an appropriately sized pad at the bottom of the large V die and using punch float to push back against the material and force it against the punch nose, making the part conform to the punch nose radius (see

Figure 1

).

In identifying punch float, you may notice an inconsistent bottom point on the stroke. Even when using the same tooling and settings, you’ll see finished parts with minor angle variations. Or, you may see it in the deflection reading on the controller.

To minimize punch float’s effect, you can try to use a firmer, higher-durometer urethane pad. You can increase the pad thickness to spread the load more evenly. You also can reduce the punch radius, if possible, or add a mechanical backup such as a steel stop under the pad. And you can ensure that deceleration timing is tuned to prevent bouncing.

Durometer’s Effect on Pad Volume

Durometer is a measure of a urethane’s hardness, which is given on the Shore A scale, with 60A being soft and 90A being hard. The higher the number, the harder the urethane, and the harder the urethane, the less compressible the pad. When stroke depth and punch radius are fixed, durometer becomes the controlling variable for how far the punch can go; how the volume spreads; and how the pad heats, fatigues, and wears.

When a punch enters a urethane pad, the pad resists deformation and displaces volume to make room for the punch nose. This volume must go somewhere: sideways, downward, or into localized compression.

A soft, low-durometer pad (60A to 70A) compresses more easily, even at low stroke depths. The punch nose can penetrate deeper before the pad offers strong resistance. This spreads the displaced volume over a larger area. The pad flows around the punch nose, exaggerating the radial deformation. This results in the same stroke displacing a greater volume (since more material is displaced), but at a lower internal pressure.

Hard, high-durometer pads (85A to 95A) offer stronger resistance to compression. The punch stroke produces less visible deformation but builds more internal pressure. The urethane will not flow easily around the punch, so the deformation stays tighter to the nose. The same stroke displaces less volume outward but does so with higher stress, increasing localized wear and heating.

The durometer also alters the shape of the displacement. Specifically, the durometer doesn’t just change the resistance; it changes the

geometry of the displaced volume itself

. The softer the pad, the more it "wraps" around the punch, following the nose radius. This makes the volume displaced appear wider and rounder than it really is. The harder the pad, the more rigid it remains. The punch forms a narrower wedge, and the volume displacement is more concentrated.

Punch Angle and Bend Angle

Sharper punch angles concentrate force at the punch tip, increasing localized stress and making urethane less able to flow smoothly, especially with harder-durometer pads. Compared to a 90-degree punch angle, a 60-degree punch angle is narrow and has a sharper tip. It will penetrate deeper into the pad for the same part angle, induce higher pressure, and have more urethane displacement.

The effects grow as the punch angle decreases further. A 30-degree acute-angle punch will create a very sharp intrusion penetrating deeper to achieve the same bend angle, dramatically increasing pad wear and strain.

As the punch’s included angle decreases, the same stroke depth produces a shallower part angle. To match the desired part angle, you must punch deeper into the urethane pad. That deeper penetration increases the volume displaced, which raises internal pad pressure and accelerates heat and wear.

What about the bend angle? Here, I am referring to the inside angle of the finished part. A 70-degree inside angle is sharper than 90 degrees, while a 110-degree inside angle is more open. The bend angle determines how deep the punch must go to achieve the bend.

A 70-degree inside angle requires the punch to go deeper, which forces a sharper bend. This leads to higher urethane penetration and more volume displacement. A 90-degree bend has moderate pad compression. Meanwhile, a 110-degree open bend angle requires less punch stroke. The material bends easily. Creating such open bends lessens pad engagement, lowers stress, and reduces wear.

So, the punch angle determines the punch geometry and how the force is applied. The part angle determines how far the punch must travel to make the bend. The two are related, but not the same. What is the combined effect?

Let’s say you use a 60-degree punch to form a 70-degree inside bend angle. The punch will need to penetrate deeply into the urethane to force that tight bend. Now let’s imagine you have a 90-degree punch forming a 110-degree inside bend angle. To create this open bend, you barely enter the pad and very little compression or stress occurs.

Working with tighter angles in the punch and the part comes with some challenges. You need to deal with deeper penetration, more urethane displacement, high contact pressure, shorter pad life, and greater hysteresis and internal heat. (As discussed last month, hysteresis is the internal resistance of the urethane to repeated deformation.)

Urethane Sheets

One more urethane product is commonly found in most sheet metal shops, and that’s

sheet urethane

, often called "die protect." This is used to decrease or completely remove the witness marks left on a product.

These help with bends where the cosmetics of the final part are very high. The sheet is laid over a V or channel die. As the part is dragged over the die shoulders, the sheet urethane acts as a lubricant, protecting the finish.

Take a Chance

There’s more to learn about urethane, its behavior, and the effects of extenuating circumstances. Regardless, now that you know a little more about the possibilities, you will be able to make an informed decision about forming with urethane.

If you haven’t already, take a chance and give it a try. Until next time …
