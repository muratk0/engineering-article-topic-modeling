## Terminology and Function

The standard term for the machine used to bend sheet metal with a punch and die is **press brake**. Clear terminology is useful in drawings, work instructions, tooling records, maintenance documentation, and technical communication.

A press brake forms sheet metal by driving an upper tool (punch) toward a lower tool, commonly a V-die. Bend quality depends on the interaction of the material, tooling, machine condition, part geometry, and forming method.

## Primary Bending Methods

### Air bending

In air bending, the sheet contacts the punch tip and the two shoulders of the V-die. The material does not fully conform to the die cavity. Bend angle is influenced by punch penetration, material properties, die opening, and springback.

This method can provide flexibility because a tooling set may be used for more than one bend angle. However, variation in material thickness, strength, grain direction, or condition can change springback and affect the final angle.

### Bottom bending

In bottom bending, the workpiece is pressed more fully into the die shape than in air bending. The process may use overbending to account for springback. It can improve repeatability for a defined tool and bend condition, but generally requires more forming force and is less flexible than air bending.

### Coining

Coining uses substantially greater force to form the material closely to the tooling. Because it imposes high loads on the workpiece, tooling, and machine, its suitability must be evaluated against machine capacity, tooling ratings, material behavior, and the approved production process.

## Machine and Tooling Factors

Key press-brake elements include the ram, punch, lower die, bed, backgauge, and any system used to compensate for machine deflection. Under load, the ram and bed can deflect. This can create angle variation along a long bend, particularly between the center and ends of the workpiece. A crowning or deflection-compensation system may be used where appropriate, but its use and verification must follow the machine documentation and approved procedures.

The V-die opening affects required forming force, bend radius, and the risk of marking or cracking. The article describes a commonly used rule of thumb in some mild-steel air-bending applications: selecting a V-opening near eight times material thickness. This is not a universal setting. Material grade, thickness, temper, required inside radius, punch geometry, bend length, and machine/tooling limits must all be considered.

Punch radius is also important. A radius that is too small for the material and bend condition can increase strain at the outside of the bend and may contribute to cracking. Tool selection should therefore be based on documented material and design requirements rather than a single general rule.

## Springback and Flat-Pattern Development

Springback is the elastic recovery that occurs after forming force is released. It can vary with material type, thickness, hardness, rolling direction, bend radius, die opening, and bending method. Production processes may use approved test bends, measured results, and validated compensation methods to establish repeatable angles.

Flat-pattern development requires bend allowance or bend deduction appropriate to the intended bend geometry. The neutral-axis position is often represented by a K-factor, but it is not fixed across all materials and conditions. Incorrect material or bend inputs can cause dimensional error in the finished part. Use validated process data and the organization’s approved CAD, programming, and inspection practices.

## Bend Sequence and Part Handling

For parts requiring several bends, bend order should be planned to avoid interference between the workpiece, tooling, machine structure, and support equipment. The sequence must also allow the part to be removed safely after each operation. Complex parts may require simulation or a documented setup review before production.

Long, wide, or awkward workpieces can move unexpectedly during bending. Their weight, balance, and changing orientation must be considered when planning support and handling. Use suitable approved support methods and follow workplace procedures.

## Safety Principles

Press-brake operations present crushing, pinch-point, workpiece-motion, electrical, and hydraulic hazards. Hands and other body parts must be kept clear of the closing zone and moving components. Workpieces must be supported and controlled so that they do not swing, whip, slip, or eject unexpectedly.

Safety devices, emergency stops, guards, and interlocks must be maintained and used as intended. Do not bypass, defeat, adjust, or rely on altered safety functions. Maintenance, troubleshooting, or work near energized electrical or hydraulic systems must be performed only under the machine manufacturer’s instructions, site lockout/tagout procedures, and applicable local safety requirements by qualified personnel.

## Process Planning Considerations

Before bending, confirm the material identity and condition, part orientation, tooling selection, bend sequence, and machine capability. Required forming force should be determined using approved manufacturer documentation, validated tooling data, or qualified engineering methods. Machine nominal capacity alone does not establish that a particular bend is safe or suitable.

Consistent bending results depend on controlled inputs: verified material, suitable tools, correct setup, appropriate deflection compensation where needed, planned handling, and inspection of the formed part. These principles apply across sheet-metal fabrication and should be adapted to the specific machine, workpiece, and approved operating procedure.
