# So you want to buy a tube bender

[TARİH: 01.03.2026 The Fabricator]

Tube Bending

Asking the right questions gets you where you need to go

By

Lincoln Brunner

There are almost as many issues to address before buying a tube bending machine as there are varieties from which to choose. Putting them all on paper beforehand can save a whole list of worries.

Lincoln Brunner

B

uying a tube bending machine is no small task, especially given shifting market dynamics, customer demands, and labor availability. That’s why it pays to think about the factors that will truly make a difference to the profitability of whatever bending machine you choose.

To ascertain what those factors are, Tube and Pipe Journal asked several tube bending machine manufacturers for their take. Here are some of the subjects they listed as paramount for potential buyers.

WHAT ARE YOU BENDING?

Among many important factors, the kind of tubes you’re bending might be the most important consideration when pondering a capital expenditure. Material type is elemental: The necessary tooling and power differences alone should give a buyer pause.

"The size and material of your parts drives the size of your machine," said Craig Van Sant, vice president, and Daniel Burns, application engineer, of transfluid, which makes both servo-electric and servohydraulic bending machines. "Bigger machines are generally more expensive, and they are not always very suitable for the smaller sizes, so it is not always possible to buy one machine that can do everything.

"The best approach for sizing a machine is usually find your sweet spot using the 80-20 rule: What range of sizes will capture 80% of your parts? Optimize your machine size for that range."

Alan Pickering, managing director at all-electric bending machine maker Unison Ltd in Scarborough, U.K., is a big proponent of first-part-goodpart—the result of bending on a machine that can deliver precise bends repeatably and predictably. He noted that the machine that deliver top speed is rarely the same machine that can deliver top quality.

He also recommends that tool change be factored into any discussion of speed. "The headline speed might be fast, but if changeover is more than two to three minutes and you get right [parts] first time with a good-quality machine, then which is truly faster?" he asked.

Misgana Mulat, national sales manager for BLM GROUP USA, noted that bending machine buyers would do well to understand the acceptable limits for ovality, wall thinning, wrinkling, and cosmetic quality—all essential factors when selecting machine and tooling packages.

"Material selection plays a major role in bending success," said Mulat, whose company offers all-electric, hydraulic, and hybrid bending machines. "Customers should carefully consider material type and also grade, which is just as important since these will determine the tensile strength, elongation surface condition, and lot-to-lot variability. These factors also impact bendability, springback behavior, surface finish, and tooling wear."

ROBUSTNESS, FUTURE NEEDS, AND TOTAL COST OF OWNERSHIP

Sticker shock notwithstanding, the bigger financial consideration for someone buying a tubing bender should always be total cost of ownership (TCO)—the combined cost of machine, tooling, lubricants and fluid (if needed), maintenance, downtime, ease of use, and required training time.

If it’s a shop’s first go-round with a particular model or vendor, the best it can do is compare the experiences of previous buyers, either via the OEM or personal investigation. But getting the skinny on expected TCO—including the cost of foreseeable future expansions—early in the buying process can save a shop expense and headaches.

"Evaluating total cost of ownership, including reliability, maintenance, tool life, and support infrastructure, is key to make a sound purchasing decision," Mulat said. "Customers should evaluate not only their current production needs but also where they expect to be in the next five to 10 years. Selecting a machine platform that allows modular automation and future upgrades helps protect the investment as production volumes and part complexity increase."

Van Sant and Burns brought up another consideration for the long term: the robustness of the machine itself. A truly robust machine ought to give a shop 10 to 20 years of reliable service, they said, so it behooves a shop to check the service records of the machines its considering.

"People tend to get distracted by the accessories and other toys that might come with the machine, but the biggest factor that will affect the ownership experience is the ability of the machine to withstand life in a fab shop," Van Sant and Burns said. "How rigid is the frame? Are the components (electronics, motors, gearboxes, pumps, etc.) of the highest quality, and are critical spares readily available?

"Are you considering the full life of the machine in your ROI calculation, or just the initial investment? Is the machine easy enough to use that a new hire can learn to run it relatively quickly? What are your costs? Are material/scrap or labor a big enough factor that it makes sense to increase the level of automation in the bending system?"

SPEED AND CHANGEOVERS IN PRODUCTION RUNS VS. SMALL BATCHES

Another key variable in any fabrication shop’s cap-ex plan is high-volume work versus what is becoming more and more the regular routine: highmix, low-volume part runs with a good bit of prototyping thrown in.

Making one or the other the mainstay of your book of business is a viable plan, as long as your machinery is built to serve and sustain it.

Pickering said he feels that shops bending expensive material or doing really short production runs with lots of tool changes need an all-electric machine.

However, "If you’re only making one part, once it’s set up, it should just run," Pickering said of production-volume fabricators. "So, you don’t need a fancy control system and are probably OK with a hydraulic or hybrid machine."

"Not every machine is fast enough to make 350,000 parts per year, and a machine that can go that fast is probably too expensive for a 35,000-parts-per-year job," Van Sant and Burns said. "If you are only ever going to make one part number on this machine, you don’t need much flexibility or agility. But if you are a job shop, or if you have a wide variety of parts, you need a machine that can change from one part—or one size—to another quickly and easily. Generally speaking, you get this from quick-change tools and software that saves machine parameters in the program, thereby making mechanical adjustments on the machine or toolset unnecessary.

"And watch out for old, outdated stereotypes. It used to be that you could only get quick-change tools on all-electric machines, but now there are manufacturers offering quick-change tools on servohydraulic machines."

AT YOUR SERVICE

With the myriad things that can go wrong with a tubing bender, especially during commissioning and the first months of ownership, knowing a vendor’s commitment to service can mean the difference between seamless startup and unrelenting ulcers.

"Beyond the machine itself, customers should carefully assess the level of technical support, application engineering, training, spare parts availability, and longer-term service offered by the manufacturer," Mulat said. "A lower upfront price can quickly be offset by extended downtime, lack of expertise, or limited local support."

One thing most OEMs constantly fret about is the availability of field engineers and techs to service their customers’ needs. Knowing that your vendor has the necessary personnel available should influence from whom you buy.

"The service department is a big part of reliability, so you should ask about it," Van Sant and Burns said. "Does the machine manufacturer stand behind their products with their own factory-trained technicians? How long after initial request will a technician be on the phone? How do they track problems to make sure one doesn’t fall through the cracks? How good are they at training operators? What do other customers say about their service experience?

"Some customers want a machine, period. Some customers want a partnership. They want a good machine, yes, but what they really want is a manufacturer that will walk with them through both the purchasing phase and the operational life of the machine. Listen to how they talk during the sales process: Is the person talking to you a technical expert helping you get and understand the data you need to make a purchasing decision, or is he a salesman pushing a machine?"
