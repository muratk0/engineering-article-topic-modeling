# Selecting and Safely Using Ironworkers, Press Brakes, and Shears

## Purpose of Each Machine Type

Ironworkers, press brakes, and shears serve different roles in metal fabrication. Selection should begin with the required operations, workpiece geometry, material form, production volume, available space, and operator capability.

An **ironworker** combines several operations in one machine, commonly including punching, cutting of bars or profiles, angle cutting, notching, and limited bending with suitable tooling. This makes it relevant where varied, relatively simple operations must be performed on plates, bars, or structural profiles. Its multipurpose design does not make it a substitute for dedicated equipment where demanding bending accuracy or high-volume sheet cutting is required.

A **press brake** bends sheet metal by pressing it between upper and lower tooling. It is used when the required result depends on controlled bend location, bend angle, forming sequence, and repeatability. Workpiece positioning and machine deflection can affect bend consistency, especially across long parts. Some machines use crowning or other compensation arrangements to address deflection, while backgauge systems position material for bends. The available axes, tooling, material properties, and setup all affect what parts can be formed.

A **shear** produces straight cuts in sheet material using upper and lower blades. It is generally used to prepare blanks for downstream operations such as punching and bending. Cut quality depends on factors including material condition, blade condition, blade clearance, clamping, and support of the sheet. A shear is intended for straight-line cutting and is not a replacement for processes needed to create complex contours.

## Considering Workflow and Workpiece Requirements

The three machines can be arranged as complementary process stages. For example, sheet stock may first be cut into blanks, then punched or notched where appropriate, and finally bent. However, the sequence should be determined by the part design, material handling needs, tooling access, and the risk of distortion or interference during later operations.

A practical assessment should consider:

- **Part geometry:** Determine whether work consists mainly of straight cuts, holes, notches, profiles, or multiple bends.
- **Material:** Identify material type, thickness range, hardness or strength condition, and whether stock is supplied as sheet, bar, angle, channel, or round section.
- **Production pattern:** Consider whether work is variable and small-batch or repetitive and higher-volume.
- **Quality requirements:** Define acceptable cut edge condition, bend location, angle consistency, surface protection, and scrap limits for the specific part.
- **Layout and handling:** Account for floor space, workpiece support, transfer between operations, and safe movement of large sheets or long profiles.
- **Tooling and training:** Include ongoing needs for punches, dies, blades, inspection, programming where applicable, and qualified operators.

Machine ratings, tooling limits, and material-specific capacity must be verified using the applicable machine documentation. General comparisons do not establish that a particular machine, toolset, or setup is suitable for a specific job.

## Process Factors Affecting Quality

For press-brake work, bend results are influenced by material variation, elastic recovery after bending, tool geometry, workpiece support, bend sequence, machine loading, and positioning. Avoid operating outside rated capacity or repeatedly concentrating work in a manner prohibited by the machine documentation. Bends near holes, notches, or cutouts may require special process planning because local deformation can affect the part and tooling.

For shearing, blade condition and the clearance between blades affect burr formation, deformation, blade wear, and cut appearance. Clearance and other setup conditions must be set only according to the machine manual, tooling guidance, and the material being processed. Materials with unknown or unsuitable properties should not be cut until compatibility with the machine and blades has been confirmed.

For ironworker punching and cutting, punch-and-die alignment, tooling condition, material support, and removal of scrap are important to safe, consistent operation. Damaged, worn, or misaligned tooling can affect part quality and increase the risk of tool failure.

## Safety and Maintenance Principles

These machines create crushing, cutting, entanglement, and hydraulic hazards. Operators should be trained, authorized, and familiar with the specific machine before use. Follow the machine manual, workplace procedures, and applicable safety requirements.

Before maintenance, cleaning, tooling work, inspection inside hazardous areas, or repair, isolate all relevant energy sources through the required lockout/tagout procedure. An emergency stop is not a substitute for energy isolation. Residual hydraulic or other stored energy must be managed by qualified personnel using approved procedures.

Do not bypass, alter, or defeat guards, interlocks, light curtains, two-hand controls, emergency stops, or other protective devices. Keep hands, loose clothing, hair, and other body parts outside cutting and forming zones. Support and restrain workpieces to prevent shifting, tipping, or unexpected movement.

Hydraulic leaks can be hazardous. Do not inspect pressurized leaks by hand or loosen hydraulic fittings while the system is pressurized. Any suspected hydraulic fault should be addressed according to the machine manual by qualified personnel.

Routine preventive maintenance should include inspection of tooling, guards, fasteners, hoses, fittings, lubrication points, and accumulated scrap. Blade, die, punch, hydraulic-fluid, filter, alignment, and calibration work must follow the manufacturer’s specified intervals and procedures. Do not use damaged tooling or operate equipment with unresolved safety defects.
