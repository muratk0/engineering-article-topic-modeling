F

or a century, press brake tools have been carved from tool steel. Some were the traditional planer-style tools manufactured from an X-Y reference coordinate, and others were precision-ground to a very tight tolerance. Both were hardened to counter wear, use, and abuse day after day.

That hasn’t changed, and it will most likely stay that way for a long time to come. However, the need for prototyping and short runs has shifted demand toward custom tooling—and here, 3D printing could play a major role.

Over the last few months, we have been learning that the 3D printer sitting on a shop bench today can make a working press brake punch or die in just a few hours (not weeks), and for a fraction of the cost of steel tooling. Let me be clear: 3D-printed tooling is not here to replace the steel in your tool rack, at least not yet anyway. However, for prototype, short-run, and one-off forming jobs, 3D-printed tooling for the press brake has a place. Even some trade schools are using 3D-printed press brake tools for teaching and classroom demonstrations. In the hands of competent and qualified people, a 3D printer can become another precision tool of the trade.

Why 3D Printing Works

Using additive manufacturing isn’t about being cool; it’s about time. In a traditional scenario, you’d call your tooling supplier for a quote and then wait a few days or weeks for the tool to arrive. With 3D printing, you can model your concept in CAD, slice the file, and hit "print." Within a few hours, you can load the tool and see how it behaves on the brake. If it doesn’t work out, you can adjust the design and hit print again.

You also can 3D-print tools when teaching new operators, or in situations where manufacturing speed or safety matters more than tool longevity. With the right plastic filament and proper printing conditions, 3D-printed tools can handle low- to moderate-tonnage jobs with surprising accuracy.

Filament Options

Most people start with

polylactic acid

(PLA). It’s inexpensive; easy to print; and delivers a crisp, clean finish. PLA’s stiffness helps maintain accurate geometry, and its low shrink rate delivers excellent dimensional accuracy right off the print bed. For verifying form angles, developing new die geometries, or checking interference in setups, PLA is perfect.

But PLA also is brittle. It doesn’t like flexing, it softens under heat, and it can’t handle sustained compression. In other words, it’s a great learning and fit-check material, but not for serious bending loads. Under a press brake, even at low tonnage, PLA tends to crack along the layer lines.

PETG

resists wear better than PLA, but it won’t match steel or even aluminum. PETG (which stands for polyethylene terephthalate glycol, if you’re curious) can handle light air forming of thin material, up to 20-ga. mild steel, especially on short bend lengths. In training applications, PETG punches and dies have been known to survive hundreds of demonstration bends without failure.

For functional, working 3D-printed tooling,

nylon

(PA6 or PA12) is at the top of the list. Nylon filaments, especially when blended with glass or carbon fibers, produce robust, flexible, and wear-resistant press brake tools.

Nylon’s toughness allows it to deform under stress and then recover, much like urethane. That flexibility spreads the load across a broader area, reducing stress concentrations at the layer lines. It’s also ideal for soft jaws, forming pads, or even flexible die inserts. For custom forming applications—especially when marring must be avoided—nylon tooling can be both practical and gentle on material surfaces.

If you need a plastic punch or die that actually works under pressure,

composite filaments

are the answer. These are standard polymer bases—like nylon, PETG, or even ABS—infused with short carbon or glass fibers. The result is a stiffer, stronger, and more temperature-resistant material.

Carbon-fiber nylon, for example, has roughly twice the stiffness of unfilled nylon and can withstand higher loads without deflection. In testing, short punches printed from carbon-fiber nylon have successfully formed 16-ga. mild steel in small V-dies under moderate tonnage.

That’s pretty remarkable for a tool that comes out of a desktop printer.

Another material,

thermoplastic polyurethane (TPU)

, is available in several durometers (hardnesses) from medium to hard, and its place on this list is very different from the other filaments we just discussed. This material varies in softness. Meant to deform and deflect, TPU basically acts as a solid hydraulic, similar to the urethane pads you might place in dies for certain forming applications. You push in, and it pushes back equally in all directions, causing the material to conform to the shape of whatever’s applying force, be it your finger or a press brake punch nose.

In the press brake world, TPU can be used for everything from creating rectangular or square parts to specific sizes or dimensions, or for printing uniquely shaped pads that meet specific forming needs or functions. (For more on this topic, you can review my columns on forming with urethane from earlier this year.)

Aside from TPU, filament materials can be annealed. In fact, in most cases, they should be annealed. We covered some basics in earlier columns, and we’ll delve further into the annealing process next month.

The Smart Hybrid: Plastic Meets

Metal Even the most rigid polymer will eventually wear at high-pressure points, especially the punch nose and die shoulders. Most modern printers allow you to pause a print mid-process, insert a different material, and resume printing right over it. That means we can embed wear-resistant metal inserts exactly where they’re needed.

Picture a 3D-printed punch: You pause the print just before the punch nose layers close, drop in a length of small-diameter stainless or hardened steel rod, and then resume the print. The machine buries the insert, locking it into place with only a few degrees of metal surface exposed. The steel takes the wear; the printed body carries the shape; and together they make a lightweight, hybrid tool.

In dies, the same method works for shoulder reinforcement. A pair of metal rods or tubes can be seated where the material slides during bending, preventing wear and maintaining consistent angles. The print resumes, fully encasing the inserts for a permanent bond.

Surface Finish and Friction

The smoothness of a punch and die’s printed surface directly affects how material flows during bending, as well as how much heat is generated by the resulting kinetic friction. A rough, layered finish increases friction and can mar delicate surfaces, such as aluminum or brushed stainless steel. To minimize this, you can print finer layer heights, between 0.1 and 0.2 mm, and try sanding the contact areas lightly with 400-grit paper. You also can apply dry Teflon or silicone spray to reduce drag.

When to Use 3D-Printed Tooling

3D-printed press brake tools excel in three areas:

1.

Prototyping

, to quickly prove out a part design, bend sequence, or tool geometry

2.

Training

, to perform safe, lowcost demonstrations for new operators or classrooms

3.

Short-run

or specialty jobs, which can require custom radii, protective tooling, or delicate finishes Besides this, you can 3D-print a host of different useful items, including jigs, fixtures, and gauging tools. These include go/no-go gauges; positioning or fit-up fixtures; side gauges and custom backstops; and even small tables that attach to the press brake die to support small parts and keep operators’ fingertips safely away from the pinch point.

You can print custom crescent wrenches; light-duty clamps; and even precision measurement tools like radius gauges, thread gauges, rules, and protractors. You could even create a set of radius gauges for each operator, or one for each press brake.

If you are air forming and want to ensure you can measure the "floated" inside bend radius, print a radius gauge that matches the required radius, and send it with the job jacket. (As you know, air forming will never achieve an inside radius that matches a standard set dimension like 1/64 in., 1/32 in., 1/16 in., 1 mm, or 2 mm.) There really is no limit to what you can build, except for the limits of the filament you have chosen.

A Technology Worth Considering

3D printing won’t replace steel press brake tooling or the skills of a steel toolmaker, but it does present new options. For the precision sheet metal trade, that’s progress worth bending toward.
