I

n the sheet metal bending world, metal folding suffers from a host of misconceptions and plenty of confusion. How does a folder form its radius and bend angle, exactly? And what are some basic factors to consider when starting to explore the technology?

To answer these questions and more, The Fabricator spoke with Dale Kroskey, vice president of sales at RAS Systems Inc., Peachtree City, Ga., about metal folding fundamentals and how fabricators can make the most out of an often misunderstood technology.

TOOLS FOR FOLDING

The forming method doesn’t use a moving C-frame with bending tools from above and below to form the flange in positive and negative directions. Instead, it uses a

folding beam

that (in bidirectional models) pivots to create positive and negative forms (see

Figure 1

). Dig deeper into an internet search, and you’ll find other terms like

swivel bending

or

swing bending

.

FIGURE 1 On a bidirectional folder, a folding beam swings up and down to create positive and negative flanges.

The blank is placed on a gauging table (see

Figure 2

) behind the tooling, against the

stop fingers

that position and move the piece forward onto the lower beam tools and below the

upper beam (clamping) tools

.

FIGURE 2 Blanks are placed behind the tools on a table, with moveable gauging fingers spanning the width of the work envelope.

The segmented clamping tools can be rearranged, manually or automatically, to provide clearance for previously formed flanges. The clamping tools themselves protrude at the bottom, providing clearance for acute bends and return flanges (roughly analogous to a gooseneck punch). Corner tools at end of each segment have rotating "feet" that extend outward, securing the bend line while providing clearance for previously formed return flanges on the adjacent edge (see

Figure 3

), analogous to winged punches on a brake. Similarly, the front edge of the folding beam has

folding beam tools

, which are also segmented and, if needed, can be arranged with gaps to provide the required clearances.

FIGURE 3 These clamping tools have corner tools that swing outward to access (among other things) bends under previously formed return flanges.

The bend cycle starts with the upper beam tools descending and clamping the material in place. The folding beam tool contacts the outside flange, swings upward to create a positive bend, and pivots outward to maneuver and position itself for the down-bend. Bends range from standard 90 degrees to acute to hems. And fingers gauging the part from behind can move a workpiece steadily forward to create a smooth bump bend, maintaining that flat part edge as a gauging surface throughout the cycle.

DIALING IN THE FOLDED RADIUS

To create the bend, the folding beam tool maintains constant contact as it swings upward, overbending slightly so the flange springs back to the intended bend angle. Where those points of contact are—between the bottom of the clamping tool and the inside edge of the folding beam tool— helps determine the inside radius, as does how the beam itself pivots around the bend centerline (see

Figure 4

).

FIGURE 4 To accommodate thicker material, the beam’s pivot point shifts outward.

All this can be adjusted to create different radii. Kroskey added, however, that because the folding process applies force to the outside surface of the flange instead of the inside radius of the bend, the resulting bend radii can be extremely sharp. "A standard radius is 1 mm for thin-gauge material," he said, adding that as material gets thicker, the inside radius will increase proportionally; for midrange gauges, an inside bend radius of 1.25 times the material thickness is a rule of thumb.

Kroskey added that the folded radius forms as the metal is forced upward, in contact with the nose of the clamping tool, before springing back slightly. Moreover, during the entire bend cycle, the folding beam tool maintains constant contact. Throughout the bend, the material never rubs against a tool surface.

FIGURE 5 To accommodate crack-susceptible material, these clamping tools have toes machined at a larger radius.

FIGURE 6 Different clamping tools allow different levels of clearance of previous forms, as does the orientation of the upper beam. Angled upper beams (as shown in Figure 1) give clearance for acute angles and extensive return flanges in large workpieces.

All this gives folding a "soft touch" as it bends, which in many cases helps create small inside bend radii without cracking. It can’t replicate a sharp corner from a press brake bottoming operation, which stamps the inside bend radius into the material, but as Kroskey explained, metal folding can create tighter radii than many people assume.

Of course, no technology can circumvent physics. Some material might require a large bend radius to prevent cracking, and altering the folding beam position can help accomplish this. In some cases, special tooling might come into play.

Kroskey described an application involving 0.125-in., high-tensile-strength aluminum (see

Figure 5

). "For challenging applications like this, where we don’t want to crack the radius, we might move to a larger-radius [clamping] tool." For instance, the nose of the clamping foot might be increased from 1 mm to 3 mm, and the beam itself might be moved farther away from the bend pivot point. "And we might alter the clamping pressure to ensure a smooth bend cycle."

FIGURE 7 An inclined folding beam tool creates a 90-degree flange in an internal cutout, without the folding beam colliding with other sections of the part.

FIGURE 8 A narrow folding beam tool allows the folder to access tight spaces.

HANDLING THE EXCEPTIONS

That example involving hard aluminum shows how careful tool selection and design can expand what the folder can accomplish. In most cases, folders use a universal toolset. The biggest consideration is choosing clamping tools tall enough to allow clearance for box forms and similar geometries (see

Certain tooling customizations help expand the folder’s capabilities even more. For instance, pockets machined in the lower beam tools allow the gauging fingers to move close to the bend line (see

Figure 9

). Lower tools also can have pockets machined into them to account for specific part geometries, like negative embosses or extruded holes created on a punch press or punch/laser combo.

Other times, parts can be redesigned slightly. To illustrate, Kroskey described common stiffening requirements for enclosure panels. Some panels might have stiffening ribs, or grooves, rolled using form tools on a punch press or punch/laser combo. Those grooves might interfere with the folder’s clamping tools.

"In this case, we could gap a tool in a strategic way, or machine a relief on certain tooling," Kroskey said. "On the other hand, instead of trying to accommodate the groove, we could simply redesign the part with a crossbreak design."

In fact, that crossbreak design—the X of intersecting bend lines—remains common on transformer housings, HVAC units, and similar enclosure designs. These subtle bend lines create a crown that can be critical for stiffening, water runoff, and other design requirements. And forming them on a folder is usually straightforward. "Most crossbreaks don’t require any special tools or tooling reliefs," Kroskey said.

LEVELS OF AUTOMATION

Labor requirements are the first thing on many fabricators’ minds, especially when it comes to equipment purchases. Some might have a product mix that benefits from high levels of automation, while in other operations, a manual setup might perform well.

"In the industrial and precision sheet metal markets, when it comes to folding, many people tend to think of high levels of automation," Kroskey said, "and that’s partly true. Automation is a huge part of the business." But metal folding has a full range of manual and semiautomated variants. "Some are basically computerized and motorized hand [or leaf] brakes. From there, you have midlevel and semiautomated machines, all the way to full loading and unloading automation."

Visit an architectural or roofing fabricator, and you might see long folders with angled upper beams, giving clearance for some extremely large flanges. These might sit next to smaller up-only, manually fed systems with operators standing behind the tooling at the gauging table. They place a blank on the table and slide it against gauging fingers. Next, they initiate the program and watch those fingers push the part through the bend sequence. When all the forms on one side are complete, the operator rotates the piece to the next side and starts again.

Beyond this comes automated clamping tool change, as well as full material handling automation throughout the folding cycle. Combine this with integrated robotics, and you eliminate all manual part handling.

That said, Kroskey emphasized that not every application requires full-blown automation. "For example, consider tool change automation. If you’re running boxes, you might need that automated tool change. But if you’re running only profiles, you don’t necessarily need a tool changer. You might be able to gap the tools to accommodate a variety of parts you run over a shift. It all depends on the product mix and the order you run those jobs."

A COMPLEMENT TO THE PRESS BRAKE

Press brakes remain ubiquitous for a reason: They’re incredibly flexible. Unlike folders, they don’t require parts to have a flat surface to hold and move workpieces between bends. And high-tonnage machines can form thick plate. Some folders are expanding into material beyond 0.5 in., but most applications involve material up to 0.25 in.

At the same time, folders have also become more flexible, especially in recent years. Clamping tools rearrange themselves automatically, and in the most automated setups, robots feed blanks and retrieve and stack formed pieces, no human intervention required.

At the same time, less-automated folders offer an easier way to form flanges that can be a bear to lift and manipulate on conventional press brakes. Operators might need to position and manipulate blanks, but they’re at least not needing to support workpieces as they swing upward during the press brake bend cycle.

Regardless, folders have come a long way from the "up-bend-only" machines that gained a significant foothold in architectural and roofing markets decades ago.

Precision folders form in the positive and negative directions and can handle flanges formed within cutouts or windows. With universal tooling, they can bump-form, hem, and create closed forms.

Like any technology, folders won’t solve every forming problem. But as Kroskey explained, in many situations, they can improve consistency and increase throughput in bending, a process that many still regard as the heart of precision metal fabrication.
