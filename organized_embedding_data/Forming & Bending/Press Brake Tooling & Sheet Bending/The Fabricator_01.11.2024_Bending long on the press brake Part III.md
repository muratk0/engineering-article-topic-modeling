Bending Basics

Why the radii of longer bends wrap around the punch nose

Why the Radius Matters

I have been teaching press brakes for the last 30 years and have been to an uncountable number of shop floors. I am always amazed at how little attention most pay to the inside bend radius.

Do you check the radius with gauges or pins? A large percentage of shops don’t. So, how do you know what it is? Much of the time, customers do not care, and neither do a shop’s programmers. So, why should you?

Well, if you plan for a radius you can achieve based on the bending method you perform and the tooling you use, then calculate the bend allowance and bend deduction based on those values, you get a valid flat blank size and a perfect part. That’s why the radius matters.

Let’s assume you’ve picked the best tooling geometry possible and achieved your predicted inside radius, at least over short lengths (see

Figure 1

). As the length of the bend line increases, the inside bend radius decreases (more on why this happens follows). A smaller radius reduces the bend allowance and the bend deduction. If we want great parts, we must base our numbers

on the radius we actually achieve

, no matter how long or short the bend happens to be.

Why this occurs has to do with how force is applied on the press brake and the resulting strain hardening that occurs as bends get longer.

Some Basic Terms

How does the radius form in long bends, exactly? To answer this, we need to define a few terms. First is

material flow

, which is the metal’s ability to plastically deform and conform to the shape of the tooling during bending. Second is

strain hardening

(or

work hardening

), which is when a material becomes more robust and less ductile as it is plastically deformed. Applied tonnage refers to the force the press brake uses to bend the material. Then we have the inside bend radius, which is the radius on the inside surface of the bend. The inside bend radius is influenced by both the tooling geometry and bending force.

Flow and Strain

In the initial stage of forming, at lower tonnage levels, the material begins to bend and flow relatively freely between the die shoulders. At this point, the material has not yet undergone significant strain hardening and retains much of its ductility. The radius formation at this stage adheres to the

20% rule

, which states that air-bent material forms its radius as a percentage of the die opening. The rule’s name comes from 304 stainless steel, which forms a radius that’s between 20% and 22% of the die opening. For mild steel, our baseline material, the radius forms at about 16% of the die opening.

As the applied tonnage increases, the punch penetrates deeper into the die. Simultaneously, the material experiences more significant plastic deformation, causing strain hardening, increased yield strength, and reduced ductility. The material becomes more resistant to further deformation. This increased resistance starts to limit the material’s ability to flow.

In the first of this series, I stated that "tonnage increases exponentially with length." "Exponentially" is not exactly the correct descriptor. If your material is consistent, its bending moment remains the same; it doesn’t change. So, what’s really going on here? The issue has to do with the press brake itself.

A longer bend requires more force, and the more force you apply to bend, the more the side frames, bed, and ram flex, reducing the applied force at the bend. That’s because some of the force is going into the brake. To compensate for that flexing, we apply more force as the material penetrates deeper into the die space. We are still increasing force to accomplish the bend. What happens when we increase force? We increase the amount of stress or work hardening of the material at the point of bend.

Strain hardening reduces material flow, and it seems counterintuitive that the material would wrap more tightly around the punch nose radius, decreasing the

inside bend radius.

Despite strain hardening reducing ductility, the increased force (tonnage) overcomes that resistance, forcing the material to conform more closely to the punch nose radius.

The Mechanics of Strain Hardening.

The higher tonnage provides additional energy to deform the strain-hardened material. Although the material resists deformation due to strain hardening, the increased force is sufficient to continue deforming it. The punch penetrates farther into the die, mechanically forcing the material to wrap around the punch nose. This physical action reduces the inside bend radius to the point where it conforms to the punch nose radius.

Note that the material adjacent to the punch nose undergoes significant plastic deformation. So, even though the

overall ductility

is reduced, the material is compelled to conform to the punch nose because of the concentrated force.

As the material strain-hardens, its yield strength increases, requiring more force for any additional deformation. The increased tonnage must sufficiently exceed the strain-hardened material’s new, higher yield strength. This is how, despite strain hardening and reduced material flow, the inside radius still reduces, causing the material to wrap more tightly around the punch.

At low tonnage, the inside bend radius is determined by the die opening, the 20% rule, where the material flows easily. The inside radius is equal to a percentage of the die opening, and those percentages vary by material type.

As the force increases, however, the material begins to strain-harden, and ductility decreases, but the increased tonnage continues to deform the material. At that point, the inside radius decreases as the material conforms more to the punch profile.

It’s not about increased flow, but

increased force.

That is, we get a tighter bend radius not because the material flows more but because the higher force overcomes the material’s resistance, mechanically shaping it.

The Effects of Concentrated Stress

The area of the bend that contacts the punch, what we call the land area, experiences high localized stress. This stress can sometimes exceed the increased yield strength due to strain hardening, allowing further deformation.

Even the material’s ultimate tensile strength (UTS) comes into play. UTS is higher than the yield strength, even after strain hardening. You sometimes need so much force to complete a bend, you can surpass the UTS locally, causing plastic deformation and fracture.

Of course, there are the material limits. Excessive deformation of strain-hardened material can lead to cracking or failure, especially with sharp or minimum-radius bends. Each material can be deformed only so much before failure. This is known as the

ductility threshold.

A smaller punch nose radius increases the risk of material failure. Adjusting die angles and radii can influence how the material deforms, so watching the die geometry is essential. Only apply enough force to achieve the desired bend without overloading the material. Slower bending speeds can also reduce the risk of sudden material failure.

So, Are We Really Air Forming?

By considering the material’s strain-hardening behavior and the effects of applied force, we can better predict and control the bending process to achieve the desired outcomes.

To review, imagine the following process when forming longer bends: (1) The material bends easily, flowing into the die with minimal resistance; (2) the material’s resistance grows due to strain hardening as force increases; (3) as the punch pushes deeper, despite resistance, the material is forced to wrap around the punch nose, and the punch’s mechanical action overcomes the material’s increased resistance.

We know how radius formation changes as our bends get longer. Considering this, are we still air forming as the bend increases pressure and penetration depth?
