T

he late Dick Kallage, a longtime custom metal fabrication consultant and Fabricators and Manufacturers Association member, used to open his operational improvement seminars the same way. "You’re not in the metal fabrication business," he’d say. "You’re in the changeover business."

He’d follow up that comment with Little’s Law. John Little, the mathematician behind queuing theory, stated that in any production system, the smaller lot size you fabricate, the less work-in-process (WIP) you have and the shorter your lead time. When shops run thousands of identical parts at a time, massive lot sizes lumber through the shop and tie up resources that could be producing other jobs that need to ship sooner. When changeover times become shorter and more predictable, lot sizes shrink and jobs start to flow.

Nowhere is quick changeover more critical than in the sheet metal bending department. Here, one innovation has helped transform part flow: the automatic-tool-change (ATC) press brake. To put this innovation in perspective, The Fabricator spoke with Irek Nowak, bending product specialist at AMADA AMERICA. His message: A press brake’s tool-change capability is becoming an increasingly critical piece of the productivity puzzle, especially for high-product-mix operations.

SIMULATE TO PERFECT

Nowak recalled how AMADA’s ATC system evolved from tool-change technology first implemented for robotic bending, complete with offline bending programming and process simulation. After feedback from several fabricators in the Midwest, AMADA developed the ATC for manual operation. During early demonstrations at FABTECH and elsewhere, attendees saw how operators called up part programs and retrieved workpieces as the brake moved and positioned tools. Within a few minutes, the operator was processing the next job.

ATC technology works best in high-product-mix environments. These could be OEMs or contract manufacturers that produce wide-ranging product families. Or they could be custom fabricators that might set up and tear down numerous, often highly disparate forming jobs during a single shift. Whatever the environment, programming every new job at the machine would take time and significantly reduce the ATC’s productive capacity.

Nowak explained how offline programming software can maximize throughput based on a shop’s production needs, especially when it comes to job sequencing. Software balances the needs of downstream operations while looking for common setup requirements among various forming jobs. Nowak called this "a batching solution [in which] you could have dozens of different jobs to form. Software can select and arrange tools to produce all of those parts with as few setups as possible."

A tool magazine has a collection of punches and dies at the ready. The top manipulator can reverse the punches as needed, per the bend program.

Software helps uncover commonalities. It groups similar jobs together (that is, those that share a similar setup) to maximize throughput in bending—though still not at the expense of overall throughput. It’s analogous to dynamic nesting in blanking. You want to group jobs for maximum yield (or, at the brake, for maximum setup efficiency) yet still avoid producing ahead and building excess WIP. Part-flow velocity, from blanking to bending and beyond, remains paramount.

This intelligence, Nowak explained, is why intelligent offline software and ATC technology go hand in hand. Complicated staged setups can be simulated offline yet still be tedious to set up manually at the brake. The ATC automates the setup and, when truly dialed in, can make the most of every move it makes. This allows quick task-switching. Hot jobs breaking into the production schedule really aren’t a concern. A hot job is, well, just the next job.

HOW TOOLS MOVE

The ATC’s tool magazine has multiple rows of punches and dies, and each is arranged in a strategic way. Racks can rotate to reverse tools as needed (like a set of goosenecks that need to be reversed to account for a return flange). Manipulators slide in longer punches and dies from the side, along the toolholders. Moving behind the bed, separate manipulators insert shorter punch and die segments in between those longer tools. Besides moving tools to and from the magazine, manipulators can rearrange punches and dies already on the bed, sliding or lifting and inserting pieces into new positions as needed.

As Nowak explained, thinking about the tool’s motion within the machine can help further optimize flow in the forming department. Say a group of parts involves a variety of bend lengths and yet no side flanges. "If this is the case, we can just slide in those longer tools, and the process can take just 25 seconds."

Optimizing tool usage and placement for the ATC calls for a shift in traditional thinking. In a manual setup, an operator might use short tool segments to bend a small bracket, even if it has no side flange, mainly because those tools are easy to lift and clamp into place. The ATC’s automation, however, eliminates the safety and ergonomic issues that long, heavy tools create. In fact, the ATC takes less time to change over if it only needs to slide in those long tools. In setups without side flanges, brake programs using just a handful of long tool segments can be more flexible.

Narrow tools give you that side-flange clearance, but if you don’t need it, why use those narrow tools, especially with ATC automation in a high-product-mix setting? A narrow tool can form only short bend lengths. A long tool can form both short and long.

Automatic tool changes at the press brake has helped shorten job changeover times to just a few minutes, significantly increasing flexibility in the forming department.

HOW OPERATORS MOVE

Many fabricators have some of their most experienced, knowledgeable operators program the brakes offline, and as Nowak explained, there’s a good reason for this. "They can review that simulation and visualize what’s actually going on in front of the press brake. We want every program going to the ATC to run smoothly, and we certainly don’t want the operators needing to make major corrections at the machine."

The ideal, of course, is attaining a first-part, good-part operation, even for new jobs. Automatic angle correction helps make this a reality. The brake underbends, physically measures the angle with a probe, then makes the final adjustment. The machine never lets go of the part.

Even without automatic angle correction, though, modern brakes still can achieve that first-part, good-part ideal. The operator underbends that first part and measures the angle with a tool that communicates directly with the brake controller, which then automatically makes that adjustment for the final bend. Alternatively, the operator can input that adjustment manually.

Regardless, those final adjustments really should take the operator only a matter of seconds—and this is where strategic tool placement on the ATC can make a difference. If a part requires tooling staged across only a portion of the bed, those toolsets needn’t be in the center. Instead, why not group them all on the left side, close to the machine controller? Again, ATCs are designed to run dozens of different jobs during a shift, so easy access to the control really makes a difference. Placing the tools closer to the control makes life easier for the operator and the entire operation more efficient.

And on modern equipment, off-center loading really isn’t a concern, as long as the job is within the tonnage limits of the machine and tooling. "If you put a part under load on the left-hand side, [modern AMADA] machines automatically create different pressure between cylinder one and cylinder two," Nowak said. "So, everything is compensated for, which means it will apply the proper tonnage."

HANDLING EXCEPTIONS

More ATC-style tools are becoming available. For example, AMADA’s recently released double- decker hemming tools, offered in four different sizes, allow the operator to bend acute angles and flatten on a single toolset.

Still, the tooling options aren’t endless. Say a job comes up that requires specialized tooling that doesn’t work with ATC technology. "You can now use the ATC as a stand-alone machine," Nowak said, "either over half of the bed or over its full length."

He cautioned, however, that the system should be used only occasionally as a conventional press brake, since repeatedly setting up the system manually defeats the purpose of investing in the ATC in the first place.

EVERY MOVEMENT COUNTS

Imagine an ATC operator processing dozens of orders in just half a shift. To do so effectively and accurately calls for a multitude of technological innovations to work together.

Bends are simulated offline, and blank sizes are developed based on the

actual

press brake tooling the shop uses. Robust communication practices are the norm, especially among engineering, brake programmers, and operators. Programmers view the simulation and can visualize what it will take for an operator to form a piece from beginning to end. They know operators will be able to slide a blank edge solidly against the backgauge for the first bend and remove the piece easily after the last bend.

They know the value of worker ergonomics—a big reason they invested in ATC technology in the first place. Tools are heavy, and the less heavy lifting operators do, the better. They realize the value of sheet followers, which support heavy or awkward blanks through the bend cycle. Workers heaving large workpieces swinging upward through the bend cycle creates an ergonomic nightmare, not to mention accuracy and repeatability issues.

Programmers still make best use of fixed-height punches and common-shut-height dies (that is, dies designed around the fixed height of the punch), allowing operators to form a multistaged setup that, thanks to batch processing from offline bend software, can form multiple pieces with little adjustment. Bend department leads observe how operators move from bend to bend, following the foot pedal to the correct tool station.

Such technology makes setups inconsequential. And because software can simulate the most complicated staged setups, part "unitization" becomes a possibility. Two or more previously welded pieces now can be "unitized" into one formed part.

Amid all this, the ATC handles dozens of dynamically nested jobs from blanking. It is designed to make best use of Little’s Law, reducing WIP and shortening overall flow time, from raw stock to the shipping dock.
