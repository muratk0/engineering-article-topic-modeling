# Selecting Folding and Press-Brake Processes for Sheet-Metal Bending

## Process Characteristics

A finger brake clamps sheet material while a bending leaf folds the unsupported section. Removable fingers can provide clearance for formed sides, which can suit trays, boxes, pans, covers, flashing, prototypes, and repair parts. Its practical advantages may include flexible setup and support for large light-gauge panels during folding.

A press brake uses a punch and die to form a bend. Bend angle and inside radius depend on tooling geometry, ram position, material behavior, die opening, and applied force. Interchangeable tooling can support a wider range of profiles, including work involving offsets, hems, return flanges, or multi-bend sequences. However, machine type alone does not establish capability: manual and automated versions of both process types can differ substantially in force, control, tooling, and repeatability.

Selection should therefore be based on the required parts and process conditions rather than on a broad machine label.

## Evaluate Usable Capacity, Not Only Published Ratings

A published thickness rating is a starting point rather than a complete capability statement. Its relevance depends on the tested material, bend length, working width, bend radius, tooling, and operating conditions. Material strength cannot be inferred from thickness alone; stronger materials can require more bending force than lower-strength materials of the same thickness.

Bending resistance rises with thickness and material strength, while longer bends increase total load. A short test piece may perform differently from a full-width panel because deflection can affect the resulting angle along the bend.

For press-brake work, die opening affects both required force and resulting bend radius. The source presents a V-die opening of roughly eight times material thickness as a common starting point for air bending, but final tooling and force selection must follow the applicable machine and tooling documentation.

Flange size is also a limiting factor. A finger brake needs sufficient material to clamp securely, while a press-brake flange must seat appropriately on the selected die. Tight radii can increase cracking risk, and springback may require controlled overbending to achieve the specified released angle. Grain direction can also affect cracking, springback, and radius requirements.

Usable capacity should be assessed as a combination of material, thickness, bend length, grain direction, radius, flange size, angular tolerance, and production quantity. A machine that can form one sample may not safely or consistently produce a full batch.

## Check Geometry and Bend Sequence

Force capacity alone does not ensure that a part can be formed. Previously bent walls, return flanges, hems, narrow flanges, and bends on multiple planes can interfere with insertion, clamping, rotation, bending, or removal.

Finger brakes can provide clearance through gaps between removable fingers, but formed features may still contact the clamping beam, bending leaf, frame, or remaining fingers. Removing fingers for clearance can also reduce support for later bends.

Press-brake tooling can create additional clearance options, but interference remains possible. Punch shape, die width, available daylight, stroke, throat depth, bed length, and tool clearance all affect whether a sequence is feasible. A completed part can also become trapped around tooling after a final bend. Additional force does not resolve a clearance problem and can increase the hazard.

Before committing to a production method, review the complete bend sequence: insertion, positioning, clamping, bending, rotation, and extraction. Full-size trials using the intended material and tooling are more informative than a single improvised sample. Qualified personnel should perform setup, tooling selection, and validation in accordance with the machine manual and applicable safety requirements.

## Compare Production Time and Cost

Batch size matters because setup time and cycle time affect different processes differently. A process with short setup may suit occasional work, while a process with longer setup and more repeatable positioning may become preferable as quantities or part complexity increase.

A useful comparison is:

`Total production time = setup time + quantity × cycle time per acceptable part`

Cycle time should include loading, alignment, tool or finger changes, part rotation, measurement, angle correction, unloading, and finishing. Measuring only the bending stroke or leaf movement can omit significant labor.

The article gives an illustrative comparison in which one process has lower setup time but longer time per acceptable part, while another has higher setup time but lower per-part time. The crossover results from accumulated cycle-time differences, not from a universal batch-size or bends-per-part threshold.

Cost comparisons should include setup labor, run labor, scrap, rework, consumables, and relevant lost production capacity. Divide costs by accepted parts rather than total attempts. Manual fatigue, springback, material variation, and repeated alignment decisions can contribute to drift and rework in multi-bend work.

## Safety and Process Controls

Press-brake operations involve concentrated force at the punch and die. Guarding, protective controls, material support, handling methods, tooling condition, and operator training must be appropriate to the machine, tooling, workpiece, and applicable regulations. Long panels may require stable support or coordinated handling.

Tooling, holders, alignment, backgauges, hydraulic systems, controls, and safety devices require inspection and maintenance. Do not substitute improvised attachments for properly rated and securely retained tooling. Installation, repair, electrical work, hydraulic work, rigging, and machine adjustments should be carried out only by qualified personnel following manufacturer documentation and site safety procedures.

## Practical Selection Framework

Assess recurring parts as complete requirements, including:

- Material type, grade, thickness, and grain direction
- Maximum bend length and flange dimensions
- Box depth, wall geometry, hems, offsets, and return flanges
- Required radii, angles, and tolerances
- Bend count and production frequency
- Setup, handling, inspection, scrap, and rework requirements
- Safe removal of the completed part

A folding process may be appropriate for accessible, light-gauge, low-volume work that tolerates manual positioning and frequent design changes. A press-brake process may be appropriate where force, controlled positioning, tooling flexibility, repeatability, or throughput are required. For infrequent difficult parts, external processing can be evaluated using complete landed cost, lead time, quality requirements, and schedule risk.

The objective is not to select a machine category in isolation. It is to establish a process that can produce the required part safely, repeatedly, and with verified geometry and handling feasibility.
