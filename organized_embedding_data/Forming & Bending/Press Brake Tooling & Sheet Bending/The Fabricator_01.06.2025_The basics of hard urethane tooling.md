Hard Urethane Basics

You can find hard urethane press brake dies in just about every standard size and profile: single or double V, acute 30 or 45 degrees, standard 88- or 90-degree V dies, and custom dies.

Off-the-shelf urethane dies are cast or sometimes extruded into standard sizes and profiles. Most are made from a 120-durometer urethane, one of the hardest and strongest in the urethane family (see

).

Deflection Under Load

All urethane deflects under a load; the lower the durometer value (softer), the greater the deflection. As a brief aside, if you want to mitigate deflection, you might consider 3D printing, which opens up a host of possibilities for press brake tooling material.

You can 3D print certain urethanes, but you’ll also experience the same deflection issues as you would with traditional urethane tools. Plus, they can be hard to work with. 3D printing alternatives include PLA, PETG, Nylon, and carbon fiber. Most of these materials are much stronger than the material used in commonly available urethane tools, so they deflect less.

Regardless, when it comes to traditional urethane tools, deflection under load is the reason you will not find urethane punches with small nose radii. The load exerted through a small punch nose radius is so concentrated that a 0.032-in. radius could become a 0.062-in. radius in the part.

Urethane Round Punches

All this said, you can have large-radii urethane punches, because the forming load is spread over a much wider area. Imagine a typical round punch, but instead of metal you have a series of concentric rings that you build up to your desired punch radius (see

Figure 2

).

Depending on your needs, you could make each ring with urethane of the highest durometer. A good friend of mine made a round urethane punch, with each concentric ring color coded. Instead of signifying a specific durometer, each color represented a different punch radius.

Why Use Hard Urethane Tools?

Urethane dies are weaker than steel and will fail under a small tonnage load. Considering this, if hard urethane dies are not as strong as their steel counterpoints, why use them? The main reason is to prevent die witness marks, workpiece marring, and excess scrap in the work area.

Another big reason is price. A double-V in urethane can be a fraction of the cost of the same precision-ground steel tool. If you need a certain die for an unusual low-quantity job that you’re not likely to see again, using a urethane die might be the way to go.

The mar-free forming abilities of a high-durometer urethane die will go away under heavy use, as the die will become impregnated with grime, galling, or residue from any material finish processes. The embedded material will build up and eventually start to mark the finish on your part.

That said, just because the die has started marking your parts does not mean the die is garbage. Quite the contrary. They still have some life left, as long as you use them on parts that can be formed at low tonnages, and where minor scratching is not a problem.

If you overload a urethane tool, it will fracture, crack, or break in two. The tool also might break simply because it’s worn out—but that’s OK. You got good use out of a tool that you didn’t pay much for, so just set it aside and get another piece of tooling.

Steel Dies and Retainer Boxes

So, you like the idea of mar-free forming, but do your forming pressures exceed the tonnage rating of high-durometer urethane tools? If so, you might try an alternative solution: What if you used your V die as the retainer box? These hybrid setups include a mid-range (soft) pad insert placed within a standard, hard-urethane die that acts as the retainer box. It works rather well. The setup uses greater tonnage than air forming but less than bottom bending.

The tonnage required to form a given piece depends on several factors, including the width of the V die and the durometer of the pad (how hard it is). Most important, you must ensure the pad is at least 10 times the volume of the punch and material being formed.

For instance, to form a small piece of 16-ga. mild steel, you might place a urethane insert into a 0.709-in. die. That’s far greater than the 0.394-in. tool you’d typically use. You use that larger die to meet the 10-times volume rule. We’ll cover exactly how to calculate this in next month’s column.

Using traditional dies, you might see witness marks caused by the part’s interaction with the steel die shoulders—even if you’re forming with the correct die opening. Now imagine the same application using a hybrid setup, with a hard urethane die and a soft urethane pad. The urethane die is larger than its steel counterpart, to ensure you can use a pad with sufficient volume. If you set it up right, you’ll see no witness marks with the larger die and pad. Why? Because the forming is done in the urethane.

Bottoming Without High Tonnage

Forming in urethane produces an inside radius similar to the radius you’d achieve with bottom bending. You are forcing the material and punch nose together, causing the workpiece to have an inside bend radius equal to the punch radius.

This means you will be able to calculate accurate bend deductions and bend allowances while using much less tonnage than a traditional bottoming operation.

Other Uses for Urethane

Die protection or sheet urethane can be used between the die shoulders and the part to ensure a bend free of witness marks. Urethane can also be used as a wing replacement or insert to enhance the mar-free properties of winged style of forming, where rotating inserts placed in the die support the sheet throughout the bend cycle (see

There is a lot to be said for forming with urethane tooling, and there is still a little more to learn, especially when it comes to specific calculations you can make when forming with urethane tools.
