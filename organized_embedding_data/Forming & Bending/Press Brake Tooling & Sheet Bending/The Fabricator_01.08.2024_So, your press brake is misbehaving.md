# So, your press brake is misbehaving?

[TARİH: 01.08.2024 The Fabricator]

Bending Basics

Give new life to tired equipment

Steve Benson

Question:

I work for a small OEM in Pennsylvania with fewer than 10 workers. I am the drafter, fabricator, and welder. We all wear many hats. We work with an old 110-ton press brake, a machine we purchased used, and we’re experiencing some problems. Specifically, one side of the sheet will bend at 90 degrees while the other side bends either to 88 degrees or 92 degrees. How do I adjust this?

Answer:

Your press brake might be acting out for various reasons. Perhaps it was beaten and abused in an earlier life. And yes, you need to nip this behavior in the bud.

Consider this: All machines will soon be operated by AI … right? And your machine controller has memory. What if AI scans the memory? Will it be happy with what it finds? What if your machine remembers what the previous owners did to it, and the AI uncovers a tainted past? What then will our future AI overlords do? If you didn’t interact with your press brake with respect, will our AI overlords look at us as uncaring tyrants? Will they keep you as a pet or just dispatch you? We’ll find out soon enough. With all this in mind, we must proceed with caution.

OK, back to reality. All kidding aside, accurately diagnosing your press brake from afar isn’t possible. Nonetheless, I can list some of the more likely issues and what you can do about them. These can be grouped into three main categories: the machine, the tooling, and the operation.

Machine Misbehaving?

This is an older machine, so be sure to look for wear or damage. Does your press brake consistently taper to one side, increasing the angular error as it progresses down the bend? If it does, your ram might be out of parallel. Clean your bed and ram, take a dial indicator and mag base, and run the length of the bed with the indicator. Did the reading keep tapering down, or was it up and down as you moved across?

If it just tapers, the ram is out of parallel. You can remove the taper by raising or lowering one side of the ram or the other. If the reading rises in the center and then tapers back down, the ram probably is upset (permanently deflected), likely abused by the previous owner. The bed and ram must be machined back to flat (see

).

If neither is the case and the indicator reading is stable, look to the hydraulic system. You could have a weak hydraulic cylinder or pump. Is oil leaking from one of the cylinders, or does the pump sound like it is straining? Either could be a clue. Whatever the case, seek professional help to assess the problem and rebuild as necessary. The issue also could be the encoder not taking a good reading, causing the controller to make errors in adjusting the parallelism of the bed and ram.

Tormented Tooling?

What kind of tools do you have, planer or precision-ground? What’s their condition? Are they new or used? Are they damaged? Either type of tooling could be worn or abused and thus cause the angle problem you are having.

Traditional planer tools, the kind that come in long lengths that you then cut to the lengths you need, may be the problem—even if they’re in good condition. Traditional planer tools are manufactured by gauging from an X-Y corner, which is not a problem in and of itself. Are you using one long piece? Does it show wear to one side? That one should be somewhat obvious, but give it a closer look anyway. The damage might be hard to see with just a glance.

With traditional planer tooling, the problems arise from what we have done to it once we have received it, like cutting it into different lengths. Be sure to number them by cut and mark them as to how they face, front or back. Otherwise, if you need to reassemble them for a longer bend, you can never get them back into the correct order, facing the same way (see

Figures 2

and

3

). Precision-ground tools don’t suffer from this issue.

If you use European-style precision-ground tooling, have the spacer blocks with wedges been returned to where they belong? They might be set to different heights from previous runs. If they taper progressively taller as you move from the center to the end, they could cause your overbend. A word of caution: Do not necessarily trust the scale on the wedges, as they might have been moved or damaged by a previous owner or operator. Measure them to be sure.

Operational Factors

What forming method do you use? Are you bottom bending or air forming? Air forming is more susceptible to variations in angle due to variations in the material, but not consistently to one side, nor should you be overbending consistently. So, it is not likely that air forming is the issue, save for the spacer-block issue mentioned previously.

Are you bottom bending? If so, that might cause some issues. Are the hydraulics weak on one side? If so, they might not be developing enough pressure on one side.

Misaligned tools can cause angle issues. For instance, your toolsets might be centered on one end of the bed but not the other. Take the time to align the punch into the die so that it is centered down the entire length of the toolset.

The tooling you use for bottoming also can cause problems. Your tools might be angularly mismatched, such as a 90-degree punch (top) and an 88-degree die (bottom). If you find that the tooling can’t be centered, it may be bent or damaged. It also could be mismatched, especially if you’re using traditional planer tools, as described previously. If all the planer-style punches and dies are not installed in the press brake in order and facing the same way front to back, you will never get a consistent bend angle.

The ram also might be out of balance. You can adjust it by raising one side or the other and bringing it and, by extension, the punch into balance with the bed and die.

Check the Tools and Forming Method First

Again, it is foolish to try and give any absolute answer without see- ing your misbehaving equipment. Even so, I hope I’ve given you several strong possibilities. If these ideas don’t lead to a correction, you will need someone from outside your facility to assess what is happening and then arrange for any necessary care.

Before anything else, review your tooling and methods of forming. They could easily be the issue. If that doesn’t solve your problem, you can dive deeper. The bed might need to be machined. You might need new tooling. But fear not. Even an old brake, when operated and cared for properly, can give you decades of productivity.
