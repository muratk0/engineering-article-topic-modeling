# Press Brake Hemming: Tooling Compatibility, Controlled Closure, and Defect Diagnosis

## Hemming is not a generic flattening operation

A press-brake hem is formed by pre-bending an edge and then closing it with a flattening operation. Unlike air bending, flattening brings upper and lower tooling into broad contact with folded sheet between them. The available clearance, material response, tooling geometry, alignment, and machine loading therefore have a strong effect on the result.

A hem should not automatically be treated as a zero-gap, fully crushed fold. Some materials or part requirements may require a remaining internal radius or teardrop-shaped space. Attempting to force full closure when the material cannot tolerate it can thin the outside bend area, work-harden the material, and contribute to cracking.

## Use compatible upper and lower tooling

Flattening depends on the interaction of both tools. A flat or otherwise purpose-matched upper tool distributes load differently from an acute punch. Using an acute punch where a broad flattening contact is required can concentrate force at the bend apex, score the part, cause uneven closure, or damage tooling.

Tooling compatibility is more than a matching overall load rating. Contact width, support geometry, usable working area, and alignment all matter. A narrow upper tool acting on a wider lower block can concentrate load locally even if each component has a nominal rating. Tooling and press-brake capacity must be evaluated as a complete setup under the applicable machine manual, tooling documentation, and qualified engineering or supervisory review.

## Control closure rather than forcing bottoming

The article describes flattening as a process in which force can rise sharply near final closure because contact area increases and the folded material is compressed. Standard air-bending estimates may not represent the loading conditions of a hemming operation.

The required final position should account for actual material thickness, batch variation, pre-bend geometry, desired hem shape, and the tooling design. Excessive closure can overload the machine or tooling and can damage the part. Conversely, insufficient closure may leave an open hem.

Stroke limits, tonnage monitoring where available, and repeatable positioning can support controlled operation. However, adjustment of stroke, shut height, controls, or machine parameters must be performed only under the machine manufacturer's procedures and by trained, authorized personnel. Operators should not improvise machine intervention or use an apparent lack of closure as justification for increasing force without verifying the complete setup.

## Maintain alignment and clean seating surfaces

Uneven hems—for example, a hem closed at one end and open at the other—may indicate a parallelism, seating, clamping, or load-distribution issue. Contamination or damage at tooling interfaces can prevent tools from seating correctly. Long tools and off-center work can also make alignment and load placement more important.

Before changing process settings, inspect the condition and seating of the tooling according to approved procedures. Confirm that the work is placed within permitted machine loading conditions and that the tooling is supported and aligned as required. Do not compensate for a suspected alignment problem simply by increasing tonnage.

## Interpret common hem defects

Visible defects can help identify process variables that need review:

- **Cracks or whitening at the outside bend:** May indicate excessive strain, unsuitable material condition, an overly tight closure, or an insufficient retained radius.
- **Open or flared hems:** May indicate springback, an unsuitable pre-bend condition, incomplete closure, or inconsistent alignment.
- **Scoring, galling, or transferred marks:** May indicate contamination, damaged tool faces, friction during closure, or unsuitable surface protection for the material and finish requirement.
- **Uneven closure along the length:** May indicate tool misalignment, poor seating, uneven load distribution, or machine deflection under the applied load.
- **Parts sticking in the lower tool:** May indicate insufficient clearance, friction, surface condition, or a tooling design not suited to the part geometry.

These signs should lead to a documented review of material, tooling condition, pre-bend geometry, machine capacity, alignment, and approved process limits.

## Consider process suitability

Flattening dies may not be suitable for every part. Large panels, difficult-to-support workpieces, narrow flanges, cosmetic surfaces, and shapes that cannot be safely pre-bent may require a different approved hemming method. Rotary or wipe-style methods are identified in the article as alternatives for some geometries, but method selection should be based on the part design, material, machine envelope, tooling documentation, safety requirements, and qualified process evaluation.

## Safety and process control

Hemming can involve high localized loads, pinch hazards, heavy workpieces, and potential tool or part damage. Follow the press-brake manual, tooling instructions, applicable guarding and handling procedures, and site safety rules. Use trained personnel for setup, inspection, maintenance, and any machine or tooling adjustment. Record validated tooling combinations and process limits so that repeat production does not depend on trial-and-error force increases.
