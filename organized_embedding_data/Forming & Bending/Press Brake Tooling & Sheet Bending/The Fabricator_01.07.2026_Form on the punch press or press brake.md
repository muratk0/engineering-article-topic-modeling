# Form on the punch press or press brake?

Forming Strategy

The answer isn’t always straightforward

By

Tim Heston

F

or years, many fabricators chose to retire their turret or rail-type punch press and opted instead for a laser cutting machine. It eliminated the need for tooling and opened the door for various blank and cutout shapes—not just round or rectangular holes, not just straight lines. The punch press was the past; the laser seemed to be the future.

Today, the punch press has experienced a kind of renaissance. The unprecedented demand for IT infrastructure calls for a lot of sheet metal, much of it with formed components like electrical knockouts, extruded holes, and louvers—shapes usually produced with form tools on the punch press. And if fabricators don’t have a punch press, they might invest in special tooling that allows the press brake to act like a punch with form tools: louvers, embosses, clips, extruded holes, and more.

These days, you might see some shops forming shapes on the press brake that could have been formed on the punch, even though they have plenty of punches in the blanking department. The strategy seems counterintuitive until you delve deeper into some operational details.

In most cases, it makes sense to use a form tool on a punch or punch/laser combination machine. You eliminate secondary operations and allow the punch to add more value. Even so, finding the optimal forming strategy isn’t always straightforward.

BRAKE VERSUS PUNCH PRESS

In an interview earlier this year, Joe Amico, vice president of EVS Metal in Riverdale, N.J., described several robotic bending cells. Robots were bending flanges that extended from interior cutouts in the blank—short flanges that could have been formed on one of EVS’ many turret punches. Why weren’t they being formed during the blanking process?

As Amico explained, the answer came down to stackability. Certain forms on the punch press make it difficult to stack parts in a reliable way. For instance, the flanges in question forced the stack of blanks to shift to one direction slightly. The robot could have been programmed to account for this, but rather than complicate matters, the programmer chose to just form those flanges on the brake.

For short flanges, you can use either a form tool on the punch (using a rotating "Pacman" component that folds up the flange) or a conventional punch and die set on the brake. Aside from this, most forms produced by the punch press can’t be made on a press brake without a special tool. Glen Shuldes, applications engineer at Wilson Tool International in White Bear Lake, Minn., prefers not to call them "punch form tool adapters." The company instead brands them as Brake Partners. "We’re not just taking the same tool out of the punch press and putting them on a press brake," he said. "The tool itself might be made in a completely different way."

PUNCHING VERSUS FORMING

The most obvious difference includes the separation of cutting and forming. The punch press makes many forms with a quick succession of cutting and forming. The lance-and-form tool is a prime example. The punch first lances the material and then creates the formed tab, creating a clip that aids in assembly.

Press brakes aren’t designed to endure the shock that comes when a tool punches through sheet metal. So, if a form’s final shape has both cut and formed elements to it—lances, louvers, extruded holes—the cutting must be performed elsewhere. In conventional applications, a fabricator performs the necessary cutting on the laser, then makes the form on the press brake using tools designed only to form. These special tools on the press brake might look like their form-tool cousins on the punch press, but the tools are not designed to punch.

That distinction between cutting and forming is important, Shuldes said, adding that knowing how each tool performs can help a shop optimize each tool’s strengths and minimize their weaknesses. Doing so reveals just how different tool design can be between the punch press and press brake.

EXTENDING TOOL LIFE

First, the punch press can process material that’s only so thick. So, what if you have a lance-and-form requirement in stock that’s too thick for the punch, or perhaps it’s made of tough material that accelerates tool wear? Here, a fabricator might choose to cut the lance on a laser, then move the piece to the brake to create the form.

Shuldes described another scenario involving a lance-and-form shovel clip. Punching shovel clips occasionally isn’t a problem, but as volumes rise, tool wear can be an issue. "For certain jobs, the [lancing action] can be very hard on the tool. The tool needs to cut around the perimeter of that form before it performs the actual forming."

So, in this case, rather than use the punch press to form the clips in one operation—stressing the tool and perhaps the process as a whole—a fabricator might choose to cut the shovel clip perimeter on the laser, then form it with a special tool on the press brake.

DIFFERENT WORK ENVELOPES

Punch press work envelopes don’t have much room vertically (in the Z direction), so tall flanges can be an issue. The press brake, of course, has plenty of room for taller forms. If material can handle the elongation, a press brake can form a variety of tall forms, like embosses or louvers.

Downward forms on the punch press can be risky as well. They’re technically possible with brush tables, but they introduce the potential for issues as the clamps move the sheet throughout the punch program.

Shuldes described one application in which a part required both positive and negative embosses. Fabricators have been known to "make it work," especially with negative embosses that don’t protrude too deeply into the table’s brush bristles. But these negative embosses were just too deep to "make it work" reliably. So, the shop chose to form the positive emboss on the punch and then, using the Brake Partner tool, formed the negative emboss on the press brake.

Embosses show how different, on a fundamental level, the punch machine stroke is from its press brake cousin. The punch press stroke is, obviously, optimized for punching. Punching’s goal is to break through the sheet as efficiently as possible. The stripper holds the metal as the punch tool strikes through. In some ways, it’s the opposite of air forming on a press brake, where every form occurs with just three points of contact: at the punch tip and on the two shoulders of the die.

With an emboss tool on the punch press, the press drives the punch to the metal surface but stops before the metal shears. Instead, the stripper holds the material as the punch forms the emboss. The punch press has some depth control, but it doesn’t compare with the press brake ram’s programmable stroke.

This means that an emboss tool—or other tool originally designed for the punch press—can be used in a different way on the press brake. Shuldes described one application in which one emboss tool on the press brake created embosses of different depths and shapes, depending on how far the emboss tool pushed the metal into the die cavity. The same variation could occur with an extruded hole, with walls much higher than could be practically formed on a punch press.

"In this case, you can form multiple heights with one tool instead of using multiple tools," Shuldes said.

The press brake also offers more space laterally, across the machine’s bed, while a punch form tool can be only so wide. Shuldes described an extruded hole to explain. A tool to form a small one fits easily within the punch press’s toolholder—but a larger one, not so much. In these cases, a laser can cut a large hole, then a special press brake tool can form the large extrusion around it.

PRESS BRAKE LIMITATIONS

While the press brake has a programmable stroke, the punch press works with material being moved by clamps, all under computer numerical control. This is what makes nibbling possible, cutting a long slot or other profile through multiple hits.

It’s also what makes progressive forming with form tools possible. To form a progressive louver, a punch form tool makes multiple hits to create louvers of different lengths. As Shuldes explained, this kind of form really can’t be transferred to the press brake. Yes, the press brake can form a louver, as long as the cut lines have already been created by blanking machine (usually a laser). But unlike a progressive-louver operation on a punch press, a press brake cannot create louvers of varying widths with multiple hits from a single toolset. A single-hit louver tool designed to create a specific louver geometry usually is the only option.

The punch press’s clamps moving the sheet also make contoured offsets or stiffening ribs possible. Special press brake tools can create those offsets in a single hit. And like the emboss with different heights, the height and radius of the offset form can change depending on how far the press brake ram strokes.

But the press brake can’t easily create a contoured offset like a punch press’s wheel tool can. That wheel tool also has just a narrow point of contact with the sheet metal, which limits the required tonnage. A press brake machine and its tools must be able to withstand significant tonnage to create a sharp offset over a long bend length.

Also, the function of some form tools on the punch really can’t be transferred to the press brake. Shuldes cited electrical knockouts as a prime example. A knockout tool on the punch press pushes the metal and leaves small uncut tabs. The result is a slug that assemblers (or the final customer) can knock out by hand to string wiring or other components through.

When they ship from the fabricator, however, "these pieces with knockouts usually need to be watertight," Shuldes said, adding that, theoretically, a fabricator could laser-cut the knockout perimeter, leaving those tabs in place, then form the knockout geometry on the press brake. "But those probably would be unacceptable, because you’d leave a gap" between the knockout slug and the cut perimeter.

In this application, a louver tool on the punch press performed a progressive forming operation to create louvers of different lengths. Such forming action isn’t possible on the press brake.

An electrical knockout, formed on a punch press, is one operation that, depending on the job requirements, usually can’t be transferred to a press brake.

A NUANCED PICTURE

In most cases, forming on the punch press seems like a no-brainer—if a punch press is available. Special tooling on the press brake, emulating the functionality of punch-form tools, makes obvious sense next to a laser-only blanking department. It’s certainly much easier than sending a job out to another shop that has a punch press.

But there’s no one-size-fits-all approach. Consider again EVS Metal’s strategy to bend short flanges on the robotic brake. Completing those flanges in the punch press would add more value in blanking and, theoretically, make less work for the forming department. Sure, EVS’ robotic press brake cells offer the ability to stack parts vertically, spaced apart to account for the forms made on the punch press. But the operation can stack more blanks horizontally on a pallet.

Reliable, repeatable part presentation in robotic bending made forming with the punch press less practical. In this case, and in most others in metal fabrication, looking at the big picture—from blank to form to weld and beyond—can make a world of difference.
