## Scope and Safety Priority

Preventive maintenance for a press brake should be based on documented condition evidence rather than on whether the machine completed its last production cycle. Normal operation does not by itself demonstrate that hydraulic components, motion systems, tooling, or safeguards remain in acceptable condition.

The machine manufacturer’s current manual, the machine-specific risk assessment, and applicable safety requirements take priority over any general maintenance schedule. Safety-system testing, stored-energy control, internal electrical work, hydraulic pressure work, accumulator work, adjustments, and calibration must be performed only under approved procedures by appropriately qualified personnel.

## Use Condition Evidence and Trends

Establish repeatable baseline observations and compare later results against them. Useful evidence may include operating hours, relevant cycle counts, temperatures measured under comparable conditions, filter-warning status, oil-analysis results, alarms, leaks, motion changes, and safeguard-test results.

Oil level alone is not a complete condition assessment. It does not establish fluid cleanliness or reveal water, entrained air, viscosity change, additive depletion, internal wear debris, or filter restriction. Changes in oil condition, pump noise, temperature, motion quality, pressure demand, alarms, leakage, foaming, or bend consistency should be investigated using recorded evidence. Such signs indicate a need for diagnosis; they do not identify a cause by themselves.

For oil analysis, collect samples from the designated point under comparable machine conditions with clean sampling equipment. Trend results such as particle count, water, viscosity, oxidation, and wear metals against prior samples and applicable limits. Confirmed contamination, wrong-fluid use, water ingress, or debris introduced during service requires containment and investigation rather than waiting for the next scheduled sample.

## Plan Work by the Earliest Applicable Trigger

Schedule each maintenance activity according to the earliest applicable trigger: the manufacturer’s operating-hour limit, calendar interval, condition warning, confirmed contamination event, or oil-analysis result. A calendar schedule should not override an active warning, and low use does not necessarily eliminate calendar-based inspections because aging, moisture exposure, oxidation, corrosion, and seal deterioration can continue while a machine is idle.

Record the model and serial configuration, inspection point, machine-hour reading, relevant cycle count, completion date, next due date, condition readings, responsible role, corrective action, and machine release decision. Confirm what the hour counter measures before using it for scheduling. If its reading is unreliable, document a conservative estimate and repair the counter.

Separate hydraulic and mechanical work into machine-specific tasks. Filters, fluid, breathers, lubrication points, hoses, cylinder connections, tooling clamps, backgauge components, and safeguards may have different service requirements. Any interval extension should be supported by evidence, risk review, and manufacturer approval where required. Required safeguard tests and legally mandated inspections must not be extended merely because no failure has been observed.

## Routine Operator and Maintenance Observations

Before production, operators should perform the approved functional tests for installed safeguards and record the results. A status indicator is not proof that a protective device will interrupt hazardous motion. If a safeguard response differs from the approved procedure, isolate the machine and obtain qualified evaluation; do not repeatedly reset or retest a failed condition until it appears normal.

Perform visible condition checks according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Observe the hydraulic system at rest and during prescribed startup conditions. Check for leakage, unusual fluid appearance, foaming, damaged exposed cylinder rods, filter warnings, abnormal sound, smoke, burning odor, and unexpected heat. Never use a hand to locate a pressurized leak.

After approved warm-up and safety checks, observe controlled machine movement for unusual hesitation, chatter, drift, synchronization changes, stopping changes, or backgauge irregularities. Capture alarm information before clearing alarms, including the operating phase and relevant machine state.

Inspect tooling, holders, clamps, adapters, and work surfaces for visible damage or poor seating. Confirm that tooling is clean, correctly secured, and suitable for the planned operation. The allowable operating condition is constrained by the applicable ratings of the machine and installed tooling system.

At planned intervals, clean accessible areas using methods that do not drive contamination into seals, guides, reservoirs, or electrical equipment. Lubricate only with the specified material and quantity. Inspect accessible hoses, external cables, connectors, covers, guards, cooling paths, and motion components for damage, contamination, poor routing, or inadequate support.

## Stop-Work, Isolation, and Release Control

Use clear machine states such as released, restricted, and out of service. Missing safety-critical evidence should prevent release. A non-safety abnormality may require defined restrictions, monitoring, ownership, and a correction deadline. Unsafe conditions require shutdown and isolation.

Examples requiring immediate stop and qualified investigation include failed safeguards, uncontrolled or unexpected movement, unexpected restart, significant hydraulic leakage, damaged hoses, structural cracks or deformation, persistent overheating, unstable pressure, severe foaming, metallic debris, or repeated critical warnings.

Before entering the die area, removing guards, opening hydraulic connections, or exposing electrical components, apply the site lockout/tagout procedure for all relevant energy sources. These may include electrical, hydraulic, pneumatic, gravitational, accumulator, and connected-equipment energy. A stop control does not by itself establish a zero-energy state. Use only the manufacturer-approved restraint, dissipation, verification, and powered-diagnostic procedures.

## Annual Review and Continuous Improvement

At the manufacturer-defined intervals, qualified personnel should complete required safety validation, condition assessment, and inspection of structural, hydraulic, electrical, motion, cooling, and lubrication systems. Preserve measurements before any approved correction so that drift is not hidden by later adjustments.

Review alarms, oil reports, filter findings, repairs, safeguard failures, bend corrections, downtime, and overdue tasks together. Repeated events can indicate a common underlying issue. Update the maintenance plan after changes in workload, contamination events, major repairs, relocation, control updates, or safeguarding modifications. Machines with different drive technologies require the maintenance tasks applicable to their installed systems.

A complete maintenance record supports an informed return-to-service decision. Each recorded inspection, measurement, function test, and corrective action should show whether the machine remains suitable for its approved operating condition.
