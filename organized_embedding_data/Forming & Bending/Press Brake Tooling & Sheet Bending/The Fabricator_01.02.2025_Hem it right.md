# Hem it right

[TARİH: 01.02.2025 The Fabricator]

Bending

Choosing the best press brake tooling strategy for hemming

By

Tim Heston

WILA USA

I

magine you notice some efficiency metrics in the press brake department that aren’t up to snuff. A bending bottleneck seems to be pushing jobs across the plant behind schedule. What’s going on?

You walk to the brake department, where you see piles of work-in-process. Stacks of panels of multiple thicknesses sit with an acute bend near one edge—a hem, half formed. At the brakes, you see operators swapping out acute die sets with a flattening die set, then returning to the stack to flatten that acute bend into a hem.

At another bending station, you see two operators lifting a large panel upward, high into the air, all to create that first acute bend. It’s tedious, slow-going, and an ergonomic nightmare.

At yet another brake, you see operators using hammers to knock a shim out from an open hem. That open hem creates an interlocking connection that should ease the assembly operation, if only you could get those parts out of the press brake department.

At another station, you see an operator struggling to hold a workpiece in a flattening die. The sheet keeps popping out toward him before the die closes. Next to him you see piles of scrap parts with cracks along the hem’s outside surface.

This Murphy’s Law scenario is hypothetical, of course, but it helps illustrate just some of the hidden challenges of hemming that can lead to some serious inefficiencies. With modern brakes and air bending processes, even extraordinarily complicated and precise parts can fly through bending without a hitch. But the simplest parts and panels can hinder bending throughput in a serious way if a few hems are overlooked and the tooling strategy doesn’t account for them.

Flattening vs. Hemming

Fabricators might use a flattening die set to hem, but there’s a subtle difference between

flattening

and

hemming

. "Hemming and flattening really are two different processes, and they do different things." That was Steve Brown, bending product manager for Wilson Tool, White Bear Lake, Minn. "For flattening, you really are using a flattening toolset like a heavy hammer. Not only can you flatten a hem, you can straighten material. Again, you’re using it like a hammer."

To create the hem, the brake makes an acute prebend and then commences the final hemming operation. A tool forms the hem partially to create a

teardrop hem

(see

Figure 1

). After you form a

closed hem

, the ram releases pressure and the hem springs open ever so slightly to reveal a near-0 inside radius.

When you

flatten

the hem, the material essentially crushes onto itself, like in a coining or bottoming operation. This effectively creates a 0 inside bend radius. Indeed, the flattening die remains one of the few true "bottoming" or "coining" toolsets used in brake operations dominated by precision air forming.

Flattening tools can form a closed hem and then continue applying pressure (the "big hammer" approach) to flatten. But specialized hemming tools usually do not do the same. They apply enough pressure to create the desired hem, and that’s it.

The act of hemming sounds simple, but the reality is full of subtleties. How a hemming tool approaches that final step of creating the hem changes depending on the hem shape desired and—most critically—the tooling the bending department uses.

Tooling ranges from the most basic—including those "big hammer" flattening die sets—to custom and special toolsets incorporating unique tool shapes, springs, and hydraulics to create even the most complex hem with just a few hits on one toolset.

Which tool to use depends on the application, how much hemming the operation really does, and the

time

those hemming operations consume. Measuring time is key here. A long hem that requires multiple setups can hinder bending throughput in significant ways.

Acute and Flattening Die

The most basic approach to produce a hem is to use an acute die set followed by a flattening die. If tooling has a common shut height, operators can stage acute-bending toolsets—with V dies to accommodate various material thicknesses that might require hemming—next to a flattening toolset. In many cases, though, especially where multiple material thicknesses are involved, operators perform hemming in two setups: one for the acute bending and another with the flattening dies. "This is why we’ve always had efficiency problems when it comes to hemming," Brown said.

Most setups using flattening dies are free forms. That is, operators place the partially formed hem on the flattening dies without backgauging. They simply eyeball the workpiece location before cycling the brake. Backgauge fingers might be able to help the operator hold the piece steady during the ram approach phase, but during bending, those fingers need to get out of the way.

Single-Station Hemming Tools

Single-station hemming tools combine all the hits hemming requires into one toolset. One of the most popular versions incorporates spring-loaded mechanisms (see

Figure 2

).

"For these, the spring pops the center section up. You move the part into the slot. You then come down with the punch and collapse the hem."

That was Don Vander Wall, technical sales engineering team leader at WILA USA, Louisville, Ky. He added that other toolsets provide a shelf or landing on the die shoulder where the operator can make the final bend for the hem (see

Figure 3

). These tools are especially useful because they can do more than hem. If a series of jobs requires an acute-angle bend with no hemming, these hemming tools can be left on the press brake to form them.

Which works best depends on a variety of factors. As a first step, Brown said that shops should look at their hem lengths. Short hems can be bent on a single acute- and flattening-tool setup with matching shut heights. Of course, this approach doesn’t work for hems longer than half the press brake bed. In these cases, operators will have no choice but to tear down the acute tool and replace it with a flattening set to create a hem. In these cases, single-station hemming tools might be a good option.

What single-station hem tool style to choose depends on the application, including the shape of the hem you need to create. Certain special tools have elements that contact the acute flange in a way that maintains a smaller radius as the hem is created. This can change the final hem shape. Again, what works best depends on the material you’re working with and the hem dimensions you need to form.

Also challenging with these tools are applications involving thicker material, like 12 or 14 ga., especially over greater lengths. As Brown explained, the die might be wide enough to form the initial bend, but forming the hem is another matter.

"The thicker material will have a larger [outside] radius, which becomes harder to collapse during the final hem. With the wrong tooling that doesn’t have enough material contact, [the hem] will want to pop out toward the operator. And the longer the part is, the more this happens."

Vander Wall added that the same applies to the tools with the landing on the die shoulder. "This design usually limits you to light-gauge material. It’s not suited for heavier gauge. You have a small landing you use to flatten for the hem. If you’re trying to hem some hard stainless, the material could push back out at you."

About Thrust

The vast majority of hemming applications involve thin material, but some do entail thicker and/or stronger stock that can be hard on a press brake. In these cases, front-to-back thrust can become an issue. Excessive force during flattening can cause the top and bottom tools to shift and twist. Even worse, the operation can overtonnage the machine and cause ram upset, over time permanently bowing the ram and bed.

Thrust usually isn’t a concern for thinner material, but as the materials get thicker and the hems get longer, the force grows. In these cases, thrust-absorbing dies can help. "If you do an occasional hemming job with a lot of thrust, you might be OK," Brown said, "but if you have a product with hems in thick, difficult material, investing in a thrust-absorbing die can be a smart thing to do. You can either do that or replace or repair your press brake. That thrust is going to go somewhere."

"When you get over 14 ga. and try to hem 12-ga. and 3/16-in. material, you can get a lot of thrust," said Vander Wall. "You prebend it to get your acute angle, but then you have a hemming operation that absorbs the side thrust." He described one setup in which a tool moves backward to accommodate the forming forces, absorbing the thrust and saving the press brake from serious damage.

Avoid Cracking

Cracking challenges can crop up as well, especially on hard stainless and aluminums, like 6061-T6. As Brown said, "If you take a hard piece of aluminum and start hemming it, you’ll likely get cracking, even if you’re bending against the grain of the material. But for certain stainless grades, like the hems on stainless steel countertops, those certainly can work."

Vander Wall added that on very thick material, the best approach to avoid cracking is to form a teardrop hem. The geometry still provides edge protection, but it also can be formed without cracking and in a stable fashion. The operator shouldn’t have to strain himself holding the workpiece in position. "With thicker stainless, sometimes you need a few people to hold a large part in place as the tool flattens the hem. That’s not ideal."

In these challenging applications, Larry Boden, product specialist for press brake tooling at Mate Precision Technologies, Anoka, Minn., prefers the teardrop hem as well—especially in stainless. Specifically, he recommends paying attention to the radius formed during the initial acute bend.

"On the acute angle hit, you want the inside radius to match the finished hem radius. When you start to crush it, it rolls, the edge hits the metal, and then starts lifting up at the edge. It looks worse than a teardrop, and you create a gap that can be a cutting hazard. The flatter you try to make it, the more it lifts up. This is why it’s best not to flatten it all the way."

Another challenge, Boden said, is paying attention to which hemming step is being performed, especially on older machines with old controls. Every hem has at least two distinct steps: an acute angle and the flattening operation. "If you’re running the wrong step, you break your tool. So, if you run the flattening step for the acute bend, or if you run the acute bend step when you’re flattening, you’ll break your tool. I’ve seen a lot of broken hemming dies over the years."

Hard material can be challenging to hem, but as Boden explained, even softer material can cause trouble when the radius is small and the hem stretches the material significantly. "In fact, even formable material like aluminum 5052-H32, a material that’s nothing like 6061-T6, will crack under certain situations when you’re hemming it. It happens when you just stretch the material too far."

Dialing In the Hem Shape

To create a teardrop hem with flattening tools requires some operator finesse, backing off the tool before it closes entirely.

"When the radius of the hem starts to crush," Boden said, "it overbends at that point. Once the [sheet edge] contacts the metal, you press it a little farther, you ensure that when the forming pressure is released and it springs back, the sheet edge is still in contact.

"The teardrop hem is by far the easiest to fabricate," Boden continued. "It takes less tonnage and you have less tool wear. But in finishing, sometimes it’s a problem," adding that chemicals can become trapped in the teardrop shape. "Outside of that, though, they’re the easiest to fabricate by far."

To create an open hem is more involved. Considering material variability and springback, most operators choose to flatten the hem around a shim or spacer. Once flattened, they remove the shim and are left with an open hem formed to a specific, repeatable dimension.

"The problem is, they can sometimes crush the material to the point where they can’t get the spacer out," Brown said. "The shorter the hem is, the better that shim approach works. For longer hems, deflection and other issues can cause everything to bind together, which can make it difficult to remove that shim."

He added that if the shim method for open hems becomes a major barrier for throughput, special tools can help form open hems that have a specific gap. Like any other special tool, the decision to invest comes down to part volume and the cost of inefficiency bending those open hems.

Horizontal Hemming

If shops work with large panels with hemmed edges, and if they have the space and resources, they might use a folder or panel bender. Those machines offer the ability to create hems with the panel flat, horizontal to the floor—no more swinging up and supporting sheets to make the hem’s initial acute bend.

Turns out, though, a shop doesn’t need a separate machine to make a bend like this. Special

flat-sheet hem tools

use a wiping action to make the acute flange, but they also allow the operator to keep the panel horizontal to the floor throughout the entire operation. During the first hit, the tool wipes the flange over a hold-down tool to an acute angle. The last hit creates the final hem shape (see

Figure 4

).

Brown added that such tools can be designed to create precise open hems. "The radius of the hem is the strongest, so it’s the last to collapse." This is why, by simply easing off on a flattening die, the teardrop shape forms naturally. The brake simply stops applying force before that radius totally collapses. It’s also why creating an open hem without a shim can be difficult.

With the flat-sheet hem tool, however, the wiping action creates a smaller inside radius on the initial acute-angle form. That small radius helps fold the hem to a tighter open dimension. Then, for the final hit, the metal wraps around a mandrel—effectively, a shim built into the tool itself. Again, whether this makes sense or not depends on an application’s requirements and the part volumes involved. Regardless, Brown said, tooling technology exists to help make those challenging open-hem dimensions faster and more consistently.

Other custom tools can help create unusual hems, like angle hems on the end of sheets, seam hems in the middle of sheets that act as stiffening ribs, and Z-hem clips that create a self-fixturing connection. As always, the application and volume requirements drive the tooling investment decisions. They often need to be tested too. Special tools might be designed to create unique hems in a single hit, with the material springing back to the exact dimension required, but they need to be tested to ensure the material behaves as intended.

When Hemming Volume Grows

As volumes grow, the economic case for more tooling investment becomes stronger, and this includes special systems for hemming. Some tools have air- or hydraulic-actuated components that help the final-stage element of the tool return quickly to its starting position—ideal for shops making hem after hem after hem.

This also applies to hydraulic hemming tables that double as the die holder on the press brake (see

lead image

and

Figure 5

). "This is more for production hemming," Vander Wall said. "You use a lower hydraulic hemming tool as an actual die holder in the press. You leave it in there all the time, in the lowered position. You use and change out your standard tools as needed, and when you need to do a hem, you put your acute punch on the top, and you press a separate foot pedal that actuates the system and moves the hemming tool up. You make your acute bend, then flatten to make your hem."

The Right Tool for the Right Hem

Of course, not every brake needs a hemming table or special tools that can create special forms in a few hits. But some jobs could indeed benefit, especially if an operation suffers from a serious hemming bottleneck.

Having multiple setups to finish a single hemming job is no fun. Neither is supporting a large panel as it swings high into the air for the first acute bend. Neither is hammering out a shim that’s stuck in a long open hem, or struggling to keep a large panel from popping out of a spring-loaded hem tool rated for thinner material. Not every tool fits every application, but finding the right tool, for hemming or anything else at the press brake, can make all the difference in the world.
