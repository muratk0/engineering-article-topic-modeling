# Press Brake Tooling Selection for Air Bending

## Scope and core principle

Press-brake tooling should be selected from the part requirements, material properties, bending method, machine capacity, and interference constraints rather than by choosing tools that visually resemble the finished profile. This is particularly important for multi-bend parts, where variation in an early bend can affect later features and overall dimensions.

For air bending, the sheet is supported at the two shoulders of the V-die while the punch applies force between them. The material does not normally contact the bottom of the die. Consequently, the V-die opening is a major influence on the formed inside radius and required bending force. The punch must provide suitable clearance, accommodate the required overbend, and avoid damaging the material or colliding with formed features.

Bottoming and coining differ from air bending because they use greater force and more closely conform the material to the tooling geometry. Tooling selection and force requirements must therefore be evaluated according to the actual bending method.

## Die opening, radius, and force

The article presents die-opening multiples of material thickness as common starting practices for standard mild steel, but these should not be treated as universal rules. The usable opening depends on material grade, thickness, tensile behavior, grain direction, target radius, flange geometry, die shoulder geometry, bend method, and machine/tooling limits.

A narrower V-die opening may produce a tighter air-bent radius, but it also increases bending force and can increase sensitivity to material variation. A wider opening may reduce force requirements but can result in a larger inside radius and may not support short flanges adequately. The relationship between die opening and required bending force should be checked using the applicable machine and tooling documentation rather than estimated from general rules alone.

The article also notes that short flanges require sufficient support on the die shoulders. If a flange cannot bridge the selected opening securely, it may enter the die opening rather than form correctly. This is a part-geometry constraint that may require an alternative forming method or appropriately designed tooling. Selection of acute or specialized tooling must be assessed by qualified personnel with reference to the part drawing, tooling data, and safe operating limits.

## Punch selection and springback

Metal may spring back after the forming load is removed. The amount of springback can vary with material type, condition, thickness, bend radius, grain direction, and process conditions. A punch angle that exactly matches the intended final angle may not provide enough clearance for overbending in air bending.

Punch selection should therefore consider:

- The required final angle and expected springback.
- Clearance needed to achieve the required overbend.
- Punch nose geometry relative to the material and intended bend condition.
- Potential collisions between the punch body and previously formed flanges.
- The full bend sequence for parts with multiple returns, channels, or enclosed profiles.

The article cautions that an excessively sharp punch tip can concentrate force and crease or damage the material, particularly when bending thicker material. Conversely, punch geometry should not be assumed to control the air-bent inside radius independently of the die opening. Tooling suitability must be confirmed using manufacturer data and approved production procedures.

## Material-dependent variation

Standard setups may not transfer directly across different materials or thicknesses. Higher-strength materials can require more force and may exhibit greater springback than standard mild steel. Material inconsistency, thickness variation, tensile properties, and grain direction can also affect final angle and dimensional consistency.

Using one V-die for a broad range of thicknesses may compromise either bend radius control or safe force levels. A die intended for heavier material may not provide stable results for thin sheet, while a narrow die intended for thin material can create excessive force demand when used with thicker stock. Each job should be evaluated against the actual material specification and the approved bending-capacity information for the press brake and tooling.

## Pre-bend planning and documentation

A useful planning sequence is:

1. Review the drawing for required inside radius, angles, flange dimensions, tolerances, and bend sequence.
2. Identify the material specification and relevant forming characteristics.
3. Select a preliminary die opening that can support the flange geometry while meeting radius and capacity requirements.
4. Verify bending force, tool loading, machine limits, and safety requirements using the machine manual and tooling manufacturer information.
5. Select a punch that provides adequate overbend and part-clearance capability.
6. Check for tool, part, backgauge, and formed-flange interference throughout the bend sequence.
7. Validate the setup through the organization’s approved inspection and first-piece process.
8. Record the validated tool identifiers, positions, bend sequence, material condition, and inspection results for repeat work.

Any setup change, unusual material, short-flange condition, or high-force operation should be reviewed under the machine manual, tooling manufacturer limits, and workplace safety procedures. Tool installation, alignment, force settings, and machine adjustments should be performed only by qualified personnel following approved procedures.
