# Safe Assessment and Escalation for Metal Transfer on Press Brake V-Die Surfaces

## Purpose

Metal transfer on a press brake V-die can mark bent parts and may indicate changing contact conditions at the tooling surface. The priority is to distinguish removable transferred material from damage to the die itself, while avoiding actions that alter the working geometry of the V-groove.

This note provides high-level inspection, safety, verification, and escalation principles. Chemical use, tooling removal, cleaning methods, and any repair work must follow the applicable machine manual, die manufacturer instructions, facility lockout/tagout procedures, chemical safety data sheets, and qualified-personnel requirements.

## Isolate and Inspect Before Taking Action

Before inspecting or handling tooling, place the press brake in a safe condition using the facility’s approved energy-isolation procedure. Tooling is heavy and must be handled, supported, and stored using approved methods. Adequate, stable lighting is important because shadows can hide the boundary between transferred material and damage to the die surface.

Inspect the affected area under low-angle or raking light. Tactile inspection can help identify whether the feature is raised or recessed:

- A raised smear, ridge, or bump may indicate transferred workpiece material or debris.
- A depression, chip, pit, gouge, or rolled edge may indicate damage to the die substrate.
- Loose debris, lubricant residue, scale, or other contamination may require a different response than adhered metal transfer.

Do not assume that every visible mark is removable contamination. A recessed defect cannot be cleaned away, and attempts to abrade it may further change the V angle, shoulder, radius, or surface condition.

## Protect the Working Surface

The article emphasizes that aggressive mechanical removal can damage a precision bending surface. Avoid unapproved grinding, filing, rotary wire brushing, carbide cutting, or hard scraping on the die’s working surfaces. Such methods can remove die material as well as transferred metal, leave scratches, and change the contact condition between the sheet and tooling.

Cleaning should be limited to methods approved for the specific tooling and contaminant. If a chemical product is considered, verify that it is compatible with the die material, coating, and transferred material. Follow the product label, safety data sheet, tooling manufacturer guidance, and site environmental and waste-handling rules. Use only the specified dwell time, rinsing or neutralization method, drying procedure, ventilation, and personal protective equipment.

The article describes selective chemical treatment as potentially useful for certain transferred metals, but it also notes an important limitation: removing transfer material does not restore chipped, gouged, or otherwise damaged die steel. Cleaning may reveal the actual condition of the substrate; it does not constitute die repair.

## Stop Conditions and Escalation

Stop field cleaning and remove the die from service for qualified inspection if any of the following are observed:

- The suspected transfer does not respond to the approved cleaning process.
- Removal exposes a crater, pit, chip, or other recessed defect.
- Inspection indicates that the die surface has been scored or altered.
- Material remains embedded at the contact surface.
- The condition cannot be identified confidently as removable contamination.

Tag and document the affected station or tool so it is not returned to production unintentionally. A qualified toolroom, tooling service provider, or die manufacturer should determine whether inspection, measurement, professional reconditioning, recoating, or replacement is appropriate. Shop-floor attempts to blend out or reshape damaged tooling can compromise geometry and may create inconsistent bends or surface marking.

## Verify Before Resuming Production

A visually clean groove is not by itself evidence that the die remains suitable for use. After approved cleaning and reinstallation, conduct a controlled verification consistent with the production and quality procedure.

The article recommends comparing a short test bend made at the affected location with a bend made using a known-clean location, using material representative of the job. Review bend angle, bend-line appearance, and any marking. Reinspect early production parts and the die surface for renewed transfer or emerging defects. If marking, inconsistent results, or fresh buildup recur, stop production at that station and escalate the issue.

## Reducing Recurrence

The source article associates galling risk with friction, surface condition, contamination, and the materials being bent. Practical prevention principles include keeping blanks and tooling contact areas clean, monitoring for early signs of transfer, and selecting lubrication, protective interfaces, or tooling surface treatments only where they are compatible with the process and approved by the relevant procedures.

A brief between-job inspection can help detect drag, debris, or early transfer before it develops into a larger defect. Any recurring condition should be investigated as a process issue rather than addressed solely through repeated cleaning.
