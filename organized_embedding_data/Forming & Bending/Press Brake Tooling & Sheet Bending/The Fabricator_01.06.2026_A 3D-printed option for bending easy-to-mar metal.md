# A 3D-printed option for bending easy-to-mar metal

A commercial 3D printer offers up an alternative to using polyurethane sheets

By

Dan Davis

B

y nature, a metal fabricator is good at figuring out how to do things. When you combine that with a willingness to discover how additive manufacturing can help an average shop, you have the opportunity to learn some important lessons.

Three years ago, Paul DeWys of Forerunner 3D got a call from a metal fabricating customer who had questions about whether 3D-printed press brake tooling could stand up to the rigors of forming on a press brake. The metal fabricator had a job that called for the use of polyurethane material that had to be pulled over the die multiple times during forming. The polyurethane material helped to protect the material’s surface from marring as the ram drove the material into the die’s V opening. Without the use of such material, witness marks could be left on the sheet metal; those marks could be visible through some coatings that would be applied to the part after forming. Press brake operators were using the same section of polyurethane material until it wore out, and then they had to get rid of the old section and pull out a fresh strip across the tooling. The engineer from the metal fabricating company was looking to see how long 3D-printed tooling could last in hopes that it could replace the use of the polyurethane material.

This is exactly the type of job that DeWys likes to tackle as the owner of an additive manufacturing company. After graduating from Ferris State in Big Rapids, Mich., in 2009, he started DeWys Engineering, a design and engineering firm that works with automation companies, agricultural equipment manufacturers, job shops, and injection molding companies. In 2016, DeWys acquired a bankrupt 3D printing business, brought its assets in, and rechristened it Forerunner 3D. The purchase made sense because DeWys had a keen interest in using 3D-printed parts on his customers’ engineering projects.

"Specifically, the customers we go after and we market to are the shop floor and manufacturing guys. No one was really servicing them," DeWys said. "And this has really exploded over the last couple of years as the younger generation gets into these manufacturing and engineering offices."

CAN 3D-PRINTED TOOLING SURVIVE?

Having some knowledge about working with press brakes, DeWys had the metal fabricating customer provide a retainer. He would focus on 3D-printing the die insert. DeWys planned to use nylon 12 (PA 12), a thermoplastic commonly used in additive manufacturing.

"Nylon 12 is like cold-rolled steel in metal fabricating. It’s used all over the place," he said.

As Steve Benson described in his December 2025 Bending Basics column in The Fabricator ("Need a press brake tool right now? Just Print one," p. 30), "[n]ylon’s toughness allows it to deform under stress and then recover, much like urethane. That flexibility spreads the load across a broader area, reducing stress concentrations at the layer lines. It’s also ideal for soft jaws, forming pads, or even flexible die inserts. For custom forming applications—especially when marring must be avoided—nylon tooling can be both practical and gentle on material surfaces."

DeWys said that some metal fabricators might have heard about the use of carbon-fiber nylon for 3D-printing press brake tools. The use of the material makes sense as it has roughly twice the stiffness of unfilled nylon and can withstand higher loads without deflection, according to Benson. But DeWys noted that the carbon is more expensive than nylon 12, which makes the economics a little less attractive for budget-conscious shops.

Forerunner 3D-printed up a 6-in. section of tooling on its HP Multi Jet Fusion machine. The initial goal was to bend some aluminum parts without marring them.

After about 50 bends of 14-ga. aluminum, the 3D-printed tooling still looked to be in good shape. That’s when the experiment ventured off in another direction, and the crew decided to try new and thicker materials, such as 12-ga. stainless steel and carbon steel. After 88 successful bends on the alternative material, the team wanted to shake things up a bit.

"We did some stainless, and it started to wear on the edges a bit more, and then everyone was like, ‘Let’s break it!’" DeWys recalled.

That’s when the team put 0.25-in.-thick hotrolled, pickled and oiled steel on top of the 3D-printed tooling. The tooling survived the first form, but the second one didn’t turn out as successfully. The top edges of the V collapsed under the pressure.

In a test that it did for a metal fabrication customer, Forerunner 3D found that its 3D-printed V die made of nylon 12 survived 50 bends of 14-ga. aluminum.

The 3D-printed brake tooling also survived multiple bends involving 12-ga. steel.

Bending a 0.25-in.-thick steel part finally damaged the 3D-printed part.

Even with the end result, DeWys said he was impressed with the performance of the nylon 12 tooling when working with the thinner gauges.

"You wouldn’t want to bend really thick stuff with it, but if you needed to bend some 10- or 12-ga. aluminum, or even some stainless steel, it would work," he said.

OTHER FAB SHOP APPLICATIONS

DeWys admits he’d like to see more projects like the press brake tooling come Forerunner 3D’s way because they scratch that itch to apply new technology to applications where it hasn’t yet been fully embraced. But he also shared another interesting fabrication application for which 3D-printed components came in handy.

A fabrication shop involved in a lot of prototype work reached out to see if Forerunner 3D could print some nesting blocks to which laser-cut fixtures with welding clamps could be affixed. The shop wanted a faster way to set up weld fixtures on its modular tables instead of spending hours building the setups for a run of 10 to 12 items.

"The question became, ‘How close can I weld to one of these blocks before it starts to destroy it?’" DeWys said.

The block in question, printed out of nylon 12 and on the same HP MJF machine that printed the press brake tooling, was in good shape after a confirmed 3,500 welding cycles. The fabricator continued to use it even beyond that point.

"The nice thing about this application is that spatter doesn’t stick to a plastic part, so you don’t have any of the antispatter issues that you may have when robotic welding and you have to put on those expensive coatings to keep spatter from sticking to nesting surfaces and causing misalignments," DeWys said.

MORE TO COME

The applications don’t end there. Fab shops have embraced 3D printing for creating end-of-arm tooling and grippers. The nylon material is lighter than aluminum and delivers the stiffness and rigidity required for such an application. "We can use 3D-printed parts all over the shop floor," DeWys said.

And he expects that to increase as the generation that grew up with 3D printers at their schools and libraries and even in their own homes takes over leadership roles in manufacturing companies. They are comfortable with additive manufacturing and see it as a real-time answer to a lot of production problems.

DeWys is betting on that. Like metal fabricators that want to deliver quality parts in a tight time frame, he wants to provide those same fab shops with 3D-printed parts that help them maintain on-time deliveries.
