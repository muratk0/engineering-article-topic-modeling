hat press brake tool should you use? Wow. What a simple question. In an ideal world, where every problem has a simple solution, you might think,

I just need to make a 90-degree bend for a relatively simple forming job. Our current tooling seems to work fine. Besides, press brake tooling is expensive, so I’ll use what I have

. Of course, the world isn’t ideal, and things aren’t that simple. If they were, you wouldn’t be reading this article.

When you observe your press brake department in action, are your operators bending quality parts, smoothly transitioning from one job to the next? Or are they struggling? Do they measure flange lengths with a caliper, then make adjustments? Then more adjustments? Operators might have old tooling, or they might simply have the wrong tools for the job.

Still, before diving into a tooling catalog and making a purchase you’ll later regret, find out what tools your operators

really

need to get the job done. Asking six basic questions—or, more accurately, addressing numerous details that we can organize into six general topics—will help ensure you’re headed in the right direction.

1. What Material Are You Bending?

You likely bend a broad range of material grades and thicknesses, but about 90% of what you form is likely some combination of carbon steel (hot rolled and cold rolled), stainless steel, and aluminum. When you specify material, you specify the grade and thickness.

You probably use the common "gauge" term to describe thickness, but know that gauge measurements differ depending on the material type.

A piece of 16-ga. cold-rolled steel is actually thicker than a piece of 16-ga. aluminum. Specifically, 16-ga. cold-rolled steel is 0.060 in. while 16-ga. aluminum is 0.050 in. Mild steel certainly has variance in hardness and finish. Cold-roll suppliers and grades vary, but generally their offerings don’t cause the troubles of hot-roll material. Hot-roll thicknesses and hardnesses can vary enough to cause inconsistencies in bending. Remember this because it plays into the decision between precision and conventional tooling.

Although stainless steel is strong and often polished, its surface doesn’t resemble the one you see in a chef’s kitchen. Dull-finished stainless steel is often stronger and used for its industrial strength and corrosion prevention. All stainless grades introduce forming tonnage consideration. And if polished, finish is almost always important.

You need to address the material’s

functional features

that become evident during bending (see

Figure 1

). How sensitive is the material to marking? Does the die’s V opening or the punch tip control the inside bend radius (IR)? When air-bent, most materials form as a percentage of the die width, so the V controls the IR.

When you form some materials, though, including soft and half-hard aluminum (indicated with an "H" in the grade, as in 5052-H32), the material wraps around the punch nose radius; in these cases, the punch tip plays a dominant role in forming the IR.

Aluminum overall can be especially tricky. Although softer aluminums wrap around the punch tip, the rules completely change with the hard, tempered aluminum grades, each indicated with a "T," such as 6061-T6. The "T"-quality hard aluminums are strong, brittle, and have a defined grain direction. These common aluminums can be extremely difficult to form until you know what you are dealing with. To prevent stress and cracking, the IR needs to be three times the material thickness or greater. Here is where larger V openings and punch tips come into play.

You can address material marking in several ways. You can rely on protective sheets on the material or draped on the die shoulders. You also can invest in dies with flat, pivoting shoulders that fully support the material throughout the bend cycle (see

Figure 2

). This ensures no marking in mild steel as well as minimal if any marking on aluminum and polished stainless steel with protective coating.

You also can consider increasing the die shoulder radii, which can decrease the depth of the mark, but the mark still might be too deep for surface-sensitive applications. Choosing a larger shoulder radius will help form coated material without ripping. A large shoulder radius also works well with urethane or a cloth drape over the die.

2. What Level of Precision Must You Achieve?

Many choose precision tools almost by default these days, but not every job requires them. If you’re forming 0.250-in.-thick mild steel to make stair tread molds so cement can be poured around them, you might not need precision tooling. Let the style and quality of tooling fit the job.

Next, consider what style of tool you need. If you don’t need precision tooling, then American planed tooling will work. If you need precision tooling, you could choose among various tooling profiles, including American precision and European precision. An American-style gooseneck has different geometry than a European-style gooseneck. If you have multiple styles of tooling, you could choose one style to use most often.

3. What Punch Profile and V Opening Do You Need?

These days, software from the tooling vendor can help you specify the right tooling geometry. You can still refer to traditional methods, of course, including bend limit graphs that can reveal interference issues for specific punch profiles; as well as air bend force charts to determine the V opening you need.

The IR you achieve—and how it forms within the tooling—depends on how hard the material is. An air bending force chart assumes you’re working with mild steel. To estimate tonnage for other materials, you apply multipliers. For the force chart shown in

Figure 3

, the multipliers are as follows:

Soft Aluminum, Brass, Copper = Tons × 0.35

Half-Hard Aluminum = Tons × 0.5

Hard/Heat-Treated Aluminum, Mild Steel = Tons × 1.0

Half-Hard Brass and Copper = Tons × 1.1

Stainless Steel = Tons × 1.5

High-Strength Steel = Tons × 2.75

Half-hard to hard material will have an IR controlled by the die’s V opening. The harder the material, the larger the IR in the same V. For example, 0.060-in.-thick cold-roll steel in a 0.375-in.-wide V die will air bend to an IR of about 0.060 in. Stainless steel of the same thickness and in the same V will have a larger IR, since it will spring open more.

To estimate your IR when bending stainless steel, assume you’ll be at least one V larger than what is called out for the V on the air bending force chart. For example, if you use a 0.375-in. V for 0.060-in.-thick stainless steel, you can expect an IR to be approximately what is called out for a 0.500-in. V, which would be 0.084 in. This holds true for materials as the tensile strength exceeds 60,000 PSI.

4. What Are the Job Requirements?

Everyone wants to be able to bend a part in one setup, of course. Today, you can see operators performing some impressively complex bend sequences, with toolsets staged across the press brake bed (see Figure 4). Know, however, that such setups do require forethought and, not least, designs that take your available tooling into account. The more bends you make, the greater number of IRs you need to achieve, and the more extreme the flange length is—short or long—the tougher it is to do bend sequencing.

Consider the part you need to bend. Is it a box that requires a specific punch height? Is the box four sided, so your punch needs to fit inside the last bend? Are there return flanges at the top of each flange? What angles are being bent? How long are the bends? How many times will you bend the part or something like it? What tolerances does your customer expect? When is the job due, and how long will it take to make? Do you need to include tooling cost in the quote? The nature of your operation comes into play here. Are you making standard products to stock, or are you making products to order? Are those products standard, configured to order, or engineered to order? Or are you in a job shop environment? If so, do you have a number of repeat jobs, or do you see new jobs every day?

Finally, what press brakes do you have? Generally speaking, as the parts become bigger or longer, the machine requirements change. Long bends require longer beds, of course, but what about the open height and stroke? If you have enough open height with the tools loaded, will you have enough space between the punch and die to remove your part after forming? What about deflection? Do you have flanges that are very short or at odd angles, making it difficult to use your machine’s backgauge?

5. Who Will Operate the Press Brake?

As time goes on, more and more knowledge is being built into the press brake. The hope is that operators can be trained and brought up to speed quickly, even with little to no press brake experience. Good shops recognize the value in automation to keep the ball rolling, but any job shop can point to the value of an experienced operator.

Before formulating any tooling strategy, never forget to communicate with your experienced operators. Involve them early and often so you can avoid duplication ("We already have that tool!").

You also need to know they can physically work with the tools you intend to purchase. It’s easy for someone who won’t be operating the brake to say, "The tool won’t be used that much, and it’s only 50 lbs." Say that several times a day, and your operator might have a better idea. Sometimes it’s as simple as ordering shorter tool lengths. Your tools need to support the most valuable part of the process—the machine operator.

6. Do You Choose a Standard or Special Tool?

From here, you can decide whether standard or special tooling will suit the job at hand. Part profiles and bend sequences might allow for standard tooling, but you still need to consider whether a special tool makes sense.

This is where part quantity comes into play. Operators could form a part with existing tools, but what if they have 10,000 parts to make—and a special tool could triple their productivity? If they can make the part in one hit, they’ll be more efficient and consistent. Moreover, a special tool could make the job physically easier.

Feed the Funnel

Think of the answers to these questions as data points that flow into a funnethl feeding your "engine" of forming productivity (see

lead image

). Inside that funnel is a dynamic environment, where a change in one answer affects others. If you miss a question or two, your engine might still run, but not well. If you answer every question completely, your tooling investments should be able to support a well-tuned, smooth, predictable, and productive forming department.

Ask these questions enough times, and it becomes second nature—but it takes time and patience. Regardless, a well-tuned forming operation is worth the effort.
