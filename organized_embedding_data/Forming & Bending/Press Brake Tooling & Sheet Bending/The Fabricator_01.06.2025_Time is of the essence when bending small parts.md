# Time is of the essence when bending small parts

[TARİH: 01.06.2025 The Fabricator]

Bending

The right-sized press brake can make a big-time difference

By

Dan Davis

A press brake designed to form small to medium-sized parts can open the door to more efficient production.

Images: Bystronic

E

ver been in a fab shop where the small press brake is the 10-footer with 150 tons of forming force? It’s probably more the norm than not. The 10-ft. brake is there for a reason. It’s large enough to accommodate most of the bending jobs, both small and large parts, and it also can be set up for sequence bending. If a shop is looking for its first brake, this is probably the size that it’s going to purchase.

That makes sense until it doesn’t. Imagine a shop that’s built up its business, but it is still relying on that one 10-ft. brake. A big order for a small part—a three-bend bracket made of 11-ga. steel—comes in, and the brake is set up to do the job. An operator stands in front of the brake, slowly forming each part. It’s odd to see this huge ram come down on such a small part. In fact, a view of the press brake from behind the operator obscures the entire forming cycle because the part is so small; the only view is a human silhouette in front of a large brake with its ram going up and down.

That 10-ft. brake is the best choice for small parts when no other option exists. However, the emergence of small brakes for small parts is changing the game when it comes to forming small and even medium-sized parts.

"The efficiency gains are dramatic. With double the strokes per hour compared to the larger brake, you can quickly implement production gains into the operation," said Chad Alan, bending product manager, Bystronic.

Defining Small

How do you define a small press brake? Alan said his company’s smallest press brake has built-in forklift rails so it can be moved swiftly into position. The quick-connect electrical plug allows for relocation to maximize shop floor space. It has a footprint of 81 by 55 in.

"Data shows the 60-in. bending length paired with 88 tons provides a solution that fabricators can utilize in their production flow," he said.

That brake size can tackle part thicknesses from 24 ga. up to 0.25 in. Even a 0.5-in. part is feasible given the right tooling is in place and depending on what type of flange is needed.

But the real difference is in the bending speed of these brakes when compared to their larger counterparts. These types of brakes can reach speeds of roughly 650 IPM on the downward stroke, but then slow down to approximately 70 IPM as it starts forming the workpiece. Any faster, problems might arise.

"If you are working with aluminum or stainless steel, for example, you might need to slow the bending speed down to make sure that the material forms properly," Alan said. "The [human-machine interface] enables these quick adjustments to fine-tune the entire process."

But the fact that the ram can move at that speed highlights how the operator is likely able to process that many more parts when compared to an operator working in front of a larger brake with slower ram speed.

Conducive to Operator Comfort and Safety

Small press brakes are designed specifically for the job of forming small parts. To get the most out of the manual operation, the brakes are designed to make life easier for the operator, who might normally be stuck in front of a much larger press brake.

These small brakes typically have support arms or a table on which operators can rest their arms while manipulating the small parts. In some instances, an operator can even pull up a chair because the nature of the work doesn’t require moving from one end of a large press brake to the other.

"The machine is focused on ergonomics as well as performance. Features such as motorized HMI, tool storage drawers, and a support table allow the operator to remain focused on the task at hand," Alan said.

These machines also can come with high-performance LED packages that help the operator see what is going on, no matter what the lighting conditions are in the facility. Intricate work can be a challenge, especially with older employees, and the extra lighting can ensure that they can see what they are doing.

In the event that a tooling changeover is needed for a new job, the operator doesn’t have to go far. These types of brakes have side cabinets and drawers incorporated into the brake for tooling storage. Quick access to the tools and easy-to-connect setup translate into a changeover that occurs in a few minutes.

Six-axis backgauges can help an operator by providing a firm place to locate the parts, even complex shapes, prior to forming.

Small press brakes are often designed so that automation can be added to create a robotic bending cell. These cells can be set up in as few as 15 minutes. They also can be separated again so that the brakes can be manually operated.

Working with smaller parts might suggest a great chance of pinch-point injuries for the parts handler, but that’s not necessarily the case with modern press brakes. That goes for the smaller units as well.

A camera-based system provides detailed coverage of the bending area. It can differentiate between the punch tip and an operator’s finger. If anything outside of the punch and the workpiece appears on the approach to the target Y position, safety valves kick in and the ram stops.

The safety system also can accommodate flanges that otherwise might trigger a ram stop. In this mode, the press accounts for the flanges and proceeds at the working speed. This provides protection for the operator without sacrificing production results.

Producing Quality Parts

These small press brakes also have options, commonly found on larger brakes, to help ensure that accurate bends are made—and ultimately helping to avoid rework.

An optical laser-and camera-based angle measuring system uses sensors to support bending accuracy. It can focus on the actual form, take a measurement once the punch pressure is released, calculate if further punch pressure is needed to counteract springback, and have the punch reengage the blanks, if necessary.

"This helps quality control because the operator has the ability to let the machine actually measure the parts on the fly," Alan said. "He doesn’t have to worry about parts getting rejected downstream."

Advanced backgauging can help with quality efforts as well. An operator might have difficulty with positioning very small parts directly against a backgauge. If that part is not flush with the backgauge, not only is that bend likely out of tolerance but all subsequent bends will follow the same fate.

That’s where something like a 6-axis backgauge can help. The fingers on these backgauges go backward and forward, up and down, and side to side.

"If I have a developed flange, then I have the freedom to backgauge in two places, where a conventional backgauge would not be able to do that," Alan said. "Using two points of contact with the backgauge finger provides a stable surface. This assists in achieving accurate bend lines."

The 6-axis backgauge provides the metal fabricator with the peace of mind for the future because it will be able to handle complex, nonparallel, and even conical parts.

Automation in the Future?

A discussion of backgauges leads to the question as to whether they should be designed to work with robots. Just as bending automation is often considered for use with larger parts, the same thinking is now applied to smaller parts too. Sometimes, if the volume is right, it might be quicker to have a robot do the bending.

What is the right volume? If the program is under 20 minutes or only has a few bends, 250 parts might be considered the volume that is good for a small automated bending cell.

"Software provides a useful tool when approaching these different projects," Alan said.

These small brakes that have the ability to accommodate a robot can be transitioned over to an automated bending cell in fewer than 15 minutes in most instances.

Alan described a bending cell setup where a company had two small automated press brake cells flanking a manual brake. As the operator went about forming parts, he also kept an eye on the status of the automated brakes, intervening when he was needed. One person was able to tend to three machines. Programming was done offline to allow the machines to continue working.

Alan added that when metal fabricating companies purchase these smaller press brakes with the option to add a robot, they typically end up using the brake in its automated configuration to increase production goals. They grow comfortable with the technology and start to feed more parts toward the machine.

The right tool for the job often leads to better efficiency. Smaller press brakes prove that with smaller parts. The brakes move faster than large machines and are designed to produce quality parts from the start, preventing the need for rework or scrapping. They also are designed with future automation upgrades in mind.

That leaves a lot for metal fabricators to consider when it comes to processing small parts. Is it time to find the right-sized brake for your operation?
