# Let’s start from the beginning

[TARİH: 01.08.2024 The Fabricator]

Bending

To keep brake operators safe and equipment from being damaged, companies need to be realistic about training

By

Dan Davis

Understanding the very basics of working in front of a press brake is important for both the operator and the machine. For instance, do new operators know where to hold parts to avoid getting their fingers snipped during the forming process?

Puchit/iStock/Getty Images Plus

J

im Blount, whose company installs light curtains on press brakes, repairs brakes, and offers training classes, knows the state of press brake operators in the U.S. In many instances, they need some help.

Press brake operators have been hard to find over the last 20 years. With the retirement of baby boomers who actually had formal training in metal forming, experienced operators are even more scarce nowadays.

That’s led to not only a lot of inexperienced operators standing in front of brakes, but also supervisors who don’t have the depth of experience to introduce good practices that lead to more consistent forming of quality parts and make the operators better. No one on the shop floor really has the expertise to correct and educate engineers on what can and can’t be formed on the press brakes.

Blount described an encounter with a press brake operator who had just bent a part on a modern press brake and then discovered that the action did not deliver the expected angle. The conversation didn’t reveal the answers for which one might hope:

"How much are you off?"

"I don’t know."

"Do you have a protractor?"

"I have one here, but …"

"He didn’t know if it was an open or closed angle.

He didn’t know if it was past 90 degrees or not," Blount recounted. "And because he didn’t know how to read a protractor, he couldn’t determine the exact change that needed to be made."

What the press brake operator knew was that he had a machine square to hold against that angle on the improperly formed part. So after checking and realizing that the form needed to be closed some more, the operator put it back in the brake. Three more cycles of forming and checking finally got the part to where it hit the 90-degree bend.

"He could have done that a lot faster if he knew how many degrees he was off," Blount said. "The machine actually had an angle adjustment feature right on it. He only needed to get the correct adjustment from the protractor and put the number in."

That’s just one example. Blount had others that stressed how important basic press brake training can be for press brake operators. Without it, these inexperienced operators can be a threat to themselves, to nearby co-workers, and to the expensive brakes and tooling inside of them.

Employee Safety

At a large manufacturing company with multiple factory sites, Blount had finished installing light curtains, and he was about to start a short training session to show the brake operators how the new safety devices worked. He asked the operators to bring over some parts, and he then noticed how they were handling them.

Just by the placement of their hands, Blount could tell that they hadn’t been in front of a press brake for very long. A conversation with the supervisor proved that observation to be correct.

"How you hold the parts is critical because even with the light curtain or a laser device, you’re still going to get hurt because there might be flanges on the outside and they’ll curl up, or because the fingers might be holding the top of the part and they are going to go right into the die," Blount said.

Looking back on that particular situation, the press brake operator needed to know not only the proper way to handle the part in question, but also how the part was going to be formed. The ability to visualize the 2D blank becoming a 3D part allows the operator to know where the fingers shouldn’t be as the bends are made.

The fingers are not the only body parts of which a press brake operator should be cognizant. The overall stance in front of the brake has to be considered as well.

Blount said he can tell when an operator doesn’t have a healthy respect for what the press brake can do because that person is lackadaisical when it comes to presenting the parts to the machine. This is particularly noticeable with inexperienced older workers or even younger workers late in a shift when they almost lean into the job, instead of standing square and upright in front of the brake.

"When you go to push that foot pedal, it should be right in front of you, so you’re not off-center," he said. "But what you see sometimes is that they’ll reach over with the other foot, and they’ll end up crossing themselves over—to the point where they’re getting ready to fall over."

There’s also the case of a part that slides accidentally instead of being firmly set against the backgauge. Without being positioned solidly and squared in front of the press brake, the operator could end up following the sliding part.

In short, a press brake is a dangerous machine, and a press brake operator needs to be familiar with how it works, how parts are formed, and how he or she should handle parts during the forming process. Uneducated operators can end up hurting themselves in a major way. It’s one of the reasons why the Occupational Safety and Health Administration continues to focus on amputations as one of its national emphasis programs.

Machine Safety Metal fabricating companies also should be concerned about their press brakes when it comes to untrained operators. A wrong decision can lead to an incredibly expensive machine repair.

Blount described how he’s seen operators with no real idea of which press brake tooling should accompany certain jobs. For instance, someone that is charged with forming a 90-degree bend might actually have tooling placed in the brake that doesn’t allow for that form. If the press brake is an older model, it’s likely going to deliver the full power of the ram into that part, trying to deliver an angle that the tooling can’t accommodate.

A new press brake operator also needs to know where to stand when handling larger parts. If the operator was too close to the brake while forming this long part, he or she might have gotten whacked in the chin.

Florin Patrunjel/iStock/Getty Images Plus

Such a situation could result in cracked dies, but it can get worse.

"Today’s tooling is a lot harder than what was used in the old days, like in a single V standard tool. When too much tonnage was put on one of those, you would just hear a big crack. Nothing would explode," Blount said. "Today’s tools will explode. They shake the whole building. Everyone is going to know."

He added that he has seen the impact of an exploded press brake tool. One part went through two walls.

In a way, even if newer brake tools are much more expensive than what was used 25 years ago, tools can be replaced for less than the cost of a press brake. But keep in mind that the same situation that cracks dies also can lead to expensive press brake damage.

Once those dies go, the punch holders are next in line to be damaged. If things aren’t caught immediately, the whole upper beam can be damaged.

Of course, that’s a worst-case scenario. But even small-scale damage to a brake because of operator inexperience can be a headache.

Blount recounted watching a new operator forcing a blank against a backgauge finger with such might that it looked like the finger might flip up. That individual didn’t recognize that the backgauge was more for positioning, rather than gripping, the part and that when the ram came down, it was going to hold that part in place as the form is created.

"They fail to realize that the machine does all the work. They are there to support the part," Blount said.

One of the ways that novice press brake operators can gain confidence is by slowing the ram speed when they are first starting out. As they grow more comfortable in the job, the bending speed can be increased.

Traits to Look for in a Potential Brake Operator

Working under the premise that an experienced press brake operator is not going to be a part of one of his training classes, Blount said that he looks for some sort of basic mechanical aptitude as a good sign for someone who can grow into the role of a brake specialist. Typically, he gathers this intelligence with some simple questions: "Can you read a protractor?" and "Do you know how to read a tape measure?"

At that point, he said he can start talking about the tools that can help them make the right decisions in determining bend deductions, for example. With the right information, they can confidently proceed in their educational process.

For those who can’t, the education begins right there. Good information leads to positive outcomes, and that data needs to be prepared with trained operators.

He also said people who have a respectful "fear" of the brake and what it can do actually have a good foundation from which to continue learning and protecting themselves from injury. While they can’t be afraid of working in front of the brake, the knowledge that the brake is one of the leading causes of injuries in manufacturing keeps them on their toes and engaged in the forming process.

Blount is blunt with one warning: Some people aren’t cut out to be press brake operators. While it might not be intentional, some people just don’t pay attention to detail, and that can lead to poor safety choices, which likely result in injuries.

One Last Piece of Advice

Metal fabricating companies pay a lot of money for modern press brakes, and they want to see these precision machines in action. The ram going up and down at a rapid pace means that more forms are being made and more parts are being produced.

When it comes to new brake operators, however, this might not be the best approach. Slow and steady makes a lot more sense, according to Blount.

"What you want is a speed that allows you to get good parts. That means none of them are going into the trash," he said.

Modern press brake rams can surprise a new operator with the speed in which they come down and engage the metal. In some instances, the action is so quick that it can rip that part right out of the operator’s hand.

That’s why Blount recommends dialing back the bending speed when brake operators are learning the job. It gives them the chance to learn the craft, get comfortable in front of the machine, and gain confidence while make quality parts.
