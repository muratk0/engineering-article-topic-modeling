# Not programming press brakes offline yet?

What you should consider before making the move

By

Edwin Diaz

Press brake software simulates the bend cycle, verifying the sequence and tooling are optimal for the job.

W

hat’s that noise coming from your shop floor? That’s the sound of your equipment running, parts bending, and money being made—and fabricators who have adopted offline programming are hearing a lot more of it these days.

Your press brake operation has been doing things the same way for as long as you can remember. The good news is the process seems to be relatively efficient and profitable. The not-so-good news is that machine-based programming robs you of production.

You likely invest heavily in your press brake department, and you want those machines to work all day. The minute a job is done, the next program should be loading. That simply isn’t practical when programming on the machine’s console. By many estimates, traditional on-machine programming cuts a press brake’s throughput potential by half.

A growing number of fabricators are finding the answer in offline programming. Unfortunately, adopting this approach isn’t as easy as flipping a switch. A smooth transition hinges on a willingness to change perceptions and challenge the status quo.

Benefits of Programming Offline

Computer simulations provide a clear visual of the most efficient bending sequence as well as interactions, errors, or potential collisions. What’s more, you can create and share programs across multiple platforms and machines.

Today’s systems integrate 3D CAD models directly into the programming workflow. Powered by increasingly sophisticated algorithms that consider factors like bending forces, tool availability, and potential for part collisions, advanced systems determine the best bend sequence to minimize material deformation, ensure part accuracy, and minimize trial and error at the machine. They also recommend optimal tool types and arrangements based on the geometry and material properties of the part. Software also calculates optimal backgauge positions.

Nearly all press brake OEMs provide software dedicated to their machines, ensuring seamless integration and automatically accounting for machine-specific nuances of staged setups. Additionally, most offer interoperability features to connect with other brands.

Software can test bending sequences in a virtual environment, reducing the chance of costly mistakes. This allows shops to take on more complex and lucrative jobs, many of which require intricate bending.

DFM and Design Unitization

If you work with customers on design for manufacturability (DFM), you probably know all about design unitization. An assembly with many parts is "unitized" into fewer parts—or even just one.

Forming one complicated part is usually much more efficient than bending several simpler parts that need to be welded downstream. Also, don’t forget the extra tracking and complicated bills of materials (BOMs) all those subcomponents create. Shops sometimes focus on increasing inches or strokes per minute on a specific machine—but a part design change can eliminate

entire manufacturing steps

from a job routing.

All this said, design unitization can create some complicated forming jobs. An experienced operator might spend serious time programming, adjusting, and perfecting a complex setup. This creates a bending bottleneck that could have serious ripple effects on the rest of the operation.

Here again is where offline programming can help. Software moves the prove-out process off the machine and onto the computer screen. Technicians see a 3D representation of the bending sequence, with tools staged across the press brake bed, and they can see where issues might occur. Virtual tryouts help eliminate that bending bottleneck, even for extremely complicated parts.

Batch Sizes and Job Mixes

Offline programming increases productivity regardless of lot size, but the efficiency gains are especially critical for small production runs. That’s because setup time constitutes a higher percentage of the overall process. Let’s say a shop bends jobs for 10 different customers in a day. How much time does it spend programming those parts? Even if it’s as little as five minutes, that’s nearly an hour of machine downtime each day. At the end of the week, month, or year, that one hour a day really adds up.

Offline programming lets you see the entire machine environment all on one screen. This includes the machine, tools, part, backgauging, and bend sequence.

Offline software pulls from a digitized tooling library, automatically selecting the best tools for each job.

Consider another scenario in which you have 80% of jobs stored in the press brake controller. The remaining 20% represents new work, meaning the operator must program from scratch at the machine. And if some new jobs are unexpectedly challenging, they can delay other work on the schedule—and the scheduling headaches ensue.

Resistance to Change

Offline press brake programming does require investment in hardware, software, and—not least—training. There may also be recurring costs for technical support, hardware and software upgrades, along with maintenance fees.

In addition to costs, one must consider the impact on the organization. Sitting behind a desk can be difficult for anyone who is used to physically working with their hands. Shop veterans have spent years honing their ability to program and adjust on the fly at the machine. They also might worry that the transition to offline programming signals a reduced reliance on their expertise, especially if programming tasks are shifted to dedicated programmers.

New software can be intimidating. Established employees may be uncomfortable with digital tools and feel that their hard-earned skills are undervalued in a system that prioritizes software-based programming.

Today’s sophisticated software generates a wide variety of bending options. The software might, for example, provide 10 alternative bending sequences, and your employee may be familiar with only the third recommended sequence. The fact that some bend sequences are faster, better, and easier to set up must be considered.

An underlying fear throughout manufacturing is the threat of being replaced by technology. In truth, software empowers programmers with more time and better tools, allowing their expertise to be leveraged more broadly. In other words, software makes press brake programmers and operators more valuable to the company, not less.

The fact that software reduces setup time and enhances repeatability enables operators to focus on higher-value tasks. Even the most advanced simulation software cannot account for every nuance of a machine’s real-world performance. Operators remain critical in validating and fine-tuning programs.

A Rising Workforce

Nearly one of every four manufacturing workers is at or near retirement age. As more baby boomers check out, the strain of the industry’s labor shortage is magnified.

Technology in the form of offline programming is not only boosting production, but just as important, it’s helping to rebrand the industry, making manufacturing more attractive to the rising tech-savvy generation. These young workers have grown up with technology; they use it in their daily lives and expect to use it in their careers. To them, offline programming makes manufacturing a viable alternative.

Where Things Are Headed

Nothing can be discussed these days without considering artificial intelligence—and AI is poised to revolutionize offline programming. Already, software can provide real-time feedback on potential issues with material, tooling, collision risks, and overbending.

AI’s impact will soon extend to predictive analytics and continuous learning. Advanced AI models will predict tool wear, optimize maintenance schedules, and adapt bending sequences based on real-world performance data.

These systems will integrate with other manufacturing and front office technologies, enabling fully autonomous operations where AI collaborates across machines to maximize efficiency and resource utilization. Machine learning will also personalize programming for specific operators, adapting to their preferences and improving their experience.

AI also will facilitate better integration of press brakes into smart manufacturing ecosystems. By leveraging data from IoT-enabled devices, AI will drive greater accuracy, faster setup times, and improved overall productivity.

Making the Transition

As a first step, assess

your current

processes. Evaluate and benchmark programming methods and shop floor workflows. Identify pain points such as excessive setup times or programming errors that offline programming can address. Encourage collaboration between programmers and operators. This not only helps to build trust and acceptance but also ensures programs account for real-world insights gained from years on the shop floor.

Next,

select software

that aligns with your equipment and production needs. Ensure compatibility with your press brakes, including support for tool libraries, material databases, and bend simulation.

To help overcome resistance and facilitate the transition,

involve operators and programmers early

in the process. Engage rather than dictate. This will alleviate fears and help to reinforce the fact that they are becoming an even bigger contributor and key asset.

Provide training

for operators, programmers, and engineers to understand the software’s features, functionality, and troubleshooting processes. Training is often available online, remotely, on-site, or at the vendor’s facility.

Compile and digitize an accurate library

of tooling and press brake specifications. This step is crucial for generating precise programs and simulating bends accurately.

Now you’re ready to

simulate and validate

your programs. Leverage the software’s simulation capabilities to validate programs before production. Identify and resolve potential issues such as collisions or incorrect bend sequences.

From this, you can

develop standardized processes

. Create standard operating procedures for program creation, validation, and file management. Standardization improves efficiency and ensures consistency across the team.

Test and implement

the software gradually. Pilot the offline programming system with a few projects to refine workflows and address unforeseen challenges. Gradual implementation reduces disruptions and builds confidence in the new system.

Finally,

monitor and optimize

. Track performance metrics, such as setup times and error rates, to identify areas for improvement. Incorporate operator feedback to fine-tune your processes.

Focus on the Future

By emphasizing collaboration, providing training, and valuing the unique skills of its personnel, a fabrication shop can successfully transition to offline programming.

Here’s the bottom line: If bending operations are just an afterthought, and you’re happy to keep it that way, then on-machine programming is likely sufficient. But if you’re looking to boost productivity, take on more work, and take things to the next level, then offline programming could well be the answer.
