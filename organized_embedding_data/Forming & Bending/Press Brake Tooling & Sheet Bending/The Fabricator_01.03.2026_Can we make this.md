Exploring Tube Benders

Proving out the details on bent tubing parts

By

Jay Robinson

Bending metal tubes properly is tricky business. Make sure you’ve asked all the right questions before you put material in your bending machine.

an we make this? It’s a question we get almost every day. A tube fabricator is asked to quote a part and doesn’t know if it should. What seems like a simple question is actually very complicated. And the answer requires several pieces of information.

THE PART

What material is being bent?

We tend to think of materials in broad categories: carbon steel, stainless, titanium, nickel-based alloys, aluminum, bronze, brass—even some plastics. But within each material are dozens of different grades and standards.

Sometimes a request for quote will come with very generic material requirements such as carbon steel, leaving it up to the bending shop to determine the grade. Other times, the RFQ will come with very specific material requirements, such as ASTM A500 series or 409 aluminized stainless tubing.

Some materials are much easier to bend than others. If you have not worked with a specified grade before, best do some investigation.

What shape is the tubing?

Tubing comes in all sorts of shapes. Is it round? Square? Rectangular? Something else?

CNC tubing benders have a maximum tube diameter they can accept, a number based on a round tube. If the shape is nonround, the shop must determine if tools can be designed for it and if the tube can be loaded in the bender.

Suppose you have a machine with a maximum round OD of 2.0 in. Could this machine accept a 2- by 2-in. square tube? The diagonal measurement on that square tube is 2.83 in., so you likely couldn’t bend that part on that machine.

How thick is the material?

As the wall thickness of the material decreases, the required machine power also generally decreases. However, because of the greater susceptibility to deformation, the part becomes more difficult to bend.

What is the required centerline radius?

Centerline radius (CLR) is the distance from the center of the "circle" being formed in the act of bending to the center of the tube being formed. It is generally accepted that with most materials, a CLR of 1x tube OD is the minimum radius a part can be formed around. So, for a 2-in.-OD tube, the minimum CLR is 2.0 in. As CLR gets larger, the amount of power required to make the part decreases.

THE MACHINE

What is the bender’s power capacity?

Unfortunately, there is no real industry standard that clearly communicates the power capacity of a CNC tubing bender. A chart like this defines the materials the machine can bend, but it does not tell the full story.

As noted previously, the CLR of the tube is being bent will affect the machine’s ability to pull the tube around, but the chart does not define what CLR was used to determine the tube sizes. Some manufacturers calculate tube sizes using a CLR of 1x tube OD, while others use 2x, but most use 1.5x tube OD. So, the capacities in the chart would be based on 3-in.-OD tubing being bent on a 4.5-in.-CLR bend die. Also, not all materials are created equally. There are high-tensile mild steels, many different grades of stainless, etc.

Remember that the real measure of a machine’s power is in the amount of bending torque the machine can produce, and that is compared to the amount of torque required to form the bend.

What is the bender’s reach capacity?

We often focus on a bender’s power, but another limiting factor can be how far the tools, especially the clamp and pressure dies, can travel to close the clamps on a tube.

If the CLR is too large, the clamps may not be able to open far enough to allow load/unload or manipulation of the tube between bends. If the CLR is too small, the clamps may not be able to reach far enough to close against the tube.

Is there enough clearance?

This question could go in either the part or machine category, but I think of it as a machine question because many different machine styles can help address clearance issues.

Multistack machines can move the head up or down vertically to allow bending farther away from the head. Machines that have very small bend heads that extend far away from the machine body allow a tube to swing below it.

In addition, there are machines that can bend in both directions. These make bends that would otherwise swing the part toward the machine body swing away from the machine body.

OTHER CONSIDERATIONS

Elongation.

As the tube is being formed, it will stretch. It is helpful to know if the material can elongate as much as required. There are a couple of formulas that will give an estimated percentage of elongation required to make the bend, and that figure can be compared to the expected elongation percentage of the material (email me at the address provided at the end for formulas).

Square/Rectangle.

It is imperfect, but a quick way to determine if your machine can bend a square or rectangular part is to compare the cross section of the maximum round tube material to the cross section of the square or rectangular material. If the square cross section is larger than the round cross section, it is likely above the machine’s capacity.

Required Torque.

Fortunately, online tools are available to determine required torque for you. I prefer

app.unisonltd.com

. If I know the maximum capacity of the machine based on tube size (as listed in Figure 1), I will enter those values into the app and determine the power required to make that part. Then I will enter the specs for the part I need to bend and compare the power required for it. If the required power for my proposed part is less than the power required for the maximum listed dimension, I can be pretty sure the machine has enough power.

Use the 80/20 Rule.

Just because a machine can bend a part does not mean you necessarily should. In general, less than 20% of the parts you make should be above 80% of the capacity of the machine you are using.

Why? Imagine two identical pickup trucks that can tow 15,000 lbs. Over a year, they both are driven 100,000 miles in the same conditions on the same days. One tows 15,000 lbs. for every one of those miles, and the other tows 5,000 lbs. for every one of those miles. At the end of the year, which truck will be in better shape? If you are consistently bending parts at 80% or more of your machine’s power capacity, it is time to start looking for a larger machine.
