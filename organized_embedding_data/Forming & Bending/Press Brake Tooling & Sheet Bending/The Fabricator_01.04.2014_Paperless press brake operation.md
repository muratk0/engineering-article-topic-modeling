# Paperless press brake operation

[TARİH: 01.04.2014 The Fabricator]

TECHNOLOGY SPOTLIGHT

A second screen on the brake controls helps to streamline bending

By Dan Davis, Editor-in-Chief

Two monitors at one workstation is not considered a luxury in many manufacturing facilities. In the front office an engineer could be looking at a spreadsheet of engineering specifications on one monitor and a CAD file on the other. A general manager could be perusing a dashboard of key performance metrics for his company on one monitor while he composes a quarterly report for the workforce.

The same scenario could apply to the shop floor as well—especially as some companies work toward a paperless future. The job router that used to accompany every order that hits the shop floor could go the way of the manual press brake.

As it relates to bending, that paperless environment can be a scary thought. The drawing typically is included in that job router, along with part description, setup guidance, and job instructions. Bending is difficult enough for an inexperienced operator, but without some sort of graphic reminder of what needs to be bent, the job is only further complicated.

But what if the press brake had a second screen to handle the additional information? SafanDarley showcased such an option on its e-Brake electric press brake at FABTECH® 2013 in Chicago late last year (see Figure 1).

Figure 1 The SafanDarley e-Brake has an option whereby two screens can be connected to the control software, giving the operator access to multiple information sources at once.

“We want to help with a paperless factory,” said Mauricio Gutierrez Matta, area export manager for Safan- Darley equipment, which is sold in the U.S. by Comeq Inc. “Normally when you are making a part, you need the papers to make it and know how to operate the machine. Then you always end up losing something.”

Using the two-monitor configuration, the operator doesn’t need to worry about documents flying out of a folder. The part drawing can be called up on one screen and the simulation on the other. If the press brake operator is very inexperienced, he could call up an actual video of the job as well—if one is available (see Figure 2).

Figure 2 Two-monitor control stations on press brakes might be particularly suitable for large metal fabricating companies with more than one location. If a video of a job is created at one location, it can be placed on the company’s network and accessed by other press brake operators at other plant locations.

The options on what can be called up on the press brake’s monitors don’t end with simulations and videos. A click on the icon at the bottom reveals several digital representations of what might be located in a typical job router. It might be a PDF of the job summary or a DWG file detailing the final part in a 3-D format.

The control for the e-Brake is PC-based and runs Microsoft Windows®. This means that the press brake operator can open up Windows Explorer or some other web browser to access a web-based application. As a result, the operator doesn’t have to move to another PC that may be located several feet from the press brake to access the company’s shop management software or a customer’s website.

This screen setup keeps all possible references in front of the operator, which boosts the potential for efficient bending operations and quality first-run parts.

“When you have a heavy rotation of operators, you can keep the information in the control. The knowledge is not going to go away when the guy leaves your company,” said Peter Wielens, product support, SafanDarley.

Of course, the press brake itself has other features that help inexperienced operators as well. One such tool is the E-Bend L feature, an angle measurement system that helps to form parts correctly no matter how challenging the material may be.

A pair of laser sensors, attached with fastening magnets and placed on the front and back of the standard bottom tool, measure the angle of the sheet metal during bending. The sensors project a laser beam on the sheet and then calculate the angle in relation to the bending tool in use. The sensors feed the bending results back to the control software, which uses that information to adjust the ram pressure and dwell time, along with the crowning, to deliver the correctly bent angle.

Matta said this patented angle measurement system can be used with any tooling, so a fabricator doesn’t have to purchase special tooling when working with special materials, such as thin stainless steel. Even when spring-back is an issue, the company says that the E-Bend L system ensures that the ram keeps the bend angle constant until the desired angle is achieved.

Another feature that SafanDarley was sharing with FABTECH visitors was the tool positioning guide. A light guide assists the operator with exact placement of the press brake tooling prior to bending. This may not seem unique to a press brake, but Matta said the SafanDarley tool positioner technology is accurate within ±1 mm, whereas other brake guides might be ±2 or 3 mm.

All of the press brake features became available to North American fabricators this year.
