# Press Brake Die Selection: Material, Bend Method, Geometry, and Load Limits

## Why die selection must be planned

Press-brake die selection should be based on the workpiece and forming method rather than on whichever tooling is already installed. The lower-die opening affects required forming force, the resulting bend geometry in air bending, clearance for previously formed features, and the likelihood of material damage. A narrower opening can increase force demand and may produce a tighter formed radius, while a wider opening generally changes the bend radius and load requirement.

Before setup, identify the material, thickness, bend length, specified geometry, bend method, and required clearances. Material condition and properties also matter: different grades and tempers may respond differently through springback, work hardening, and resistance to cracking. Confirm the applicable material documentation, part requirements, tooling ratings, and machine limits.

## Distinguish air bending, bottoming, and coining

The article distinguishes three forming conditions:

- **Air bending:** The workpiece is supported by the shoulders of the V-die without being driven to the die bottom. In this condition, die opening has a major influence on the formed inside radius and force requirement.
- **Bottoming:** The workpiece is driven farther into the die so that it contacts more of the die geometry. This can require substantially greater force than air bending.
- **Coining:** The punch is forced deeply enough to imprint the material and tool geometry. This is a high-load operation and must not be treated as an air-bending setup.

A process can unintentionally shift from air bending toward bottoming or coining if the ram is over-stroked in an attempt to correct springback or achieve a tighter angle. Therefore, the selected bend method must be established before force estimates and tooling choices are made. Machine manuals, tooling documentation, and qualified setup personnel should be used to verify stroke limits and load conditions.

## Die opening and bend geometry

For air bending, the article presents thickness-based die-opening rules as starting points rather than universal values. It describes an “8× thickness” opening as a common baseline for mild-steel air bending, while also noting that material type, thickness, required radius, and cracking risk can require a different selection.

The article states that, in air bending, the lower-die opening can have more influence on the resulting inside radius than a simple change in punch-tip size. This principle is conditional: actual formed radius depends on material behavior, tooling geometry, bend method, and process conditions. Do not assume that a single multiplier or radius relationship applies to every alloy, temper, thickness, or drawing requirement.

Narrowing a die opening may be considered when geometry or flange dimensions require it, but it also increases forming load. Widening the opening may reduce localized bending strain and may be useful where a larger bend radius is acceptable. Any proposed die opening should be checked against the material’s allowable bend condition and against both machine and tooling load ratings.

## Material-specific considerations

The article highlights that stainless steel, aluminum alloys, and high-strength plate may require different setup decisions than ordinary mild steel. These materials can differ in springback, required force, and sensitivity to tight bend radii.

For materials with notable springback, a setup may require an overbend capability or tooling geometry that provides adequate clearance. This should be established through approved process development and verified parts, not by uncontrolled over-stroking.

For materials prone to cracking, tight radii and narrow die openings can increase tensile strain on the outside of the bend. The article suggests that a larger radius or wider opening may reduce this risk. The required minimum bend radius must be taken from the applicable material and design requirements; it should not be inferred from a generalized rule.

## Select tooling for the formed feature

A standard V-die may be appropriate for many single bends, but some part geometries need purpose-selected tooling:

- **U-shaped or multi-flanged profiles** may require tooling that provides clearance and supports the intended sequence of bends.
- **Return flanges** may require relieved punch geometry so that previously formed legs do not interfere with the tool.
- **Offsets or Z-shaped features** can be formed using dedicated tooling when the required profile, production method, and available load capacity support it.
- **Large-radius forms** may require radius-oriented tooling or a controlled forming strategy to avoid excessive local marking or strain.
- **Acute-angle tooling** can provide clearance for overbending where springback compensation is required.

Specialized tooling is not automatically safer or lower-force. Forming multiple features in one stroke, using slender relieved punches, or using complex profiles can introduce distinct load and clearance constraints. Verify the tooling manufacturer’s rated limits and use only approved setup procedures.

## Pre-setup verification

Before operating the machine, calculate or obtain an approved estimate of required force for the specific material, thickness, bend length, die opening, and bend method. Compare that requirement with:

1. The press brake’s applicable capacity and any length-dependent limits.
2. The punch and die load ratings.
3. The selected tooling arrangement and support conditions.
4. The part’s required bend radius, angle, flange clearance, and allowable surface condition.

If the desired drawing geometry conflicts with material bend limits, tooling capacity, or machine capacity, stop and escalate the issue through the approved engineering and production process. Do not compensate through unapproved parameter changes, over-stroking, or improvised tooling modifications.

## Practical decision sequence

A disciplined selection sequence is: material and condition; thickness and bend length; required radius and angle; bend method; tooling type and clearance; die opening; then load verification. This approach helps prevent convenience-based tooling choices from creating avoidable force, quality, and damage risks.
