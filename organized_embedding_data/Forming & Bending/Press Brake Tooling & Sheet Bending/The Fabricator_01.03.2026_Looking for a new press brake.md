# Looking for a new press brake?

[TARİH: 01.03.2026 The Fabricator]

Bending

These 10 questions can help you determine what makes sense for your shop

By

Dan Davis

T

he impact that faster and more powerful laser cutting machines have had on the typical metal fabricating shop floor is undeniable. Just ask the bending department.

It wasn’t too long ago that one press brake was suitable for keeping up with one laser cutting machine. That soon grew to two brakes for every laser as those machine tools became prolific blanking machines. Now, even that math is kind of fuzzy because automation has entered the picture, and automatic tool changers and robotic press brake cells have proven to be gamechangers for many shops.

The Fabricators and Manufacturers Association’s Forming and Fabricating Job Shop Consumption Report for Q4 2025 revealed that when metal fabricators were asked about their greatest manufacturing need, 70.87% said they plan to look at press brakes in 2026. That was down slightly from 72.04% in Q2 2025 but does indicate a strong need for additional bending capacity in the marketplace.

With that in mind, small to medium-sized shops looking to add a press brake to the shop floor have a big decision to make. These modern forming machines aren’t inexpensive, so it’s important to find the right press brake that makes the capital equipment investment worthwhile.

To find out what metal fabricators should keep in mind as they consider adding a press brake to the shop floor, The Fabricator spoke with John Kemp, vice president of Maintecx, a metal fabricating equipment distributor serving the upper Midwest, Plains states, Alabama, Mississippi, and Tennessee. We covered some of the questions that a job shop might want to answer as it determines what press brake to purchase.

"With press brakes, it’s a different animal than, say, a laser cutting machine, where you can compare different models and you get various throughput. That can be relatively easily measured," Kemp said. "With the press brake, it’s completely different. It’s more a question of flexibility. Larger machines in tonnage and length, more open height to allow taller tools, 6-axis backgauges all make for a much more flexible machine, but these items also come at a cost, so it’s important to determine what is really needed."

1. WHAT PARTS WILL BE FORMED?

Any discussion about press brakes begins with the reality of what needs to be formed. The longest part that’s regularly formed will influence the length of the bed, and the most commonly bent thickest part will help to determine the tonnage.

Those two aspects of the material being processed on the brake help to narrow down the machine options. For a shop just starting out or one looking to add a machine that can accommodate as much work as possible, that may lead to a longer and higher-tonnage machine. But a shop with multiple press brakes can size machines to specific part groups based on length and tonnage requirements. The most common size is around 170 tons and 10 ft. long. But that’s just the beginning.

2. CAN MACHINE FEATURES HELP TO OPTIMIZE PRODUCTIVITY ON THE PRESS BRAKE?

Metal fabricators need to realize that a press brake can only contribute to a shop’s overall profitability if it is actually forming parts, Kemp said. If that ram is not moving up or down, it’s not making the shop money.

"The average usage is somewhere between 30% and 40%," Kemp added. "The rest of the time, the operator is setting up tools, getting the parts, making the corrections, and everything else except bending the actual part. If you can reduce that time with features on the machine, your throughput is going to go way up."

That’s where a simple features comparison between two press brake models on a form is a little misleading. Whereas two press brakes might have the same speed, the same stroke, and the same length, a brake with advanced features is going to outperform a bare-bones model in terms of throughput.

So what’s the pain point for most shops? More than likely, it’s the dearth of press brake operators available in the local labor pool. Schools simply don’t produce graduates with a knowledge of forming metal on press brakes, and other companies tend to hold onto those individuals that are reliable operators. With fewer experienced operators available to run machines, shops need to embrace that reality.

3. HOW CAN THE PRESS BRAKE HELP WITH ACCURACY?

Accuracy is of utmost importance for any shop. Without quality parts, you don’t have a quality reputation, and you don’t have customers.

Luckily, today’s brakes don’t require the mechanical and bending knowledge that operators once needed to start producing quality parts. Kemp wrote about those days of working with a bare-bones press brake and the length of time needed to make good parts in "Press brake operators: The next generation," which appeared in The Fabricator in 2011:

"Consider a machine with a single-axis CNC backgauge with no memory. Operators needed to determine the desired flange lengths on each of the bends as well as the bend order. Next the operator input the flange lengths and required tonnage into the controller to make the first bend. When the first angle was wrong (notice I didn’t say "if"), the operator would add shims under the dies or adjust wedges in the toolholder to correct the angle—an action probably required in several areas of the brake bed, depending on the bend length."

"How many shims did the operator need to correct the angle? Will one tool set work better than another? The answers often came from trial and error. If an operator worked on a brake with no backgauge, that added 30 minutes or more to the setup as he laid out the part, scribed the bend lines, set the hard stops, adjusted the flange length and squareness, and performed only a single bend at a time."

With so much effort involved to get the first part right, parts destined for the press brake 25 years ago were simple in nature. It was easier to weld the basic formed parts together to create a final assembly.

Today, accuracy has been built into most press brakes, regardless of price. The advancement of CNCs has helped with this. Still, not all brakes are created equal.

"Repeatability is extremely high on basically all models, so the difference really becomes how the machine can react to changing materials and conditions," Kemp said. "From a chemistry and thickness perspective, those things come into play."

While most press brakes can deliver repeatable results once the first part is dialed in, only more advanced machines will be able to adjust for inconsistencies in material. Advanced brakes have laser-based angle measurement offerings that can ascertain the actual bend, compare it to the original specification, and make an adjustment in a second hit to get the form needed. Also, modern crowning systems ensure that bends are consistent anywhere on the machine bed, as well as from one end to the other on long bends.

4. IS OFFLINE PROGRAMMING NECESSARY?

Almost all press brakes have the option of offline programming, which allows operators to spend most of their time forming parts instead of standing at the controls and plugging in parameters for the next bending job.

Having said that, today’s controls have 3D graphics, so whether offline or at the machine, creating a job doesn’t take as long as it used to.

Kemp did say that having some sort of 3D graphics as part of the controls is key for operators nowadays. Less-experienced operators can see how the part fits into the machine and how the bend sequence goes.

5. IS A 5- OR 6-AXIS BACKGAUGE IMPORTANT?

A multiaxis backgauge might sound like one of the bells and whistles to avoid when adding a new press brake to the shop floor. And it’s true that upwards of 75% of parts can be bent successfully with a 4-axis backgauge. But what happens when you get into more complex forms?

"A 5-axis backgauge gives you added flexibility for angle bends that are not parallel to the edge," Kemp said, "and a 6-axis gauge gives you the ultimate flexibility, where you can have different heights on the two backgauges that allow you to gauge off one surface of the bend on one end and a different surface on the other end. That gives you the ability to gauge just about any part that comes through the shop."

A brake with a 5- or 6-axis backgauge accommodates a wide variety of parts, from simple to complex. That’s helpful if a shop has only one or two press brakes.

6. IS RAM SPEED IMPORTANT?

According to Kemp, the benchmark for ram speed is roughly 10 mm/sec., with the fastest machines achieving up to 25 mm/sec.

Some customers absolutely want that speed, but for the most part, it really doesn’t matter too much in terms of overall productivity. The ram achieves that top speed for only a short period of time. However, if a shop is working with very large V dies with a bend stroke of 11/2 in. or larger, speed matters more than it would for typical sheet metal work.

"For the most part, it’s a consideration, but I wouldn’t say a top one," Kemp said.

7. HOW IS THE PRESS BRAKE SET UP FOR SAFETY?

Most press brakes sold today come with some sort of safety system, which creates a protective barrier in front of the machine’s ram or down the tip of the top tool. The safety system comprises a transmitter that sends a laser to a receiver situated on the other end of the bed, and if that light beam is broken by an operator’s hand, for example, the system instantly sends a signal to stop the machine before any injuries occur.

More sophisticated press brakes come with laser guard systems that actually are able to focus on the tip of the upper tool. If an operator’s finger breaks the laser beam at the tooling tip at any time, the system stops the bending action.

Kemp said that any shop looking to purchase a press brake without a safety system is putting the company at risk. ANSI B11.3-2012, Safety Requirements for Power Press Brakes, section 8.5.1, recognizes a number of safeguarding methods, including point-of-operation guards, safe operational speeds, and safe distance safeguarding.

Kemp added that most modern press brakes typically use a combination of the first two. A light curtain or close-proximity system protects the user when the machine is running at high speed. If the safety system is interrupted, the machine automatically shifts to a safe speed of 10 mm/sec.

Shops with machines that don’t have a builtin safety system must use safe distance safeguarding, which is a last-resort operator protection method allowed by OSHA only when physical barriers are not feasible. Safe distance safeguarding requires maintaining a minimum 4-in. clearance between the operator’s hands and the tool’s point of operation during bending. Relying on safe distance safeguarding requires a shop to have a documented safety program, work procedures, training program, and risk assessment. This safeguarding technology also is meant to be used when parts are unique or in small quantities.

Note that none of these systems is designed or intended to protect the operator from secondary pinch points, either between flanges on a part or between the part being formed and the machine itself (typically the upper beam or tools). Therefore, while manufacturers are making the machines easier to use for novice operators, they cannot be 100% safe. Training and risk assessments are still extremely important.

8. WHAT ABOUT THE PRESS BRAKE FRAME?

Deflection is going to happen on a press brake. The amount of deflection that occurs will depend on the press frame design.

Of course, the way any press brake compensates for that deflection and helps to ensure accurate forms is through crowning technology. The amount of crowning and other compensation necessary depends on the strength of the side frames and how the bed flexes as pressure is applied to the lower tools.

"The more you can minimize that deflection, the less compensation is needed and the higher the part accuracy is going to be," Kemp said.

9. ARE NEW TOOLING INVESTMENTS REQUIRED?

The simple answer is "yes." If a shop is adding a new press brake, it likely needs more tooling to accommodate the additional bending work expected for the brake.

A new press brake is likely to perform better with precision-ground and hardened tooling. The tooling is the only thing that touches the part, so a shop wants to ensure that the tooling matches the performance of the new brake.

Longtime users of American-style tooling considering a move to newer quick-change tooling will likely need some in-depth conversations to determine what that means for productivity and what will happen to the existing press brake tool inventory.

Taller tools allow for greater flexibility in the parts that can be bent, but this also requires a machine with more open height, both of which come at a cost.

Discussions about tooling should also cover automated tool change. It won’t necessarily be faster, particularly if an experienced brake operator knows the jobs and the machine has hydraulic clamping, but if a press brake operator is not experienced and struggles with timely setup, the tool changer can do the job quickly and reliably shift after shift.

"If your operator can complete his other tasks while the machine changes the tools, you’re basically eliminating tool setup time completely. Depending on the number of tool changes that you have to do throughout a day and how big your lot sizes are, this can really increase throughput on a stand-alone press brake from that 40% range to 60% or higher. That’s a pretty big gain," Kemp said.

10. DOES THE FOUNDATION MATTER FOR THE PRESS BRAKE?

Any shop that believes a 4- to 6-in. pad is sufficient for a 350-ton press brake might want to think again, according to Kemp.

The machine is heavy (a 150-ton machine weighs more than 50,000 lbs.), and the dynamic loads during forming are substantial. A 6-in. pad might not be able to keep up, and that results in accuracy issues.

Kemp recommends a 6- to 12-in.-thick pad for machines in the 60- to 350-ton range. It’s typically not until a shop has a brake of more than 500 tons that it requires something more heavily engineered.
