In longer bends, the radius wraps around the punch nose. Why does this happen? This series is digging deep, getting into the weeds, and staring into the abyss to uncover the answer. We’re almost there.

Are You Still Air Forming?

If, as the bend length grows, the radius no longer air-forms as a percentage of the die opening, are you still air bending? The answer is yes … well, maybe. That depends on the tooling you use to form the material.

Assuming you are using precision-ground tooling, odds are you will still be air forming. For most precision-ground tooling, the included die angle and punch angle are less than 90 degrees. If, say, your die angle is 88 or 86 degrees, your punch angle is at least 2 degrees less than that (see

). This creates angular clearance between the punch and die. Theoretically, you could bottom into an 88-degree die. If that happens, though, you’ll bottom-bend a 92-degree outside bend angle (that is, an 88-degree inside bend angle, matching the die angle).

You could have precision-ground punches and dies with 90-degree angles, and if that’s the case, you could be bottoming your longer bends. The same holds true if you’re using traditional planer tools, for which the dies are almost universally 90 degrees. This makes the probability of bottoming very high, and it’s usually a common practice for those using them.

If your tooling angles are selected for air forming, then even if you were to bottom the punch into the die completely, you would still have no contact between the die and punch faces. This means that even though you are penetrating deeper into the die space, you are still air forming.

Material Factors

Crystal size and material inclusions also can affect how the bend radius transitions from a traditional air bend to one in which the material wraps around the punch nose. These crystals and inclusions are in the grain of many different materials after rolling. Not all material has a grain, of course, but for those that do, the bend angle can change depending on the bend line’s relationship to the grain direction.

The bend line could be perpendicular, diagonal, or parallel to the grain direction, and these properties can affect the radius transition in longer bends. That’s because bending across (perpendicular to) the grain requires more force to complete and has a greater amount of springback.

Bending with the grain (that is, the bend line runs parallel to the grain direction) requires less force to complete and produces a smaller amount of springback. A with-the-grain bend often requires a larger inside radius to keep the material from cracking on the outside of the bend.

Accounting for the Transition

We know the way the radius forms transitions in long bends, from a traditional air bend (with the radius forming as a percentage of the die opening) to one where the radius wraps around the punch nose. Can you calculate exactly when this transition takes place? You can—but do you really need to? No.

Sure, you could write an algorithm that considers the bending moment and how the forces are affected by strain hardening. You could calculate the force increases over the bend length and why you need to increase the bending force over a specific length because of the loss of force to machine flexing. You could include all these factors and more—but again, it’s just not necessary.

The force being added to compensate for a long bend is greater than the linear tonnage per foot value, but it’s only enough to compensate for the loss due to the flexing of the press brake side frames—even though you increased the force and penetration into the die space. In essence, you are balancing the forces out.

Even though you are only balancing for the loss of power to the frame, those forces still cause the material to strain- or work-harden. That’s because the material being formed feels all the increase in force as the total power passes through it, before the machine flexing takes it away. Strain hardening reduces the material’s ability to flow, yet brute force from the press brake overcomes that hardening. This causes the material to wrap around the punch nose.

With all that being said, why don’t we need to consider all of this and calculate an exact answer? The reason is simple: The final amount of change in the bend radius, including its effect on the bend allowance and bend deduction, will be smaller than the variations you will face from the tolerances found around every aspect of the sheet metal. However, just because you don’t need to calculate it doesn’t mean you shouldn’t account for it.

Realize that the radius transitions are there and that they can be easily adjusted for because of their minimal amount. Yes, the radius gets smaller as it transitions from "floating" (forming as a percentage of the die opening) to wrapping around the punch nose. Look at your project and any extenuating circumstances and estimate how a smaller bend radius will affect the bend deduction and bend allowance.

Inspect the Radius

You can use shop-grade pin gauges to check the radius. They aren’t cheap, though, and they tend to get lost when used on the shop floor. Standard radius gauge sets, metric or imperial, are usually too limited for checking an air-formed bend.

Your best option is usually to simply make a custom radius gauge or gauge set. You can draw one in CAD and scale it up or down to create a complete set, or draw one for a specific job, then send it to your laser cutting or waterjet machine. If you cut a job-specific gauge, place it in the job traveler. That way, you can quickly gauge the inside bend radius. Another advantage: If you make a radius gauge yourself and end up losing it, you can replace it easily.

Again, achieving a specific radius is critical for accurate, repeatable bending—hence the importance of checking it. The customer might not care about the bend radius, but you should, especially if you’re making precision parts. When the radius changes, so does everything else: the bend allowance, the bend deduction, the flat blank size, and the final part dimensions.

All About the Radius

So, what was the point of this series? For one, it was to increase the general knowledge about sheet metal forming. More important, it communicates the importance of understanding and monitoring the inside bend radius. The bend radius changes in an air form; it is never constant. Regardless, it remains the most important part of anything made of sheet metal requiring a bend—especially if making tight-tolerance, precision parts is your goal.

Predict and calculate the final radius, achieve it in the workpiece, and you will always be able to make parts as close to perfect as humanly possible.

If you walk away learning nothing more than just how important the inside radius of the bend is—that you need to calculate for it, achieve that radius, and measure it to ensure its correctness—then you get the whole point of this exercise. Now, let’s stop staring into the abyss, get out of these weeds, and go build some perfect parts.
