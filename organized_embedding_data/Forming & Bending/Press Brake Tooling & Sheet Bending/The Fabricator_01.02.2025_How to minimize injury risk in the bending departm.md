# How to minimize injury risk in the bending department

T

he bending department tends to be the manufacturing bottleneck for a lot of metal fabricating companies. Something as simple as an unplanned absence can throw a shift into disarray.

Press brake operators, particularly experienced ones, are hard to find, and companies can’t go down to their local community colleges and hire a student with ample metal forming knowledge. In many instances, companies have to educate and train their own operators, if they are lucky enough to find people interested in or capable of following such career paths.

That means that a lot of shops are likely leaning on older workers to keep the bending department on schedule. The Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS) reports that the median age of manufacturing workers in the U.S. in 2023 was 44.1 years old. Lightcast, a firm specializing in labor market analytics, says that in 2023 just over 51% of manufacturing jobs in the U.S. were held by people 45 and older. It’s easy to see how someone working in front of a press brake might be susceptible to a joint pain or a muscle sprain. More than likely, they just happen to have reached the age at which pains and sprains are more common, especially when compared to their nimble and spry 20-something co-workers.

"It’s become much more relevant that we figure out ways to keep people in the workforce active longer and keep them fit and healthy," said Anjua Patil, CNA Insurance’s risk control product consulting director. "But we can’t just depend on people making the right choices to be healthier. We need to make the workplace safer and make it more accessible for different kinds of personnel. That’s the right way to go about it."

Why Ergonomics Matter in the Bending Department

Musculoskeletal disorders (MSD), a term used in the medical community to cover a whole range of conditions that affect bones, joints, muscles, and connective tissues, really is a scourge for metal fabricating companies. Many times these injuries occur in the bending department because people, typically older workers, are susceptible to them, and the nature of the work might maximize the risk of such an injury.

Think about life in front of a press brake. In low-mix, high-volume settings, a press brake operator might be handling the same part over multiple shifts and ultimately develop something like carpal tunnel syndrome. In a heavy fab shop, a brake operator might be forced to handle oversized workpieces and risk straining back muscles. The injury possibilities are numerous, and the injury risks are very real.

Do you see press brake operators leaning over a cart to retrieve blanks? An adjustable lift cart can help situate the cart at a comfortable level to minimize bending.

Nordroden/iStock/Getty Images Plus

The BLS reports that in 2021 and 2022, private industry in the U.S. had 502,380 MSD incidents, with a median of 14 days away from work related to those injuries. (After 2020, BLS went to a biennial reporting model for MSD incidents.) These injury types have been on the decline for several years, with 307,640 reported in 2013; 286,950 in 2015; 282,750 in 2017; and 266,530 in 2019.

Unfortunately, these numbers don’t tell the whole story, because workers who experience MSD injuries often need time to be reacclimated to the job. They don’t just show up ready to start handling awkward sheet metal parts or processing the same small job with four bends repeatedly through a shift. They might be required to do something else so as not to reaggravate the injury or at least ease back into the old role in a scaled-back way.

The BLS says that in 2021 and 2022, private industry reported a median of 20 days of restricted work activity or job transfer related to MSD injuries. Combining that with the median absence of two weeks, employees with these injuries can be gone for an extended amount of time, greatly affecting a bending department of any size.

The direct costs related to MSD—the medical bills and workers’ compensation insurance, for example—are immense. Some industry observers estimate these costs to be $20 billion annually.

But it’s the indirect costs that often are overlooked. These are costs connected to lost productivity and training of new employees. Some believe these indirect costs to be as much as five times that of direct costs.

"You definitely want to keep people healthy and working efficiently, especially in this kind of industry where expertise is important," Patil said.

Match the Job to the Employee

One of the first things a metal fabricator can do to minimize an operator’s risk of an MSD injury is to fit the job to the worker and not the other way around, according to Patil. Not all press brake operators are the same, so why should they be expected to do the same job in the same manner?

A tall person might not have difficulty with a larger, awkward part, but someone smaller in stature and with a shorter armspan might find the same job very challenging. In that instance, some sort of material lift device might be necessary to help the operator, or an additional set of hands might be required.

In another example, people of different heights might not necessarily be comfortable in reaching for blanks the same way. If sheet metal blanks are sitting on a table that comes up to the waist of a 5-ft., 5-in. person, anyone over 6 ft. might be straining to pick up those same blanks on the same table.

Patil also has this reminder for shops: Improve your lighting.

"One of the bigger things I see is that lighting always seems to be poor in these environments," she said. "Poor lighting can really have an impact on older workers."

When the lighting isn’t bright enough in the bending department, an operator can be forced to adopt an awkward posture to see things like part markings. Repeatedly assuming bad posture can increase the risk of some sort of strain, Patil said.

Talk to the Brake Operators

A shop owner or manager who really wants to know what’s happening in the bending department can get the straight dope by speaking to the people doing the work. It’s amazing what can be learned from the people who actually stand in front of the press brake all shift.

Of course, the manager could choose to talk to the bending department supervisor or a team lead instead. But this type of conversation at the grassroots level is a great way to generate ideas for continuous improvement.

And this is where the commitment to change has to be real. Going through the motions and not acting on them can be detrimental to morale and a notable obstacle if continuous improvement activities are attempted later down the road.

"If you gather feedback, you better be ready to do something with that feedback," Patil warned.

Conducting a

kaizen

event is one easy way to act on suggestions and foster a culture dedicated to continuous improvement.

"Tackling it as a project and having a fixed timeline in which you address those issues ensure that everyone’s on board," Patil said. "They see that there is a plan to assess the risk and then implement a solution."

These types of conversations also can be a part of larger initiatives. Chris Byrnside, quality and safety manager for Ludlow Manufacturing Inc., Waukegan, Ill., said that safety is part of the conversation at the company’s advanced product quality planning (APQP) meetings. These meetings provide different department representatives (engineering, cutting, bending, quality, shipping, and even sales) an opportunity to learn what’s happening, what’s changed, and what needs to occur to ensure parts are shipping on time at the end of the day.

"We look at what the risk is in what we’re doing and how do we control it and mitigate that," said Byrnside.

Part of that risk management is safety. For example, if a new job involves a large blank that needs to be bent, the APQP team can discuss if one person can handle the job or if some sort of assistance, mechanical or human, is needed. Because a shop floor representative is part of the conversation before the job even hits the shop floor, this safety concern is not overlooked, and a potential monkey wrench is kept out of the bending operation.

Look at the Layout

Most metal fabricating companies look like a hodgepodge of equipment and storage racks. That’s usually because the main focus has been on serving customers, resulting in additions being made just to accommodate new fabricating technology or to open an area for more inventory. As a result, not a lot of thought is going into material flow.

That’s a mistake, particularly in the bending department, because people need room to move, according to Patil. When workers are in close proximity to others, they need to be careful in how they move and manipulate parts and equipment. They are only a few feet away from a wrong move that could result in an injury.

"Layout is very important," Patil said. "People can avoid awkward positions. They have room to walk and move comfortably. They have easy access to things."

The extra room also provides an opportunity to install the right ergonomic controls. For heavy-duty bending, does the shop have the room for jib crane? Is the foundation thick enough to support the crane? Is there enough room to manipulate large workpieces without the risk of colliding into another person, another piece of equipment, or something else?

Other equipment is available to help make a press brake operator’s job more ergonomic. Lifts can be used to tilt bins so the operator doesn’t have to constantly bend over to place parts or scrap in them. Adjustable tables can present blanks at a comfortable height for the operator. And something as simple as a padded mat on which the operator stands can go a long way in making the job less painful over a shift.

Seek Guidance

Although small and medium-sized shops are unlikely to have full-time safety professionals on staff to help with some of these ergonomic-related decisions, they aren’t without available resources. Help might be a phone call or email away.

Speaking with an insurance provider is a good start. Many of them have a risk control expert that can offer suggestions related to documentation guides and workstations. They also can connect you with partner organizations that might have more detailed knowledge of subjects like press brake safety and lift-assist mechanisms.

Safety organizations like the American Society of Safety Professionals and the National Safety Council can provide general safety guidance. The Occupational Safety and Health Administration website also is a resource for information on personal protective equipment, hazard communication, and specific industry segments.

"I also would advise them to reach out to their trade organization to see if they have resources," Patil said.

In the metal fabricating world, it should be noted that the Fabricators and Manufacturers Association will host its 17th Annual Safety Conference on May 13-14 in Elgin, Ill. CNA Insurance is a sponsor of the event’s Safety Dinner where the Safety Awards of Honor, Merit, and Most Improved Safety Record are given to companies in the fabricating, steel distribution, and steel processing industries.

Addressing ergonomics in the bending department makes sense for metal fabricating companies. It’s a sign of an industry evolving in an effort to attract the best and brightest from a labor pool that has a lot of choices.

"With the focus on boosting recruitment efforts, adopting new technologies, and retaining and maximizing the productivity of older workers, ergonomics can help you do all of that," Patil said.
