# Heating at the bend line

[TARİH: 01.03.2025 The Fabricator]

Bending Basics

Why it occurs and how you can account for it

STEVE BENSON

L

ast month, we looked at bending speed and its effects on forming sheet metal. Bending speed affects another bending variable that might not come to mind immediately:

bending temperature

. Bending causes the bend line and adjacent area to heat. The faster the bending speed, the greater the heat generated.

Why this happens would take several columns to explain in detail—a trip back into the weeds we will save for a later date. Briefly, this is what happens: When the press brake applies pressure up to a material’s yield point (its elastic state), the energy the press brake introduces is stored as potential energy. When the material is released from pressure, that energy is released and the material returns to its original flat shape.

As the material reaches and exceeds the yield point, it enters the plastic state and will remain bent. The mechanical energy from the machine is converted into heat due to movement within the crystal structure of the material; friction at grain boundaries; and interaction between the internal structure and the applied external forces.

Because the bend line is subjected to the highest stress and strain, the outside of the bend experiences tensile stress and the inside of the bend experiences compressive stress. The applied energy causes crystal dislocation, internal friction, and work hardening. All of that generates heat. This heating can affect your results, like the amount of springback you need to account for and the level of bend consistency you can achieve. And—you guessed it—along with the bending speed, the bend radius plays a significant role in how much heat is produced during bending.

Types of Bends

We have four different orders of bend radii, ranging from narrowest to widest.

Sharp bends

have a much smaller radius than the material thickness, often approaching a "knife edge." A rule-of-thumb value for a sharp bend would be a radius that’s 63% or less of the material thickness. When you bend sharp, the material’s deformation is highly localized at the bend line, leading to significant plastic deformation and heat buildup in that narrow region. Anytime a small or sharp radius bend is used, there is always a risk of cracking, especially with less ductile materials where grain structure and work hardening effects dominate. Even if the bending is fast, the resulting heating at the bend line will have little impact on crack prevention.

Perfect bends

have an inside radius that equals or is close to the material thickness. The range starts with the minimum radius—the dividing line between sharp and perfect representing the smallest bend that can be formed to avoid cracking. Perfect bends can have radii up to about 125% of the material thickness, though this value varies by material type. Perfect bends produce heat that is dispersed more evenly, but it is still localized around the bend line. They produce a "safe" radius that balances formability and strength. The natural heating at the bend line tends to be somewhat beneficial in maintaining that consistency.

Surface bends

have inside bend radii between eight and 10 times the material thickness. This creates a gentler curve. Heat buildup is less intense because deformation spreads over a wider area, reducing heat at the bend line. And because the heating is less, it has little effect on the bend.

Profound-radius

bends have inside bend radii greater than 10 times the material thickness. This type of bend suffers from multibreakage, which causes the material to separate from the punch nose, creating a bend radius that’s smaller than the punch radius. Since the deformation is spread widely, profound radius bends produce minimal heat.

Bending Speed and Heat

Here is what we know so far. Sharp-radius bends require higher forces and careful monitoring of material limits to avoid cracking and greater variations in the bend angle. Perfect bends provide a practical balance for many applications. Meanwhile, surface- and profound-radius bends exhibit minimal effects from heating.

The interaction of increased bending speed and heat can amplify forming issues. Faster forming speeds often generate more heat, reducing springback slightly in ductile materials but risking inconsistency. Slower speeds allow heat dissipation but can lead to more springback as the material retains its elastic properties.

It is common practice for shops to use an oxyacetylene torch to heat the bend line from below, along the outside radius (see Figure 1). Heating metal before forming is appropriate when the material properties, forming requirements, or specific challenges in the bending process indicate that additional ductility is necessary.

Using an oxyacetylene torch to preheat the bend line reduces the material’s yield strength at the bend location, making hard-to-form materials easier to bend.

However, bending speed still significantly influences consistency. Slower bending allows heat from the torch to penetrate more evenly through the material, ensuring consistent softening along the bend line. Heating improves consistency because the material has time to stabilize thermally and deform uniformly. However, bending too slowly can lead to excessive cooling between heating and bending, causing uneven deformation if the material’s temperature varies.

Slower bending can lead to less springback, if the material stays sufficiently hot during the process. Still, if the bending process is too slow, heat loss can create temperature gradients that lead to hardness and bend quality inconsistencies.

The Effects of Bending Fast

Rapid bending doesn’t allow sufficient time for heat to distribute evenly, leading to localized softening and potential inconsistencies in the bend quality. Faster bending can compromise consistency, especially if the heat from the torch is unevenly applied. The material may experience more springback because less heat penetrates deeply or the metal cools unevenly, leaving cooler, harder areas that experience deformation differently.

Bending fast after heating the bend line does have its advantages, though. It reduces the risk of oversoftening as well as burn-through in thin materials. It also limits time-dependent effects like excessive oxidation.

Maintaining an even temperature along the bend line is critical. Inconsistent heating results in varying material properties, leading to bend angle variations. Thicker materials retain heat better and are less sensitive to speed variations, while thinner materials cool quickly and demand more precise speed control.

Don’t overlook the effects on tooling. Faster speeds increase friction and potential tool wear, affecting the quality of the bend. Also, faster bending may not allow enough time for heat to dissipate uniformly, especially at the edges, causing inconsistent bends.

How Hot Is Too Hot?

Determining the optimal heating temperature for bending without annealing the material requires understanding the material’s properties, especially those that relate to critical temperatures.

Avoid exceeding the recrystallization temperature to prevent annealing. Identify the softening temperature range where the material becomes more ductile for bending without altering its microstructure. And, of course, stay well below the melting point to avoid damaging the material.

The following heating guidelines are for common materials. These methods and ranges can help you optimize bending temperatures while avoiding annealing and preserving the material’s attributes.

Mild Steel:

Heat to 900 to 1,100 degrees F (482 to 593 degrees C) but stay below annealing temperatures (1,200 degrees F/649 degrees C).

High-Carbon Steel:

Heat to 1,000 to 1,200 degrees F (538 to 649 degrees C) but stay below annealing temperatures (1,300 degrees F/704 degrees C).

Aluminum:

Heat to 300 to 400 degrees F (149 to 204 degrees C); annealing typically begins above 650 degrees F (343 degrees C). Note that aluminum does not change color when hot, so be cautious of burns. Also, aluminum melts from the inside out. The aluminum oxide coating, which forms from aluminum’s exposure to oxygen in the air, melts at over 5,000 degrees C, and the aluminum itself starts to melt around 200 degrees C. You do the math. Be cautious!

Stainless Steel:

Heat to 900 to 1,200 degrees F (482 to 649 degrees C) for softening without full annealing; annealing typically begins above 1,300 degrees F (704 degrees C).

Copper Alloys:

Heat to 500 to 900 degrees F (260 to 482 degrees C) for softening without full annealing; annealing typically begins above 1,200 degrees F (649 degrees C).

To measure the metal’s temperature, you can use temperature crayons (thermal chalks or infrared thermometers). If you’re working with steel, you can simply observe the color. When steel turns dark red, it’s likely between 1,000 and 1,200 degrees F, where it is typically safe to bend without annealing. Avoid brighter red or orange hues, which indicate higher temperatures that approach annealing. (Again, none of this applies to aluminum, which does not change color when heated.)

To avoid overheating, keep the heat distributed. Move the torch in sweeping motions to avoid concentrating heat in one spot. Also, allow the material to cool naturally after bending. Rapid cooling can cause hardness changes or warping, depending on the material.

Nothing to Gloss Over

Knowing exactly why, when, and how bending speed and heat affect your results at the press brake can make a world of difference. There is much to know—heating material and bend speeds are some of the least understood and employed on the floor. At least now you do not need to gloss over the bending speed function on the press brake controller or hesitate to get the torch out when necessary.
