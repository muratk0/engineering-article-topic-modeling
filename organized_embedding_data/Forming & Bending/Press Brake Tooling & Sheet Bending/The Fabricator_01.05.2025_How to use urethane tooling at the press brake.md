# How to use urethane tooling at the press brake

Bending Basics

Sizing your tools, predicting the radius, and more

STEVE BENSON

U

rethane pads provide a soft yet resilient forming surface, reducing marking on the sheet metal. Last month, we examined urethane and its properties, including its durometer, a measure of its hardness, strengths, and weaknesses. This month, we explore how to use urethane tools to form sheet metal in the press brake, starting with the use of urethane pads.

The Retainer Box

Urethane acts like a solid hydraulic, distributing the force from the press brake ram equally across all the inner surfaces of the retainer box, including the bottom and sides (see

Figure 1

). The retainer box consists of a bottom and either two or four sides. Because the urethane pad must be at least 10 times the volume of the penetrating punch and material, the pad itself and the retainer box can get rather large. In fact, the retainer box will often exceed the width of the bed. In that case, the retainer box may require support through gusseting.

Depending on the durometer of the urethane used, forming tonnages can become quite intense. They are not as high as bottoming or coining, but they are still greater than air forming.

Regardless of the type of retainer box, most will have an air channel under the pad, allowing for reduced tonnages and greater penetration of the punch and part. Many larger pads have several holes passing through them, allowing for the insertion of rods or tubes. The pressures during forming can be varied to improve the results.

FIGURE 2 When springback occurs and the bend angle opens slightly, the inside bend radius opens slightly as well. This is why, when using a urethane pad, the inside bend radius does not match the punch radius exactly.

Large, soft, low-durometer pads might be topped with a thinner, harder, higher-durometer urethane pad. This allows the forming of short flanges; the flat will equal one to two material thicknesses from the part edge. Without the extra topper pad, the part wouldn’t bend but instead just dig into the softer pad.

Because of urethane’s elastic memory, it will return to its original shape. However, higher-durometer pads will require more time to relax and return to their original shape.

Urethane pads might be chewed up or even have chunks missing. Nonetheless, if the pad is 10 times the volume of the penetrating object, those areas should fill with urethane under pressure, and your bend will be fine—well, almost always, anyway.

Hysteresis

Urethane is tough; oils, solvents, and paint do not phase it. What destroys urethane is overworking. Again, these pads need to be at least 10 times the penetrating volume of the material and punch. If your pad is less than that, it will develop internal heat, which causes the pad to disintegrate.

This is called

hysteresis

, an energy loss that occurs when the material is subjected to repeated deformation and recovery cycles. A portion of the input energy is lost as heat rather than being fully recovered. Continuous deformation leads to heat generation and the degradation of urethane properties. Hysteresis can lead to reduced force transmission over multiple cycles, requiring periodic replacement or cooling periods.

The phenomenon impacts the performance of the urethane pad over time. It will not be a dramatic change; you may not even notice any degradation, but over time, the consistency and quality will gradually fade. Eventually, hysteresis will reduce the effectiveness of urethane tooling by lowering the resistance force during forming operations. Repeated cycles cause a microstructural breakdown, leading to permanent deformation or compression.

To minimize the effects of hysteresis, you need to select the proper urethane tool. For heavy-duty applications, use high-performance urethane grades with lower hysteresis. Pad thickness matters here. Thicker pads experience more internal friction and heat buildup, so choosing the right thickness can help minimize energy loss.

Allow time between press cycles to dissipate heat and prevent degradation. Some applications benefit from preloading the urethane to reduce excessive deformation. Using urethane with higher rebound properties (lower hysteresis) improves efficiency and longevity.

Predicting the Inside Radius

In urethane forming, the inside radius is produced in a manner that in some respects resembles bottom bending. The urethane supports the material so it presses against the punch, forcing it to take on the shape of the punch nose radius.

Still, the inside bend radius on the part will not match the punch radius exactly. That’s because of springback. Once pressure is released, the bend angle springs open slightly—and the same applies to the bend radius (see

Figure 2

). For more on springback, including the springback factor, type "Springback factor on the press brake" in the search box on

thefabricator.com

.

Because the urethane forces the workpiece against the punch face, it can resolve the issue of

multi-breakage

. This occurs when the material separates from the nose of the punch, as shown in

Figure 3

. This is caused by the large amount of springback encountered in large-radius bending, resulting in an inside bend radius much smaller than the punch nose radius. A urethane pad placed in the die forces the material against the punch nose, eliminating the problem (see

Figure 4

).

The depth of penetration into the urethane pad determines the bend angle. The deeper the penetration, the greater the angle of the bend (less the amount of springback, of course).

Shaped pads aid in the production of difficult-to-shape bends. U bends are a good example. Certain U-shaped pads can achieve bend angles of 200 degrees. In most cases, this allows for sufficient overbending to compensate for springback in a 180-degree U bend.

Additional Uses for Soft Urethane

Some shops use softer low-durometer urethane for "load balancing" across a die and punch, or even the entire length of the bed. Consider a combination acute/hemming tool. In this tool, the ram takes hold of the workpiece and only begins to form after the hemming portion of the tool closes. This works great if you use the entire length of the tool, but if you work off to one side, the tool closes unevenly. Your part slips and forms inconsistently in both angle and dimension.

Urethane can help here. You can place a small piece of urethane in the die opposite the forming area. When the punch comes down, it encounters both the part and the urethane, balancing the load across the tool (see

Figure 5

).

Beyond Bending

There are many other ways to utilize these softer-durometer urethanes in the general shop environment beyond the forming department. For example, urethane can be used as a stripper for hand-operated or single-station punches. Whoever you choose as your supplier will be more than happy to help you select the right durometer, a simple pad, or a shaped pad tailored to your specific needs.
