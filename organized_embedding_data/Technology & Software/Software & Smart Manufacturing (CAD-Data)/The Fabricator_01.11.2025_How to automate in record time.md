# How to automate in record time

or fabricators everywhere, quality and precision are irreplaceable. But sometimes, it really does come down to speed.

Some recent Bystronic installations at CO Building Systems Inc., a manufacturer of pre-engineered steel buildings in Ephraim, Utah, bear that out in a couple of ways. The more prominent of the two, the installation of a modular automation system, stands out not only because it was the first large-platform automation system shipped from Bystronic’s factory in Switzerland, but also because of the remarkably short amount of time it took the two companies to install it this past June.

But more about that later.

Cutting Faster as a Bonus

What came first for CO Building Systems was the Bystronic 12-kW ByStar Fiber 6225 flat laser that was installed in November 2024.

In one six-hour shift, the 100-person company runs about 35,000 lbs. of mild steel through the laser daily. A big portion of that load is built-up members for steel construction: I-beams, brackets, web sections, and occasionally connection plates. The bulk is flat building panels, the majority of which are 3/16-in. flat carbon steel.

CO Building’s laser includes an edge detection camera, a parts (and scrap) conveyor, and an automatic nozzle changer. In less than a year, the machine has revolutionized how the company produces metal structural parts. Previously, the company used a plasma cutter to process those parts, which worked to its satisfaction for years—that is, until the fiber laser came along.

"We’re probably 50% more efficient than we were," CO Building Systems Floor Manager Dean Holden said. "We went from a plasma cutter that was running about 120 in. per minute, and I think we’re into the 600 mark now.

"A lot of people purchase these things for quality, but we’re after speed—speed is our game here."

That’s not to say that precision has been a problem. Holden said that on thicker material—the company will cut up to 1-in. plate for some of its parts—the laser isn’t any faster than the plasma cutter, but "these lasers are a lot better with accuracy and quality," he noted.

Automation in Record Time

As big a deal as that boost in productivity has been for CO Building, the even bigger deal was probably that the two companies installed and commissioned a modular automation system in a matter of just a few weeks in May and June to support the laser.

CO Building placed two shuttle tables on the backside of the massive system, which measures 112.4 ft. by 47.5 ft. A large vacuum unit loads sheets from one table into the laser, and while the laser cuts that first sheet, the loader places the next sheet on the other shuttle table. When the laser completes the cuts on the first sheet, it conveys it out of the cutting zone and pulls in the second sheet to start the next round of cuts.

A fork unit then travels perpendicular to the table to pick up the finished sheet of parts and drop it off on one of two carts. That cart then moves outside the system to a spot where operators can remove the finished parts from the skeleton one sheet at a time or wait for the stack of finished sheets to reach a predetermined limit before they remove the parts. The latter option allows the system to run lights-out when and if CO Building desires it.

This kind of operation wasn’t always the norm for Bystronic.

Several years ago, Bystronic purchased Antil S.p.A., an Italian company that specialized in automation systems for fabricators. Udo Drafz, regional service operations director for Bystronic, said that Antil S.p.A. was very good at designing and building one-off custom systems but not as good at implementing standard manufacturing practices.

That’s why Bystronic transferred that operation to Switzerland—a switch that helped the company create the modular automation system that it is now selling worldwide. It was the first of three moves that yielded the solution now running at CO Building, which was the first such Bystronic system to be manufactured in the land of stunning mountains, fine watches, and Roger Federer.

"If you needed something 32 ft. by 721/3 in., Antil S.p.A. would put that together very neatly and do it, but they weren’t very good at making 100 of them," Drafz said. "And now Switzerland took over the process and improved the way they did it so that now the system is less flexible—it has some standard sizes, widths, and lengths—but it’s modular. So now, it’s like Lego pieces, where you say, ‘OK, I have one laser that I want to load from the back and I need 60 different materials stuck on 30 shelves.’ And then Switzerland takes the modules to make that up and puts them together in the set."

A second notable change made by Bystronic: Unlike at any time before, the automation systems are now tested and approved in Switzerland before they are taken apart, crated, and shipped. That alteration in the company’s M.O. helped decrease the installation time needed at CO Building, simply because the system didn’t need to be checked and rechecked on-site. It already had been.

Through a combination of modular construction, pre-installation testing at the factory, and a host of help on the ground, CO Building Systems was able to knock down the installation time of its automation system by several weeks.

Bystronic

Previously, "the standard was that they would build the parts according to the cut drawings, put them all in boxes, they would arrive on-site, we would put it together, and then we had to go through the startup and commissioning phase," Drafz recalled. "Here, we save time because the modules were already put together once in total, tests were run, verified, and then it was taken apart and put in boxes in a way that we knew which box had what.

"It was something we decided to do with the system because of the issues we had solved," he added. "We had found in the previous models that it took several weeks to finalize installation. As for us, the customer site is much more valuable than the site inside our factory. We would rather spend the verification time in the factory than on-site in front of the customer. In today’s day and age, the customers are time sensitive. They spend a lot of money on an investment and they want to get their return."

The third change that helped get the new setup running so quickly was the number of dedicated personnel who worked with CO Building’s people on-site to set and finalize the machinery once it hit the shop floor. Bystronic’s installation team included two Italian technicians and two local Bystronic technicians—in addition to many CO Building employees pitching in to do whatever tasks they could.

Having so many people on-site helped shave weeks off the time that normally would have been allocated for the project.

"I’d have to look at the Gantt chart to see exactly what we pulled it off in, but I want to say it was like three-and-a-half weeks of a projected six weeks," Holden said. "It think we kicked butt there. We supplied some help to facilitate these guys, to help them along their way to the installation.

"[We were] just trying to be as efficient as we could," he added. "We have overhead cranes, we have forklifts. We have willing bodies here to help. We were assertive. We did what we could do to help them. Anything they’d let us do, we did. And they sent a good crew out. They put in a lot of extra hours to help us out, so we definitely appreciate that."

Drafz noted that helping customers like CO Building is a two-way street for Bystronic.

"CO Building is a good partner," Drafz said. "We see a long partnership with them. They expect to grow in the near future, and we want to be a partner with them."
