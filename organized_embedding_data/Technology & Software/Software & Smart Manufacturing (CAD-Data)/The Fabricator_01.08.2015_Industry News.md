Talent shortage grows as risk for the manufacturing industry, reveals BDO study

While the manufacturing industry is in resurgence, concern over the industry’s talent shortage is escalating. Findings from the third annual BDO USA LLP analysis of risk factors listed in the most recent 10-K filings of the largest 100 publicly traded U.S. manufacturers reveal that challenges persist when it comes to closing the industry’s skills gap. Ninety-eight percent of manufacturers mentioned risks related to labor concerns, up from 97 percent in 2014 and 75 percent in 2013, according to the "2015 BDO Manufacturing RiskFactor Report."

Manufacturers face a one-two punch of struggling to attract workers, while their talent on the factory floor and in the C-suite is increasingly approaching retirement. By 2030, more than 20 percent of Americans are projected to be aged 65 and over, compared with 13 percent in 2010 and 9.8 percent in 1970, according to a recent study from the U.S. Census Bureau.

Amid the retirement of leading innovators and strategists, 74 percent of manufacturers mentioned concerns about attracting, retaining, and motivating key personnel and management, up from 69 percent in 2014 and 62 percent in 2013.

With many jobs to fill now and in the future, the manufacturing industry is feeling the pinch to properly execute business strategy, as well as create next-generation product. Risks related to the implementation of corporate strategy are mentioned by 91 percent of manufacturers, up from 82 percent in 2014 and 74 percent in 2013. Continued product innovation also is a growing risk, mentioned by 85 percent of manufacturers, up from 78 percent in 2014 and 69 percent in 2013.

"Thanks to the resurgence, manufacturers are on even economic footing and as a result are growthfocused," said Rick Schreiber, partner, manufacturing and distribution, BDO USA. "However, the challenge remains of filling their talent pipeline in order to pursue these expansion goals and drive the innovation that is needed to remain competitive in a global economy."

Report from ALAW: Laser’s ubiquity here to stay

Annual event shows just how far lasers have come

Lasers have evolved to become the ultimate soft tool to process metal. Admittedly, the word

process

doesn’t stir the imagination, but it applies to lasers because they perform so many, well, processes. They do not just cutting, welding, and cladding, but inspection and measurement, cleaning, heat treating, peening, and additive manufacturing.

That point hit home at this year’s Advanced Laser Applications Workshop (ALAW

®

), held in June in Plymouth, Mich., and organized by the Fabricators & Manufacturers Association International

®

. When it launched in the early 1990s (back then it was called the Automotive Laser Application Workshop) by Frank DiPietro and Dr. Jyoti Mazumder, with the support of the University of Michigan, speakers championed the use of lasers in the North American automotive sector. Resistance abounded, though in the job shop arena the CO

2

laser was starting to come unto its own.

Now lasers are everywhere, and not just the CO

2

variety. Solid-state fiber and disk lasers are gaining traction in various areas of metal manufacturing. And as presented at this year’s ALAW, yet another variety may be set to gain ground in the coming years: the directdiode laser.

Direct-diode lasers use the output of laser diodes directly, rather than being "pumped" into another medium. In traditional approaches to direct-diode technology, there has always been a trade-off between high power and beam quality. To get higher powers you need to combine diode lasers. But when laser diodes with identical wavelengths combine, they don’t travel in the same direction or merge neatly to produce a small spot size. This degradation in beam quality becomes detrimental at high powers.

But recent technology advancements have made high-brightness, high-power direct-diode lasers possible. Such lasers are now being used for conventional sheet metal cutting as well as remote laser beam welding. Francisco Villarreal Saucedo, senior director of laser application developments at Wilmington, Mass.-based TeraDiode, described the company’s wavelength beam-combining technology. As previously reported in

The FABRICATOR

, this technology combines similar but slightly different wavelength beams from multiple diodes into one powerful beam.

Axel Luft, applications manager at Laserline in Germany, showed the company’s direct-diode applications in the automotive sector, including the brazing and welding of galvanized steel; welding of aluminum; and cutting of small holes for antennas, cables, and other assemblies.

Solid-state laser cutting systems are also pushing the envelope. Mustafa Coskun, global product line manager, beam delivery subsystems, for Oxford, Mass.-based IPG Photonics Corp., described a 12-kW fiber laser cutting setup. Using nitrogen assist gas, the high-powered laser cuts 0.25-in. mild steel at 500 IPM. "The cutting speed increased exponentially with laser power," he said. "But in order to get these results, the important parameters to consider are laser power, modulation, the assist gas, the speed of the cutting process, spot size, nozzle diameter, and standoff height. It definitely takes expertise to develop this recipe."

A big focus of this year’s event was the welding of dissimilar materials. For instance, Masoud Harooni, laser welding specialist at Greenfield, Ind.-based Keihin North America, described some of his research performed at Southern Methodist University. There, he and his team demonstrated how real-time observation of weld behavior, when welding galvanized steel to aluminum, allowed him to find the optimum preheating speed to stabilize the weld condition.

Lasers have become a quintessential component of many critical processing applications, which is why, as in past ALAWs, this year’s event had a variety of presentations on process monitoring. Presentations from Xiris Automation, Blackbird Robotics, Precitec, and others all detailed the state of the art. John McCauley, product specialist at North Logan, Utah-based Ophir-Spiricon, boiled it down to power, spot size, and location, including travel speed. During his presentation, McCauley described a monitoring system that measures not the beam itself but instead the Rayleigh scattering (the scattering of light off the molecules in air) created by the beam.

Arnold Braunsteiner, owner of Vienna-based Plasmo GmbH, described a photo diode-based technology capable of monitoring direct metal laser sintering, a growing process in metal additive manufacturing. When a laser builds up a part layer by layer, what’s the porosity level? Is there surface roughness, or any lack of fusion? To answer these questions and more, photo diodes measure the light from the process and transfer this data to a monitoring system that interprets the information.

Tom Kugler, fiber systems manager at Novi, Mich.-based Laser Mechanisms Inc., described technology for monitoring process conditions on and beneath the workpiece surface. "It would also be best if we could look at the weld penetration in real time to see that we’re getting the weld we need when we finish a part."

He described a weld monitoring system that looks ahead of the weld (for seam tracking) and detects the standoff distance between the laser head and workpiece. It also monitors the weld surface as deposited behind the laser beam. Perhaps most significant, it detects the penetration depth of the weld itself in real time. It does this through inline coherent imaging that uses a separate sensing beam. "Any extra distance that’s traveled is indicated as a change of penetration of the weld," he said.

Bernard Swiecki, an assistant director at the Ann Arbor, Mich.-based Center for Automotive Research (CAR), returned to ALAW this year to give a report of the automotive market. His forecast for most vehicle types: steady growth, though not as dramatic as seen during the years immediately following the recession. The North American production forecast is expected to grow to 17.9 million vehicles this year, a 2.8 percent increase. Still, the volume of vehicles is expected to reach 19 million by 2018, far surpassing the 15.4 million vehicles produced in 2007.

In this healthy business environment, the use of the laser will continue to grow, and Frank Geyer, product manager, laser systems, at Farmington, Conn.-based TRUMPF Inc., described just how many processes the laser can perform in the manufacturing supply chain.

Although broadly applicable, Geyer’s presentation focused on hot forming. Once workpieces are work-hardened after hot stamping, trimming with hard tooling usually isn’t practical, so in these applications the 3-D cutting laser fills a need.

Look before and after hot forming and subsequent trimming, and you find laser usage everywhere. High-volume blanking, traditionally done with the mechanical stamping press, now can be performed with cutting lasers. In the making of tailor-welded blanks (TWBs), short-pulse lasers ablate the coating on the edges to clean and prepare the joints for welding, after which the actual joining of TWBs is performed by—you guessed it—another laser.

Moving down to the forming press, the dies themselves are repaired or modified using laser metal deposition. After stamping, the parts are trimmed with a laser, then another laser may be used to partially soften certain areas of the formed part—allowing, say, the bending of small features without risk of fracture, or the bending of a hole perimeter to form a collet. And of course the parts can be laser-marked for traceability.

As presentations at this year’s ALAW showed, the laser has become a powerful, incredibly flexible tool in metal manufacturing. Its ubiquity is probably here to stay.

—

Tim Heston, Senior Editor

Domestic aluminum extrusion consumption grows 7.6 percent in 2014

The Aluminum Extruders Council (AEC), Wauconda, Ill., and the Aluminum Association, Arlington, Va., have released industry estimates of aluminum extrusion shipments in North America by major markets for 2014. Among key findings in the "End Use Shipments of Aluminum Extruded Products" report, domestic consumption of aluminum extrusions in the U.S. and Canada, which includes imports, totaled an estimated 4.90 billion lbs. in 2014, an increase of 7.6 percent over the revised 2013 total of 4.55 billion lbs.

This is the fifth year in a row that shipments of extrusions have grown and reflects a nearly 44 percent increase in annual shipments compared to 2009. Aluminum extrusions are the third-largest product segment in the domestic aluminum industry, representing about 18 percent of the total market.

Building and construction continues to be the largest market segment in the extrusion sector, with domestic shipments of 1.83 billion lbs., or 37.4 percent of the domestic market for extrusions. In 2014, shipments in this market segment grew 8.3 percent compared to the previous year. Doors and windows, representing 31.9 percent of this market segment’s total shipments, grew 10.4 percent over 2013. At 31.7 percent of the building and construction market segment in 2014, shipments to curtain wall, storefronts, and entrances increased by 9.0 percent over 2013.

Transportation is the second-largest market segment, totaling 1.50 billion lbs. in 2014 and representing 30.6 percent of total domestic shipments. In total, shipments to transportation end uses increased by 10.8 percent over 2013. Passenger cars, trucks, and buses accounted for 42.8 percent of shipments within this segment in 2014, followed by trailers and semitrailers at 28.8 percent.

Other market sectors include consumer durables (9.6 percent), machinery and equipment (10.2 percent), electrical (10.2 percent), and other end uses (2.0 percent). While not for domestic consumption, U.S. and Canadian producers also exported 287 million lbs. of aluminum extrusions in 2014, representing 5.4 percent of total industry shipments.

High-volume joining process expands use of aluminum in autos

In partnership with General Motors, Alcoa, and TWB Co. LLC, researchers from the Department of Energy’s Pacific Northwest National Laboratory (PNNL) have transformed a joining technique called friction stir welding (FSW). The technique now can be used to join aluminum sheets of varying thicknesses, which is key to producing auto parts that are light yet retain strength where it’s most needed.

The PNNL-developed process also is 10 times faster than current FSW techniques, representing production speeds that, for the first time, meet high-volume assembly requirements. It delivers strong, lightweight parts, such as a car door that is 62 percent lighter and 25 percent cheaper than that produced with conventional manufacturing methods.

The two-phase, six-year project is funded by the Department of Energy’s Office of Energy Efficiency and Renewable Energy with in-kind partner contributions from each of the participating companies.

An FSW machine looks and acts like a cross between a drill press and a sewing machine. Lowered onto two metal sheets sitting side-by-side, the pin tool spins against both edges. As it travels along, the pin creates friction that heats, mixes, and joins the alloys without melting them. The process welded 0.5 MPM, however, which is too slow for auto industry production standards.

PNNL created dozens of unique tool designs with varying shapes, lengths, and diameters of the pin. These were assessed against a variety of weld parameters, such as the depth, rotation speed, and angle of the tool. Through statistical analysis, the team identified the optimal combination of tool specification and weld parameters that could consistently withstand high-speed production demands.

PNNL provided the weld and tool specifications to TWB Co. and GM. TWB then independently welded, formed, and analyzed more than 100 aluminum blanks in close coordination with GM. GM subsequently stamped its first full-sized inner door panel supplied by TWB—free of imperfections—from aluminum sheets in varying thicknesses.

TWB now has a dedicated FSW machine at its production facility in Monroe, Mich., based on PNNL’s process that can produce up to 250,000 parts per year.
