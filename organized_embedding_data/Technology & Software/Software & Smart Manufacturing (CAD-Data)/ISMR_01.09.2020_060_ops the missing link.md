# OPS: the missing link

[TARİH: 01.09.2020 ISMR]

FOR THE PAST few years, process efficiency has been one of the hottest industrial topics and is one of the founding pillars of the Industry 4.0 paradigm. Systems with advanced performance, interconnected and able to communicate, and partial or total automation solutions for minimising low added-value activities are now an integral part of the strategies of many businesses, not only the most advanced.

“But today, to understand what process efficiency really means in the sheet metal industry, we have to identify the individual activities that make up the production process, the environments in which these activities take place and the stakeholders involved in each of them,” explained Italian sheet metal specialist, Salvagnini.

## Identifying bottlenecks and process issues

“We generally come across three different environments in all the companies we visit,” explained Stefano Cera, Salvagnini product manager. “The first is the company’s information management system – the ERP/MRP software. The second is CAD/ CAM programming and the third is the workshop and its machinery. Obviously, this is a heterogeneous mix of real and virtual environments, and this adds a further level of complexity to each potential intervention.”

The information management system is a large container which collects and indexes the raw materials, the type and quantity of semi-finished products available, the products available for the customer and, of course, the orders and items to be produced. This information container is also the starting point for each of the company’s production processes.

“The information management system is generally what gives the green light to each activity, by generating a production list,” Cera continued. “Very often this is still a static list, printed on paper, not organised by materials and thicknesses, which is transferred by hand to the second station in the process, the CAD/CAM programming.”

The CAD/CAM programming, where the programmer organizes production, is the connection between the information management system and the workshop – but is not a transparent or cost-free connection.

“What we usually find in companies is a tray where production orders are progressively stacked in a pile,” Cera explained. “The programmer picks the first one, reads it, pairs the product items to the relative machine programs, analyses the information management system requests manually and then rationalises them according to urgency criteria, material type and size. He chooses the sequence for producing the parts, selects the machine programs in the database or programs new ones, and so on.

“Here we see the first weak point, relating to the efficient use of material and the optimisation of waste: let’s imagine that the programmer is already busy, managing nesting for instance. He has no way of knowing that a new order has just arrived and is in the pile which includes a whole series of parts to be made in the same material, and could easily be included in the current production so as to further optimise use of the material. It is also clear that this activity has low added value and very high risk of error and is also a blind operation: when there are urgent tasks, there is no immediate visibility of the new parts to be produced,” he told ISMR.

He also pointed to another paradox, related to the rapid development of production technologies: the balance between workloads which has in some way been upturned, with increasingly rapid systems that have to be guided by specific and increasingly numerous machine programs, which mean very costly manual operations. In context with high code rotations and extremely small batch sizes, production bottlenecks may well lie in the CAD/CAM programming, he explained.

“Unfortunately, we also see a number of recurrent issues on the machine,” Cera stated. “These are in some way linked to the process and the sequence of activities. Static production lists on paper have a limit: at factory information management system level, there is no visibility of what has effectively been produced, and what on the other hand has not been produced. This problem is solved only if the operator provides feedback, inevitably manual, at the end of a production list.

“This is clearly a limit in a market which today is characterised by increasingly tight schedules, in which production is no longer stock-based: unless the production plan includes appropriate buffers for managing any problems, the feedback arrives when it is already too late, when there is no more time for correcting any delays. And this is where urgent tasks occur which have repercussions on the whole workshop: the operator always has to cope with extremely variable production to manage this and perceives the working environment as highly disorganised.”

Another activity, typical of cutting and punching, is the unloading and stacking of the machined parts: the paper sheet with the nesting drawing is the only information the operator has available to physically recognize.

In 1993, Salvagnini launched its first Order Processing System (OPS), modular production management software. Within the production equation, this acts as the central coordinator, managing and distributing information among all the environments and stakeholders involved, designed to eliminate critical points and drastically improve process efficiency.

## The order processing chain

“OPS receives the production list from the factory information management system in real-time, and delivers an updated version to the programmer. Where required, it provides support for the programmer’s activities by defining the priorities, automatically generating the machine programs and sending them to the workshop. It checks the availability of raw materials or semifinished parts and generates feedback to the factory information management system, updating it in real-time, part by part. It can make autonomous decisions according to production logic – or according to a mix of multiple production logic. It integrates labelling, traceability and warehouse management upstream and downstream of the cutting, punching and bending activities.

“OPS can define the rules and algorithms required to automate the process, adding intelligence to the system,” Cera explained. “For instance, we may decide to add constraints relating to maximum scrap tolerances, efficiency or the number of kits to include in the same nesting. OPS ensures that the system complies with these logics, following the customer’s production methods, whether make-to-order, make-to-assembly or make-to-stock.

“We exploit this form of intelligence to eliminate low added-value activities,” Cera continued, “and also to reduce waiting times to zero. OPS is also an extremely scalable solution, as it consists of standard modular units that are easy and immediate to install, without interfering with existing company activities. The scalability of the solution lies not only in its modularity, but also in the possibility of adopting it one step at a time. The subsequent expansion of its function is therefore a natural and fluid progression within the architecture, as OPS adapts to the customer’s actual situation.”

OPS can also be used when working with parametric programming - some products (such as doors, elevator units or air treatment systems) have highly repeatable shapes and highly variable sizes and/or options, often managed by variables that progressively become precise values.
