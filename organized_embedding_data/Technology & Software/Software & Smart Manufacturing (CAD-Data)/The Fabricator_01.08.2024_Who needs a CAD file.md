# Who needs a CAD file?

[TARİH: 01.08.2024 The Fabricator]

Tech Spotlight

How robots adapt to the job in front of them

By

Tim Heston

An operator manipulates a "pencil," which a robot mimics to perform an operation (in this case, sanding).

Nordbo Robotics

L

ess than a decade ago, Jimmy Jørgensen and Leo Zhou met, by sheer coincidence, at a factory in China. Representing separate organizations, the two were brought in to evaluate an automation project. Although unique to the application, the problem had an all-too-familiar root cause: Reality doesn’t always reflect what’s simulated or expected, nor do digital files have all the information needed to produce a part.

Jørgensen had written his PhD dissertation about how robots can learn not through a laboriously programmed teach pendant, but by quick, intuitive demonstration, a feat that ideally can replicate the expertise of experienced, skilled people on the shop floor—especially in pressure-sensitive work like buffing. After that initial meeting, Zhou and Jørgensen went back to their hotel, sketched out an idea for enabling robots to mimic a skilled worker’s movements, then presented the idea to company executives. They liked it immediately. "All this happened about a year and a half before we started the company [in 2017]," Jørgensen said, "but that experience started it all."

That company, Denmark-based Nordbo Robotics, today focuses on automation of processes like grinding, sanding, buffing and polishing, blasting, and painting, all usually used near the end of the metal fabrication value stream. And all historically have been difficult for robots to tackle. Robots in these applications aren’t just moving from point A to B or following a limited set of prescribed motions. In many of these applications, robots need to emulate the nuanced way skilled people wield their tools.

Two approaches Nordbo offers aim to solve these challenges. First is Mimic. Focused on large-batch production, workers use a wireless "pencil" to program. The operator moves the pencil in a specific way and the robot arm mimics that motion. In many applications, the robot or cobot has an external force control unit, mounted between the arm and end effector, that allows it to adapt the force level in real time.

Again, the idea is to mimic the operator’s motions as closely as possible. And all of this happens without a CAD file or any other part data. Like an operator, the robot moves based on what’s in front of it. As Jørgensen explained, this approach works well for shops trying to augment the skilled labor they have for their high-volume work.

Still, most metal fabricators have a wide product mix, with a few high-quantity jobs being processed alongside a long tail of low-quantity work. Mimic works for that high-quantity work, but not that long tail of low-quantity jobs. Programming takes time, and workpiece placement needs to be consistent. Fixturing isn’t too arduous when automating large batches, since one or a handful of fixtures will cover thousands of workpieces. But what if a shop has thousands of disparate, low-quantity parts? Fixture development would take time, and storing them all would take a lot of space.

To tackle this problem, Nordbo developed the CraftMate platform. In these setups, the robot scans the workpiece, identifying complex curves and contours. Using a touchscreen interface, the operator draws what areas the robot needs to sand and polish (or other task); the robot suggests an optimal toolpath over each surface to be polished; and the automation takes it from there. "The system really excels on large workpieces that are difficult for a human to reach manually," Jørgensen said.

Here again, the setup does not require a CAD file but instead builds a program on the workpiece that’s in front of it. This eliminates the need for complex fixtures that position work exactly the same way every time.

Some applications that require more information do incorporate CAD models. In one foundry application using the CraftMate platform, for instance, the robot needed to compare the digital model with the scan, so it knew what elements to remove. Digital models also help provide information that the vision system cannot see, like interior walls, should the robot need that information to do its job. For the most part, though, the act of calibrating the digital model with reality is more trouble than it’s worth.

The technology overall reflects a growing trend in manufacturing technology. In the past, engineers redesigned entire value streams around automation. Robots aren’t people, the thinking went, and having a robot replicate what a person did just wasn’t practical. Thanks to emerging programming platforms, that thinking is starting to change.
