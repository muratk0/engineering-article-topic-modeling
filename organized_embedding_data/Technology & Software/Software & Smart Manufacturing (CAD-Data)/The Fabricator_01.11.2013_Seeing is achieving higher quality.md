# Seeing is achieving higher quality

[TARİH: 01.11.2013 The Fabricator]

Technology Spotlight

Metal fabricator commercializes its approach to preparing job instructions

By Dan Davis, Editor-in-Chief

CMP is a multinational, multimillion-dollar fabricating company based in Chateauguay, Quebec, Canada, but it still shares the same production headaches that other metal fabricating companies share. In this instance, however, it’s internally developed pain reliever may also prove to be a remedy for other fabricators that struggle with properly communicating work instructions to shop floor employees or with fabricating quality parts.

About three years ago, CMP realized that it had the development muscle and the information technology architecture to begin working on software that would allow it to share work instructions with its shop floor via computers. Since then the virtual work instructions have moved beyond simply replicating word directions to now including pictures and video. The job details, called up when an employee scans a bar code, are delivered over the Internet.

To prove the concept, CMP implemented it in its hardware insertion department at its Chateauguay fabricating facility. As any metal fabricating company can attest, hardware insertion is a necessary, but sometimes troubling, activity. In CMP’s case, the difficulty was having the correct inserts placed in the correct holes. Some of the more complex hardware insertion jobs had as many as 20 different holes with a slightly different piece of hardware being inserted into each individual hole. Even complete drawings were difficult to understand with so much insertion activity taking place.

The first step in the quality improvement exercise was building visual instructions for the most challenging hardware insertion jobs. CMP personnel snapped photos of the correct sequence of insertion actions and then included them on the internally developed knowledge-sharing architecture it had developed. The development team also connected the software to the hardware insertion equipment, which meant the machine operator couldn’t progress until the right insert was placed in the right hole.

For a more complete picture, imagine the operator beginning a new job at the hardware insertion machine (see Figure 1). He scans the bar code on the traveler document, and the job details appear on the touchscreen next to the equipment. The operator sees the first insertion and places the insert into the hole. The hardware insertion machine communicates to the software that the hardware was placed, and the operator is able to move on to the next set of visual instructions on the touchscreen. The instructions are always in sync with the hardware insertion operation, and the operator can’t advance to the next step until all the pieces are inserted into the correct positions for that particular step.

“Right away our internal and external rejects from that department started to decrease immediately,” said Ryan Zimmermann, who is the grandson of the company’s founder and who was involved in the software implementation. “We were able to show them the sequence and which one goes where. It helps to guarantee that we’re getting them all in all the time. Previously they were just looking at a drawing, and it was a lot easier to forget. Now it’s a visual trigger for them.”

Today CMP has rolled out its Visual Knowledge Share (VKS) platform to other departments, such as its assembly, finishing, and welding areas. It has even prepared visual work instructions for its joint-venture fabricating operation in Guadalajara, Mexico.

Buoyed by its own internal successes, VKS is now being marketed to the manufacturing industry. It was unveiled at the American Society for Quality Conference in May. “We want to help other North American manufacturing companies to attain what CMP was able to attain,” said Kyle O’Reilly, VKS sales and marketing manager.

That’s a big statement considering that CMP has a goal of being a zero-defect manufacturer in 2013. “It’s a really hard goal to achieve, but we’re on track to coming very close to that. It’s exciting,” said Zimmermann, who is now acting as operations manager for VKS.

He is quick to point out that VKS is different from other software products on the market that display visual work instructions for manufacturing and other work processes that are dependent on correctly followed step-by-step instructions. He said that VKS is a dynamic system that always ensures that the most recent revision is the only version of work instructions that an employee can access (see Figure 2).

Figure 2

Bar code scanning is probably the most efficient way for operators to access work instructions. A simple scan of the bar code on the job traveler initiates a project search and the work instructions appear on the screen. Companies also have the option of simply having the employee type in a project identification code, project name, or company name to find the right work instructions.

“If we decide that a guy has a better process, we can update it instantly,” Zimmermann said. “The second the process gets changed, the guy on the shop floor—because he’s on the Internet—gets an automatic pop-up saying ‘The visual aid that you were working on has been updated. You will automatically be redirected to the newest version.”

In addition, VKS has many other features that separate it from competitors’ products.

It is a cloud-based system, which means it can be accessed with any computer or tablet through the Internet. Users don’t need to download software on their computing devices to access the work instructions that have been prepared with the software. All programs are stored on secure servers located miles away from the Chateauguay headquarters.

VKS also has a form-based communication tool that allows it to enhance the interactivity between the work instructions and the user. Within these visual work instructions, a form can appear to ask the operator a question, to send a notification, or have the operator input information.

For example, CMP employees in the finishing department are recording variables such as paint thickness, gloss, and production time on these forms as they work on high-quality external doors for a transportation project. As the information is recorded, it is simultaneously sent to the customers as well.

This approach is useful in reminding workers that following production steps is critical.

“We could have forms appear later [in a project] asking them, ‘Are you following the instructions? Have you done this? Have you done that?’ Zimmermann said. “And they have to confirm and actually save the form, which gets stored in the reporting section of the software. We have physical evidence that the operator is actually following the work instructions in the process.”

“That’s one of the main things that sets VKS apart from many competing solutions. You can give your employees and operators work instructions, but who’s to say they’re actually looking at them?” O’Reilly said.

Because all of this production information is permanently archived and anyone can access it at any time, VKS can be used to generate reports to provide a key glance into manufacturing operations. A manager can call up a dashboard type of user interface through the software and can review key performance indicators. On a macro level, the manager can tell if a department is exceeding the expected time scheduled for a certain fabricating operation. On a micro level, a manager can drill down to see if an operator is not producing parts at an expected pace.

To create these work instructions, Zimmermann said that the software’s “editor” mode is designed to be as intuitive as possible. The creator of the work instructions is presented with a template with prompts such as “Add text” or “Upload image.” Arrows can be added easily to draw attention to something in the photograph.

He added that editors of visual work instructions within the CMP family come from all types of backgrounds. In Mexico, engineers build the instructions. In Chateauguay, an employee with an English literature degree and a certified welder are among the people creating visual work guides.

The focus right now for the VKS team is manufacturing, but O’Reilly said the product makes sense for other industries where step-by-step procedural guidance might be useful. The company planned to unveil its knowledge-sharing tool to metal fabricators at FABTECH® in late November in Chicago.
