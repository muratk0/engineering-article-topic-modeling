# The streamlined draft

Technology Spotlight

Toronto AI tech company automates the conversion of 3D CAD model to 2D drawing

By

Robert Colman

DraftAid

T

he key value of automation anywhere in your business is taking the repetitive work out of employees’ hands and freeing them up to do the value-added work that makes your business stand out. Artificial intelligence tools are being adapted in several ways to help with this process, and DraftAid’s software is yet another iteration of this.

DraftAid is a Toronto-based company. Its tool automates the process of converting 3D CAD models into 2D drawings, empowering engineers to focus on innovation instead of repetitive tasks.

"We are popular with two groups of customers," said Abdullah Elqabbany, application engineer and co-founder of DraftAid. "The first are mechanical engineering designers, especially those in the automation machine building world, because they have a lot of drawings to make. The second group are fabricators that get a lot of STEP files, because they need to quote those STEP files and manufacture from those drawings. Our fab shop customers are about 20% of our users but make up about 45% of the drawings produced using the tool. That’s mainly because they use it to generate drawings for both quoting jobs and manufacturing them."

Company engineers, meanwhile, are there to check that what is generated is accurate.

"The engineer moves into a reviewer role," explained Elqabbany. "After initial configuration, the AI typically reaches 70% completion. As the engineer finalizes the drawings in SolidWorks or Inventor, the AI learns and adjusts, often reaching 90% accuracy within the first year. It handles plates, sheet metal, and simple machined parts perfectly most of the time, while novel parts may hover around 60% completion."

COMPANY ROOTS

Elqabbany was working at a company in Toronto a few years ago as one of 40 drafters making tens of thousands of drawings from scratch for the city’s Crosstown Place and 160 Front Street West buildings. He spoke to a friend, Tahsin Rahman, who was working on an AI-focused PhD. (Rahman would become CTO of the company they eventually formed.)

"I said, ‘there has to be a better way to do this,’" said Elqabbany. "So we started working on generated drawings through AI as a fun technical project. Then through a mutual friend, we were introduced to our CEO, Mohammed Al-arnawoot, who understood how to turn an idea into a startup company."

After getting seed funding in February 2024, the company landed its first three customers and, by the end of 2024, had made 10,000 drawings for them. By the end of 2025, that number had grown to 210,000 drawings for engineering teams across North America and Europe.

AI LEARNING

Creating drawings can be a tedious task, especially when you’re dealing with many parts with very similar design parameters. Having engineers review the AI-generated drawings is key. Not only does it ensure accuracy, it also helps the AI improve.

"Any changes you make to drawings are fed back into the AI," said Elqabbany. "Our mechanical engineering design customers really appreciate how the AI gets smarter and better over time, and that the quality keeps increasing."

For fabricators and metal shops, the value is mainly in the speed of the process, Elqabbany said.

"They can upload 100 STEP files at the same time and get the drawings back a few minutes later, review them all, and generate a quote," he said.

During a demonstration of the tool, Elqabbany showed how once the drawing is generated, it can be edited in SolidWorks or Inventor (the company plans to expand to support CREO and Siemens NX in 2026, and SolidEdge and Catia in 2027). Any changes made to the file are then uploaded, and the AI learns from that drawing.

"We configure the AI to follow the specific standards and templates our customers already use (e.g., an ANSI SolidWorks template)," noted Elqabbany. The title block on the drawing can be customized, as can any other details on the drawing. "For example, shops that use really accurate lasers to cut out holes will say, ‘You don’t need to really draw the holes; the DXF does it all for me,’’ said Elqabbany. "Shops where they’re using plasma cutting, which is less accurate, will require the dimensions on the holes, whether they need the flat pattern or not. Some don’t need the flat pattern. Shops that use manual bending machines will say, ‘I need a drawing that shows me exactly where the bend lines are from the nearest hard edge.’"

ONBOARDING AND CHANGE

According to Adam Euerby, senior product manager, customer onboarding takes about two weeks.

"That two weeks starts when the customer explains their drawing standards that will be key to perfecting their drawings," said Euerby. "Then we go through several fine-tuning rounds as they submit their drawings and find items that they forgot to tell us to incorporate into the engine.

"One of the key values for them is that they have access to our full engineering team," he continued. "This isn’t a low-touch digital tool. We are a team that works with its customers."

Euerby noted that it does change a company’s working culture.

"Inevitably, you go from having the person who was drafting the drawings taking on the role of a reviewer," he said. "The methodology also changes in that you may have to implement a batch review flow."

DraftAid does not currently support assembly/weldment drawings, although the company is launching a beta for this in June 2026.

"We started where we did because in any given assembly, you have many parts," said Elqabbany. "It makes the most sense to take care of those many drawings and let the engineer address the weldments. But we want to expand. Customers certainly want that capability."
