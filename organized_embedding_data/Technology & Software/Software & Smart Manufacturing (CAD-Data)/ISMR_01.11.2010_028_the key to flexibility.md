# THE KEY TO FLEXIBILITY

[TARİH: 01.11.2010 ISMR]

The trend in flexible manufacturing systems (FMS) is towards small versions of the traditional FMS, called flexible manufacturing cells (FMC).

Today, two or more CNC machines are considered a flexible cell and two or more cells are considered to be a flexible manufacturing system.

Thus, a Flexible Manufacturing System (FMS) consists of several machine tools along with part and tool handling devices such as robots, arranged so that it can handle any family of parts for which it has been designed and developed. An FMS is designed to combine the benefits of an automated transfer line and the flexibility of a job shop to produce a variety of parts on a group of machines and other workstations connected by an automated material handling system.

## PRODUCTION AGILITY

The University of Kentucky has outlined the history, benefits and continuing applications for FMS systems.

“First of all, FMS is a manufacturing technology. Secondly, FMS is a philosophy. ‘System’ is the keyword. Philosophically, FMS incorporates a system view of manufacturing. The buzz word for today’s manufacturer is ‘agility’. An agile manufacturer is one who is the fastest to the market, operates with the lowest total cost and has the greatest ability to ‘delight’ its customers.

“FMS is simply one way that manufacturers are able to achieve this agility. An MIT study on competitiveness pointed out that US companies spent twice as much on product innovation as they did on process innovation. German and Japanese companies did just the opposite,” explains the University.

## BENEFITS AND LIMITATIONS

There are numerous benefits, and limitations, to FMS systems.

The benefits include less waste; fewer workstations; quicker tool/die change; reduced downtimes; better quality control; more efficient use of machinery; increased capacity and reduced labour costs. There is also greater production flexibility with FMS – particularly useful for manufacturers choosing to compete on this basis, rather than on cost. For example, batches can be produced in lots as small as one piece per batch (for applications such as aerospace).

The drawbacks include a limited ability to adapt to changes in product or product mix, longer planning and development periods and cost (which can sometimes be heavier in the initial and maintenance stages).

However, flexible manufacturing systems offer various advantages in low- to mid-volume, high-mix production applications. Future systems may see lower and lower quantities per batch.
