# A KEEN OBSERVER

[TARİH: 01.11.2018 ISMR]

Dr. Jan Antonis, Managing Director, InspecVision, highlights how Augmented Reality, Industry 4.0, increased automation and IoT is impacting the future of sheet metal inspection.

ISMR SAYS: “Keeping down the cost and time spent on inspection requires the systems to be automated and easy to use.”

InspecVision Ltd. was established in March 2003 to develop a range of precision inspection machines for industrial applications. The founder of the company, Dr. Jan Antonis BEng PhD, carried out advanced research in computer vision systems at Queens University Belfast in Northern Ireland and saw that there was a gap in the market for a system which could measure parts quickly and easily. Sales of the Planar inspection system, for quality control and reverse engineering applications, began in 2005. InspecVision products are now sold in over 30 countries.

ISMR spoke to Dr. Jan Antonis, as a specialist in the design and manufacture of computer vision-based measurement systems for industrial applications, about the company's latest new sheet metal inspection developments.

ISMR: Please outline any recent successes and achievements?

JA: In the last 12 months, we registered an almost ten-fold increase in our sales to the U.S. While there are probably lots of environmental reasons for this, much of it was down to a new distribution model. Over the years, we have seen that sheet metal and metrology are a bit like oil and water. They have little reason to mix. Historically, the standard set of metrology systems just do not meet the needs of sheet metal manufacturers (they are too expensive and slow and difficult to use). So, sheet metal manufacturers don't contact metrology dealers, and vice versa, as they have little to talk about...

InspecVision was started by sheet metal manufacturers with the goal of creating an easy to use, rapid, low-cost metrology system which met the needs of the sheet metal industry. However, just having the

right product was not enough. The trick was to create a partnership between us, the manufacturers of sheet metal production equipment (in this case, Mitsubishi in the U.S.) and a solid metrology dealer, PQI. These three ingredients were vital in reaching the customers and ensuring that we had the skillset to demonstrate, install, train and support the system.

We are a company of engineers, so having new ideas for products is never really a problem. Around half of our personnel have PhDs and a background in sheet metal, so creating new products is not an issue. We have had to apply ourselves more to the commercial side of the business. We are still a small, specialised company (there are 15 of us). Production is very busy at our facility in Northern Ireland so we are hiring at the moment. Our product has no moving parts, can be outsourced for production without losing any IP and offers future possibilities for scale-up.

Also in terms of success, InspecVision was among the winners at the Chartered Institute of Marketing (CIM)'s prestigious Ireland Marketing Awards on 9 November 2018. We were awarded winner of the 'Most Marketing Impact on a Small Budget' category, sponsored by Ulster University Business School.

ISMR: What is your approach to Industry 4.0 as a company?

JA: As it happens, our first IoT machine passed Factory Acceptance Testing a few days ago. Our automation levels have always been extremely high. Taking operator skill out of inspection has always been a crucial means of reducing the cost of inspection. For example, by scanning a barcode the operator can import the shape, dimensions and tolerances from a CAD file, inspect the part and output a report for SPC to a unique location. That's set-up and inspection without touching the computer...

Adding an IoT JSON interface on top of this automation was relatively simple. Now, the set-up and inspection can even be triggered by a smart phone. This kind of connectivity is particularly useful when creating in-line applications.

As the machines around our systems are becoming more evolved and IT- and computer-driven, customers are coming to us with more custom software requirements to integrate our machine with their other computer systems. We did have an interface for this but we are making it more web-driven. Now, you can even trigger the inspection from a mobile phone if you wish.

Automating the inspection process and providing the data to set up the report is crucial. Setting up the first report is always a challenge. In sheet metal, you can change part and product design very quickly (from receiving the CAD drawing to outputting the part) – it is a streamlined process, so you must be able to create a new inspection report in a very automated and low-skilled way. We can import relevant data (such as dimensions and tolerances) so, with a single click, you can automate the set-up and inspection of the part without touching the computer, maybe even by scanning the barcode. That level of automation has been there for a while, but Industry 4.0 is now about enhancing the interfaces in the machine. There is no need for customers to program the machine or even trigger the inspection.

ISMR: What are your views on the current business climate for sheet metal professionals?

JA: We are based in Northern Ireland so we are in the eye of the Brexit storm and that is certainly a distraction. The fall in the value of sterling has helped our exports but we are concerned about disruption to our suppliers and increased tariffs. I am sure we are not alone in this...

We are pretty well placed to weather Brexit, however it pans out... Our customers are worldwide - I think we have more customers in Tasmania than in Northern Ireland. This wide customer footprint protects us. However, I can see that this is a very uncertain and difficult time for other manufacturers in Northern Ireland.

We have looked at establishing offices in Europe - this is particularly feasible for us as we are very close to the European border. The uncertainty means that we cannot enact any definite plans right now, but just ensure that our stock and supply is looked after and see how Brexit develops...

ISMR: Which issues are of prime importance for your customers and how are you addressing these issues for them?

JA: Our customers are always concerned with the time and cost of inspection. Keeping that cost down requires the inspection system to be automated and easy to use. On the other hand, their production environments are becoming more computer-driven and integrated so it is essential that the automation integrates with their existing computer systems. This often involves custom software to set up, trigger and output the reports.

Augmented Reality (AR) is also garnering a lot of interest. Inspection can be a complex question which requires a complex answer. The ability to distil that answer by projecting the colour green onto the good parts and the colour red onto the bad ones is pretty neat. We have a new feature where we use AR to project the results back onto the part so, for example, customers with 300 parts on the table to be inspected will see green (if the part is good) or red (if the part is bad) on the parts, so that they can sort the items. It is an instant visual inspection process.

This is of particular interest in the U.S. - our customers there would like to take the expensive metrology machine out of the process. They want the measuring machine on the shop floor, run by sheet metal operators who can see if parts are good and bad (and where they are good and bad, by projecting colours).

ISMR: What is your strategic and technical focus/vision over the next few years?

JA: Strategically, I would like to set up more partnerships with manufacturers of sheet metal production equipment and metrology dealers. Our two biggest markets now are China and the U.S. We are also hoping to grow our market share in Europe (i.e. Germany). Partnerships are key. All the main manufacturers have their offices there, so you need a footprint there and that means more investment for us. We are looking at that now.

We have gone from selling directly to using sheet metal distributors in Europe, as well as metrology dealers, and if we can get the two groups together in Europe, this would be the right way forward for us in Europe. Customers need inspection equipment but suppliers of production equipment lack the skill and knowledge to install, train and support metrology equipment. Metrology dealers have this skill but lack the contacts in the sheet metal industry.

Re our technical focus, we see an extra application for AR as production machines are becoming pretty reliable, certainly in 2D, for cutting. One of the main problems for customers is when there is a manual error-prone process (inserts or weldments on a part). There are interesting ways to apply the machine where you quickly scan the parts and then project the deviations back onto the part. So, for example, if you're putting inserts into the part, it would project where you have forgotten to put the inserts, or put the wrong inserts into the part or incorrectly welded the part etc.

It is this kind of manual in-process feedback, as you are making the part, that turns the inspection machine almost into a production tool and could be a very interesting focus for us over the next few years.

For example, an operator can cover one of our tables in parts and, after a few seconds, the system will project green onto the good parts and red onto the bad ones. It can even project the deviations back onto the part to show where a given part has failed. While this can be a useful way to visualise an inspection report, it becomes really interesting as a way to identify gross errors in any manual operations during production (e.g. a welded part can be checked in a few seconds to determine if a lug has been welded on upside down, or if some inserts or pins have been missed). This in-line process feedback turns your inspection machine into a production tool.

It is this kind of manual in-process feedback, as you are making the part, that turns the inspection machine almost into a production tool and could be a very interesting focus for us over the next few years. The measuring speeds for scanning into 3D can be very high. This means the ability to check a part (even without a CAD file), to make one good part and then store the 3D data for that part and then make another part and confirm that you have welded it correctly etc. This can leverage all the technologies that we already have for inspection for almost instantaneous feedback.

ISMR: Which trends do you see developing in inspection/sheet metal markets?

JA: The trends I note are the need to keep automation up and skills levels down, and then integration with existing computer systems. As mentioned, one application that seems to be gaining traction is Augmented Reality (not just for inspection but also for feedback so that the inspection machine becomes a production tool). In this mode, the 2D or 3D scanner projects the errors in the part back onto the part.

We mostly produce, develop and design our own hardware and software (except for third party software to compare the 3D scan data to the CAD file). I think that manufacturing offers great possibilities for AR – anything involving a manual step to train operators or to give them visual steps to follow (e.g. bend this, punch that). A vital element of that is feedback, however. The computer must be able to scan and sense what has actually been done and verify that an action has been done correctly. This is one step beyond what our customers have asked for, but we see the enhanced potential for AR here. It also chimes with the current skills shortage in this industry.

ISMR: Which challenges have you encountered when inspecting different types of materials?

JA: We use vision systems to measure the parts. The parts can look different, depending upon the materials used i.e. copper parts can be quite reflective. Electrical cabinets are becoming more complex and customers are trying to measure large thick copper parts in 2D when the object is actually a 3D object. So, giving a 2D answer to a 3D project is a challenge....

When softer materials are punched, for example, they are designed as 2D objects but become 3D objects (from a metrology perspective) so there are definite technical challenges. This is particularly true for some of our automotive industry customers who laser weld different material grades together for doors etc.

ISMR: Have you launched or are you launching any new products this year? Did you launch any new products/ software at EuroBLECH in Hanover?

JA: This year, we have really been focusing on fulfilling orders but we recently created a 6m long inspection flatbed machine (a vision system) for a German automotive parts manufacturer. It was a little big to bring along to EuroBLECH but it can scan the upper surface of a flat part and project deviations back onto the parts and is a production tool as much as an inspection tool.

Our camera-based technology lends itself to large changes in scale. For example, our smallest scanning area is 119mm at around one micron accuracy and the largest 6m long machine is 100 microns accuracy.

We do get a lot of requests for custom machines but we also have our own product portfolio. The 2D technology is pretty modular and, to that, we can attach 3D scanning capability. We have a rotating table with optical markers on the outside. We use the markers to locate the table. Every time that the table is moved, it doesn't matter because the machine locks onto the markers. This means that, as camera resolution get higher and higher, our accuracy gets better and better. On some of the first machines we ever shipped, for example, the main end-of-life item is the Windows operating software. We can keep upgrading them very easily.
