# Waterjet Picks Up Production of Robotic End-of-Arm Tooling

[TARİH: 01.10.2016 The Fabricator]

Applications

Bastian Robotics chose a waterjet cutting table to produce parts from a variety of materials for its end-of-arm tooling.

SITUATION

Business at Bastian Robotics, a division of Bastian Solutions in Maryland Heights, Mo., has really picked up over the last five years. That’s about when the company’s focus zeroed in on the custom design and manufacture of end-of-arm tooling (EOAT) that integrates with material handling robots. A particular bump in volume in the last two to three years led to expanding the employee base, bringing the count up to 60.

"While we are capable of handling a wide variety of products, our niche is custom equipment designed for companies with products that are out of the norm," said Ben Viorel, manufacturing manager. "We’ve provided EOAT to handle things like blocks of aluminum scrap, bags of feed, and various products that are produced in extreme heat or cold temperatures."

The variety of materials affect the design. "For example, we had to use 316 stainless steel or plastics that survive in an acidic environment for handling automotive batteries. We use any kind of material you can think of based on the project.

"About 70 percent of our customers are food and beverage companies, between 10 and 15 percent are involved with automotive machine tending, and the rest are whatever you can think of that needs to be handled."

Until December 2015, a few pieces of equipment—a lathe, an ironworker, horizontal saws, and welding machines—handled the fabricating done in-house. Local job shops were contracted to produce the majority of the components used to build the EOAT.

"We were running into production issues. We would need something in a hurry and have to either pay an expedite fee or wait. And when you’re working on a custom project, details and drawings sometimes change. An adjustment would require an engineer to stop, request a change, email a print to the supplier, and hope the change wasn’t missed on the finished piece. This slowed the process quite a bit," Viorel said.

"Scheduling was one big part of the problem. The other consideration was that we wanted more control—particularly over critical jobs."

SOLUTION

Bringing all production in-house was the logical answer. Nearly two years of research, tucked in between the rapidly increasing number of projects, resulted in the purchase of two pieces of CNC equipment. They were chosen with the expectation that outsourcing would become unnecessary. The machine tools were an OMAX

Center and a Baykal APHS 41160 175-ton press brake. "The two pieces go hand-in-hand since most of our pieces have bends in them," said Viorel.

The large number of different materials that need to be cut to produce the custom gripping mechanisms made a waterjet the logical choice for the first stop in the production process. The ability to cut large parts was also important.

"We can cut steel up to 8 in. thick and 12 ft. 8 in. long. We haven’t needed anything close to that thickness yet, but some of our side frames for the pallet dispensers are 2 in. thick by 4 ft. wide by 12 ft. long." The waterjet work envelope is 13 ft. 10 in. by 6 ft. 7 in. so the company has a little leeway to increase component size.

A terrain follower option that adjusts the height of the waterjet as it cuts material that is not completely flat was an add-on to the system. It protects the nozzle from potential collision damage and keeps the cutting going. A MAXJET 5i one-piece, diamond, integrated nozzle and a higher-horsepower direct-drive pump were also chosen options. "We went for the diamond-head nozzle and a larger pump to give us a faster cutting speed and quicker turnaround," said Viorel.

The abrasive waterjet cutting system, the press brake, and a material storage rack that was built in-house surround a crane. "The two pieces of equipment and the material rack are positioned in a triangle to efficiently organize production. The crane can grab material from the rack and move it to the table. Then we can use the crane’s pneumatic lifting system to transfer large pieces to bending or welding."

There was a bit of a learning curve with the new equipment, but one machinist with previous waterjet experience eased into the waterjet operator position. A second-shift operator was hired shortly after the waterjet was up and running and the hiring of a backup operator wasn’t far behind to ensure continuous cutting capabilities.

"We’ve moved 95 percent of our production in-house," said Viorel. "We can run double shifts if necessary, and we control much bigger portions of the jobs than we used to.

"With the waterjet here, when an engineer needs to adjust a part, we can make the change on-the-fly and continue with assembly, testing, or whichever phase of the project we are in at the time. Speed is the biggest advantage to bringing production in-house. We can move these projects through much quicker now," said Viorel.

"Having these production capabilities in-house means we are able to drastically reduce our manufacturing costs. We are currently measuring cost savings and working on formulating new pricing to reflect the savings we are realizing."
