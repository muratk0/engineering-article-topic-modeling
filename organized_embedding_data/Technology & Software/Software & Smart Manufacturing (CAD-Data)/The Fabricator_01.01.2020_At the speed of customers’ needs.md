# At the speed of customers’ needs

Velocity Machining & Welding expands fabrication footprint to deliver on orders faster

By Robert Colman

Editor’s Note: This feature originally appeared in the December 2019 issue

of Canadian Fabricating & Welding.

V

elocity Machining & Welding in Dartmouth, N.S., was launched by cousins and machinists by trade Craig Langille and Sean MacPhee in 1999. The primary focus of the business has always been to provide precision, custom-machined components and excellent customer service.

The ability of the pair has seen their business grow steadily, serving a broad selection of customers in offshore oil and gas work, mining, transportation, ocean technology, and construction. In 2015 they purchased Techtronics Machine Works (TMW) in Musquodoboit Harbour, N.S. Many TMW customers required Controlled Goods Program registration (which is necessary for those companies that fabricate components that have military or national security significance in Canada) and ISO 9001:2008 certifications to produce complex parts. This added a new breadth to the business partners’ work.

Between the two shops they had 20,000 square feet of shop floor space. Although the focus was primarily on machined parts, customer demands also made it valuable to have fabricating capabilities at the Velocity facility. These included welding, shearing, bending, additive manufacturing, and waterjet cutting.

That focus on customer service has allowed the pair to continually grow their capabilities as the needs of customers have changed. Last year that meant expanding Velocity’s fabricating capacity by adding a second press brake and a fiber laser and growing yet again into a new facility.

Fiber Precision

As Langille explained it, every addition to Velocity’s capabilities is intended to support current customers better.

"Our waterjet machine runs about 18 hours a day," Langille said. "We bought that for in-house work. For laser-cut sheet metal parts, we had to farm out some jobs to as far away as Ontario. Ultimately, with the volume we were producing, it made more sense to make an investment in new equipment. Of course, we always hope that we’ll pick up other work specifically for the laser, and I think there’s real opportunities for that in this market. Right now the laser market in Nova Scotia is not very big. There are two or three CO2s, but we’ve got the only fiber in the Halifax area. And we’ll cut whatever needs to be cut."

The company decided on an Ermaksan Fibermak Raptor fiber laser cutting machine from Ferric Machinery. The unit is a straightforward fiber laser table with a 4-kW head and a shuttle table for loading and unloading. The shop uses it to cut steel, aluminum, and stainless.

"The training on the machine was quite straightforward," Langille said. "Our current operator, Adam, already had some experience with laser cutting machines so he picked up the new controls quickly. The other operator only required a few days to get a basic working understanding of it."

The shop uses its new fiber laser to cut steel, aluminum, and stainless.

At the same time the company added the laser, it also introduced a new Ermaksan Power-Bend Falcon press brake to run alongside another Ermaksan press brake it already had in its Dartmouth shop. The new brake is a 12-ft., 220-ton model.

As a machinist, Langille was impressed by how the fiber cuts.

"I had a customer in the shop recently who couldn’t believe the cut quality," he said. "But as I told him, we’re a machine shop, we always look at the functionality of the part but also cut quality. The fewer hands that end up touching a part, the better it is for our business. The last thing I want to do is spend time on a part once it’s cut. The fiber laser has saved us time in that way."

Fab-specific Shop Space

At the same time the company added the laser, it also introduced a new Ermaksan Power-Bend Falcon press brake to run alongside another Ermaksan press brake it already had in its Dartmouth shop. The new brake is a 12-ft., 220-ton model.

These new purchases meant the need for more space as well. Velocity was lucky enough to find an 11,000-sq.-ft. facility it moved into with the two press brakes, a Hyd-mech saw, a plate roller, a shear, and several welding machines. All told, 14 people currently work at the property on Thorne Avenue in Dartmouth. With the move-in, Langille had all new electrical installed and a compressor.

The layout of the shop gives the team plenty of room to work on large assemblies, if necessary. Just as important for Velocity, however, is that the relocation of some of its fabricating machinery at its headquarters frees up about 3,000 sq. ft. of space for more machining equipment there.

These racked assemblies will be used for traffic control units. Having a dedicated fabricating team, including a fiber laser, makes delivery of this type of assembly simpler for Velocity.

"We still do our waterjet cutting and a lot of precision welding at our headquarters, but the new facility gives us space to work on large fabrication jobs in a dedicated space."

Next Steps

Langille explained that he and MacPhee are always interested in acquiring new businesses and more work. For the moment they are in the process of honing operations at the new fab shop and planning for its growth, which they are confident will be rapid.

"Ultimately, I’d like to get a couple operators for the fiber laser, freeing up Adam to spend time quoting and programming for the machine, but we’re taking it a step at a time," Langille said.

For the first time Langille and MacPhee recently hired a salesman to promote the work that they do.

"We have always been word of mouth, and that has worked very well, but having someone on the road has reminded some people of us who haven’t worked with us for a while and don’t necessarily know all of our capabilities. It is bringing in new work that is good to see and helping us re-establish some old relationships. That’s important—we still think of this business as very much one where relationships matter. I still like to see the guy I’m doing work for."

There are other processes that MacPhee and Langille might consider investing in in the future, but the biggest challenge for their businesses is finding the kind of tradespeople they want as they continue to grow.

"Welders are probably the easiest to find," Langille said. "But it’s a struggle every year finding the right people."

And it’s not just knowledge Velocity hopes to get in a new team member. They want tradespeople with deductive as well as technical skills, and who feel a sense of ownership and pride in performing their jobs.

For a young person new to fabricating, welding, and metalworking, Velocity and TMW are something of an ideal training ground. As businesses that cover everything from basic laser cutting to managing the requirements of ultra-precise machined components, they are something of a rare combination.

But Langille thinks of it in simpler terms—they get the work done the customer requires, whatever it takes.

"Having a better-equipped fab shop just allows us to get some of those jobs out the door faster," he said.
