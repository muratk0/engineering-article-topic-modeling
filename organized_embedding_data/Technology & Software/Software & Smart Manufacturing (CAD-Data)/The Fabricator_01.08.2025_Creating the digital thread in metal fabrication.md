F

or years, talks at FABTECH and numerous other industry events have circled around the "digital thread" of manufacturing—building that "single version of the truth" throughout and between enterprises in the supply chain. Listening to these presentations, fab shop engineers and CAD technicians might think this all sounds nice, but it doesn’t reflect reality.

When they go back home, they still receive PDFs of paper prints from customers, no 3D CAD. And even if they do get a CAD file, they find themselves spending a lot of time hitting "save as," exporting the file into STEP or another file format. A day later, the customer sends a part revision. That’s usually no problem, except this time someone forgot to hit "save as" and export the file. Now, two versions of the same part file float around the shop, and it’s not discovered until the part is made and the customer complains.

Other people in the fab shop office might spend hours perusing purchase orders and quality documents, trying to find the information they need. They might email a customer, leave a voicemail—and wait. And wait. Knowing the clock is ticking, they might look at the purchase order again.

Ah, there’s the spec I need! Why is it buried on p. 42

? Then the customer contact, at long last, emails back, but with a different spec that was buried in the purchase order.

OK, which is the right one?

They email back for clarification, leave a voicemail—and, again, wait.

To most, the digital thread sounds like a nice idea, but for now at least, it’s frayed and broken into pieces. How can these be put back together? Earlier this year at POWER 2025, a user conference of quoting software provider Paperless Parts, executives at three software vendors sat on a panel to discuss ways in which that digital thread can truly be realized: no frays to worry about (email file attachments), no awkward stitches (file exporting, etc.), just strong and seamless throughout the supply chain.

About the Ecosystem

"The digital thread means so many things to so many different people. But to me, it means a consistent digital representation of a part through each piece of software that you are using. The question is, how often are we breaking that digital thread?" That was Jason Ray, CEO and co-founder of Paperless Parts, who added that his company aims to help "connect manufacturing across the shop floor and beyond." Ray invited Nipun Girotra, CEO of 1factory, and Jeremy Stadtmueller, director of product management, design engineering, and documentation at Autodesk, on a panel to discuss the digital manufacturing thread—and what happens when that thread breaks.

"You get an RFQ. We download it and put it onto our desktop. You open the files and try to decide if we’re going to quote it. Today, you’re probably not connected into your buyer’s purchasing system. Your CAM system might not be connected. Neither is your QA system. It’s been this way for far too long."

Ray and his fellow panelists described the ideal of an open-architecture ecosystem where every software platform works seamlessly together: CAD, CAM, quoting, purchasing, scheduling and production planning, QA management, and more. "I see the digital thread as a continuous process of moving information back and forth. That’s what makes the digital thread real," said Stadtmueller, who works with Autodesks’ cloudbased Fusion product line. "You shouldn’t have to care what a file type is. You need the right information when you need it. That’s why we want to open our architecture in a secure manner so that anyone can integrate with it. It’s your data in the software—your part numbers, your process, your material specs—and you should have access to it."

He added that although Fusion has a broad swath of functionalities—CAD, CAM, computer-aided engineering, simulation, and more—no one software package can be everything to everyone. "That’s why we try to be a best-in-class tool where we think we can deliver, but then partner with others and allow them to connect to the system. We want to make it easy and simple."

Automate the QA

Girotra held up the lid to his coffee cup. "Let’s say you’re making this lid. It’s described by a number of parameters: its diameter, the position of the drinking slot, its dimension. All this goes from a drawing to a control plan, which lists all these parameters you need to control. You define how each parameter is measured. What gets measured and when? What’s the sampling rule? Do we check every part? Every tenth part? That’s in the plan. We use it for first article, for PPAP, and ongoing inspection data collection."

He added that 1factory aims to streamline the plan creation and automate the data collection. So, if someone uses a CMM or scanning arm to measure a dimension, the data is transferred automatically, no keying-in required. The same holds true for calipers and other handheld inspection data, which connect to laptops either via USB or over Bluetooth. Creating customer reports becomes automatic.

The system also automates supporting processes for QA, tracking gauge use and when each was calibrated, and it generates internal nonconformance reports (NRCs) to document a root-cause analysis of the QA issue. On top of this, data can be made visible and available to customers. "We aim to build out the digital thread all the way through the quality," Girotra said.

This includes quality within the factory’s four walls and at the customer, when the product is in use. He described a scenario involving an airplane malfunction. The culprit was found to be consistency with a pipe ID, wall thickness, and ovality. Extraordinarily rare, the failure simply wasn’t caught in testing. Still, because data was shared immediately, every other plane using that engine got the necessary upgrade, eliminating the possibility of the same failure reoccurring. A robust digital thread could make this kind of data-sharing easier and far more common.

Girotra also spoke of streamlining compliance activities. "I had a friend who worked in QA, and he used to joke that he’d spend four months out of the year preparing for audits, be it an ISO 13485, AS9102, customer-specific audits, or anything else."

The idea is to automate the process as much as possible, from standard procedures to work instructions, access control, assignment of control, and the tracking of training. "With a click, you can see if you have the right documents and that you’re covered for your audit."

Necessary vs. Unnecessary Waste

Consider everything that happens from quote (or initial sales contact) to cash (receiving customer payment for a job) at a custom metal fabricator. Review the lean manufacturing literature, and you find you can divide all of it into three categories: value-adding activities, necessary waste, and unnecessary waste.

Unnecessary waste is the low-hanging fruit of improvement: wasteful back-and-forth trips to and from a toolroom (put tools near the point of use); extra processing steps that customers don’t require (overprocessing); and so on.

Value-adding activities occur when tools touch metal to make a product. It’s a necessary step, without which the product couldn’t be made. Upgrade equipment or shift manufacturing methods (machining to 3D printing, for instance), and if the change is successful and makes economic sense, you add more value in less time.

Finally, you have the necessary waste, steps the customer might not directly pay for, but they’re still necessary to get quality products out the door. To avoid starving your constraint work centers, you might have some level of workin-process. A product needs to be transported between work centers and to outside suppliers.

The trick here is to first uncover any unnecessary waste hiding within what’s deemed "necessary" waste.

Can a work center be moved even closer to another work center? Is there a more efficient way to transport products to the outside supplier?

Such improvements basically change what everyone thought was a necessary process into an unnecessary waste that can be eliminated.

Those on the software panel discussed perhaps what remains the most significant "necessary" waste in metal fabrication: the flow, sharing, and management of information. Shops need information to fabricate and ship, so all the steps taken to manage that information are necessary—right? Not necessarily, especially as software, AI, and other digital technologies continue to evolve.

On the panel at the POWER conference, Girotra recalled working at a prior employer in the semiconductor capital equipment industry. The firm built extraordinarily complex machines. "Every day something would go wrong; a part wouldn’t fit; a process wouldn’t go as expected. We’d produce nonconformance reports, and nearly all of them stated it was the suppliers’ fault."

Chuckles in the audience commenced. Most were from custom fabricators or machine shops. Evidently, this wasn’t the first time they had heard "supplier fault."

Girotra then described a project in which he read through the granular details of more than 1,200 NCRs. "When we dug into them, we found that the true root causes of these problems weren’t being accurately documented. Originally, 99% of these NCRs was assigned to supplier fault. It turned out to be just 40%. Another 40% was bad engineering and design, and 10% were other problems, like parts being dropped on the floor or certain pieces being damaged because of improper packaging."

A lot of these reports were handwritten. Some were hard to read and understand. Girotra called this "unstructured data," and it’s here where AI could help in a significant way.

Stadtmueller followed up by describing foundational models that, in essence, help software understand what’s being drawn. "You could say, ‘I need a bracket with holes like this,’ and the system itself could draw the model accurately." Humans obviously would be involved to review, but for simple parts in particular, trained engineers wouldn’t spend their days manually drawing simple brackets with simple holes. Software will help them along the way. "That’s the North Star, and that’s where we’re headed."

Girotra emphasized that AI remains probabilistic; it isn’t perfect, so it’s not a tool that can automate an entire process and take it out of human control. Still, it remains a tool that could streamline myriad activities and turn necessary wastes, like manually reviewing all NCR documents, into unnecessary ones.

Add interoperable software that shares data in an expansive ecosystem, and you may find more hidden wastes that can be deemed unnecessary and eliminated, as data flows freely from customer to supplier and beyond—and back again.
