Vision-enabled robots help improve nut welding productivity

Courtesy Tim Jacobs, photographer,

www.timjacobsphotographer.com

SITUATION

D&D Automation, based in Stratford, Ont., is primarily a controls integrator that designs and implements PLC-based control systems for industrial machinery, manufacturing lines, process environments, and other automated facilities.

The company’s engineers had the idea of providing a pre-engineered system for seam welding using vision-enabled robots. With a traditional seam welding machine, it is problematic if the seam moves a millimeter to one side or the other, as the final assembly will likely have to be scrapped. So the company developed a system that uses a 3-D vision system to track the position of the seam and provide instructions to a robot that follows the seam regardless of how much it moves.

The prototype worked well, but the first potential customer that D&D showed it to said, "This is very nice, but our real problem is nut welding."

Nut welding is required in many high-volume manufacturing industries to attach a fastener to a part so that an assembly can be completed. It commonly is performed on machines that require an operator to position the part in a special fixture and then load the nuts and activate the welder. This approach requires a significant capital investment for each unique part and also requires a dedicated operator for each machine.

While the seam welding machine remains a viable technology, the company’s focus shifted to fasteners. Engineers went back to the drawing board to apply the vision-enabled robot assembly (VERA™) technology to the nut welding problem.

RESOLUTION

The first system built by D&D welds nuts on an A-pillar made by an automotive supplier. D&D used ABB IRB140 and IRB2600 industrial robots and Cognex In-Sight 7200 vision systems for this application.

"We selected In-sight 7200 vision systems because they provide 800- by 600-pixel resolution—six times the processing speed of the base In-Sight Micro 1020 model—with PatMax pattern matching capabilities at an economical price," said Michael McCourt, president of D&D Automation. The vision systems, which also provide autofocus capability that simplifies setup, are suitable for production situations requiring regular part changes or with limited access to the vision systems.

Nut welding is required in many high-volume manufacturing industries to attach a fastener to a part so that an assembly can be completed. It commonly is performed on machines that require an operator to position the part in a special fixture and then load the nuts and activate the welder.

A single operator loads parts onto an infeed conveyor, and a vision system mounted above the conveyor identifies the part using the PatMax pattern recognition algorithm. D&D engineers used the Cognex EasyBuilder user environment to program the vision operations. Configuring the system to locate and identify a part involves just capturing an image of each part.

The vision system is programmed to look continuously for all of the parts being run. When the pattern recognition algorithm recognizes a part in the image, it signals the PLC to stop the conveyor. The vision system then captures another image to determine the exact location and orientation of the part and passes X, Y, and theta coordinates to the robot controller, which directs the first robot to pick up the part and place it on the resistance welder. No special fixture is required.

A second vision system then examines the assortment of nuts on a nut handler and picks out a nut of the proper size, again using the pattern recognition algorithm. This vision system also sends a signal to stop the nut handler, and the vision system captures a second image of the nut to determine its position. A second, smaller robot then picks up the nut and positions it correctly on the part.

After each nut is in place, the PLC actuates the resistance welding machine. The first robot then indexes the part for the next nut or removes the part from the welding machine after all the nuts are in place and puts it on an outfeed conveyor.

The system requires 50 percent of the attention of one operator, so that one operator can tend to two cells. The operator’s only tasks are to load parts onto the inflow conveyor from a bin and offload parts from the outflow conveyor into another bin.

Electric press brakes set the pace for lighting products manufacturer

SITUATION

Kenall Mfg. is a manufacturer of energy-efficient lighting products for challenging environments. In late 2014 the company moved its production and office operations from Gurnee, Ill., to Kenosha, Wis., while also reducing its fabrication operation from three shifts to two.

Continued and expected growth in business made the company realize it needed to create additional fabrication capacity to address its forming requirements in one less shift.

RESOLUTION

Kenall decided to purchase five new E-brake electric press brakes from Safan-Darley to help increase productivity. The brakes set the pace for the company’s flexible linear cell environment, called fabrication tracks, which begin with forming. Once formed on the brakes, the parts move through the tracks for welding, hardware insertion, and spot welding until they are complete for the next department.

Kenall’s previous press brakes didn’t have safety guarding, so the purchase of these new brakes—which feature integrated light guard technology—eliminated the need to retrofit light curtains to the old machines.

"The patented light curtain system requires zero setup when going from job to job," said Mauricio Gutierrez Matta of SafanDarley. "And it is the only light curtain system in the world that can detect a safe condition and, through the CNC, allow for ram travel to occur automatically."

Some employees who were seasoned on the old press brakes had reservations about the new machines, and the new brakes required a learning curve because of new controls, but the transition went smoothly. Offline programming from the previous press brakes continues on the new machines. Maintenance is minimal, primarily involving some greasing of fittings. Hydraulic oil changes have been eliminated. And most important, forming quality has remained high.

Currently about 90 percent of the company’s products are formed on the electric press brakes. It moved one of the old press brakes to the new fabrication track to allow flexibility for the 10 percent of jobs not run on the electric brakes.

Shotblasting system brings finishing in-house for hydraulic cylinder producer

SITUATION

The transfer of power is one of the most important and demanding processes in modern machinery. More times than not, it is achieved with hydraulic cylinders.

RDC Control, Blainville, Que., a manufacturer of industrial hydraulic and pneumatic cylinders, was looking to improve the surface finish of its cylinder end plates, while reducing processing times.

The company was using a subcontractor to air-blast the end plates to clean and improve the contact surface before applying a layer of corrosion-resistant paint. It wanted to find an in-house system that would provide a uniform surface finish in an efficient, cost-effective manner, but the parts could not be tumbled in bulk loads because the part-on-part contact would cause nicks and surface damage.

RESOLUTION

The company purchased an RHBE spinner hanger shotblast machine from Rosler Metal Finishing USA.

The machine has two direct-drive 7.5-HP blast turbines that use carbon steel cast shot to achieve the desired finish. The parts are mounted by hooks on a Christmas tree-style fixture in the 35-in.-dia. by 51-in.-tall blasting chamber. The simultaneous rotating and oscillating movement of the spindle helps ensure that the blasting media, accelerated by multiple turbines, reaches every component for uniform blasting results.

A Y switch-type overhead monorail system allows the operator to load and unload one fixture while the other fixture with the parts is being processed in the machine. This process allows operators to process multiple parts at a time with a cycle time of two minutes, as opposed to air blasting the parts one at a time.
