# Introducing order to the chaos of creating BOM tables

[TARİH: 01.08.2024 The Fabricator]

Tech Spotlight

An engineer creates software to help out with this important—and time-consuming—task

By

Dan Davis

BOM Tabulator software is designed to pull information from PDFs and assemble BOMs and tables to avoid excessive manual data inputting, which can eat up a lot of an engineer’s time. One of the benefits of the software is its ability to create a summary BOM that covers the part numbers, suppliers, part descriptions, and quantities.

A

$2 million project for a job shop sounds like a big win, but it also can be a tremendous challenge just to get that job ready for the shop floor. James Nelson, mechanical engineer for Samson Metal Engineering and Manufacturing, Lakeland, Fla., witnessed this scenario at the end of 2021. It took six 40-hour weeks for four engineers to extract information from PDFs and create bill of material (BOM) tables, which then could be used to create items such as procurement lists, labor cost estimates, and production process plans.

"The whole shop was waiting on us to plan labor and order material to get the job done," Nelson recalled. "So there’s no progress really until all drawing takeoff is done. You can’t really start a job until all of this is figured out."

Meanwhile, as is typical, the customer kept changing prints and drawings with engineering revisions. Nelson said each revision was almost like a restart on the project, requiring a manual update of anything connected with the project.

This big win for Samson uncovered the inefficiency associated with creating tabulated information from PDF documents, according to Nelson. Sure, a metal fabricator might occasionally get a CAD model from which it can extract data cleanly because it shares the same CAD platform as the customer, but for the most part, a job shop works with customer PDFs from which data is not easily pulled. In some instances, data can be copied from a PDF if it was created digitally, but even then, the task isn’t completed very quickly.

Nelson has been involved in metal fabricating for seven years, having previously worked with some of the larger theme park companies in central Florida. Over that time, he recognized the need to find a better way of extracting much-needed data from a file format that was never really designed to offer that.

Ted Van Winkle, director of engineering, Samson Metal, shares some of the same amusement industry experience as Nelson, but he has almost 10 years’ experience on the job shop side. He’s been at Samson Metal long enough to see the company take on more customers in the aerospace and power generation sectors, so he also is aware of the inefficiencies that frustrate Nelson.

"There’s a new generation coming up in the business, and they can’t be successful without us advising them," Van Winkle said. "The other part of that is that we can’t be successful without them helping us with software and technology."

Nelson has been helping Samson Metal with some software enhancements of his creation in recent years, and he thinks that the rest of the metal fabricating industry might be interested in these developments. He’s ready to share his BOM Tabulator PDF BOM extractor and labor planning tool with the public.

What the Software Can Do

Nelson said other expensive software products might be able to pull data from CAD models, but they struggle with PDFs or don’t cover the wide range of fabricating activities, such as sawing, machining, and welding. That’s how he knows that his software product can fill an important void in the market.

"We’re talking easily a 70% decrease in the time associated with takeoffs and planning," he said.

The software uses optical character recognition functionality to scan each PDF and pull out the important information, such as part numbers, dimensional shapes, part quantities, and purchasing requirements. It then takes all of that information to create:

Formatted BOM spreadsheets with consistent column formatting for part number, supplier, part description, and quantity.

A drawing tree/indented BOM spreadsheet, which can be used as a project summary.

Plate and sheet metal nesting suggestions that can help determine how much material is needed for the job and cutting instructions for the laser or waterjet machine.

Beam, tube, and pipe nesting and a cut list for saw operators. (Nelson said that cut list drawings are to be a part of later software versions.)

A summary of all fasteners and commercially off-the-shelf items, such as motors, that are needed for the job. The software can recognize the relationship between packaging and the fastener product (such as one box typically holds 100 bolts), which helps to create detailed purchasing directions. The software also adds a few extra fasteners to each order in case spares are needed.

Estimates of the surface area of the parts to be painted or powder coated, the cost of the coatings materials, and the labor required.

Calculations of part and assembly weights to help plan for shipping.

Nelson said that the software does an "acceptable" job of pulling in data from the PDFs. All of these BOMs and tables need to be checked to ensure the accuracy of the information, but for the most part, hours of collecting data and inputting it manually into a spreadsheet should be eliminated.

And when those inevitable engineering changes come and the BOMs need to be updated, an engineer only needs to change the numbers on the master table. The software takes care of updates throughout the related BOMs and tables.

"You don’t have to figure all of that manually," Nelson said. "You don’t need to keep track of how it affects everything else."

In a future version of the software, the BOM Tabulator will have the ability to extract geometries and use that information in formulas that will help to deliver more accurate estimates. It also will allow users to assign labor hours based on those estimates and then make it possible to send that information to an ERP/MRP system.

Samson Metal has been the beneficiary of the BOM Tabulator programming for the past several months, and Nelson said that he thinks other metal fabricators might be looking for similar help. It’s one of the reasons why he will be sharing the software at Booth W5584 at FABTECH in Orlando, Fla., Oct. 15-17. (The other reason is that Nelson is looking for companies to help test the software. He said that he’ll begin testing with these metal fabricating companies in early 2025.)

Reflecting back on that $2 million job that his company won, Nelson said he thinks a full-fledged PDF BOM extractor and labor planning software tool could have allowed one engineer to do the work of the four engineers who tackled the massive project over that six-week period. Maybe the work could have been completed in four weeks by that one engineer. Leaning on software to take on those administrative tasks frees up engineers to spend their time on activities that can save a company money or possibly generate new revenue, which is a recipe for financial viability in today’s incredibly competitive metal fabricating market.
