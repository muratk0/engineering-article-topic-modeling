» Holding true to their roots, Carvist recently took on one of the largest curtain wall and aluminum panel projects on the West Coast of the US, designing and installing curtain walls around elevator shafts and immense storefronts at the Santa Monica Place mall, west of Los Angeles.

Hundreds of glass panels as thick as 1-5/8 inches and as large as 80 square feet, some weighing more than 1,000 pounds, were placed without error. These panels were assembled using free suspending spiders, not traditional methods. “One mistake due to one poor field dimension could have cost us tens of thousands of dollars as there would’ve been a compounding effect of setting adjacent panels,” said Egan.

Carvist began its work at the Santa Monica Place project by scanning it and then feeding the data into 3D CAD. The aluminium and glass panels were designed using the scan data. The results: every panel was a perfect fit and provided a speedy installation. The system could be applied to virtually all aspects and phases of construction, O’Connell said. “Precision construction has arrived,’’ he said. “This is the future of the industry.”

Carvist has other applications for its 3D scanning equipment, including wooden or adobe structures that have little to do with metal or glass. Carvist recently scanned a late 19th century barn in the heart of Orange County.

Carvist knows the old-barn scans will be a rewarding experience someday by assisting historical societies with exact replicas of their sometimes fragile buildings. ■

INDIA IN VIEW

India is the world’s seventh largest country. As an emerging nation, India is part of the promising BRIC Group (Brazil, Russia, India, China); its gross national product (GNP) expanded at an annual rate of 8.9 per cent in the third quarter of 2010. India survived the recent global financial crisis better than most large economies. Its economy is poised to grow by 9.2 per cent in 2010–11, propelled by strong performances from agriculture and industry.

The country’s rapid economic growth has resulted in the creation of a large number of investment projects to cover ever increasing consumer demand and provide the necessary infrastructure. In particular, the automotive industry and its suppliers, the construction, home electronics and white goods industries as well as the steel production industry have benefited from this positive trend.

Manufacturing with sheet metal is a rapidly growing basic industry sector within the Indian economy. In addition to cars and other modes of personal transport, on a consumer level there is still considerable scope for growth in domestic appliances, home electronics and air conditioning units. This has led to increased demand for high quality machine tools for which India is one of the world’s largest import markets, with equipment originating mainly in Europe.

## POSITIVE TRADE FIGURES

India is now a primary trading partner for countries such as China. The Asian Development Bank’s (ADB) ‘Asia Capital Markets Monitor’ report stated that the “Indian equity market has emerged as the third biggest, after China and Hong Kong in the emerging Asian region, with a market capitalisation of nearly US$ 600 billion.”

Trade is now settled using local currencies rather than dollars, indicating the move away from the more US-influenced markets of the past. One further reason for India’s branding as an emerging market force is its young workforce. India Brand Equity Foundations, www.ibef.org, believes that “India is among the world’s youngest nations with a median age of 25 years”.

“India is projected to stay the youngest and has a working-age population estimated to rise to 70 per cent of the total demographic by 2030. This is the largest in the world and India will see 70 million new entrants to its workforce over the next 5 years,” it stated.

India is also creating national investment and manufacturing zones, designed to be incubators of new innovation and technology, to attract more foreign interest. The first will be in Rajasthan, alongside the Delhi-Mumbai industrial corridor.

## MANUFACTURING MARKET TRENDS

The manufacturing industry in India has made significant efforts to improve quality, design and innovation. The challenges include access to technology, funding, poor availability and the high cost of raw material and certification processes. Additionally, the globalisation of MRO services, manpower cost competitiveness, the availability of talent, location advantages and the presence of specialist capabilities combine to make India a potential global/regional MRO hub.

The most important consumers of sheet metal are the automotive industry, the household appliances industry, the railways, defence, aerospace, mechanical engineering and the construction sector, especially for roofing and cladding but also for ventilation and air conditioning ducts. Some current market trends are listed below:

■ Many of the international car manufacturers in India plan to expand their production and aim to have the majority of their vehicle components produced locally.

■ The Ministry of Steel estimates that India’s steel production will quintuple to 300 million tons by 2020.

■ High-tech machine tools from Europe, Japan and the USA are still in great demand, while Indian machine tool production primarily covers the low-price market segment. Indian companies are very interested in working with international suppliers of high end machine tools.

For companies in the field of sheet metal working, Mumbai, on the western coast of the sub-continent, is the ideal point of entry to this market. A large number of foreign companies have already settled there (the region attracts 25 per cent of the foreign investment in India) and the administration is familiar with the needs and wishes of new investors.

## AN EYE ON AUTOMOTIVE

The automotive sector (including the SUV market) is the driving force behind India’s economy. The government of India has set out an automotive mission plan for 2006 – 2016 which aims to position India as the “destination of choice in the world for design and manufacture of automobiles and auto components with output reaching a level of US$ 145 billion accounting for more than 10 per cent of GDP and providing additional employment to 25 million people by 2016”.

Domestic passenger car sales grew by 20.79 per cent to 161497 units in November 2010 as compared to 133703 units in the same month in 2009. Sales of motorcycles in the country during the month grew by 15.61 per cent to 710182 units from 614274 units in the corresponding month in 2009, according to the latest figures released by the Society of Indian Automobile Manufacturers (SIAM). Similarly, two-wheeler sales in November 2010 grew by 17.68 per cent to 930370 units from 790613 units in November 2009. Sales of commercial vehicles increased by 18.26 per cent to 48314 units in November 2010 from 40855 units in the previous yearly period, according to SIAM (Society of Indian Automobile Manufacturers).

The growth of the Indian middle class along with the growth of the economy over the past few years has attracted global auto majors to the Indian market. India provides trained manpower at competitive costs making the country a favoured global manufacturing hub. The world’s major car manufacturers continue to invest in India and now the supplier segment is also attracting private equity (PE) investments.

With a healthy metals industry and a young workforce, the future looks bright for India especially as, according to the Investment Commission in India, “India is also rich in mineral resources with large reserves of several primary metal ores like iron ore, bauxite, chromium, manganese and titanium.” ■

## THE ‘SMART’ APPLIANCE

There is much current interest in smart appliances; that is, devices that can modify their operation following signals from an electricity company. A new report from IMS Research, The World Market for Smart Appliances, forecasts their adoption will begin in the US, followed by Canada and then Western Europe.

A small market for smart appliances is now emerging; greater market growth will be fuelled by investment by governments and the utility companies themselves, and by the introduction of financial incentives for consumers. IMS Research predicts that, by the end of 2020, nearly 10 million appliances will have been installed in US homes.

GE plans to begin shipping ‘smart’ washers and dryers by the end of 2011 or early 2012, to be followed shortly by refrigerators. These appliances will have a port, to which a communication module can later be inserted. This architecture, with external communication radios, allows GE to move forward with appliances that will be quickly, and fairly easily, modified to connect to the grid once the technology and communication standards are agreed upon.

Other manufacturers are taking a different approach to designing smart appliances. These companies are waiting for a firm agreement on communication technology standards so they can integrate the entire package within the appliance. The benefit of this approach is that it will require no action from the consumer to modify the appliance to connect to the grid. Liz Cruz of IMS Research argues that dynamic pricing is needed to create demand for smart appliances. She says that “with dynamic pricing a financial incentive and therefore consumer benefit is created for purchase of smart appliances.”

The continued adoption of other peak-load management devices is a positive indicator for the future growth of a smart appliance market. Cruz believes “smart thermostats will lead the way for smart appliances, as a low-cost device that can shift a large percentage of residential energy usage with little investment and no change in utility pricing structures.” The acceptance of these similar technologies will open the door for other energy management equipment in the home.
