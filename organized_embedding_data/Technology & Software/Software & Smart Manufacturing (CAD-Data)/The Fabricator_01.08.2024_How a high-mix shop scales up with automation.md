# How a high-mix shop scales up with automation

-Dimensional Services Group bills itself as a specialist in prototype and low-volume production. "Volume" is a relative term in manufacturing, of course. Low volume in the automotive world would be considered high volume in sectors like aerospace, machinery, or ag equipment. Still, when most see the word "prototype," their minds don’t immediately turn to robotics. But at 3-Dimensional, that’s exactly what they’d see.

"Automation is a key differentiator for us. The ones that don’t go all in [on automation] don’t see the progression of great jobs coming their way. That’s why our Founder and CEO Douglas Peterson started pushing for automation not long after he launched the business 30-plus years ago. We’re always investing in technology to make things easier for employees and to get parts to customers faster."

That was Zachary Burns, operations manager, describing just how far the company has come, especially within the past decade. Today it employs more than 500 people. Besides comprehensive metal fabrication, the company has long had extensive machining capabilities, used for in-house tooling and fixture development and, of course, manufactured products. Along with this is a host of stamping presses, plastic injection molding systems, press brakes, lasers—the works.

Much of the success stems from a feat that might go unnoticed amid all the technological marvels on the floor, including welding automation of all kinds—arc, resistance, even laser welding. It’s all the innovative ways the fabricator presents workpieces to robots, including the fixture development and weld cell design. Combine the project planning and engineering that go into those fixtures and the processes they serve, and you begin to see how a low- and medium-volume operation can truly scale.

About Volumes

Not long after launching the company in the early 1990s, Peterson began focusing on automation, including the industrial laser. He and other company leaders didn’t want to build just another prototype shop, at least one too reliant on highly manual processes.

"He has always pushed the manufacturing technology envelope and replaces anything that isn’t best in class," Burns said, adding that he and subsequent company leaders "wanted to redefine the prototype market" with the ability to scale, and the only way to do that was through automation. They would never produce parts at the volumes of automotive OEMs or Tier 1s, of course, but they did see potential in bridge production—orders in the thousands or tens of thousands that could support (or offer a "bridge" to) customers in advance of full-fledged production.

For certain automotive components, this might call for some serious cutting, bending, welding capacity, and inspection, even at the prototyping level. "We might quote a seating package, and the bill of materials for a vehicle could have hundreds of different line items that require some level of manufacturing," Burns said.

As 3-Dimensional expanded its reach into automotive, aerospace, and beyond, customer demands evolved significantly. "Every situation is different, and we make sure we are prepared for anything by having available equipment at all times," Burns said. "Maybe the customer hasn’t finalized their entire design, but they still want production of a specific part. Maybe it’s a dynamic environment where the customer expects product designs to change consistently over the next 10 years. They need help getting through it, and that fits our world very well."

These needs spurred automation investments at every stage of production, including the primary blanking process. For years the company managed expansive racks of sheet metal inventory on the manufacturing floor that simply took too much space.

"When earlier generations of our 2D laser cutters were in use, a fair portion of our manufacturing floor space was consumed by material management," said Jeff Bischoff, director of engineering. "Since the investment and implementation of TRUMPF’s TruStore in our blanking development, of which we have three systems, much of our material inventory has moved to a warehouse dedicated to sheet metal and tooling. Actively transferring daily material requirements allows existing plant space to be reallocated to manufacturing capital and activity."

3-Dimensional sells its ability to respond quickly to highly variable demand from various industries, and having the right stock on hand in blanking helps make that happen. Too little inventory could starve downstream processes in a hurry. Here, laser cutting automation helps reduce the blanking footprint and, not least, allows for continuous, unattended operation.

No Welding Bottleneck

Welding is metal manufacturing’s classic constraint. Automation delivers cut blanks from laser cutting. Press brake and stamping operations are simulated, and automated welding cells process high-volume work amid a number of manual welding stations.

3-Dimensional has more than 50 welding robots from KUKA, FANUC, and Panasonic, and its own cut and etch lab onsite for weld testing and process validation. Most welding robots perform gas metal arc welding, but a fair number also perform resistance spot welding and even gas tungsten arc welding.

The lion’s share are traditional articulating-arm robots, but the company also makes use of cobots (from Universal Robots and FANUC) for some light assembly work as well as certain welding applications. One, for instance, positions work at a resistance spot welding station. Several cobots are stocked on shelves to assist at a moment’s notice.

Then there are the lasers. Not far from the company’s 2D and 3D laser cutting systems is a row of eight laser welding cells, with KUKA robots and TRUMPF solid-state disk lasers ranging in power from 6 to 12 kW. Some have rotating tables, so operators outside can load while the robot inside the light-tight enclosure welds the workpieces. Most fixtures have typical toggle clamps, but a few are pneumatically actuated, saving the operator time.

Critical to welding throughput is, of course, the ability for quick changeover, and this is where 3-Dimensional’s fixturing department plays an important role. Well before an order is released to the floor, the team builds fixtures in CAD and mocks them up in a separate room. Fixture components consist of a combination of off-the-shelf stops and clamps, laser-cut plate (oriented such that plate thickness tolerances don’t affect accuracy), and machined components.

They work around weld tables that mirror the ones on the floor and use laser scanners to make sure all the gauge blocks are in the right position. As Bischoff explained, their goal, no matter how diverse the product mix becomes, is to start with a common foundation of fixturing best practices. Many fixtures start with alpha plates with an array of locating and holddown features, on top of which are adapter plates.

"Here’s where the balancing act starts," Bischoff continued. "How big of an adapter plate can you use? It could be a standard base plate that supports all components of a smaller assembly. But for larger assemblies, that no longer makes sense. You then might use the weld table itself as a base plate, then place a range of smaller adapter plates on top."

The capabilities of laser welding take fixture development a step further. For instance, several laser cells have custom-built turntables and fixtures with adjustable supports and window cutouts in the base plates. Those windows allow space for the laser beam to pass through and access weld joints that it otherwise couldn’t.

A few laser weld cells have not only dual-operation turntables but also adjustable headstocks and tailstocks. Historically, most laser weld programs have incorporated independent positioning and weld head movement. That is, the positioner moved into position for the weld, then remained stationary as the laser welded the programmed locations.

Now, the team is just starting to develop methods of programming certain welds in which the head remains stationary and the positioner moves underneath. The idea is to expand the possibilities of joint access, a never-ending effort that goes back to the company’s founding.

Working Backward

"You have those who understand a welding process, but do they understand how the parts are manufactured and where the variation is? Do they know what to focus on and adjust to really refine that weld pool? That’s why we work backward," Bischoff explained. "We understand the fit-up of the components and the local welding environment around the melt pool, and we work backward to ensure the parts fit, the fixture is robust and properly constrains the critical geometries, and the head or weld torch has access. Taking this approach allows our team to make quick work of complicated assemblies."

3-Dimensional does this, he said, while still maintaining the original design. Most of the shop’s customers have experience designing for a specific welding process, including laser welding. Of course, the shop’s engineers would love if every mating surface was a lap joint, optimized for easy welding—but that, of course, isn’t reality. Some joints have base metals of different grades, melting points, and thicknesses.

"Some seating components we weld entail thick brackets of standard- strength material welded to high-strength, thin-gauge track," Bischoff said, adding that the depth of penetration in a lap configuration simply wouldn’t be adequate. This particular setup demanded an edge joint designed to tight tolerances and minimal gap. Yes, the tolerances are tight, but the joint configuration remains the best way to get adequate fusion across the thickness of both thin and thick base metals.

"This gives you an unbelievably strong assembly out of very light material," he said. "But you’re also looking at a very small process window and incredibly tight tolerances. Everything has to be built to support that requirement."

The team starts from this requirement and works backward. The fixture needs to not only locate but also constrain the part geometry, mitigating the material’s tendency to twist, warp, and distort—all controlled with strategic fixture-component placement, providing heat sinks and allowing movement where it doesn’t affect weld accuracy or quality. From this, the team works with standard risers (used repeatedly for different jobs), adjustable pads, block clamps, combined with custom laser-cut and machined components. The idea is to constrain the workpiece for a stable joint and yet also account for the physical aftereffects of welding, like distortion, as well as the needed surface cleanliness and proper outgassing to avoid weld porosity and induce optimal postweld cooling—all for a strong, clean, burr-free, crack-free joint.

Bischoff referred to a gas tungsten arc welding setup involving aluminum 6061 and 6063, both with an oxide layer that, if not accounted for properly, can lead to incomplete penetration. The setup demands good thermal management, accounting for the oxide layer’s higher melting temperature as well as the aluminum’s low-viscosity melt pool. "The positioning of the weld must be just right, and the fit-up has to be perfect. If you use a heatsink block below the weld to manage distortion, you need that location to be accurate. If you have just a 1-mm space in your heat-sink blocks, the weld will find it. The sensitivity is just unbelievable."

Calming a Turbulent Weld Pool

From a metallurgical standpoint, laser welding’s concentrated and controlled heat-affected zone is often what makes the process so attractive. The laser fuses metal and quickly gets out of the way, leaving the underlying microstructure strong and uniform.

That attribute, however, also makes the process less forgiving to poor fitup and material imperfections. Vapor doesn’t have time to escape and, hence, creates porosity and related internal weld inconsistencies. Also, metal vapor making its way through a turbulent weld pool forces small globules out of the melt pool, creating spatter.

In the past, the company has accommodated for this by introducing weaves or wobbles to the weld program. When keyhole welding a thick section, programmers would integrate an initial pass to, in effect, "preheat" the joint before performing the final, full-penetration weld pass. The solid-state laser was more than powerful enough to weld that thick joint in one pass, yet again, the intense and dramatic changes in localized heat didn’t create an environment for quality welding. The "wobble" and other approaches helped calm a turbulent and previously unpredictable weld pool.

To simplify and streamline these challenging setups, 3-Dimensional has started working with TRUMPF’s BrightLine technology. The "two-in-one" fiber emits a laser beam with a concentric, ring-shaped core around the primary center core. And it’s adjustable.

Specifically, it produces different processing modes optimized for the application, with power concentrated in the center, in the ring, or a mixture of both. That additional process control effectively calms the weld pool. "For us, a lot of weld strategies that used to take multiple passes now can be done in a single pass," Bischoff said. "It really saves a lot of time."

A Flexible Operation Evolves

When 3-Dimensional first dove into disk laser welding 15 years ago, the initial cells were quite large, accommodating various weld tables and part geometries. At that point, having a laser weld cell with a small work envelope just didn’t make sense, because that one cell had to accommodate a wide range of products.

As time passed and more work came in the door, its laser welding department evolved, with some cells designed for certain part geometries and setups, like a dual-operation configuration with a rotating table. Scaling up like this maintains flexibility (with different-sized weld cells) and yet also increases throughput per square foot—even while maintaining a high mix of products.

One resistance welding job is a prime example. In it, two robots with resistance welding heads weld an assembly moved on a simple track system comprised of aluminum extrusions. It’s highly customized for a product family, yet at the same time it’s not a manufacturing monument designed to process millions of workpieces.

As Burns explained, the company wouldn’t have been able to accomplish that without embracing automation early in its history. Automation investment attracts customers who’ve automated their own factories. Success leads to more work, greater variety, and more volume.

Today, 3-Dimensional processes the classic mix of a large custom fabricator—a healthy amount of high-volume orders alongside a long tail of low-quantity work. And the operation uses the gamut of automation to process it all: cobots in assembly, machine tending, and certain welding setups; 5-axis laser cutting; robots performing GMAW, GTAW, spot welding, and laser welding; and inspection technology that includes robotic part handling and vision systems.

"It’s been said that you have price, timing, and quality, but you can only pick two," Burns said. "Automation allows our customers to have all three."
