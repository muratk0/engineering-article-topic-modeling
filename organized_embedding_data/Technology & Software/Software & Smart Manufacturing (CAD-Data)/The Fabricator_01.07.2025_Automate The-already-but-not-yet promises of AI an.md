# Automate: The-already-but-not-yet promises of AI and robots

Automate W Rap-Up

Amid sea of robots, Automate show reveals flood of AI coming to everyone’s doorstep

By

Lincoln Brunner

Humanoid robots like Agility Robotics’ Digit appear ready to roam a factory floor near you, once the proper ISO safety protocols are ratified

.

F

rom above, the show floor at Automate 2025 looked like a huge high-tech terrarium: part smart factory, part anthill. The booths full of robots made for a living floor map of motion within motion: clusters of shiny sticks and claws picking, placing, reaching, and waving as an army of ants coursed organically around them.

Automate, held May 12-15 at Huntington Place in downtown Detroit, brought together more than 40,000 attendees and 800 exhibitors, according to the show’s sponsor, the Association for Advancing Automation (A3). In addition to keynote addresses and panel discussions from industry analysts, the show featured more than 200 presentations on everything from interoperable digital twins to machine learning to elevating safety standards for humanoid robots.

On the floor, the picture that emerged from exhibitors was just as wide-ranging: numerous booths featuring autonomous mobile robots and robotic arms ready for deployment as well as next-wave software to help businesses simulate entire factories before a single shovel of dirt is turned. And while much of the technology being touted centered around tools that can be purchased and used today, it was clear that automation itself is evolving capabilities faster than it can be adopted by the masses.

Humanoids in a Box

Perhaps no one at Automate demonstrated that hype-vs.-reality state of automation better than Digit, the humanoid robot created by Salem, Ore.-based Agility Robotics that was making its debut appearance in Detroit.

Unlike the sci-fi hype surrounding industrial mobile (humanoid) robots, there were no talking robots walking down the aisles, serving drinks, welding parts, or anything that resembled free-range movement.

Instead, inside a plexiglass cage at a booth dwarfed by the likes of FANUC and Universal Robots, Digit performed tasks like bending down, picking up a bin labeled "CAR PARTS," placing the bin on a rolling ramp, and walking from station to station within its cell.

Digit—the lone humanoid robot on display at the show—is now in its fourth iteration and looked ready for prime-time work. But unlike its AMR cousins rolling freely about many booths, Digit will have to stay in that cage for the time being—that is, until the International Organization for Standardization fully establishes safety protocols like ISO 25785-1, the new safety standard for humanoids being developed by a committee that includes Agility Robotics Project Leader Kevin Reese as well as representatives from A3 and Boston Dynamics (a completion date has not been determined).

Humanoids like Digit use large language models (the foundation of AI icons like ChatGPT) to decipher voice commands and take appropriate action based on their environments. They can perform complex tasks, like picking up pieces of trash and placing them in different receptacles, but they can’t yet be set free to do those tasks autonomously on a factory floor. Why? It all has to do with dynamic stability—in other words, what the robot does when it runs out of power. Does it bend down? Fall over? Drop its load? While AMRs (essentially flat, self-guided boxes that are regulated by other protocols like ANSI/RIA R15.08) simply stop rolling, humanoids present distinctive challenges because of their human-like form factors.

"Right now, there are no humanoids on the market that are safety-rated to work outside of a safety cell in any OSHA-regulated environment," said Director of Product Marketing Amel Ali at Agility’s booth. "To truly scale and show value to our customers, they (humanoids) need to be outside of a workcell. These environments are really tricky to begin with. They’re very dynamic. We need to have very specific safety standards in place to make sure that they’re working together cooperatively."

Digit might be stuck in the present, but it’s moving quickly toward the future—or perhaps more accurately, Digit is moving the future more quickly toward us.

"This is my third robotics company," Ali said. "What I’ve found is that a lot of people like to use the buzzword, ‘connecting islands of automation.’ Digit is actually connecting those islands of automation. If you have a tote on an AMR, something has to take that tote off. There’s still that final piece of moving one thing from one spot to another. You need to fully connect the islands of automation. I feel like this is where Digit shines."

Moving in the Dark (Accurately)

Agility Robotics might be placing its bets on Digit and its vision-based operation, but companies like Vancouver-based Apera AI are using AI to automate material handling based not on vision systems but on 3D CAD models, mitigating the need for parts to be lit sufficiently for cameras to see them. Apera’s system uses the digital models to locate parts and robotically move them through a cell, whether it’s lifting them from a D-rack or bending and picking them from another fixture—tasks that operators usually have to do currently.

That can allow for fully automated fabrication operations for parts like large automotive body panels, which are historically difficult to light, said Colin Beck, a business development manager for Apera. In addition, flowing 3D models directly into the software can eliminate the programming work for operators.

"We start with a 3D CAD model of the part, we run that through a neural network, and that does all the vision programming for you," Beck said. "Our vision system speaks robot, so all of our system is going to be robot programming. This takes things that could only be done by an operator previously.

"The body shop is someplace that vision has historically been a nightmare because stamped metal parts are impossible to see. So that’s been a huge thing that we’ve changed—making automation more full lifecycle, where full cells can be automated and just eliminating the need for human interaction."

Robot-Human Integration Is Here

While some companies were touting their ability to take humans out of the equation with robots, others, like Dale DiMassi, were showing off just how elegantly the two can be merged with his bionic arm—the Ability Hand from PSYONIC, a San Diego-based bionic technology company.

Invented by PSYONIC founder and CEO Aadeel Akhtar, PhD, the Ability Hand enables its wearers to move and articulate their new fingers with far more organic-like dexterity than traditional prosthetics. Akhtar, 38, received his doctorate in neuroscience from the University of Illinois in 2016. He has working relationships with several medical institutions in the San Diego area and even struck an investment deal with several tycoons on TV’s "Shark Tank" in early 2024.

Even with its brightly lit internal cables, it looks and feels like a natural arm. Such are its smooth movements and light weight that DiMassi, a creative specialist at PSYONIC, shakes hands readily and naturally with it—a welcome departure from old technology, he said.

"Without overstating it, this is definitely a gamechanger," DiMassi, 45, said. "Prior to getting this hand about a year and a half ago, I hadn’t worn a prosthesis in over 30 years. Being younger, being into sports, it kind of got in the way more than anything."

The device, which the company says is the fastest multitouch-sensitive bionic hand on the market, transmits open-close commands from DiMassi’s muscles to the device through electromyography sensors connected to his right arm right below the elbow. In addition, an app on his phone gives him a menu of grip options, which he can cycle through—a power/key grip, finger wave, pinch, and point. He also has custom grips for playing pool and even hunt-and-peck typing.

While DiMassi talked about his arm, Akhtar held up a demonstration hand that passersby could control by gesturing into the camera on Akhtar’s phone. PSYONIC didn’t have a booth at the show, but that didn’t stop Akhtar and DiMassi from attracting plenty of attention in the aisles—something they hoped to replicate with exhibitors. With several Ability Hands already being used at companies like Mercedes-Benz and Meta, PSYONIC has set its sights on partnering with every heavyweight represented at Automate.

"We’ve optimized our hands for humans to do human tasks," said Akhtar. "It makes sense that the robots use the same thing. There’s a very easy translation from everything that our human users can do to what the robots are trying to do as well. That synergy is something that we want to explore and really just take advantage of."

And while attendees in the aisles could articulate the fingers on the Ability Hand demo individually, the 200 or so people like DiMassi across the U.S. who currently use an Ability Hand have devices that use surface electrodes, so they can’t directly control individual digits—yet.

"The next generation of the hand that we’re working on will be directly integrating with the nerves," DiMassi said. "We’ll be picking up the signals to move the fingers individually."

For PSYONIC’s Dale DiMassi and other wearers of bionic prosthetics, the future of robotic-human synthesis is here today

Service Is the Solution

The fingers wiggling on PSYONIC’s bionic hand provided an apt metaphor for what is undoubtedly the biggest current need amidst all the futuristic thinking at Automate: how to make AI and automation useful for the masses right now.

One vital piece of the rapidly burgeoning AI puzzle is how to service all the automated machinery and software that manufacturers are installing—equipment that has made once-unthinkable leaps in sophistication the past 20 years.

"A lot has changed since then: The outside frames of the machines might be similar, but the core underneath has gotten a lot more intelligent," said Greg Earle, managing director at HAHN Automation Group, an integrator that focuses heavily on automated solutions for the automotive sector. "You’re keeping an eye on what needs to be upgraded to optimize for the latest technology that they can actually use."

For Fredrik Ryden, co-founder and CEO of Seattle-based Olis Robotics, providing service to businesses of all sizes starts with giving them control over and clear vision into the manufacturing processes—particularly when their equipment fails.

The company aims to accomplish that through its app, which stores visual input from on-site cameras with telemetry from the PLCs running a plant’s robots. The combination of the two, visible on any third-party Android tablet, provides users with event data they can use either to troubleshoot a problem themselves or send to a robotics expert off-site who can help them do it more accurately than they might have been able to otherwise.

"It’s recording episodes that experts can use, coupled with the PLC data that you’re troubleshooting from all different angles visually and what’s going on in the machine," Ryden said. "When you have the video and the telemetry … it just tells that whole story, basically."

Travis Langford, automotive industry sales manager for Universal Robots, a division of Teradyne, said that for the small and medium enterprises (SMEs) looking at his company’s cobots, one-size-fits-all solutions don’t even get mentioned—it’s all about customizing a retrofit for operations that are often badly in need of technology updates. In particular, UR specializes in power- and force-limited robots for the cobot market.

"The more you go out to these rural areas … you find a need for automation," said Langford, who has been with UR for nine years. "And really, collaborative robots are the best fit, due to the ease of use, the low footprint, and the ability to rapidly deploy these robots and retrofit your environment without it being very invasive or having to redesign the entire shop floor."

Finding the Holy Grail

In the world of fabrication, welding is perhaps the one technology that has pushed the boundaries of automation the furthest. And it just keeps pushing.

While simple tasks like spot welds have been done by robots for decades, Burnaby, British Columbia-based Novarc Technologies has brought AI into the forefront for all kinds of welding procedures, including TIG welding of pipe. And with NovAI, the company’s AI-powered robotic vision system, it has gotten as close as anyone to fully automating pipe welding, even in jobs with unforeseen variables.

"It’s all about adaptive welding, which would be combating those fit-up variations," Novarc CEO Soroush Karimzadeh said. "Robots simply don’t have any way to perceive and react in real time to changes in the process. Historically, they were either programmed offline or, if it’s a cobot, they’ve been taught by hand … to go from A to B. But once the weld starts, there’s fit-up variations—now you’ve got programs.

"Now, we have married the power of computer vision, AI, and the data that we’ve gathered, and we’re giving that perception and cognition that typically only humans have—because humans have learned over a long period of time what welds should look like—and you leverage that to make changes during the weld."

For Pierre de Giorgio, president and CTO of Nashville-based machine integrator Spartan Robotics, the key is a capability bundle that he wrote years ago—essentially a programming plug-in that is designed to make welding extremely easy for end users.

"It was really important to us to make it simple for the end user to use," de Giorgio said. "What we find is a lot of the people who are using these systems really don’t have a lot of robotic experience. Customers were coming to us, and we were seeing that it was really difficult for them to find skilled labor. For palletizing or pick-and-pack, you’re able to find unskilled labor, but in terms of welding, we can’t just put up a welding torch and put it in someone’s hand and say, ‘Weld.’"

In response, Spartan offers a system that requires users to set just two weld positions, a start and a stop. The software calculates everything in between, even on a job with, say, 10 stitch welds.

"It calculates, and we automatically space them," de Giorgio said. "So even if you have 50 positions that you would traditionally have to teach … we require only two, and we do all the rest of the work for you."

The innovations that AI and other advanced automation are bringing come at a time when technology is speeding up as the workforce is slowing down and retiring. In the meantime, businesses need more welding done as the economy continues to grow.

"I think we’re at a pivotal time in the industry," de Giorgio said. "The welders are aging out of the workforce. So that’s really what drove us. We truly believe that our solution solves that problem."
