Entry-level software doubles production on HVAC manufacturer’s aging punch press

Situation

During its first year of operation, HVAC system manufacturer AirSource Ventilation, Manchester, England, used a commercial CAD/CAM software that it inherited with its 25-year-old Nisshinbo MTP 1000F punch press.

“Every tool had to be placed manually on the part,” said Engineering Director Andy Pinchbeck. “We had to select which tool we wanted to use and tell the software which line we wanted it to go on, then crop the end short so that it tagged the part. That had to be done manually for every single line.

“And it was manual nesting as well,” Pinchbeck continued. “The machine was probably designed for repeat sheets to be run off hundreds of times, so it was very cumbersome for the constantly changing nests needed for our three- or four-offs.”

In addition, the software sequenced punches in such a way that it signaled a program error on about one in 10 sheets. Those errors had to be fixed or recovered, taking up to 30 min. each time. The only way around it was to sequence each sheet manually, selecting which punch to do, and in what order.

And because all parts are labeled, the punch operator was manually typing out 15,000 labels over a six-month period.

As the company grew and the number of parts it was manufacturing increased, it needed to reduce machine downtime considerably, but didn’t want to invest in a new machine.

Resolution

AirSource Ventilation decided to upgrade the software with Radan’s entry-level system, Genesis. This gave the manufacturer traditional CAM functionality and ease of implementation, resolving its production problems.

Machine setup, tooling lists, nest graphics, part identification, and machine program all are produced automatically, directly from the nest schedule. All sheet metal components, such as double-skinned panels that provide the casework for the HVAC equipment, heavy-duty steel base frames, internal blanking and structural parts, control panel back plates, enclosures, and roofs now are produced through the software. The number of sheets going through the punch press has doubled, and machine downtime because of problems is nonexistent.

The machine now runs for 13 hours a day, and the company has not had to upgrade to a new punch press. The software creates subroutines for producing complex parts. When there are too many dissimilar parts for subroutines to handle, the software splits the job into a few different programs and sends them to the machine in three or four sections.

To solve the labeling issue, Radan’s area manager organized a bespoke macro that automatically produces the labels directly from information on the nester. A second macro, for sheet slitting, now has been included in the main release.

Roller pinion system helps increase efficiency of plasma cutting machinery

Situation

Pinnacle Industrial Automation, Guelph, Ont., builds plasma cutting machinery tailored to its steel industry customers. When the company began designing and developing the Whistler HP plasma-cutting table, its engineers knew they would need reliable components to develop the multipurpose cutter, especially the linear drive system that would serve as the track for the plasma torch.

The goal was to create a reliable, fully zoned downdraft table that could cut clients’ custom-designed equipment components for mass production. Since the table would be suitable for a variety of industries and needs, the company wanted to make it modular, allowing users to adjust table sections to customize its length.

Resolution

To achieve accurate and reliable functioning, engineers incorporated linear-drive systems, AC brush-less digital servo drives, high-precision gearboxes, and dual drive technology, powered by CNCs and a precise, cost-effective plasma cutting torch.

Rather than opting for a traditional rack-and-pinion system, Pinnacle used Nexen’s roller pinion system, designed to eliminate backlash and increase accuracy for linear-motion applications.

Instead of using pinion teeth to control the rack’s movement and positioning, the system uses bearing-supported rollers that help minimize downtime and equipment wear.

With its low-friction operation, the roller pinion system does not lead to jams. The pinions engage several tooth flanks simultaneously in opposing directions and allow clearance between the other teeth. This design helps ensure continuous, smooth motion, and the absence of jams helps minimize production loss.

The low-friction option also allows unlimited run length. No heat is generated, which helps increase equipment life and decrease maintenance needs. In most cases, the system needs lubrication every 2 million pinion revolutions—about every six months.
