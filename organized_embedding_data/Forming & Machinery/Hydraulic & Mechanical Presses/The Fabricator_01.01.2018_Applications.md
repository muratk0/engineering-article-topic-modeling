Manufacturer achieves two-month ROI with nesting software for punch presses

SITUATION

HC Coils Ltd., based in Gosport, U.K., is a manufacturer of heat exchange products. The company relied on a simple computer-aided manufacturing (CAM) system purchased alongside its two punch presses, but that was causing some problems.

"The interface to the old system was very poor and still appeared to be DOSbased, even in 2010," said John Yeomanson, drawing office manager. "There was no automatic nesting, meaning that I had to employ someone all day literally to play ‘Tetris’ to lay up nests, which is a mundane and boring job for a relatively highly paid member of staff. Support was very poor, with questions often going unanswered. I recall that we paid quite a lot for maintenance but never seemed to get any updates."

The company looked into purchasing a new punching machine and saw a demo of nesting software provided by the machine vendor. Yeomanson was initially impressed with the software, but the company decided to delay the machine purchase, so he could not buy the software because it drove only that machine.

The firm began researching the market for alternative nesting systems.

RESOLUTION

HC Coils purchased Jetcam Expert Premium with free-form nesting, along with Jetcam Orders Controller (JOC) to allow for simple, remote ordering of parts. Installation and training took just two days for both punch presses, and staff learned to use the software quickly.

The company had several thousand components that needed to be imported into the software. All of the original DXF files were placed into a folder, and the Single Component Automatic Processing (SCAP) automatically processed them, creating component files and placing tooling automatically so that all of the parts were ready for nesting on either machine.

"SCAP is generally better at tooling parts than an operator might be. We even accidentally discovered a better way of punching our fan plates," Yeomanson explained.

Jetcam’s Tool Teach mode allowed the software to learn complex tool placements. "We use it on form tools, and many of our parts would be much more difficult to make without it," said Yeomanson. "Every job has at least two of the form tool plates, which are automatically tooled with Tool Teach."

Once parts had been created, the manufacturer used JOC Lite to structure assemblies and create order lists. Complex assembly structures were created by dragging and dropping components and specifying their quantity and revision number. Worksheets were created in the orders screen, and components and complete assemblies were dragged onto the worksheet and sent to the software for automatic nesting.

Previously programming at HC Coils was a full-time, manual, labor-intensive job. With the combination of automatic tooling and nesting in the software and the ability to order components quickly, the company decreased programming time by about 85 percent. A full day of production now can be programmed in less than an hour, and any staff member with 20 minutes of training can produce a nest file.

With the remnant sheet management feature, remnants can be created during nesting and then reselected for orders, reducing material waste. Using a single cut to separate parts rather than leaving a skeleton between them also decreased material waste.

Two years later, when HC Coils decided to replace both of its punch presses with a new turret punch press with sheet loader, the company purchased a replacement postprocessor, which was installed and up and running in one day. SCAP could again be used to retool all components, minimizing downtime during the transition.

"With the savings in programming time alone, the system paid for itself easily within a couple of months," said Yeomanson. "We simply would not be able to manufacture the amount of products we’re making now with the previous system."

Rail group addresses safety concerns with training from grinding tool maker

SITUATION

Canadian Pacific (CP) Railway moves millions of carloads of freight across North America each year. Its 13,000 employees keep 51,000 railway cars moving safely across the logistics and supply chain group’s 13,700-mile rail network. Doing that efficiently requires constant maintenance of CP’s rolling stock, work equipment, and rail infrastructure. It goes without saying that effective tools and safe work practices are vital parts of the equation.

When CP implemented an indefinite ban on grinders for performing cutting and thin-blade tasks after two employees were injured using power tools, staff faced a big challenge. CP shops use 70 percent of their grinders and cutting tools every day, especially for high-volume sheet metal work, grinding, and rail maintenance.

As the ban continued, the rail group turned to less efficient alternatives, sometimes replacing powerful cutting tools with less productive tools that were more challenging to use.

"We had complaints from the field," said Peter Shaw, a work equipment specialist at CP. "It was causing us all sorts of problems and delaying our work."

RESOLUTION

Walter Surface Technologies’ Bill Hunter and Daimon Mussell went to CP with a two-pronged approach: better equipment bolstered by a comprehensive safety training program.

At the rail group’s Calgary shops, Hunter demonstrated the Combo Zip™ cutting wheel on a hardened steel pin that CP had chosen as a demanding test application. "It cut the pin beautifully," Hunter recalled. "Then I flipped the grinder over into a grinding position and deburred the pin."

Ultimately, the rail group approved the use of 4.5-, 5-, and 6-in. Walter grinders, each equipped with double-action paddle switches to stop the devices when not held by users, and gear drive-integrated safety clutches to control torque and eliminate backlash. After a six-month review and recognizing a need in the field, CP also approved the Combo Zip cutting wheels and Zip ALU™ wheels for nonferrous metals.

While equipment performance was essential, Shaw said training and safety seminars helped tip the scales. "We wanted to stress both the benefits of training and safety features of the tools," he said.

"A 10,000-RPM grinding wheel turns at about 125 MPH," said Hunter. With that kind of power in its equipment, Walter focused intently on safety training at CP, delivering seminars to show proper use of grinders, cutting wheels, and wire brushes.

"Our approach with CP isn’t just about products," Hunter continued. "It’s about Walter training sessions to make CP workplaces safer. And there’s a huge variety of applications within CP."

So far the company has offered training at 80 of the rail group’s locations across the country, reaching more than 1,000 workers.
