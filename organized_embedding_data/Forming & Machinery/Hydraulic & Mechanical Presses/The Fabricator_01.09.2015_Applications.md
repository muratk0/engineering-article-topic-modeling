Custom manufacturer powers production with collaborative robots

SITUATION

Standby Screw Machine Products Co., a third-generation company in Berea, Ohio, manufactures and delivers custom parts for the outdoor power equipment and automotive industries. Because of the repetitive nature of many of the factory’s tasks, the company sought technology that would help lower costs while still maintaining the flexibility to handle new parts and production lines.

RESOLUTION

The firm decided to install two Baxter robots from Rethink Robotics in two separate applications.

In one high-volume cell, the two-armed robot helps produce more than 1 million parts per year, cleaning and packaging worm shafts—a long, cylindrical gear used for self-propelled walk-behind lawnmowers. The robot is fed parts from a traditional caged industrial robot and, using both arms, moves the parts into a slot to clean the machine oil before placing them into two boxes on the factory floor.

In some cases, the robot works overnight. The added production time and capability to package exactly 169 parts per box are helping to contribute to the company’s ability to maintain competitive pricing for its products.

A second robot tends to a milling machine, using two arms to pick up pivot rods designed for snowblowers out of a feeding unit and place them into the machine to cut a flat end on the rod.

According to Drew Rabkewych, sales manager at Standby Screw, "We’ve increased efficiency in two critical lines and now can leverage our staff in more complex roles throughout the factory."

Dust collector with vertical filters solves steel fabricator’s maintenance problem

SITUATION

Steel fabricator JPW Companies, Syracuse, N.Y., was experiencing problems with a dust collector installed on a shotblasting machine. The company, which offers AISC fabrication and advanced steel erection, uses the shotblaster to prep metal for a variety of finishing processes, including galvanizing, painting, and epoxy coating.

"The dust collector filters were stacked horizontally, two across and three down," said Shop Foreman Tim Toolan. "When the collector was in shake-off mode, the dust would get stuck on the filter below and the filter below that one. The collector couldn’t shake them clean, so the filters were constantly dirty and air purification wasn’t good."

The company doesn’t run the shotblast machine constantly, but on the occasions when the machine ran for 40 hours a week, operators had to change filters every six to 12 weeks. The collector also was blowing dust and discharging outside to prevailing winds, damaging the siding of the building.

RESOLUTION

Air filtration distributor R.P. Fedder Corp. contacted JPW management to offer a trailer demonstration of the Farr Gold Series

®

dust collector manufactured by Camfil APC. JPW decided to replace the existing collector with the Gold Series unit designed to meet its specific application requirements, which included space constraints dictated by the collector’s indoor location.

The final design uses six vertically mounted cartridge filters. The filters provide high-efficiency dust capture but shed dust readily during cleaning. The vertical design and open-pleat configuration of the filter media allow efficient pulse cleaning, eliminating uneven dust loading and helping to ensure reliable performance and increased service life, the company states. After 16 months of use, the original filters were still in place and working well.

The collector features an abrasion-resistant inlet, silencer, secondary HEPA filter to discharge air back into the shop, side-mount blower for height restrictions, remote start at the shotblasting machine to limit operator travel time when starting the collector, and offline cleaning interlock so the dust collector cleans while the blast machine is not in use.

Welding process saves production time on wind turbine generator brake discs

SITUATION

Valmont SM of Rødekro, Denmark, part of the U.S.-based Valmont Industries, supplies constructional steelwork for wind towers and manufactures the rotary housing with integrated brake discs for the wind turbine generators. About half of the company’s 650 employees perform welding tasks, so joining technology is highly important.

About 50 welders are involved in the construction of the rotor housing, with six of them working exclusively on joining the brake discs. The workers cut four ring segments out of a large steel plate to minimize waste. The quarter-arcs are mounted on a clamping table so they can then be connected to form one circular piece.

RESOLUTION

About two years ago, Valmont installed EWM’s forceArc

®

welding technology, initially ordering four Taurus 451 Synergic S welding machines, followed by another two.

The power sources are used to join the four pieces to make one brake disc, which involves welding S355 structural steel in thicknesses of 50 and 60 mm. To do this, Valmont uses a flux-cored wire electrode with the classification T466MM1H5 and a M21 shielding gas mixture.

Previously GMAW with flux-cored wire allowed only an included angle of 60 degrees, but the company’s welders were able to reduce this angle to 34 degrees using the new welding processes.

"We were able to shorten the weld preparation and the entire production time for the welding of a brake disc by up to 30 percent, which means a considerable saving in a high-wage country like Denmark," said Frede Holdt Jensen, welding engineer and project manager. "In addition to power consumption, material consumption was reduced by up to 50 percent, as fewer passes are now required because of the smaller included angle."

The energy-reduced, heat-minimized joining process also results in less distortion. Previously welders had to turn the workpieces three times, but they now need to do it only twice, which results in reduced downtime.

The new technology has helped reduce working time by so much that welding activities now account for only a small part of the whole manufacturing process.
