W

hen discussing technological advances in fabrication, the focus often turns to software, robotics, or integrated automation. Yet in mature, well-proven technologies, some of the most impactful improvements are mechanical. These upgrades may appear deceptively simple, but they reflect a high level of engineering sophistication, optimizing performance through precision mechanics rather than digital complexity.

Years ago, David Logan was working with an oxyacetylene cutting torch on a job and carried it, along with his other tools, around the workspace in a 5-gal. pail. One day, gas started to leak from the torch into the pail. Unaware of the leak, he grabbed an angle grinder and started to grind. The sparks ignited the gas in the pail, causing what was luckily only a small explosion. But it was enough to prompt him to think there must be a better, safer way to work with cutting torches. That’s when he began to develop the Safe-T Torch system.

The Safe-T Torch lets operators extinguish the torch flame and stop the flow of gases to the torch within seconds by releasing the trigger on the handle. If the torch operator is unaware of a leak or becomes incapacitated for any reason, this safeguard prevents gases from continuously flowing into the workspace.

REFINED DELIVERY

Since Logan conceived his original prototype, he joined with Keith Davies, Armen Jeknavorian, and George Shahnazarian to create TorchRite Solutions, which has continued to refine the design and add necessary tools to make it conform to CSA and UL approvals by a third party.

"We have been working on the testing and development since 2021," said Davies, head of sales for the company. "We’re now finally ready to go to market."

In 2025, the design won the city of Maple Ridge, British Columbia’s $10,000 Innovation Challenge Award in the business-to-business category. And the company’s first sale of the unit was to the municipality.

"The oxyacetylene torch has been overlooked for many years," Davies said. "I say it all the time: Everybody’s still using their grandpa’s torch. A standard torch has two valves for your gases to turn on and shut off, but you have to do that manually, and you need two hands to do so. We put a pistol grip on the torch. We have an 18-V battery-powered control box that mounts close to the bottles that shuts the gases off at the source."

An emergency stop button on the control box enables anyone to quickly shut off the gas flow if they notice any incident or unsafe condition that may not be visible to the torch operator.

"It takes between three to seven seconds after releasing the trigger for the flame to extinguish," said Jeknavorian, TorchRite’s president. "So it’s very quick."

"It’s also versatile in the sense that if something happens and you have to drop the torch, the trigger turns the torch off and everything goes to safe," said Shahnazarian, controller for the company.

TESTING AND BATTERY MANAGEMENT

Before launching TorchRite, Jeknavorian and Shahnazarian co-founded MGA Connectors, a company focused on manufacturing engineered building hardware. They understand the testing and approval necessary to bring a product like the Safe-T Torch to the market successfully.

"It took us some time to refine the design of the product," Shahnazarian said. "There were no testing procedures for it, so we had to hire third-party engineering firms to do the risk analysis and design those procedures. We weren’t satisfied with what was presented to us, so we engaged with Saskatchewan Polytechnic to go through the process and look at it in detail. They came back with some key changes."

The company is already on the fifth generation of the product and did some field testing with large potential customers to get their feedback on the system.

"Now we have something that is not only robustly engineered but can also withstand temperature extremes and rough environments," Shahnazarian said.

The company also designed fail-safes to protect the battery from fully discharging.

"When the battery gets below 16 V, it will shut the system off," said Jeknavorian. "If the temperature of the battery rises above 55 degrees C [131 degrees F], it will turn itself off, and when it gets down to about 50 degrees C [122 degrees F], it will turn back on again. It protects itself."

NEXT STEPS

Although this is the company’s first product, Shahnazarian insists it won’t be its last.

"We have worked with Saskatchewan Polytechnic, looking at other possible products that can similarly help industry," he said. "But of course, we want to make sure this model is well accepted in the market first before introducing additional models."
