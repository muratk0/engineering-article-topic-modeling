# AIDA 630-tonne DSF MCX progressive servo press

[TARİH: 01.04.2018 ISMR]

- Double-sided frame, direct drive through main shaft and servo motor, linked to two eccentrics allowing variable stroke. 
- Ability to replace blank, pierce and form single stage operations with one combined stage process, reducing tooling costs.
- Capable of producing deeper drawn parts at higher speeds.
- Prevents rippling, cracking and reduces flow stress.
- Extended tool life, especially with high strength and hard metals by significantly reducing the reverse tonnage element.
- Self-clean coil feed allows production of ferrous and non-ferrous components.
- Productivity improvements eliminate secondary and machine operations.
- Improves tool life by reducing shock and vibration.

Weller Wheels Ltd. is also designing a classic steel wheel for a major automotive OEM (where tooling is no longer available) on the servo press. Weller is designing the tooling for this project and the 4mm steel centre of the wheel will be ready for the servo press by August 2018.

"Our first real involvement in the rail sector was a UK customer who had a part pressing problem for the seats in rail carriages. It had a super formed aluminium seat-back component, an integral part of the crash testing in the seat. It was previously super formed, with a long cycle time, and was very expensive to produce. After our meeting with the customer, we proposed making the panel conventionally (after simulations) at a fraction of the super forming cost and we now manufacture these parts in serial production," explained Chris Oscroft Jr.

"Demand is increasing for pressing aluminium at the moment, especially in the automotive sector, so having that formability expertise in triple tempered aluminium helps us with other customers. We used the AIDA 630-tonne servo capability to draw the aluminium seat panel."

## The business climate

Chris Oscroft is optimistic about the business climate in the UK, believing that more vehicles will be built in the UK with more local content.

"There is still a lot of traction in the UK market and low-cost country factory moves are more expensive now. The UK has become more competitive again, with the drop in the value of the pound, particularly in the manufacture of more niche and complex products – this is where we want to take the business and I see a lot more opportunity and a requirement for more capacity going forward. I think Brexit will enable more opportunities for innovative SMEs in the UK although it may get worse to get better….Some companies are, of course, holding back investment to see what happens after Brexit but I would say that now is actually the time to invest and work together on building up our capacity and expertise and trying to fill the skills gap to prepare for the future," he told ISMR.

"We did, some time ago, consider whether to set up in a low-cost country but we took the view that there is still a significant requirement in the UK for niche, more complex, short

lead time work. That is where we feel the UK is at its best, when time is short on a job, if changes are needed or when work is too complex – our engineering prowess really comes into play and there is still a niche for UK engineering skills."

## An eye on the future

"We are looking to further develop our tool room, increase capacity and increase our prototyping capability within the business. If we win the right work, we will buy more servo presses in the future. I believe that servo is the future for stamping technology. This was our first servo press and we are very happy with it," Chris Oscroft told ISMR.

"We want to become more integrated with our customers and branch out even more into other sectors and show what we can offer to people with products (adding value, saving money and saving weight). We also want to continue developing aluminium and copper products. It is important to strategically grow the margin within the business so that we can invest in the latest technology and more automation."

A.E. Oscroft also wishes to grow the Weller Wheels product and brand, perhaps even into its own building/entity, with similar plans for its tool room.

"With an eye on the future, we are watching the development of additive manufacturing with interest – it's a long way from competing with stamping work but I can see a possible role for it in tooling, particularly when the tooling has to be heated or cooled. Costs need to be in line so that it is viable from a tooling or one-off prototyping perspective," concluded Chris Oscroft.
