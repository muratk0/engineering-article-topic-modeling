A deep dive into EVS Metal’s blanking strategy

J

oe Amico remembers the relatively "little" thin turret machines he operated during EVS Metal’s early days in Riverdale, N.J., back in the 1990s. The contract fabricator has always served the electronics components market; blanks with card guides, stiffeners, and other forms created on the turret punch were always in the product mix. Even today, the technology remains central to the fabricator’s reinvestment strategy.

And as Amico, EVS’ vice president, explained, the reasons go beyond the turret punch’s forming and holemaking capability (it’s hard to beat the simplicity and efficiency of a cluster punch). "In our turret punches, we’re tapping all the time, and we use form tools heavily with all the secondary processes we do. But even beyond the forming, with the newer technology, the electric-servo-drive turrets are extremely fast, and the automated tool change on the turrets makes them flexible too. Considering all that, it’s really hard to replace the turret punch."

Punching fits EVS’ overall blanking strategy, which considers material yield and efficiency and—most critically—puts it all in context of what the value-adding processes downstream need.

ANALYZING THE PRODUCT MIX

EVS Metal touts a highly diversified product mix, with production work running alongside a long tail of low-quantity orders. Thing is, even the production work can vary significantly, much of it being engineered and configured to order.

Consider EVS’ point-of-purchase kiosk business. The kiosks themselves involve a variety of sheet metal parts that flow downstream to powder coating and assembly. The shop manages the entire process, including purchased components. At the same time, each product is essentially built to order. Quantities can be high, but so can variety. Taken together, though, those kiosks make up only 14% of total revenue.

Overall revenue in 2025 reached a record $97 million, and it didn’t grow this large on the backs of a few big accounts. The company’s largest customer usually comprises less than 11% of total revenue. Amico said this does vary somewhat—especially with the recent uptick in data center and warehousing work—but maintaining that revenue diversity has been key to the fabricator’s resilience.

Beyond blanking and assembly, EVS’ core selling point has been its value-adding services, including assembly. That fact has helped the sales team land orders in an increasing variety of industries. More than 70% of customers make up no more than 4% of overall revenue.

All this helps put EVS’ blanking capability in context. The blanking machines nearly always produce a high mix of parts. Rarely will you see a nest full of identical blanks, either from the punch or from one of the fabricator’s numerous fiber lasers.

Joe Amico, vice president at EVS Metal, operated turret punches during the fabricator’s early days. Today, the punch press remains central to the company’s capex strategy.

Instead, you’ll usually see dynamic nests (generated automatically) on the fiber lasers and manually generated static nests on the punches. The static nests usually have numerous parts on them tied to multiple jobs, yet all are placed with intent, balancing the need for high material yield with specific part orientations for stable, efficient operation. Part placement minimizes turret indexing and makes good use of common-line punching. Parts are nested close to the clamps. And when possible, EVS keeps all the parts for specific assemblies together. The faster a kit of parts (or, usually, a collection of kits) can be blanked, the faster those parts can move downstream to assembly.

On every EVS Metal plant floor—in New Jersey, New Hampshire, Pennsylvania, and Texas—each turret punch press has a tower with load/unload automation. As Amico explained, AMADA turret punch presses remain the company’s blanking automation workhorses. Nests are carefully crafted so that when a machine runs overnight, pieces are denested easily (thanks in part to the triangular microtabs modern punches create) and sent directly downstream, usually to forming. Meanwhile, the lasers usually handle the "long tail" of high-product-mix work.

Yes, some lasers do handle higher-quantity work, especially blanks with contoured edges not suitable for punching. But again, a significant amount of EVS’ work involves products—enclosures, racking, and hole-intensive panels for airflow ventilation—that fit the turret punch press sweet spot.

HOW CAPEX INVESTMENT STRATEGY EVOLVES

EVS’ range of work could be divided into two groups, a

production product mix

and a

custom product mix

, and the company makes its blanking investments to meet the needs of each. Most of the production work goes to the punches, while a good portion of the lower-quantity work goes to the lasers.

Plenty of outliers exist. That’s just the nature of custom and contract fabrication. Some production work goes to the lasers, and some low-quantity work is squeezed in on the turrets. The key is focusing on the sweet spot—the peak of the normal distribution of work.

That focus is why EVS has yet to invest in a punch/laser combo system. On rare occasions, a skeleton of laser-cut blanks is placed on a turret punch to create the necessary forms, but as Amico explained, the inconvenience doesn’t occur frequently enough to warrant a combo machine. Even so, the fabricator hasn’t ruled out a punch/laser combo for the future if the product mix changes and space allows.

"Believe me, we have internal debates about this, which is healthy," Amico said. "But at least for now, the combo hasn’t been necessary, just because the parts that really require it remain a small part of our business."

Product mix, shop floor space, and evolving technology remain three primary factors governing EVS’ capex strategy in general, and those factors certainly apply to punching. Automated parts denesting from punching is a prime example. For years, Amico and his team have debated the benefits of the process. At first glance, operators accustomed to the traditional shake-and-break method might see denesting automation and think of the additional cycle time: The machine punches the part profile and as soon as the part is released, grippers move in to grasp and lift it out of the skeleton.

The cycle time is longer, but the punching cycle isn’t the whole story. Blanked parts aren’t truly "complete" until the next major process (in this case, usually forming) can do something with them. That fact, Amico has argued, remains the primary benefit of automated part removal. The punch fully parts-out the blank as the grippers lift and carry it away. Stacked parts have no nubs from microtabs that need to be removed later.

Still, the argument for automated part removal isn’t straightforward, especially considering how dialed-in EVS’ manual denesting operation is. Instead of shaking and breaking, workers lift pieces held with triangular microtabs placed near the corners. "Those triangular, or half-diamond-shaped, tabs really make a difference," Amico said. "They break easily. That really matters, especially thinking back to the way we tabbed 20 years ago."

The remaining nubs on the blank edges often don’t need to be removed. Programmers ensure they don’t interfere with backgauge fingers on the press brake and, when possible, keep them hidden when they’re part of formed and welded assemblies.

And when the nubs are removed, systems are in place to make the manual, tedious work more efficient. Workers stack blanks and knock the microtab nubs out of dozens of sheets simultaneously.

Amico pointed to a stack of recently denested blanks near the punching automation—pieces that would be formed by an AMADA bending robot. How a blank stacks is key here. It’s why you won’t see many short flanges formed on the company’s punch presses.

Sure, the punch machines are fully capable, especially considering the die’s below-the-brush-table position. But short flanges cause stacking complications. Stack one blank on top of another, and the top blank "shifts" back one material thickness. That’s not ideal for robotic bending. The company’s robotic brakes do have the capability to stack blanks vertically in a rack that separates each blank slightly, giving clearance for flanges, extruded holes, and other punched features that don’t stack neatly.

Even so, Amico said that the shop always tries to "engineer out" the stacking problem when possible—like forming an internal window flange on the press brake instead of the punch press. After all, no matter what a particular job’s routing is, blanks that don’t stack well after punching add material handling complications.

ABOUT FLEXIBILITY

The robotic bending cell came equipped with an automatic tool changer (ATC), as did an adjacent manual AMADA brake where operators were bending large enclosures with knockouts formed on the punch press. Press brakes with ATCs have been critical for maintaining EVS’ flexibility, as have the ATCs at the company’s most advanced turret punches, the AMADA ZRTe models with 100-plus tools in the magazine.

Mechanized arms grasp punch tools in the magazine, and all tools have QR codes that track the number of hits between sharpening. From there, tools enter a buffer turret that presents the tools to another arm that loads the tools into the turret.

That magazine, Amico said, broadens the number of product families that the company dedicates to the automated punching system. "We can set up multiple jobs, and then just walk away and know you’ll see the jobs complete when you return, especially with this newer ZRTe system with its autoloading, tool utilization tracking, and the fact that the tools ride below the brush table. Unexpected tool breakage just isn’t really a problem anymore."

He also appreciates the effectiveness of the slug-pulling vacuums underneath, a subtle technology that’s easily overlooked, especially considering all the particulate involved when metal breaks away as the punch and die collide. If slugs weren’t so reliably evacuated, the punch machines’ uptimes wouldn’t be as stellar as they are.

Walking downstream, Amico pointed to the ATCs on the manual and automated press brakes, which broaden the part flow possibilities through forming. Batchstyle part flow still dominates production through robotic bending, but the manual ATC brake could process well over a dozen jobs during a shift.

Walking back to the turret punch, Amico said that the need for toolchange automation is the first question he and his team ask before purchasing another turret punch. "When we invest in a punch, we always go with [material handling] automation. That’s a given. The question then becomes whether we need tool-loading automation [with the separate tool storage cabinet]." The answer depends, as always, on the product mix.

DENESTING STRATEGY: MANUAL VERSUS AUTOMATED

Scrutinizing all the steps between blanking and bending—denesting, microtab removal, sorting, and stacking—continues to guide the company’s blanking investment strategy. This is why, Amico said, the company will likely invest in punch presses with automated denesting (including parts picking and stacking) within the next few years, especially as the operation replaces some of its older punching machines.

Parts-stacking automation in punching does require some adjustments. Unlike laser cutting, punched sheets continue to move after the grippers remove individual parts. Part removal sequences need to prevent warping and birdnesting as the clamps move a relatively flimsy skeleton near the end of the cycle, when nearly all the parts have been removed. Still, automated denesting today is a relatively mature technology, and preventing these issues can be somewhat straightforward—specifically, by dialing in the nest layout and part removal sequence.

As Amico explained, having both automated part stacking and traditional load/unload automation could give EVS the best of both worlds. Automated denesting eliminates tedious manual labor, which has become more critical now than ever. These days, it’s become more difficult to "throw more labor" at a problem, because the labor simply isn’t there. At the same time, traditional load/unload provides the flexible throughput the shop needs.

Amico clarified, of course, that specific investment strategies continue to evolve with the customer and product mix and, hence, they’re always subject to change. Regardless, EVS has fully embraced automation for years, and that fact isn’t likely to change anytime soon.

MANAGE BY THE CONSTRAINT

Amico pointed again to those parts slated for the robotic bend cell, complete with threaded holes. Workers with rags methodically wipe the oil off to prep them for bending. Sure, automated technology is available to reduce surface oil’s effect—especially in robotic bending, with its special blower, clamping, and gripping technology—but giving those blanks a quick wipe helps simplify downstream processing beyond bending.

And considering the needs of downstream processing is key. Most of EVS’ products have complex routings that go far beyond blanking and bending, and downstream is also where the constraint processes usually are. The last thing the fabricator wants is to starve those constraints. Preventing that requires a rock-solid blanking operation, and EVS Metal’s collection of automated turret punches remains a critical piece of the puzzle.
