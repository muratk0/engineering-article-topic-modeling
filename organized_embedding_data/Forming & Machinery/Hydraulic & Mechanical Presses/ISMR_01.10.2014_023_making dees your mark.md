# MAKING DEES YOUR MARK

[TARİH: 01.10.2014 ISMR]

Rolern Lee, Vice General Manager at DEES in China, explains how the hydraulic press manufacturer has increased orders, improved quality and reduced costs

ISMR SAYS:

“DEES supplies almost all of the main Chinese automotive OEMs, as well as Tier 1 and Tier 2 suppliers, with press technology”

DEES Hydraulic is a well known hydraulic press manufacturer with factories in Taiwan and China. Its main products include deep drawing and die spotting presses as well as related hydraulic press technology.

Rolern Lee, Vice General Manager at DEES in China, outlined to ISMR how the company has increased its manufacturing flexibility, constructed global distribution networks, improved quality standards and reduced manufacturing costs as well as turn-around times.

ISMR: Please give a brief background to your company and its products. Please explain your ethos, mission and objectives?

RL: DEES Hydraulics was formed in 1976 in Taiwan, so we are almost 40 years old. We have been in China since 2000 with complete manufacturing facilities which we built from scratch.

Our mission from day one has always been to develop people. We have people working for us who have been with us for almost 40 years. We are a family-owned business – our president has emphasised the importance of developing people and creating the correct mindset that would translate into excellent quality control and great products. Our sole focus and specialty is the hydraulics press business.

In 1976, the first machine we built was a hydraulic press brake. We then produced shears (although press brakes were still our main business). In 1998/9, we started supplying more to the automotive industry.

We shipped hydraulic presses and press brakes all over the world. Now, we have a full product line that serves the entire automotive industry from tandem lines, die spotting and tryout presses to hemming presses. Our specialty now is hydraulic presses – we no longer make press brakes.

Our mother company in Taiwan has bought new land and is planning to build a new factory in 2015. This will triple the manufacturing space available. At the beginning of 2014, we moved to our new factory in China, which is almost double the size of our previous factory.

ISMR: Please outline any recent successes and achievements?

RL: Sales of our hydraulic presses have been reaching new heights over the past couple of years. We have a factory in China and one in Taiwan. Taiwan is our corporate office. I am involved in all operations in China, where we have been for 14 years. We deliver a lot of presses to automotive manufacturers in China and business is good here.

In terms of achievements, we recently delivered a 2400-tonne double action hydraulic press to Volvo in China. We also recently delivered a very specialised die spotting press to Land Rover/Jaguar’s new factory in China.

We have actually sold two presses to Volvo – one is at its first plant near Szechuan (a die spotting press) and the 2400-ton double action hydraulic press is for Volvo’s Daijing factory.

Volvo is using this press to perform initial forming operations for its cars as well as for die tryout. It will probably manufacture about 20,000 XC60 models, change the design in a couple of years and then manufacture them here in China. It bought the double-action press because tooling from Europe is double-action. We should complete installation of this press next month.

Our mission from day one has always been to develop people

Jaguar is using our press as a die spotting machine and to check tooling. The press uses a servo motor that controls the input/output of the oil through the cylinder. It is a very precise machine for die spotting with slide repeatability of 0.005.

We are currently utilising servo motors to increase the production capacity of our hydraulic presses and adding accumulators to increase press speed (on our tandem lines, for example). Every year, we submit patents and have received more than 12 patents in the last 14 years in China. We have devoted a lot of time and effort to becoming a strong hydraulic press manufacturer and a reliable partner for our customers.

ISMR: Financially, has this year been good for DEES? Are you on track for a better year this year than last year?

RL: Yes, definitely – we are looking at growth of 40 per cent this year. This comes mainly from the new projects we have in China, especially electric car projects. The Chinese government has put in place policies, incentives and tax breaks to encourage electric car manufacturing, so a lot of people are building electric cars. It will probably be public transportation at first, followed by more consumer products. We are selling a lot of presses to these manufacturers. Ford, VW and General Motors in China are moving their factories around and setting up new factories so there is a large migration of stampers moving along with these electric car projects.

A lot of our business in Taiwan comes from the electronics industry and this business has changed over the past few years. For example, many of our customers are now buying equipment to supply Apple, for example. In the past, Apple would supply all the equipment to them.

Next year will also be good for us – in fact, I project strong growth for us up to 2016, at the moment. Generally, from our Chinese factory, we ship 50-60 large capacity presses per year.

ISMR: What are your views on the current business climate for the industries you serve? What are your views on the business climate in 2015?

RL: I think that the electronics market may fall off a little next year, but the automotive market will remain strong. There are a few new car models coming out and a lot of new companies are building stamping lines and increasing their production. Our main focus will continue to be the automotive market although we also supply presses to other industries, such as home appliances.

RL: 70 per cent of our business (for the Taiwanese and Chinese factories) is based in China. The remaining 30 per cent is scattered around the world (mainly Asia, US, South America and Europe). Of our share (70 per cent) of customers in China, roughly 40 per cent is made up of international companies who are based in China, 30 per cent are Taiwanese companies in China and the rest are domestic Chinese manufacturers.

We supply our press technology to all of the main Chinese automotive manufacturer OEMs as well as Tier 1 and Tier 2 suppliers (with tandem lines, for example). We have agents around the world selling our products.

The most important issue for our customers is always service. The automotive industry is so demanding that downtimes must be kept to a minimum. Preventative service and assistance is always needed. You need to be based in China to properly support and service customers in China, which is one of the reasons that we built our factory in China. In China, we have over 160 employees (120 in the factory – over 15 electrical engineers and 40 hydraulics engineers). These are the people that can service presses – they can usually be with a customer within 24/48 hours, depending upon transportation issues. If the customer is more local, we can be there within four-eight hours.

China is a specialised market because the hydraulic press manufacturers there were all state-owned. The mindset is therefore slightly different from us – they were supported by the state, so the business was always going to be there… For us, as a family-owned business, service and quality are always going to be key to compete effectively against them. We want to be close to our customers in China to fully support them and provide the service or training that they need on-site. There is often a high turnover of manufacturing staff in China, so there is always training that needs to be done.

ISMR: What is your strategic and technical focus/vision over the next few years?

RL: One of our main targets is to build faster hydraulic presses. We are spending a lot of research investment into developing a faster hydraulic press. Most hydraulic presses run four parts per minute – we want to double and triple this speed. This is crucial, from our perspective. Right now, we are at about six-eight parts per minute (depending upon the depth of the deep draw). We will probably launch a new product line late next year to reflect these new developments.

We are also going to grow the press technology that we initially built for Land Rover. This was a very special press that uses a servo motor as the main energy source for the piston pump. We want to grow that part of the business. Right now, most people are using AC motors but, to be energy-efficient, you have to move towards servo motors. We are therefore developing an entire hydraulic press product line based on employing servo-motors to save energy, increase the flow rate of the hydraulic pump and make the press more accurate. Servo motors are getting better and more reliable – overheating problems have also been addressed.

In China, we generally focus on tandem lines. We have allocated an R&D budget to build a 2000-ton hydraulic press for the automotive industry with a massive bed size of 2.6 x 5m. This is not an order from a customer – these are R&D budgetary funds in preparation for our new product launch. Up to now, we have built small capacity presses (up to 200 tons) using servo motors with hydraulic pumps for the Land Rover product. We are working on a different project for a three-way press where we use two sets of servo motors to drive the left and right cylinders.

Our new product line will be based on using servo motors but our main focus is to use the servo motor on a 2000-ton hydraulic press which can be used as a stamping line. That is our key priority right now. We will start building this press next year so I expect that, by the end of next year, this should be completed. It will be a continuous testing process.

Normally, we spend less than 2 per cent of our annual turnover in R&D but this new R&D project and machine-build is going to take more than 10 per cent of our annual turnover. We will learn a lot through continuous testing and we prefer to refine the press in our factory first before selling it to customers. Not many press manufacturers actually build a machine in this way – they usually only research a new technology based on the orders that they receive.

ISMR: Which trends do you see developing in sheet metal markets?

RL: The sheet metal market is changing. Mechanical presses are moving towards servo-design. Hydraulic presses need to be more energy-efficient for customers. The new trend is therefore towards integrating more servo-motors into these presses. Greater power and speed, more control, more energy efficiency and higher productivity for presses are real requirements in the market.

The sheet metal market in China will be good for a while – most people are increasing their production levels – but it will also level off quickly. I believe that there will be some bubbles. The automotive market will always be high – there will always be demand for cars in China. However, eventually it will peak and a lot of the stampers are financing most of their purchases with loans that may become a problem in the future. There are always new competitors on the horizon that can stamp more quickly or more cheaply etc. Some traditional market players may be forced out of the market, as a result.

A lot of high-tensile materials are being pre-heated for forming. One of the criteria for that is to have a fast hydraulic press, so we are building fast presses to accommodate this market. We have sold three hot forming presses in China, ranging from 800 – 1200 tons. Some people will do this but these presses are mainly for luxury automotive models, rather than Chinese-branded cars, at the moment. A lot of overseas automotive manufacturers, such as Audi, make use of this technology.

ISMR: Where do you see the greatest challenges and opportunities?

RL: The greatest challenge for us is the Chinese market. This is our focal point. Without faster service, there is no opportunity. Our strategy is therefore to maintain this rapid service concept and continue to support our customers.

I believe that our customers will end up asking us to fix other machine brands for them. When the new equipment rush dies off, there will be a lot of customers needing support on machines that they have bought elsewhere. So this is another opportunity for us in China – to become a service partner to our customers on other branded presses that they have bought…

ISMR: In which geographic (ie country) markets do you see projected growth for your products?

RL: In China, certainly. However, I also see great growth for us in markets such as South America (Brazil and Argentina) and Indonesia (which will be one of the main markets for us in the next five years). Europe is a well-developed market. Every year, we attend a variety of international exhibitions to promote our brand and unveil opportunities all over the world.

There are a lot of Chinese car manufacturers moving around the world to set up new factories. They see that the growth here in China is levelling off and they need to move elsewhere.

Global shipbuilding, marine and maritime markets have sailed rough waters through the worst period of the global economic downturn, but the future is looking brighter as much-needed reforms, restructuring and consolidation has taken place. Market forecasts for the sectors are more optimistic and rising global GDP is playing an important part.

Shipbuilding activity is characterised by long development phases and long manufacturing cycles (for example, 12-24 months for merchant vessels and 24-36 months for passenger vessels). This means an alteration of periods where factories operate at full capacity with others where they appear to have spare capacities. The exchange of subcontracts among shipyards also facilitates the management of manufacturing personnel in terms of levelling temporary over- or undercapacity.

## The shipping forecast

Rising global GDP is one of the key growth drivers for the shipbuilding industry. Major challenges associated with shipbuilding are environmental regulations and rising fuel costs.

The shipbuilding industry in general is currently undergoing various industrial transformations such as virtualisation, robotics in shipyards and green shipbuilding. It is chiefly dominated by players such as Hyundai Heavy Industries, Daewoo Shipbuilding, Mitsubishi Heavy Industries and Samsung Heavy Industries.

“In 2013, new global shipbuilding orders rose dramatically from last year’s levels. The start of the global economic crisis saw the downfall of many shipping companies and shipyards as well as a dramatic decrease in the number and cost of new building orders. However, 2013 marked a significant change with a strong revival in ship orders, which tripled compared to 2012, and increased demand in shipyards’ order books,” commented analyst, ResearchMoz.

“The shipbuilding industry is mostly dominated by Asian players, such as South Korea, China and Japan. In 2013, China led the market with the largest order book, while South Korea led in terms of contracting. The European shipbuilding industry recorded remarkable growth in 2013, with new orders and increased order books. Emerging nations, such as India, Vietnam, Brazil, the Philippines, and Turkey, also recorded significant growth in shipbuilding industry.”

In April 2014, Moody’s Investors Service revised its outlook for the global shipping industry to stable from negative, for the first time since June 2011.

“The revision reflects our expectation that the global industry’s aggregate EBITDA will rise by mid-single digits in percentage terms year-over-year in 2014, in line with our -5 per cent to 10 per cent growth range for a stable outlook,” said Mariko Semetko, a Moody’s Assistant Vice President and analyst.

“And, while overcapacity remains a concern, we believe industry conditions are at a trough and that the supply-demand gap will not worsen materially. In this environment, we expect the supply of vessels will exceed demand by no more than 2 per cent, or that demand will exceed supply by up to 2 per cent.”

Cost reductions – including the effects of lower bunker prices, as well as the application of slower steaming speeds and efficiency savings – have driven the growth in EBITDA. At the same time, market conditions remain tepid but are not deteriorating, with freight rates for the dry-bulk segment showing some improvement but those for the container segment remaining under pressure. The sector is also saving on costs through postponing and cancelling deliveries of new vessels, scrapping the oldest and most inefficient vessels, and idling vessels.

In recognition of the need to reinforce targeted R&D and innovation efforts, the industry has been working together to develop a Public Private Partnership with the European Institutions’ ‘Vessels for the Future’. A comprehensive roadmap has been put together to conduct further research into solutions for safer, more efficient and cleaner ships and focusing on the opportunities that Horizon 2020 can offer to the sector.

SEA Europe, with the European Commission, also launched the LeaderSHIP 2020 strategy which forms the basis for the maritime technology industry’s policies in the coming years, emphasising the importance of the sector for Europe. Four key pillars were identified which are largely reflected in the ‘Athens Declaration’. These are employment and skills; improving market access and fair market conditions; access to finance and Research Development and Innovation.

Companies in the global naval shipbuilding industry operate shipyards or boatyards. These are generally fixed facilities with dry docks and fabrication equipment capable of building a naval ship. They manufacture frigates, destroyers, cruisers, amphibious transport ships, aircraft carriers, patrol ships and submarines. They also provide naval ship repair and conversion activities. Boat manufacturers build boats, cabin cruisers, dinghies, jet boats, patrol boats and rowboats for navies and coast guards.

## Marine and maritime markets

The average annual world demand for marine supplies was in the range of 149 billion euros. The Asian frontrunners (China and Korea) are by far the biggest national markets, followed by Japan, USA, Singapore, Norway, Italy, India, Germany and Brazil. Shipbuilding, oil and gas and offshore wind markets have buoyed demand for marine supply products.

In a recently published Balance study commissioned by DG Enterprise, Europe (including Norway and Turkey) is highlighted as a world leader in the manufacturing of maritime technology with a 43 per cent market share and total production value of over 61.8 billion euros. This segment of the maritime industry accounts for up to 500,000 (direct and indirect) jobs in more than 29,000 enterprises.

The report also demonstrates that the European maritime technology industry continues to undergo an evolution, with the manufacturing emphasis today being placed on highly specialised and integrated technologies and a focus on global value chains. Up to 60 per cent of the manufactured products are exported to third countries.

The sector will continue to see growth as there are a number of drivers such as environmental developments, European emphasis on exploiting blue growth developments and the need to ensure energy security (offshore, renewables etc.). Local content requirements in certain geographical regions, regulatory uncertainty (delays to implementation of rules) and overcapacity in the global shipbuilding market can threaten the position of European industry.

Last year, the Society of Maritime Industries undertook the fifth iteration of the annual trends survey of the UK marine science and technology industry at the Oceanology International exhibition. Seen as a key indicator of how the industry has performed over the previous year, the survey also provides a view of where the industry expects to develop its business in the future.

The survey showed that 56 per cent had grown their businesses over the previous 12 months and 64 per cent expected further growth this year. Exports remained strong with 81 per cent reporting they were in overseas markets versus 77 per cent in 2012. Companies were also taking on staff and there was a 4.8 per cent increase in the number of employees per company interviewed.

The oil and gas sector still dominates the market and 39 per cent of companies supplying this sector, although renewable energy had shown a large increase from 12 per cent in 2012 to 18 per cent last year. Although some weakening has been reported in the European market (down to 28 per cent from 35 per cent) it still remains a large market in terms of size and growth.

## Marine coatings

Marine coatings are applied in military and commercial ships in offshore oil rigs, yachts, and in boats. An interesting market research report this year expects that the marine coatings market will witness a double-digit growth rate in the next six years owing to higher demand for reduced fuel consumption of ships and the eco-friendly nature of coatings. Marine yachts and vessels require specialised marine coatings to prevent fouling above and below the waterline areas.

The marine coatings market can be segmented into deep sea, coastal, leisure craft and containers. Some factors that are responsible for the success of the marine coatings industry include oil prices, defence spending and the level of sea-borne trade and ship-building. Oil prices have a major impact on the demand for marine coatings used on offshore tankers and other facilities. The marine coatings market is primarily driven by expansion of the oil and gas market, implementation of IMO ballast tank coating rules and the extensive use of coatings for the long-term protection of marine facilities.

The marine coatings market is dominated by commercial shipbuilding followed by leisure boats and containers. The Asia-Pacific region is the largest consumer of marine coatings. The demand for coatings is increasing in this region owing to new ship builds and dry-docking. Strict environmental regulations and customer preference for eco-friendly products is boosting innovation. Scientists and industrialists are developing metal-free, anti-fouling coatings which will not affect marine life. Some of the challenges faced by the industry are the consolidation of shipping companies and the projected decline in shipbuilding production.

## Recreational boating

In May, the National Marine Manufacturers Association in the US announced that the recreational boating industry in the US saw healthy growth in 2013, generating US$ 36.7 billion in retail expenditures (new and preowned boats and engines, trailers, accessories and services), a 3.2 per cent increase from 2012. An estimated 166,800 new powerboats and sailboats were sold at retail in 2013, an increase of 2.2 per cent, coming on the heels of the industry’s 2012 rebound when new powerboat and sailboat retail sales increased 10.7 per cent – the industry’s first sign of recovery. The NMMA is anticipating continued momentum with an additional 5-7 per cent increase in retail sales of new boats in 2014.

Ski and wakeboard boats, as well as fiberglass and aluminium outboard boats, led the US industry’s growth in 2013. Ski and wakeboard boats continued to grow in popularity, with an 11 per cent increase in the number of new boats sold at retail in 2013 to 6,100 units; outboard boats (pontoons, fishing boats and small family cruisers) were the most popular type of new powerboat sold in 2013, comprising approximately 84 per cent of the powerboat market and up 5 per cent to 134,800 units.

The personal watercraft (PWC) category increased 2 per cent compared to 2012 with 39,400 new craft sold at retail; and inboard cruisers leapt in retail sales 10 per cent compared to the previous year with 2,200 new units sold in 2013. 2013 was also the first time since pre-recession (2009) that the ratio of pre-owned boat to new boat sales decreased, indicating a shift in consumer demand for new boats.

“In 2013, we saw more manufacturers producing innovative boats that offer an entry into the boating lifestyle at a variety of price points. A trend in boat manufacturing is the versatile boat – one that can pull tubers or wake boarders, be used for fishing outings, relaxing with the family or entertaining,” noted the NMMA. “We’re seeing manufacturers shift their manufacturing processes, becoming leaner and more efficient, to be able to offer these innovative boats at a lower cost, in turn introducing more people to boating. ■
