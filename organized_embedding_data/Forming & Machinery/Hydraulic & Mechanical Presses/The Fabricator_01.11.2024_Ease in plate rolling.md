# Ease in plate rolling

Dan Davis

A

dvance Products & Systems (APS), a manufacturer of corrosion prevention and protection products that help maintain flow in the water and wastewater, oil and gas, and chemical pipeline industries, had a flow issue of its own prior to this year. The shop floor had a bottleneck in the plate rolling area.

The older four-roll machine was used to make wall sleeves that, in turn, were often matched up to other APS products like mechanical seals, the combination of which are used to seal pipes quickly and effectively. The machine could get the job done, but it was time-consuming because everything had to be done manually. Each roller had a pin that needed to be adjusted to put the roller in the right place, and then that information had to be punched into the controller.

The machine also was at least 15 years old, and the APS manufacturing team had worked it hard over the years. It truly became a challenge to repeat the results from one job to the next one that followed.

That inability to keep a consistent tolerance was a concern because APS required a tolerance of 1/16 in. on these wall sleeves so that they could work properly with the other high-tolerance fabrications the company produced. Once installed, these products need to work as advertised, or the corrosion or leaks threaten pipeline performance, a very expensive problem to fix and one that most would rather avoid.

"That machine was definitely on its last legs," said Kelby Richard, an APS senior engineering technician.

Meanwhile, the APS automation team, which had been formed to find more efficient ways of fabricating parts and to build custom machinery where no equipment was commercially available, had its eye on a plate rolling machine that touted "autonomous bending" in its promotional materials. Upon further investigation, the team liked what it found.

In February 2024, European Technology Center, the U.S. representative of Haeusler plate rolling machines, installed a four-roll EVO machine with automated measuring of the rolled radius and auto-correction of the machine settings to achieve the desired form.

New Tech, New Results

Richard described the transition to the new technology as a big one because the gap in technology was large. The older plate roll had six buttons. The EVO not only had a touchscreen interface on the control, but it had cameras and a measurement arm to check on the workpiece radius during forming.

"It’s definitely been a big transition, but not an impossible one," he said.

The APS team decided to have engineers create the programs offline and feed them to the machine for the operators to run. But before any plate rolling job begins, the workpieces are loaded up on a prep table for both ends to be beveled, to assist with welding, and then to have marks made laterally on the side of the plate, which is typically from 0.25 to 0.375 in. thick.

"The software programming will actually give you the dimensions to mark the sheet. That’s for the camera to check and make sure it’s in the right position," Richard said.

After the prep work is complete, the plate is placed on roller tables and moved into the plate roller. Once the plate is aligned in the machine and the program is started, the operator can walk away from the machine and return to find a finished form, according to Robert Walther, president of European Technology Center.

How does this occur? As the machine begins to roll the part, a camera identifies the points that have been marked. This is done to ensure that the traveled arc length of the radius or the plate in the machine matches the programmed arc length of the radius.

After a part of a radius or a complete radius is completed, a measuring arm springs into place to check the rolled radius and provide the value to the machine control. Once complete, the measuring arm returns to its rested position to allow the forming to continue.

At this point, the control software is taking into account how much the actual radius differs from the programmed radius and makes adjustments based on the hardness of the plate. Walther called this process "learning," adjusting pressure to deal with the higher or lower yield strength characteristics against the default value known to the control software.

As this is all going on, the four-roll machine is operating as expected, always in contact with the material and moving the rollers, particularly the side rollers, in a way that creates the cylinder shape. The control also relies on the machine’s side rolls to accommodate the pressure changes necessary to address the real mechanical characteristics of the plate.

This closed-loop process—checking the radius, measuring the profile, sending the information to the controller, and adjusting the rollers to get the form closer to the specified measurements—continues with each rolling pass. The machine stops after the program has delivered the programmed shape.

A camera captures the lateral marks on the plate. These images provide some of the real-time data that allows the control to adjust the forming pressures during plate rolling.

A measuring arm conducts an actual check on the radius to ensure that the material is performing as expected. If not, the control software can make in-process changes to get the plate to the desired cylindrical form.

Advance Products & Systems reports that it doesn’t need to worry about postprocessing of rolls because of its new equipment.

Richard admitted to some hiccups early on related to getting the EVO plate rolling machine up and running. However, once everyone got used to the machine—how to present material and learning more about the BENDtronic software—things began to operate smoothly, producing a cylinder in about 40 minutes.

"Once you get the piece in there, you take your initial measurements and positions and press go. You make sure there are no errors in programming, and you let the machine go," Richard said. APS also is able to get the consistency from one part to the other, something that was a struggle before, he added.

The cylinders also meet the tight tolerances required for the pipeline jobs. Walter recounted telling the APS team that such high tolerances aren’t normally associated with rolled plate and finding their reaction amusing.

"We were actually not aware that this type of precision is unheard of in plate forming," said Kidd Culotta, APS’ operations manager. "We hold ourselves to such a tight tolerance because most of our products need to have that to work in the pipelines."

The EVO plate rolling machine is flexible enough to not only deliver on highly accurate rolled forms, but also those that have much lower quality tolerances. It has a "faster" mode in which fewer rolls and quality checks are made, and the result is a cylinder that is only acceptable if the tolerance range is big enough. The option is a reflection that not all metal fabricators require the precision rolling results of companies like APS.

Big Change, Big Opportunities

Since the plate roller has been in operation, APS hasn’t had to worry about bottlenecks anymore. Cylinders are being produced at a rate that keeps product flow on the shop floor smooth and uneventful.

This success on the shop floor also has been beneficial for sales. Lead times for APS products have dropped, which is critical for many of the company’s customers.

"Most of our products are custom made. Being that’s the case, we sort of work out of this corner of the market," Richard said. "The only way for us to really lose business is for the lead time to be too long. That’s the biggest thing."

What was once four to six weeks for certain products is now two to three weeks. The plate roller literally helped to improve production efficiency that much.

The plate roller also provided the APS team, which makes up about 130 folks both on-site and in the field, an idea of what automation can mean to a manufacturing organization. Going from equipment that required so much manual intervention to something that requires an operator to just get the material into the machine has been quite the learning experience.

Richard described it as a new experience for the shop floor. Plenty of them had worked with the company’s automation team in developing machines to help with processes, but that was a gradual exercise, where the shop floor got to contribute and see the whole process play out. With the arrival of the new machine, the company’s plate rolling past was wiped away in a matter of days. Tomorrow’s plate rolling automation was now available today.

That lesson was important because metal fabricators in the U.S. have to be smarter. Customers become more demanding, and there is always the threat they’ll find something cheaper from another source, whether it be down the street or over the border.

"We keep moving toward being fully automated. That’s the only way to reduce lead times," Richard said. "We can’t change anything about shipping processes or materials, but we can work faster and more efficient."
