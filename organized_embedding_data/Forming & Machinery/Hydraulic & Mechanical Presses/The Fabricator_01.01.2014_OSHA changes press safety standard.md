The goal is to reduce the fabricator’s time spent on paperwork

By Stephen Barlas, Contributing Writer

The Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA) has changed a couple of requirements under its mechanical power presses standard. (OSHA defines a power press as any equipment that “shears, punches, forms, or assembles metal or other materials by means of tools or dies attached to slides or rams.”)

The agency issued a direct final rule Nov. 20, which means the changes go into effect on Feb. 18, 2014, unless some party raised a major complaint by the Dec. 20 deadline. OSHA uses direct final rulemaking because it expects the rulemaking to be noncontroversial, provide protection to employees that is at least equivalent to the protection afforded to them by the previous standard, and impose no significant new compliance costs on employers.

There are two parts to the standard. Paragraph (e)(1)(i) currently requires employers to inspect all parts, auxiliary equipment, and safeguards of mechanical power presses on a periodic and regular basis and to maintain certification records of these inspections. The main revision OSHA is making to this paragraph is to

explicitly

require that employers perform necessary maintenance or repair, or both, on all of the parts, auxiliary equipment, and safeguards of each press, not just the clutch/brake mechanism, antirepeat feature, and single-stroke mechanism delineated in existing paragraph (e)(1)(ii). The latter mechanical features are subject to weekly inspections according to regulations and will continue to be, even with the revision to that paragraph. In short, OSHA is emphasizing that the inspections must be done on “a consistent, systematic schedule.”

In addition, OSHA is revising this paragraph to remove the requirement that employers prepare certification records for detailing weekly inspections and tests. However, the agency is retaining the requirement that employers maintain some sort of certification records for maintenance work.

The overriding stimulus for the change, however, is minimizing the burden on employers. That was the major objective of President Obama’s executive order issued Jan. 21, 2011. OSHA estimates that these revisions will result in a paperwork burden reduction of 613,600 hours for the metalworking industry when the changes go into effect for a full year.

Emerging Congressional Support for Manufacturing Network

Some evidence of congressional interest in President Obama’s national manufacturing networking initiative is emerging finally. The president requested $1 billion in each of his last three budgets, including the fiscal 2014 budget, which began last Oct. 1, for this manufacturing network.

Congress had turned a deaf ear up until now. No one, neither Democrat nor Republican, had introduced an authorization bill, which would have set up the administrative parameters for a Network for Manufacturing Innovation program. Now identical House and Senate bills have been introduced to do just that, and each has Democratic and Republican co-sponsors.

The bills anticipate a string of institutes around the country providing facilities and funding for development of new manufacturing processes, copying the America Makes facility (formerly called the National Additive Manufacturing Innovation Institute) in Youngstown, Ohio. That organization is now working on the advancement and commercialization of 3-D printing technology. The Obama administration seeded that effort with $30 million, and the participants kicked in $40 million.

The Department of Defense plans to fund two new such institutes, one of which will deal exclusively with metals, and the Department of Energy has one on its agenda too. But Obama wants at least 15 institutes.

To get an idea of the project scope, consider the Senate bill, sponsored by Sens. Sherrod Brown, D-Ohio, and Roy Blunt, R-Mo. It calls for $600 million to be deposited into a new Network for Manufacturing Innovation Fund administered by the Treasury Department. First, the Brown/Blunt authorization bill must pass Congress. Second, Congress must appropriate the $600 million.

Neither step is a slam-dunk, especially the latter. Yianni Varonis, Brown’s press secretary, said the senator is trying to come up with $600 million in savings elsewhere in the budget so that money can be redirected to the Innovation Fund.
