# AN EYE ON DEBURRING

[TARİH: 01.05.2013 ISMR]

A NEW ORDER received by Fladder Danmark A/S challenged the creativity of its development department. A manufacturer of heat exchangers in the USA placed an order for a machine for deburring large and heavy work pieces up to a width of 3.2m and a thickness of 5cm. The work pieces are produced with several thousand holes where the edges need deburring and rounding.

“Tests were made for the customer and considered satisfactory so we accepted the task knowing that some modifications and developments would need to be made to accommodate the customer,” Fladder told ISMR.

“We have developed and delivered machines before for finishing work pieces up to a height of 3.5m where the work pieces were fixed in vertical position. However, the new machine has to finish work pieces horizontally – with full weight on the conveyor table. This problem was solved by solid strengthening of the conveyor system so the work pieces are fed by a conveyor belt supported by heavy double-T irons and driven by no less than four synchronous drive motors.”

For effective deburring across the full working width, the machine had to be equipped with two synchronously-working gear heads, rather than one. The gear heads are equipped with a new type of spindle to enable the mounting of 12 long abrasive brushes. For normal sheet deburring, a diameter of 300 – 350mm is sufficient but, for this customer, Fladder developed even larger abrasive brushes with diameters of 450mm. The machine was produced in two halves ready for assembly.

“It was a mammoth and interesting task that we took on, meeting the challenge to develop a strong solution for our customer. We see it as preparation for future machines that customers will request. We have, for example, already produced machines with working widths of up to 2m which is the maximum width of sheets at the moment,” Fladder concluded.

The machine was delivered and installed in the US and has been running in production for almost one year. Fladder’s subsidiary in Atlanta is monitoring the machine and its performance.
