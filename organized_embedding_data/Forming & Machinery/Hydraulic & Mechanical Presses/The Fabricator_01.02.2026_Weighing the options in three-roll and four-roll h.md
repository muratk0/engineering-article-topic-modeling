# Weighing the options in three-roll and four-roll heavy plate rolling

[TARİH: 01.02.2026 The Fabricator]

Heavy Plate Rolling

Needs for speed, precision, flexibility drive the decision

By

Lincoln Brunner

Rolling a 5-in.-thick plate of steel into a perfect cylinder on a threeroll requires the finesse of an experienced operator—a luxury not every shop has.

Lincoln Brunner

W

atching a plate rolling machine forcibly massage a massive carbon steel plate into a cylinder is a mesmerizing sight— so much so that the equally massive number of nuanced adjustments necessary to do it well might get lost.

The Fabricator recently attended a demonstration of the latest plate rolling technology at Davi Group’s headquarters in Cesena, Italy, where the company displayed the capabilities of its three- and four-roll plate rolling machines. The largest manufacturer of hydraulic and all-electric plate rollers in the world, Davi offers a range of rolling machines for relatively thin material all the way up to the thickest plate. Its machines predominantly process thick plate used in pipelines and pressure vessels common to the power generation, petroleum, chemical, and defense sectors.

No matter the end use market, however, fabricators looking to serve those markets still must ask themselves which configuration—three or four rolls—makes the most sense for their operations.

That’s so much easier said than done, but there are some general guidelines.

Three-roll machines afford a high degree of variability in their geometry with the in-and-out motion of the two side rolls. But an advanced degree of expertise is required to manage that variability, especially when it comes to the length of the material’s flat spot (usually 1.5 to 2.5 times the roll thickness).

Four-roll machines offer much greater control than their three-roll counterparts by providing constant pressure between the top and bottom and two side rolls, which operate with a fixed geometry relative to one another. In addition, because of that fixed geometry, an operator knows precisely how much flat spot to expect in a given workpiece.

One easy way to begin the decision process, of course, is simply to consider your primary customers and buy accordingly.

HIGH-PRODUCTION MODE

A common example is the production of wind towers, an application that often calls for the repeatability of a four-roll plate rolling machine because of the high-production requirements. To that end, four-roll machines often offer automated programming that three-roll machines usually do not.

"Most of the people that build wind towers, as far as they need high production, they go with a four-roll," said Matteo Rossi, Davi’s business development manager for the southeast U.S. "Nowadays, the four-roll machines have the fourth roll right beneath the top roll, and the fourth keeps the plate pinched at any given time during the rolling process. It allows automation so you can have a part made almost automatically—or at least you’ll have repeatability."

Repeatability isn’t usually associated with three-roll machines because at no point is the workpiece ever pinched like a four-roll machine. As a result, it is prone to slippage with a less-experienced operator at the helm.

A three-roll unit basically operates like a giant press brake, with the side (bottom) rolls acting like a V-die: The larger the V-die, the heavier the plate you can fabricate. However, that flexibility demands knowledge of how to set the rolls precisely for the dimensions of the material being rolled, especially for conical pieces.

The minimum bend radius achievable by a given machine is a function of the top roll diameter, the thickness of the material being processed, and the length of the piece being rolled—the chief reason that shops running three-roll machines often purchase units with interchangeable top rolls.

The expertise to handle all those variables is in less supply all the time as the keepers of that knowledge retire with no one to take their place.

"It’s a lot easier to use a four-roll than a threeroll," Rossi said. "I can probably use a four-roll, but I highly doubt that I would even have the guts to start rolling on a three- roll variable-geometry machine. With the variable geometry, you really need to know what you’re doing. The plate is not fully pinched—it’s more like an operator feeling. So, you need to have a little bit more experience for using a three-roll."

DEALING WITH VARIABLE DEMANDS

Many companies, Davi included, offer fourroll units that fabricate plate that’s as thin as 9 mm—much more within the realm of small shops than the gigantic three-roll units bending, say, 6-in. plate with 7,000 tons of hydraulic force. That range of material thicknesses—a common reason that customers opt for machines with interchangeable top rolls—lends itself well to the unpredictable demands of a typical job shop’s customer base, according to Rossi.

For example, a common machine bought by U.S. fab shops is a 10-ft.-wide unit rated for ½- or 5/8-in. material. Once the machine is installed, customers inevitably ask for parts that are ¼ or 3/16-in., in which case, shops often will shim the rolls to compensate for the gap between what the machine is built for and what’s being put through it.

Whatever customer needs a company is trying to meet with its four-roll machine, just having that configuration is helping many companies deal with the lack of skilled operators with anything close to the experience necessary to run a three-roll unit.

"A general fab shop in the United States, they lean more towards a four-roll than a three-roll because it’s easier to use for an operator."

— Matteo Rossi, Davi

"A general fab shop in the United States, they lean more towards a four-roll than a three-roll because it’s easier to use for an operator," Rossi said. "And also, with the fact that you have the plate pinched between the top and the bottom roll at any given time during the rolling process, it’s safer."

SOFTWARE EASES THE PAIN

In addition to introducing two new four-roll machines at last fall’s FABTECH show, Davi released a software upgrade for its three-roll units.

A demonstration of one of the company’s powerful three-roll machines in Italy told the tale of the typical operator’s plight.

Even though experts employed by the manufacturer were running the demo, it still took them several minutes to fabricate a plate of 130-mm-thick steel more than 19.5 ft. long into a cylinder on one of the company’s 4,000-ton three-roll machines.

The new software, while not making the machine automatic like many of its four-roll counterparts, at least makes the rolling process a bit less scary for the uninitiated.

"The software will aid you, the operator, and tell you, ‘Hey, if you want to achieve that diameter, you’ve got to start roughly from this position on the bottom roll left side, that position on the bottom roll right side, and a wide position for the top roll,’" Rossi said. "It takes a little bit of guesswork out of it, even though a three-roll is still a fairly manual machine. There will still be operator intervention, more than a four-roll, but at least it gives you some guidance."

With all the nuances involved, companies searching for just the right roller must, as a final measure, ask themselves what kinds of business they might pursue in the future. Right now, defense contractors—especially for navy vessels—have become a hot market for fabricators, as are power generation sector customers (including new small nuclear power stations) serving the massive additional energy demands of AI and the server farms that enable it.

In addition, with the current uptick in petroleum and petrochemical activity, it’s a wide-open market for anyone willing and able to supply it.

"There’s still a ton of old equipment in fab shops throughout the United States," Rossi said. "There’s still a lot of old equipment that needs to be replaced. I think that people are looking a little bit more into bringing in fab equipment right now than outsourcing [work] because when you outsource, you don’t have control of the process."
