Mobile heavy-equipment manufacturer eliminates manual cart pushing with custom AGV

Creform’s sales engineers came up with an approach that would use the existing carts with no modification, but eliminate the need to move them manually over the long distance.

SITUATION

A mobile heavy-equipment manufacturer was manually moving heavy spare parts and components from its shipping and receiving area to a parts storage area using custom-built aluminum carts with push-pull handles. The carts also were used as picking carts for fulfilling orders.

Although the carts were easy to move and manipulate, a full-time associate on each shift had to shuttle them the 1,200-ft. distance between departments. The company was looking for a way to eliminate the need to tie up the associate for each shift, but the amount the firm had invested in carts already made other approaches seem prohibitive.

RESOLUTION

The company presented the problem to Creform Corp., which had been serving this manufacturer for several years, building various material handling structures. Creform’s sales engineers came up with an approach that would use the existing carts with no modification, but eliminate the need to move them manually over the long distance.

Creform designed and built an automated guided vehicle (AGV) that follows a magnetic tape guidepath. The AGV has a heavy-duty steel frame and a custom hitch, allowing it to pick up and deposit the existing carts automatically. The AGV arrives with its towing arm rotated out to one side. After engaging a cart’s handle and verifying its hookup, it proceeds down the guidepath while slowly rotating the arm back to tow the cart behind the AGV. Once the AGV arrives at its destination, a powered latch releases the handle of the cart, leaving it behind, and the AGV proceeds to the next assignment.

The AGV’s upper portion is built using high-visibility orange pipe and metal joints for simplified maintenance and long-term flexibility, the company states. Although primarily used as a tugger, it features an upper load deck for miscellaneous boxes or supplies. The steel frame end caps can be removed so the AGV can be raised by a forklift when maintenance is required. A foot pedal raises the unit’s spring-loaded AGV drive wheels off the floor, allowing it to be pushed by its handle just like a cart.

The AGV has a single-direction drive unit bolted under its frame with an 82-FPM speed, 1,500-lb. capacity, and 24-in. minimum turning radius. It also features a complete safety system, including audible warning, flashing light, noncontact obstacle laser scanner, and e-stops. Its 24-V system is powered by two 12-V lead acid batteries. The batteries are carried in battery boxes on a tray and supported by low-friction rollers on the AGV, battery transfer cart, and charging station. No battery lifting is required.

Automated cutting system helps pressure vessel manufacturer work around skilled-worker shortage

SITUATION

Energy Weldfab occupies a 30-acre compound and a nearly 130,000-sq.- ft. facility in White Oak, Texas, where 200 people work two shifts a day to fabricate enormous pressure vessels for the oil and gas industry. Workers bend and weld metal, cut holes and edges, fit pipes together, and finish and paint vessels for customers all over the world.

For nearly a quarter-century this family-run company has supplied the oil and gas industry with processing equipment. The metal can vary in thickness from less than 0.25 to 8 in. The products usually have numerous connection points, requiring a lot of cutting and welding. Until recently the company did all of the cutting by hand using an oxyfuel torch.

"It’s been our industry standard since the beginning. It’s what we’ve always done," said Vice President Stoney Lake.

The problem with doing things the old way was that the company could no longer keep up. Booming business meant the old way wasn’t fast enough, but hiring more workers wasn’t an option.

"There is a real shortage of skilled labor," Lake said. "It’s not like it used to be when people who had pipefitting experience were knocking on our door. Now it’s a struggle to find guys who can manually lay out a vessel and cut the holes on orientation."

The company knew it had to make a major change if it wanted to protect its business.

"We’ve condensed five steps into one."

—Stoney Lake, Energy Weldfab

RESOLUTION

Energy Weldfab worked with Mueller Opladen, Leverkusen-Opladen, Germany, to purchase a 3-D pipe profile cutting machine to do the work traditionally done by hand. The model selected was part of the company’s Heavy Duty line, a CNC system that can cut pipes weighing up to 30 tons.

"We had seen other cutting machines out there, but nothing like what the Mueller Opladen could do," Lake said. "The capacity and size of the cylinders this system can handle are much bigger than anything else."

Though Mueller Opladen makes systems that can profile pipes up to 160 in. dia., Energy Weldfab selected a smaller system, one with the ability to handle 100-in. pipes. The completely automated system can profile round pipes and dished ends (domes). Software manages jobs from start to finish, smoothly coordinating the exchange of information among the machine and its six axes, the cutting torch, and CAD/CAM application software.

Energy Weldfab’s system is equipped with two cutting torches. The system uses an oxyfuel torch to cut pipes with walls of up to 6 in. and a Hypertherm HyPerformance HPR400XD, a 400-amp high-definition plasma system, to cut mainly stainless steel parts or parts with thinner walls.

According to the company, tolerances are twice as good, and productivity is through the roof. A hole-cutting job that used to require two people and six hours now takes 18 minutes. "In the past we would have needed two employees to lay out lines and mark holes to be burned," Lake said. "After that they’d call in quality control to double-check the dimensions and orientation."

After the quality control person had signed off, the two employees would cut the holes and clean up the edges. When that was done, they’d have to call a quality control staff member back in for a final check. Today the pipe profile cutting machine does it all.

"We’ve condensed five steps into one," Lake said.

Another result is decreased part variance. Cuts and holes are consistent from job to job and operator to operator. An unexpected benefit for Energy Weldfab was the bevel quality.

"Holes come out as smooth as glass," Lake said. "The cut quality is very surprising, and the bevel cuts are spot-on." The cuts are so precise that no cleanup is required, saving the company yet another step and more time.
