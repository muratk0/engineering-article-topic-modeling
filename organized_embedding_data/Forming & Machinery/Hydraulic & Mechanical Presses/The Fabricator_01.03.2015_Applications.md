Hydraulic drive on plate lifter improves plate feeding from stack

SITUATION

SSAB, a global provider of high-strength steel plate, was using an electromechanical plate lifter to lift 40-ft.-long plates in a packaging operation at its Axis, Ala., plant.

The drive was designed to move the unit’s lifting legs in and out to allow lifting of single plates. However, the company discovered that efficiency could be improved if the lifter could straighten and lift a multiplate stack.

The hydraulic package includes four hydraulic cylinders that produce sufficient squeezing force to straighten the shuffled plate stack.

RESOLUTION

The company hired Bushman Equipment to retrofit a hydraulic drive system to the plate lifter. Bushman’s main design challenge was to determine the working forces that the existing lifter structure could safely withstand.

The hydraulic package includes four hydraulic cylinders that produce sufficient squeezing force to straighten the shuffled plate stack.

Sam McAllister, the SSAB contact on the project, said, "Bushman did an impressive amount of detailed preplanning to accomplish the on-site conversion in just five working days."

Metalworking company finds increased asset utilization by focusing on scrap management

SITUATION

A metalworking company that produces heavy-gauge, stamped metal fasteners, progressive-die metal stampings, and high value-added assemblies for customers in the automotive, heavy truck, and military industries, from OEMs to tiered suppliers, was awarded a sizable contract from a new customer. The contract would extend several years and require investments in new presses and dies.

The company was prepared to make the changes necessary to provide its new customer with high-quality product and on-time delivery; however, the expansion that would be required to achieve those goals would be challenging.

The first step toward adding the required additional capacity was to install three new presses in an existing facility that was landlocked. This meant the expansion would have to be contained within the existing footprint of the building. In addition to the presses, the project also had to account for the scrap handling requirements.

The scope of work required determining the best location for the presses where both finished parts and scrap could be handled quickly and effectively. This meant the company had to not only select the proper type and size of each press, but also an automated scrap handling system.

The goal of this project was to install the press and remove the scrap while working with existing infrastructure and maintaining the tight project schedule and budget.

RESOLUTION

The company turned to Prab for assistance. The first project objective was placement of a new 1650 AIDA press. The first of three presses to get installed, this press was the key to satisfying the new customer’s contract requirements. The goal of this project was to install the press and remove the scrap while working with existing infrastructure and maintaining the tight project schedule and budget.

Prab engineers worked with the metalworking company’s project team to complete a comprehensive RFQ form structured to help identify required components for scrap removal, the full nature of the scrap to be handled, key performance indicators, potential conflicts, and critical path project elements.

In addition to the physical constraints, the project also had a tight completion deadline because the new contract required parts on a JIT production basis. Further constraining the progress of the project was a series of completed parts design changes, which also changed the thickness and configuration of the scrap coming off the press. This required changes to the scrap system with no movement in the project completion date.

The companies decided to daisy-chain the new equipment off of an existing conveyor system, allowing that system to carry all of the scrap into the transfer system and to the load-out area. Next, engineers fit an underground conveyor into an existing 25-ft.-deep in-floor pit and elevated the scrap above the machine but not above the floor level so material could be discharged into the existing oscillating conveyor.

The size of the scrap coming from the press would be 10 by 10 by 0.25 in. thick. Since the existing space in the pit was not large enough to accommodate a typical 6-in.-pitch steel belt to convey parts that size, Prab custom-engineered a 2.5-in.-pitch steel belt conveyor with a standard 0.125-in.-thick belt with a reinforced plate.

According to the metalworking company’s lead project engineer, "Prab worked with us all the way through the project with all of the changes, budget adjustments, and timeline requirements. Most importantly, Prab stuck with us on the last weekend before start-up. It was a fire drill but we came through it on a very positive note."
