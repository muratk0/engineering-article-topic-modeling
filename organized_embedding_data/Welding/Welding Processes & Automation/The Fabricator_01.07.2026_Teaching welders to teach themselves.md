Charli Wright smiled and laughed quietly as she said this. For the past six years, she’s taught welding at Texas State Technical College’s Williamson County campus, though she came to the job with decades of experience, entering the industry back in the early 1980s.

"You were like a unicorn walking [onto a plant floor], because they had never seen a woman weld before."

For Wright, pursuing a welding career was about earning a living, not breaking barriers—and it all began with a spark, both real and metaphorical.

"I watched my grandad weld in his workshop behind his house, but I never thought I’d be doing it myself. I was going to go to a four-year college, but I lost interest, and soon after my friend asked me to take welding classes at the local community college with her."

Today, she continually tells her students to "watch the puddle," and for good reason. Welding is a craft one learns by doing, observing, and adjusting, all to get closer to perfection. Still, plenty of welders learn by doing, then leave the industry. And the U.S. needs more welders.

What can a weld instructor do to ensure welders stick with their craft? Three elements in Wright’s approach to teaching could help. First, teach the fundamentals completely. Second, recognize students’ strengths and interests, and convey the realities of a welding career. Third, observe and guide, but don’t provide every answer.

FUNDAMENTALS FIRST

That third step sounds counterintuitive, especially for a teacher. But as Wright explained, providing every answer as new welders perfect their skills can do more harm than good. Welding codes provide a common baseline, and TSTC students are taught to create welds that meet or exceed code requirements. But at some point, welding becomes an individual craft. The destination is the same—a weld made to code (if applicable) that passes a job’s inspection requirements—but the roadmap to quality isn’t set in stone. An approach that works for one welder might not work for another.

All this said, none of this works without solid fundamentals. As Wright explained, "You need the basics, to know how to set the proper current and polarity. You need to select the correct amperage as well as your correct approach and travel angles. If you, say, do a 2G weld, the approach angle is going to be a little different than if you were welding flat or overhead." In these cases, Wright certainly will correct a student if she sees the travel angle grow too large or a work angle inappropriate for a joint type and welding position.

Wright considers the fundamentals as being not just about welding technique but also postweld finishing and joint prep, including various gaps at the root and different land thicknesses.

"We teach what an optimal gap looks like, as well as the size of land you use. In fact, the prep might not be exactly how they like it. But they need to learn how to deal with it."

Weld preps might be dictated by code, welding procedures, or an inconsistent bevel surface or land width. "I have them take out a tape measure to measure those lands," she said. "If you think it’s 3/32 in. but it’s really just 1/16 in., you’re going to run into problems. They learn how to adjust amperage and utilize backing to attain a beautiful bead. It takes a lot of practice.

"The ultimate goal is for our students to succeed in the real world," she continued. "No matter what prep comes from their fitter, they can say, ‘OK, I know how I can make this work.’"

SPECIALTIES AND PERSONALITIES IN WELDING

Wright can tell when someone’s drawn to welding. After all, the same happened to her when she was young.

"Our counselors bring in many groups of students from middle school," Wright said. "You have a few come in who are really interested, especially the girls when they see me give a tour. They never thought about welding, and then they see me and think, ‘Wow, this is something I could do.’ The tours light a fire within them. And some of those kids, when they choose to come here, they’re on the ball and ready to rock ‘n’ roll. It’s great to see."

As Wright watches her kids practice the fundamentals, she notices their strengths and potential interests. Some might have the attention to detail and tenacity needed to become a pipe welder. Some really embrace advanced tech-niques with the gas tungsten arc welding torch. Sill others might move toward robotics. "We have an automation class here. Some really aren’t interested, but others see that robot for the first time and go, ‘Whoa, this is so cool.’"

Besides automation classes, the college offers instruction in nondestructive examination; students receive in-depth training on visual inspection, magnetic particle inspection, and dye penetrant testing. They also learn about ultrasonics and radiography, although the lab doesn’t have that equipment.

Beyond the fundamentals, these classes help give students "tools for their career," as Wright put it.

She’s also a realist, however. "I want them to be able to make a decent living. That’s the goal. But I also tell them how the job market is. You’re probably not going to find a job, spend 30 years there, and then retire. You’re going to need to hustle."

Life as a welder can be very different depending on the chosen path, and each can be a good or bad fit depending on a welder’s strengths, interest, and personality. Certified pipe welders who work on pipelines, in the oil fields, or travel to do plant shutdown work (repairs while processing plants are down) "are going to make some decent money," she said. "But it can be rough. You move from job to job. It can be hard."

Some thrive with this kind of career, but it isn’t for everyone. So some students opt for jobs at local fabricators and manufacturers, while others choose to become certified welding inspectors or delve into automation. Whatever the path, Wright emphasizes that welding isn’t a getrich-quick scheme. It takes effort, engagement, and an eye toward continual improvement.

LEARNING TO LEARN

"I spend so much time on the fundamentals for a reason," Wright said. "I tell my students, ‘The most important thing I’m teaching you is to teach yourself how to weld."

She added that a solid foundation of fundamentals helps on several fronts. First, it helps students adapt to various welding challenges that come their way. The industry is extraordinarily diverse, with various jobs, career paths, job expectations, and (not least) company cultures.

Second, a solid grasp of welding theory and fundamentals helps during the job hunt. "We train them on how to write a good resume and give good interviews. We do what we can so that students can go out there and get the job they really want."

Third, those fundamentals—combined with advanced training and knowing the full range of career options—help students define what that best-fit job could really look like. No job is perfect, and Wright isn’t one to sugarcoat. TSTC teaches students to weld to industry standard codes, but certification tests in the real world can be difficult. As Wright has found, those who persevere connect with the welding process and, especially, the shop culture.

"I remember one shop I worked at had high turnover. Every two or three years, we had a whole new welding team. I turned into the person who was there the longest and knew all about most projects we fabricated there. That put me in a position to start helping people out. I think it all started there, really.

"Like I do today, I gave them the tools they needed and taught them to teach themselves."
