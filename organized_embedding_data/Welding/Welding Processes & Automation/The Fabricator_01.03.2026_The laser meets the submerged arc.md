# The laser meets the submerged arc

[TARİH: 01.03.2026 The Fabricator]

Heavy Fabrication

Discovering the potential of a hybrid welding process

By

Tim Heston

A

fabricator that needs to weld thick plate in as few passes as possible often will turn to submerged arc welding (SAW), a legacy process that has dominated heavy fabrication for decades. Variations like tandem SAW, along with multiwire SAW using multiple power sources and torches simultaneously, boost deposition rates even more. Laser beam welding (LBW) has entered the fray as well. High-powered fiber lasers, either on their own or combined with gas metal arc welding (hybrid laser-GMAW), continue to push the deposition-rate limits.

In the coming years, heavy fabricators may well have another option. At a 2024 conference in Greece, sponsored by the International Institute of Welding, Michael Clemens took the stage. Clemens works for the Welding and Joining Institute at RWTH Aachen University in Germany. During his presentation, he pointed to two weld cross sections through 40-mm material. One showed an X joint prep filled with multiple passes from an SAW setup. The other image showed a double Y joint prep that presented two relatively shallow grooves on either side of the plate.

The system that welded the double Y joint completed everything in two passes, one on top of the plate and another on the bottom, incorporated a process called

laser beam tandem submerged arc hybrid welding, or LUPuS-Tandem

(an acronym based on the original German). Research on hybrid LBW-SAW goes back to 2004, when Dr. Simon Olschok, senior engineer and Clemens’ department head at the welding institute, tested the first iteration of LUPuS using a single SAW torch and CO

2

laser beam. Since then, the project has evolved significantly, now incorporating a solid-state disk laser and two torches in a tandem SAW setup.

The process aims to make the most of tandem SAW’s best qualities, including the relatively high fit-up tolerance and slow cooling that give time for gas and impurities to evacuate the weld. It combines all this with the speed and penetration offered by LBW. Dialed in and perfected, the hybrid process could give heavy fabricators the best of both worlds.

BENEFITS OF SUBARC

SAW has dominated the thick plate, structural, and pipe welding arenas for years, and there’s a good reason for that. The newest fiber laser systems can weld thick plate extraordinarily quickly, even in the butt-joint configuration. Still, high-powered lasers create an enormous amount of heat incredibly quickly. The weld also cools quickly, and those fast cooling rates can, depending on the application, create some undesirable localized hardening. The same can apply to electron beam welding (which also has the disadvantage of requiring a vacuum chamber). Then come the concerns around tight fitup tolerances.

The laser-GMAW hybrid process helps mitigate these issues, but as Clemens and his colleagues explained in a 2025 paper published in Welding in the World, "Due to the high weld penetration depth in combination with high cooling rates, the melt solidifies in such a short time interval that low-melting alloying elements such as manganese and sulfur cannot escape via the weld surface." The result: solidification cracking along the weld centerline.

A submerged arc weld cools relatively slowly, and that can benefit certain applications significantly. At the same time, the high-powered solid-state laser offers extraordinarily high energy density for penetrating deep into the weld. The laser had already been combined with GMAW; combining it with SAW was the logical next step.

THE HYBRID SETUP

The laser beam leads the weld, creating a keyhole, while the two tandem SAW torches trail behind. The first torch points straight downward while the second tilts 20 degrees from perpendicular. The beam (specifically, a 16-kW disk laser with a 200-μm delivery fiber) is set at an angle of 15 degrees from perpendicular to the workpiece in the welding direction. That beam oscillates transversally to the weld seam (see

Setups have used 4-mm copper-plated wires along with conventional welding flux covering the bead. A vertical separating plate between the laser and SAW torches helps keep the welding flux away from the laser beam. And as with conventional SAW, the flux in the hybrid process shields the weld from the atmosphere; the setup uses no shielding gas, even around the laser beam.

The laser process zone, located just in front of the separating plate, was recorded using the Photron FASTCAM SA4 high-speed camera. This, Clemens said, allowed researchers to record the interaction between the leading slag and the separating plate. That interaction turned out to be an important variable.

DIALING IN THE WELD CHARACTERISTICS

Clemens emphasized that, despite the separation plate, the laser beam and SAW torches work in concert as a hybrid welding process. In fact, the position of that separating plate seems key to the process’s hybridity—effectively increasing the area in the weld where the laser and SAW work together.

Researchers now are finding that adjusting the separating plate’s offset height from the weld surface can alter the weld characteristics. Evidence suggests that both the laser and SAW work together no matter where that separating plate is, but the manner in which those processes work together changes.

Researchers ran tests on a Y joint prep. When the separating plate is just 4 mm from the workpiece surface, the weld penetration digs deep and narrow—evidence of the laser’s work—then transitions quickly to a wider weld area where the SAW dominates.

"When we increase the plate height to 6 mm and 8 mm, the hybridity of the process increases," Clemens said in a February interview with The Fabricator.

Specifically, higher plate positions created wider welds with greater mixing between the two processes. "And at 8 mm, the entire structure of the weld becomes very blended from top to bottom," he said. "In this position, we do have flux material escaping into the laser zone, but we’re finding the process to be very robust against these interferences. When we inspect the cross sections, we don’t find any defects or even changing penetration depths."

Clemens explained that a big reason researchers experimented with the separating plate’s position was to test how resilient the overall process would be, especially with flux interacting with the laser. After all, a weld process extremely sensitive to outside variables would be difficult to commercialize, especially considering the harsh environments of many thick-plate welding applications.

Describing this, he pointed to a final image showing the weld with no separating plate at all—and the weld remained robust. "We still have high weld quality," he said, "though the penetration depth does decrease slightly."

So, as the latest research shows, the separating plate’s purpose is less about protecting the laser and more about dialing in the weld characteristics: that is, the extent of the hybridity between LBW and SAW, along with the weld penetration depth.

SOLID BACKING, WITH A MAGNET

In recent studies, researchers conducted tests on 40-mm high-strength, low-alloy structural plate (specifically, S355). Two 1,000-amp SAW power sources worked in concert with a disk laser welding with 16 kW of power, all producing a constant welding speed of 0.6 m/min.

Previous studies had proven the process welding 40- and 50-mm-thick material in just two passes, one layer on top and another on the reverse side—dubbed the

layer/counterlayer technique

Researchers also tested single-sided weld joints (a shallow Y joint prep, with a thick land) in plate up to 26 mm. The setup worked well, except for what was happening at the bottom of the weld. The weld pool would melt through without proper backing—but because this process incorporated a high-powered laser, conventional backing methods couldn’t withstand the hybrid process’s high energy density. They found that the high-energy beam caused the ceramic to become brittle and break. They also found that copper backing melted, which led to some inhomogeneities in the weld root. They prevented that melting by changing the laser’s angle of incidence from 15 to 20 degrees.

Even so, just having that backing bar adds another variable to the setup. Here, the team incorporated a magnetic backing system developed by WeldNova GmbH. Positioned underneath the weld, the system (specifically, the Lorenz force generated by the electromagnet) effectively "pushes up" the molten weld metal trying to melt through, keeping it within the joint gap until it solidifies.

"Using this backing technique, we could weld this 26-mm plate with just one layer [weld pass]," Clemens said.

The technique achieves a full-penetration, single-sided-access weld on extremely thick material. And thanks to the weld magnet underneath (see Figure 1), the weld metal at the root looks as if it’s defying gravity. The pool remains molten long enough to let impurities evacuate, while the magnet ensures nothing melts through at the bottom of the joint.

COMMERCIALIZATION

Tests in the lab have focused solely on hybrid welding, with LBW and SAW working in concert. That said, potential setups in industry could involve both the hybrid process itself and a tandem or multiwire SAW. "Imagine an application where we are joining 100-mm-thick plate thicknesses," Clemens said. "We could make two root passes with the hybrid process, first from one side and then the other side, and the rest could be filled with the tandem SAW process."

Clemens described how sectors like wind energy could boost production significantly. Instead of laying down pass after pass after pass on a 100-mm-thick weld joint, "we could complete as much as 50 or 60 mm of the weld—or even more, depending on available laser power—in just two weld passes, first from the one side and then the other."

Heavy fabricators have relied on SAW for decades. It remains an industry workhorse. In the years to come, adding a laser into the mix could introduce a host of possibilities.
