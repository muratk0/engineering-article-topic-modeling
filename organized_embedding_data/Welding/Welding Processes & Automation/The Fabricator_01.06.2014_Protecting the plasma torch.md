# Protecting the plasma torch

[TARİH: 01.06.2014 The Fabricator]

TECHNOLOGY SPOTLIGHT

Manufacturer devises a new way to keep the plasma torch safe from table crashes

By Dan Davis, Editor-in-Chief

Most metal fabricators are interested in finding a better way to do something. It’s in their genetic makeup, and often those efforts improve business performance. Mike Boss falls into this category.

Boss, 55, started working with metal in high school and has been hooked on it ever since. “I was a metal shop junkie in high school and couldn’t get enough of it. It’s never left me,” he said.

He’s done all kinds of welding during his life, from nuclear work to pipelines, and has spent the last decade running his own manufacturing business. When he purchased his first entry-level CNC plasma cutting table, he recognized almost immediately that he wasn’t satisfied with it and started to make his own retrofits. One of the first areas he targeted for improvement was the plasma torch protection system.

These entry-level tables often relied on magnets to hold the torch, which worked well for high-speed, high-impact collisions when the torch hit a piece of metal. At that point the torch would break free, most often damaging the electrode and nozzle. Also, sometimes the plasma arc would stay on, and this would cause the torch to just slide across the metal it was supposed to cut, looking like “a baby giraffe walking for the first time,” Boss said.

Some brief research on the Internet revealed that more advanced holders were available, but their costs were substantial when compared to the price a fabricator might pay for an entry-level plasma cutting table. Some were as high as $4,000.

“I wanted something that would stop arc and gantry movement as fast as I could,” Boss said.

Developing the Prototypes

About five years ago, Boss began working on prototypes that could sense the slightest impact on the torch and shut off quickly as a result. By this time he had a second CNC plasma table on which he could test the new product designs.

While doing so, however, he learned something about plasma torch crashes: They didn’t occur in the way that he always assumed.

“To me it seemed like there was a misconception out there that crashes occur when the torch is traveling, not necessarily cutting, and it’s occurring at high speed,” he said. “What we found is that our incidents occurred because of tip-ups, where the material tipped up while cutting and the torch ran into it, or the torch would plunge [looking to touch the metal surface to determine the appropriate piercing height] and hit the metal, the result of a bad electrode.

“It seemed like 99 percent of our problems occurred when the arc was on—which was cutting,” he said.

He learned this over hours of prototype destruction—literally programming one of his tables to crash over and over again. The final prototype design had more than 800 hours of use on his table before it was ready to be released to the public. He also had other metalworking shops he knew in the area beta-testing his torch holders.

When he got the OK from his peers, Boss said he knew it was ready for general use. He started selling the Plasma Torch Protection System (see Figure 1) last year.

Figure 1 The Plasma Torch Protection System is designed to stop gantry movement and shut off the arc at the slightest hint of a collision on the table. The goal is to protect the entire plasma torch, its consumables, and the material being cut.

How Does It Work?

Boss said his plasma torch protection device, which is CNC machined out of aircraft-grade aluminum, has a 5-amp, 250-volt microswitch built into the controller. (It is prewired so that it can be used in normally open- or closed-circuit control systems, which allows for installation on most cutting tables.) This microswitch is integral to the torch’s ability to freeze all movement when it encounters even the slightest evidence of a crash.

—Mike Boss, Boss Manufacturing

The torch holder itself sits on hardened steel balls. Anytime the torch rocks in the X-Y position in its secure setting or experiences excessive downward pressure in the Z riser drop, a signal is sent to stop all drive motors and shut off the arc. Boss said it’s comparable to an operator sitting with his hand over the emergency-stop button, turning everything off at the exact moment the torch encounters a problem—a scenario that is not too unfamiliar to some shops.

If a fabricator is worried about the system being too sensitive and perhaps shutting off when the CNC plasma torch advances down to touch the plate before it retracts to a preset pierce point, Boss said he can adjust a sensitivity screw to allow for fine-tuning of torch touch pressure.

“I wanted something that faults with very little movement, but doesn’t fault on its own for no reason,” he said.

The plasma torch holder has the added benefit of providing an owner of an entry-level CNC plasma cutting table a way to cut bevels. The hardened steel balls lock the torch into place at 15, 30, or 45 degrees. The torch can swing in these degree settings in both right and left directions, allowing for bevel cutting along the X direction of travel for most tables.

The holder also supports oxyfuel torches and provides the same protection, but Boss said some long-barrel torches may require a spring change in the holder to accommodate the added weight of the larger torch.
