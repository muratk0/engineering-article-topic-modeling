# How to automate your welding intelligently

Welding Automation

Making the best use of technology isn’t just about speed

Lincoln Brunner

A cobot (collaborative robot) is designed to work alongside people to enhance both the quality of their workpieces and their work life by taking over rote tasks and leaving them with more challenging welding assignments.

E

veryone, it seems, is automating everything, everywhere—cutting, bending, and yes, especially welding.

Well, maybe "everyone" is a stretch. But even for the smallest shops, figuring out ways to speed up production and transfer rote tasks to an automated welding machine affordably is always on the table.

But first, a few considerations are in order.

KNOW YOUR BASELINE

The first is knowing how long it takes your welders to do things.

Christian Lefebvre, welding engineer and application manager for robotic welding solutions at Pemamek, said that a shop should begin an evaluation of its automation needs by getting the best data it can from the production line—by any means possible.

"Know your current situation. How are you doing it now? How many welders, fitters, shifts per day?" said Lefebvre, whose company makes automated welding systems with adaptive welding controls, among other machinery. "Obviously, someone will want to know if it was justified, if it was a good move to introduce robotics. So, how do you compare if you don’t know the current situation?"

Lefebvre has even advised customers—in addition to machine-driven data—to stand by welders on the line with a stopwatch and manually record key numbers like arc-on time and cycle times.

"Go pay attention," he said. "Have a look at what your guys are doing. Get some metrics. Manual welding is typically between 5% and 20%—10% to 15% arc-on time is what you find in normal shops. And that’s a sad number, but it’s realistic."

THE SOLUTIONS AT HAND

For shops looking to automate both intelligently and affordably, the options increase all the time. Cobots, simple track finders, tabletop rotary positioners, fully automated orbital pipe welders with vision-based AI controls, and even standard industrial robots can offer even small shops increased abilities that will provide the necessary ROI—if in fact the contracts, part mix, and part configuration will support it.

What’s more, system integrators will work with customers to construct a customized welding cell that includes whatever torch, power supply, robot arm, and control software they want. In fact, automation solutions that can immediately provide time and cash savings to a shop can be had for as little as $20,000. But even at that price point, shops must buy carefully.

"That’s sometimes the challenge when you look at automation—what type of automation do you want?" said Andrew Pfaller, segment manager for welding machine manufacturer Miller Electric. "The first thing you want to look at: Is the work going to be consistent enough long-term to justify that initial capital expense? You don’t want to buy something that you don’t have a project for long-term."

And that’s the key: Purchase what you need to solve your shop’s bottlenecks, not what a salesman says you need.

"We look at the problem first," said Dan Colvin, vice president of robotics and digital solutions for ESAB, another manufacturer of welding machinery. "We have many different solutions, but we want the solution to solve the problem. If it’s a welding tractor, that’s fine. That’s automated. If it’s a cobot, that’s fine. It’s also OK to need an industrial robot. They all have a place. They all have a function."

AUTOMATION DEMANDS GOOD PREP

A second key item is weld prep. The best robotic welding cell in the world can only do so much to compensate for poor fit-up and, depending on the system, might not be able to at all.

"Do you have the means to prep the pipe or tubing well enough to be consistent?" Pfaller asked. "Because anytime you automate something, you’re taking off the human element—and the human element has the ability to adjust for variation throughout the welding joint. So, if there’s maybe a little bit of a gap or the fitting isn’t perfectly straight because of some other dynamic in the welding environment, you can make up for a lot of it as a human operator. The equipment is going to run a predetermined path, so the joint fit-up and the upstream consistency is going to be a major factor."

That upstream prep is critically important for robotically automated welding procedures, simply because many automation cells are not equipped with the type of controls—be they vision-based, AI-enhanced, or others—that automatically compensate for wide variations in part presentation. Though these systems are improving all the time, automation loves sameness, and lack of it can make your process even slower than it was with strictly manual welding.

"The number one problem people have when they say ‘Hey, let’s automate’ is they have poor fit-up," Covin said. "If you have poor fit-up, whether you’re the manual guy or you’re the robot, you have a problem that you have to solve first.

"A manual welder can make adjustments for every part—an automation system is expecting the same parts to be in the same place in the same profile every time. If you don’t do that, you’re not going to get great results."

Lefebvre also noted that compensating for inconsistencies in incoming raw material also plays an important role in proper weld setup—again, before any specific automation solution comes into play. One step is beveling and/or coping—ensuring that the ends of parts are beveled properly—and a second is mitigating mill scale as much as necessary to ensure good weld quality.

"No one likes to sandblast or remove mill scale, because it is an expensive pre-weld operation and mill scale provides some protection against rust," Lefebvre said. "But it has a huge cost for welding. I could talk for hours about overwelding because of mill scale. So, if you can afford to sandblast, shotblast, and prepare properly, the welding operations will usually benefit from it."

WHEN AUTOMATION DOESN’T FIT

Sometimes, automation isn’t the best solution for a certain shop, whether it’s the complicated nature of the welds it makes or the particular parts it’s producing.

"Absolutely, there are certain shops that shouldn’t go in [on automation]," Colvin said. "There are certain places they just can’t get at the same way twice. It’s not a good fit—I don’t care what anybody tells you. The robot expects you to have that piece in the same place, presented the same way—within reason—every time. If you can’t consistently do that for whatever reason, it’s going to be a bad fit, or at least a struggle, or you have to invest more. You have to know what you need to do before you have to do it."

Lefebvre mentioned that there are certain situations in which a shop is so at the mercy of incoming material that it makes automation impractical.

"It’s really good what adaptive welding can do nowadays, but there’s always a limit," Lefebvre said. "Anything that goes from zero to a ¼-in. gap, something that’s a bit more challenging, then I would likely just keep a human there.

"Some welds are still extremely complex with tight access. Anything that requires really smart human judgment, we’re not quite there with AI yet."

The past few years have seen a sharp rise in the use of cobots because of their relatively low cost and ease of programming, and they have streamlined work for many a shop. However, their popularity should not alone convince a business to purchase one, Lefebvre said.

"I don’t want to be dismissive or negative about cobots. However, the industry I feel really has been pushing a lot of cobots to companies that really may not benefit as much as they initially thought," Lefebvre said. "It’s a good tool; it can be an amazing tool, in fact. But a lot of companies just don’t have the actual need for it. And they end up being limited by what it can do. I’ve had great cases with cobots where it was like, ‘Wow, this is exactly what we needed.’ But in many, many cases, it was gathering dust—like, ‘Yeah, we’re not using it.’"

However, Colvin said, just because a machine is sitting idle because you’re no longer making that one part that convinced you to buy it in the first place doesn’t mean that machine has to remain idle. Far from it, in fact. Often, it’s just a matter of overcoming the fear of messing up an expensive machine that already has become an embarrassment to management and the people on the floor.

"Anything can be reused," Colvin said. "All the robot does is provide motion. Just change the welding process, maybe change the vision system that’s on it or maybe the seam tracker. There are 100 different ways to put it. Invest a little bit and get a lot instead of just sitting around and letting it collect dust.

"People don’t do that with manual fixturing and manual welding stations. If you get a new part, you start welding it. Yet it seems to be the third rail for robot guys: ‘Oh no, we don’t want anybody to touch it. They’re going to screw it up. It took us three months to get the thing working.’ Well, yeah, but it hasn’t been working for six months. So who cares? How do you break it if it’s unused?"
