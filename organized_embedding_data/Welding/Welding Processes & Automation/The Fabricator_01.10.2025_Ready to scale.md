N

ot far from Phoenix Sky Harbor International Airport, in the shadow of Chase Field, home of the Arizona Diamondbacks, sits Capitol Engineering’s new 11-acre, 120,000-sq.-ft. metal fabrication plant, three miles south of its former facility with acreage for growth. A plasma cutter sits adjacent to a robotic plasma-arc beam cutting line. Downstream from that is a robotic pipe welding system with two welding guns, one following the other: the first lays down the root pass, the second the cover pass, completing certain pipe welds in just one rotation.

Three years ago, the company employed about 38 people. Today, the headcount is at 64, and the company is looking to expand that by 25% this year and another 50% in 2026. The 88-year-old shop is

more automated

and employs

more people

than ever.

CE isn’t the kind of company that usually grabs headlines. But its growth spurt could indicate just how much growth opportunity exists for small shops, many of which have operated quietly for decades. What’s driving that growth? Every situation is unique, but in CE’s case, the primary driver has been an organizational transformation—away from the traditional job shop and toward a dynamic organization ready to scale.

Setting the Stage for Transition

"We’ll be celebrating 90 years within a couple of years. We were founded in 1937, and the business was owned by the founding family until 2022. That original family maintained a small job shop environment for decades. It’s a story a lot of small job shops around the country share."

That was John Carney, who took over as CE’s president in November 2024. Between 2010 and 2018, Carney worked for another industrial fabricator in southern Arizona that competed with CE on many projects. He already had worked with many of CE’s customers, and he came to the job knowing just how much growth potential there was.

As just one example, Carney recalled one meeting several months ago with an Australian material handling company representative looking to build conveyors for the copper mines in the U.S. The prospect was looking for CE’s engineering and metal fabrication expertise to make that happen. He cited other work related to data centers and communication infrastructure as well as lighter-gauge fabrication for the region’s cotton processors, EV infrastructure, and a variety of work for commercial construction.

CE has a geographical advantage. Yes, the global trading order might be changing, and uncertainty around tariffs remains, but geography (and geology, for that matter) isn’t changing. Arizona is home to some significant copper mines, and some of its farms produce the highest-quality, highest-yield cotton available. These industries need metal fabrications, and many of them are large and expensive to ship. Building a shorter, local supply chain simply makes good business sense.

All this potential demand made CE an attractive acquisition candidate, and in 2021, the organization was sold to an outside investor, a family office based in Arizona that became only the second owner in the 85-plus-year-old company’s history. In the ensuing years, the fabricator underwent some leadership changes before Carney became president.

"Since I’ve been here, we’ve had a fair amount of staff turnover, which is actually a good thing." He emphasized that the turnover hasn’t been on the shop floor, which employs some technical experts who have been with CE for decades. Most of the change has occurred at the leadership level, which isn’t uncommon when a business makes a significant change over a short period. In many respects, CE isn’t the company it was just four years ago.

That said, the shop’s previous family owners developed very close customer relationships over the decades, and a previous president, who led the company for more than 30 years, still advises CE’s leadership. The fabricator’s technical experts, existing customer relationships, and market position made it attractive to an outside buyer. "Our new owner is a growth-oriented individual," Carney said. "He purchased and moved us to this new facility, and he has some grand visions for growth."

Three Workflows Under One Roof

For years, much of CE had a typical process-centric layout: plasma cutting and a band saw for cutting beams up front; press brakes and plate rolls in the center; final welding and assembly at the end. Since 2022, the company added robotic pipe welding and a robotic plasma cutter for beams, tubes, and other structural shapes, as well as a new plasma cutting table.

Within CE’s plant, plate rolling and bending occur in Bay No. 2, along with automated pipe welding.

This dual-gun robotic pipe welding system can lay down a root and cover pass, completing certain jobs in just one pipe rotation.

The company’s initial cutting operations, including its plasma table, remain shared resources that feed the three bays in the plant, each dedicated to a certain type of job.

Work flowed pretty much how it always had until May of this year, when CE moved to its new fabrication facility, one that’s nearly triple the size of its former operations. Some of the space at the new property is leased to another company and, hence, gives CE some runway for future growth.

The operation now is separated into three 400-ft.-long bays. For the most part, job flow occurs in a linear fashion. "We analyzed the number of times a forklift has to go into the fab area to retrieve a workpiece," Carney said, "and we also observed how often we rely on our overhead cranes and the jib cranes at various workstations." He added that upstream cutting and forming remain shared resources, but the fitting, welding, and final assembly now occur in specific bays.

Organizational management and part flow in custom fabrication has always been a challenge. The customer mix defines the fabricator, and until somewhat recently, CE really defined itself solely as a job shop. Every job was different, the thinking went, and the mix was far too high to automate or implement anything resembling lean manufacturing.

This mentality, Carney said, has changed. It began with those initial automation investments, including the robotic pipe welding and beam cutting system. But the thinking really started to change when everyone moved into the new facility. The three bays in the plant represent the three primary workflows:

structural steel

(which includes long parts),

industrial

(including forming and automated pipe welding), and

quick-turn work

(which involves smaller workpieces).

Carney explained that these workflows describe the size and nature of the fabrication work, not a specific customer segment. A mining customer might order a conveyor that involves some long structural shapes as well as some rolled and welded components, requiring workflow to "change lanes" to the adjacent bay.

Material handling requirements guided the layout. Much of what CE fabricates is large, which means jobs can spend more time waiting for transport between workstations than actually being cut, formed, or welded. That robotic pipe welding system, for instance, is incredibly productive, yet if it spends much of its day waiting for work to be moved, tacked, and fixtured, then jobs really don’t ship any faster. The result: Overall efficiency doesn’t change.

In Bay No. 1, near the far wall, a robotic plasma system cuts and copes beams, hollow structural sections, and structural shapes, which then flow straight down the bay to the fitting and welding stations. "This work tends to be very long," Carney said, "so we wanted to be able to send these straight through the plant." Rotating a large, long beam can take a lot of time—and that’s time the customer might not be willing to pay for.

Bay No. 2 handles plates that require bending or rolling, as well as automated pipe welding. Fork trucks and cranes are positioned to ensure work spends minimal time waiting between work centers. Meanwhile, Bay No. 3 handles quick-turn work involving smaller workpieces that don’t require an overhead crane.

CE remains a high-product-mix operation, and most jobs still involve discrete projects, so various jobs might jump between bays as fabrication requirements dictate. Still, the arrangement avoids the classic, "tangled" spaghetti-diagram workflow of the traditional job shop. Most work moves down the plant in linear fashion, toward welding and final assembly.

Push, Not Pull

The shop floor might have some new technology, but the most profound changes at CE have occurred in the way work flows through the entire organization, from sales to engineering and detailing to order processing and, finally, the shop floor.

Within the past year, the fabricator made strategic hires to expand its detailing and engineering capability. "Many customers need a company like ours to do some modeling and fabrication-level detailing," Carney explained. "The fact we have in-house engineering and design makes us an attractive partner."

To make the office workflow scalable, the leadership team made some fundamental changes. In the past, a single project manager shepherded work through the entire enterprise, from the initial quote to the final shipment. This made a world of sense in the job shop world, especially for a small company in the industrial fabrication space. Having one person manage projects from beginning to end gave each account a single point of contact. That person also knew where everything stood. If he had a question on an order, he just walked to the floor and asked.

"This year alone, we’re seeing revenue grow by 40%, and we expect to grow by 30% to 35% over the next few years. We’re hoping to be a $50 million company within four years. … We’re market-agnostic. [When opportunity arises], we’ll staff up for it, we’ll buy the equipment if we need it, and get ourselves positioned to be in that market."

—John Carney, Capitol Engineering Co.

To prep orders for the floor, one person would shepherd a job through purchasing, the creation of drawing packages, layout and detailing, and numerous other tasks. Sometimes he’d backtrack through certain steps to adapt to external or unexpected changes, like a customer request or feedback from engineering or detailing.

This works at a fabricator with a few dozen employees—but what about one with 80, 100, or 200-plus employees? Carney and his team knew that as the work grew more complex, the lack of formal procedures in the office could become an albatross.

To that end, Carney and his team analyzed the current state of office workflow—what exactly needed to happen to release a job cleanly to the floor. Ideally, job packets should tell those on the floor

everything

they need to know, with no need to walk to the office for clarification.

"From this, we built a 15-step workflow for every project, and within every project workflow, we have specific task assignments," Carney said. "Now, we use project management software that sends notifications to specific people, telling them that a task is available for them to complete. Many of these have a previous task of dependency. That is, the previous task needs to be complete before the next task can start."

Some tasks can happen concurrently, but the nature of metal fabrication means some steps

must

happen in a certain sequence. "For instance, a work order is issued, so now the engineering team needs to create a drawing package. Once that task is complete, the layout team creates a nest for plasma cutting. It’s not a pull-style system. It’s a push system, and for our type of business, a push system tends to work best."

This is where CE (and other industrial fabricators like it) fundamentally differ from product line manufacturers that pursue traditional forms of lean manufacturing. These promote a "pull" approach to ensure machines upstream don’t overproduce and create excess work-in-process. Capacity at downstream processes "pulls" work from upstream. Once replenishment points are stocked, there’s no need to produce more until the next pull signal.

Industrial fabricators like CE manufacture discrete, often very large fabrications and, hence, operate very differently. Each project is supported by pages of engineering drawings, certification data, and other information. A job moving forward with incomplete or incorrect information can snowball into significant waste downstream, including some extraordinarily expensive scrap. Rushing orders through the office, just to keep every machine and person on the floor busy, can lead to some serious mistakes and overall inefficiency.

"With our system, the people on our team see the task in front of them," Carney said. "Once they complete it, they push the project on and receive the next task to complete. For us at least, this system helps create an orderly workflow."

Set to Scale

Before FABTECH, the Fabricators and Manufacturers Association released its latest Forming and Fabricating Job Shop Consumption Report, revealing results of a periodic survey that asks fabricators (among other things) about their greatest technology need. This includes cutting, forming, and various secondary processes like hardware insertion and deburring. In the latest survey, more respondents cited

software

—including enterprise resource planning (ERP), quoting, nesting, and scheduling packages—than any other technology category.

This makes it no surprise that Carney and his team are on the hunt for ERP software tailored for the fabricator’s way of working. He added that whatever software the shop chooses, it needs to be tailored to handle some extremely complex projects.

"We have a potential project now, for the mining sector, for a conveying system that’s larger than an 18-wheeler. These are massive pieces of equipment, and our proximity to the mines is a big selling point. As the customer told me, the reduced shipping costs alone make CE a preferred solution. We’re aiming to ship these products in large pieces, so there’s a minimal amount of field work."

Carney conceded, however, that CE’s geography advantage only goes so far. The last thing managers want is to become overly dependent on a single industry, or industries that just happen to be local. CE has plans to expand its footprint and further diversify its revenue streams.

"With changing material prices and all the market changes, we’ve seen some scaling back of certain projects," Carney said, adding that CE continues to grow by broadening its sales efforts. All the changes CE has made, including its ramped-up sales efforts, have paid off. Despite all the uncertainty and pullbacks in certain areas, CE’s revenue continues to climb.

"This year alone, we’re seeing revenue grow by 40%, and we expect to grow by 30% to 35% over the next few years. We’re hoping to be a $50 million company within four years.

"We’re market-agnostic," Carney continued, adding that as long as a job fits CE’s strengths, "we’ll staff up for it, we’ll buy the equipment if we need it, and get ourselves positioned to be in that market."
