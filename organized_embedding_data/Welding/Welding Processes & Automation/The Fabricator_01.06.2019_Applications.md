Using pneumatic and hydraulic arms, shop keeps tapping and profits in-house

Situation

Working in stainless, mild steel, and aluminum, Springfield, Ohio-based fabricator Spradlin Brothers Welding handles parts and projects for a variety of industries, including manufacturing, construction, school systems, food and beverage, and retail.

In the early days Jeff Spradlin’s father and uncle ran the welding shop and sent drilling and milling of parts out to other machine shops in the area. Later Spradlin would tap small runs manually with a drill and large batches in a mill. But tapping with a CNC machine can cause production backups and increase the risk of broken taps and scrapped parts. Tapping in a mill adds programming time even with rigid tapping capability, and putting a tap in, touching off the tap, and switching parts add time. On projects with multiple holes, each additional hole slows down production.

And Spradlin still had to send some large batches out to neighboring shops for tapping, spending up to $4,000 a year on outsourcing—not to mention the cost of production downtime while waiting for parts to return.

Resolution

Two years ago Spradlin added two FlexArm tapping arms, the GH-60 hydraulic tapping arm and a smaller A-32 pneumatic tapping arm, to the shop floor. They feature adjustable gas counterbalanced springs, so the operator simply positions the tap over the hole and the tapping arm stays where it is positioned or automatically returns to the home (folded) position. The tap head is always at 90 degrees, ensuring perpendicularity and reducing operator errors.

Each tapping arm counterbalances the weight of the motor and absorbs the torque created by the tool to help reduce fatigue and the risk of operator injury. A quick-change chuck allows operators to change tap sizes in seconds with no need to change gears, head height, or stops.

Tapping offline moves each part through the process more quickly, saving seconds per hole.

An adjustable torque clutch allows the tap to stop turning when it encounters any obstruction that might cause breakage. While a CNC machine averages a broken tap every 300 holes, the tapping arms can go 3,000 holes without breaking a tap, according to the manufacturer.

Spradlin’s bread-and-butter business is working with mild steel, tapping mostly ⅜- to ¾-in. holes. Once a year a 2-in. project will come through the door, and he has one customer that requires all metric sizes. Changing among projects just involves popping one tap out and another one in, metric or standard, ¾ or 2 in.

With tapping done offline, the CNC machine is free to keep drilling parts, speeding up production and using each machine in the line effectively. For Spradlin, in addition to the convenience of having everything in-house, it means more parts out the door faster and sharper profit lines on each part.

Ceiling-mounted linear module saves floor space for part transport

Situation

Automation products manufacturer Güdel recently had a customer with an application that required transporting extremely fragile parts quickly between workstations. Because of the delicate nature of the parts and the speed and repeatability with which they needed to be moved, the manufacturer’s ZP-3 double-axis linear module would have been suitable, except for one thing: Floor space in the factory was very limited, and there was no way to mount the gantry using standard legs without interfering with the work flow between the cells.

Even with all of the professionals and customization required to complete this project, costs remained low, and mounting from the ceiling proved to be the best option.

With no room on the floor, the applications engineers went straight to work on a ceiling mount for the equipment, but that came with some challenges. Several factors need to be considered when mounting a 2-ton piece of equipment to a ceiling that was designed simply to conceal the supports for the floor above it. The ceiling supports and trusses needed to be reinforced to support the weight of the equipment.

And because the equipment is moving, vibration presented another challenge. Repeatability was also a big concern, as the highly automated manufacturing process required picking up and dropping off very fragile parts.

Resolution

Güdel engineers worked directly with architects and civil engineers to create a system that would not only support the heavy equipment, but also provide the stability required for repeatability in the manufacturing process. The manufacturer used a standard ZP-3 doubleaxis linear module, along with standard legs modified to mount the gantry in the opposite direction.

Even with all of the professionals and customization required to complete this project, costs remained low, and mounting from the ceiling proved to be the best option.
