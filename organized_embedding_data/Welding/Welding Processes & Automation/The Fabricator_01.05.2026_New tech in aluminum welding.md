F

or several decades, fabricators have relied on well-known welding processes such as shielded metal arc welding (stick), gas metal arc welding (MIG), and gas tungsten arc welding (TIG) in their shops. Aside from some power efficiency and performance improvements to the traditional welding processes, the industry has grown stagnant in delivering innovative technologies that could be widely used because of patents or other barriers. Recently, however, some of those patents have expired, allowing for a surge of innovation that has been pushing the aluminum welding industry forward.

PLASTIC-STATE WELDING

Friction stir welding (FSW) is not necessarily a new welding process, but the implementation of it was slowed significantly by patents. In the past few years, those patents have expired, allowing companies to improve on the previous shortcomings, which has resulted in broader implementation.

Friction stir is not like other fusion welding processes. Instead of melting the puddle with an arc, it uses the heat generated by a rotating tool to achieve a nearly molten, or plastic, state in the material, and then the material is mixed or stirred together. Because the base material is not going through a phase change, the process was found to be useful for specialty materials like 2XXX and 7XXX series aluminum, which are generally considered nonweldable.

A laser typically produces a narrow, deep, and clean weld. Even though the joint may look thin, laser welds still can provide the strength needed for frame members.

Originally, the process used a large, mill-like head to drive the friction stir bit through the base material in straight lines. In its early days, the process was limited to flat planes because of the heavy tooling that was required to hold and support the sheets. Derivations of the process now use a backer attached to the rotating tool instead of the robust tooling, which allows for the process to be more easily implemented in configurations that have complex geometries such as curved surfaces and dome shapes.

CONTINUING TO ADD

The most recent process breakthrough has been additive FSW. This process uses wrought bar material that is driven down through the center of the rotating tool for the purpose of "printing" metal components. Like standard FSW, the process has proven effective on previously nonweldable alloys as well as better material properties. For example, instead of making a unique tool to have a part forged, companies now can have a part created in near-net shape with additive FSW and simply machine weld the final critical profiles. This has proven to significantly reduce both cost and time, often by months or years, making additive FSW especially attractive for low-volume or highly complex parts where traditional tooling lead times are prohibitive.

SUPERIOR PRECISION

Another welding process that has really taken off in the past five years is hand-held laser welding. Rather than using an electrical arc, this method delivers energy to the weld pool in the form of focused light. Similar to children in grade school using a magnifying glass to focus sunlight to a highly concentrated spot, laser welding uses lenses to focus the generated light onto the metal.

Although it can be done autogenously by adding a wire feed, the process is nearly automatic because the wire feeder acts as a drive system pushing the torch along the weld joint. This means companies can now produce high-quality welds with novice welders—freeing their skilled staff for complex projects.

At more than 1,000 times the energy densities of MIG or TIG, the process opens doors to previously challenging joints, such as thin material welded to a thick material. Because of the highly localized heating, these are now very simple to complete without fear of melting holes in the thinner part.

Another benefit is the speed of the process. Most TIG welding is done at 4 to 6 IPM. The hand-held laser welding process is commonly used at 15 to 20 IPM, with some companies reaching more than 35 IPM. An application welding a ½-in.-thick piece to 20-ga. sheet that previously took an hour to complete was reduced to two minutes with a hand-held laser.

The combination of fast travel speeds and reduced heat input has a noticeable impact on part distortion. It is not uncommon for companies to significantly reduce or even eliminate post-weld operations. One company reported its post-weld straightening and finishing went from two days down to two hours.

WELDING SAFETY

Although hand-held lasers have many benefits, metal fabricators need to ensure their safe implementation. In the U.S., ANSI Z136.1 Safe Use of Lasers dictates that companies need to have a laser safety officer on staff and equipment needs to have a minimum set of safety-related features. These consist of an emergency stop, a method to prevent unauthorized usage, and the ability to hook interlocks into the weld area or cell to establish a laser controlled area.

BARRIERS TO IMPLEMENTATION

With both the hand-held laser and FSW processes being in their infancy, the welding industry does not have an overabundance of published knowledge. This means that companies need to do their own development or rely on manufacturers to supply parameters that can be used.

Regardless of the technology being considered, the real potential for those welding aluminum is here now and here to stay!
