# 10 lessons for building a healthy welding business

[TARİH: 01.01.2026 The Fabricator]

Mobile Welding

Scott Davis of ProRanch Welding kept his new business alive while fighting cancer

By

Dan Davis

Scott Davis of ProRanch Welding built his welding business by seeking out any jobs on commercial sites he could find. He found that if the weather was rough, he had a good chance of getting work because he knew that others likely stayed home that day.

S

cott Davis spent a lifetime welding, but he was doing it on his own time. That’s something he wanted to correct.

He started welding on the family farm when he was seven. He said he remembered a cousin asking him if he wanted to try stick welding, only to have that cousin turn down the amps on the power source, which caused him to get his electrode stuck. Even with the awkward introduction to welding, he knew he wanted to continue doing it.

"It was neat seeing something where you have an issue, you repair it, and then you look to see it fixed. That’s really rewarding," Davis said.

In the ensuing years, Davis kept on welding while working for the federal government in law enforcement and pursuing a master’s degree. Whether fixing things for friends or making a smoker, he didn’t go too long without striking an arc.

As he got into his 40s, however, he thought that he might want to pursue welding further and turn it into a career. While he had never really welded for anyone outside of his friends and family, he thought he was good enough to sell his services to others.

"I was like, ‘Let’s do this,’" he said, "so I went whole hog."

In 2016, he attended what was then Pikes Peak Community College (now Pikes Peak State College) in Colorado Springs, Colo., to get what he called "official training." "I flew right through it, but I enjoyed it immensely," he said.

He followed that up with his American Welding Society certifications at an accredited testing facility in Grand Junction, Colo., and after that he started to seek out work. He filed the paperwork to incorporate ProRanch Welding in 2018.

In the seven years since he launched a welding business in his 40s, Davis has learned countless lessons. Here are his top 10.

1 TAKE ADVANTAGE OF EVERY OPPORTUNITY

One of the first gigs that Davis got was working on a parking garage. Involving seemingly countless vertical up lap joint welds using 7018 electrodes. The job didn’t pay much, but the experience was just what he wanted at that time.

"He offered to bring me in, and I accepted because I really wanted to practice that orientation over and over. I wanted to get really good at it," he said.

In addition to the work, Davis also made some key connections. Because the work environment was well organized and well managed, he was able to focus on his work, which caught the attention of the foremen and the superintendent on the job. Davis struck up relationships with these folks, some of which last to this day.

2 KEEP MOVING FORWARD

About a year after formally starting ProRanch Welding in Black Forest, Colo., Davis experienced some hallucinations on the job, seeing figures out the corner of his eye but later realizing nothing was there. Around this time, he also started having weird taste sensations, like scrambled eggs reminding him of sour gummy worms.

After an initial visit to the doctor and a false diagnosis of a clogged salivation gland, Davis underwent a more extensive battery of tests. That’s when he found out he had cancer in his head, more specifically around the bifurcation of the carotid artery, which splits into two channels to supply blood to the face and the head. Because of the golf ball-sized tumor’s location, surgery wasn’t an option. This would have to be addressed with an aggressive treatment of radiation and chemotherapy.

Once treatment began, Davis realized that he wouldn’t be able to work. Although he has had his share of serious injuries in his life, Davis would find out that this cancer treatment was going to be his greatest battle.

Several weeks of daily radiation treatment and weekly chemo treatments made it difficult for Davis to drink water or eat anything. In fact, he went 98 days without eating a single thing, instead being fed through a port in his chest. He also struggled to sleep laying down, so he spent all of those days sitting in a chair.

Despite his situation, Davis knew he wasn’t going to let his health derail his newly launched one-man business.

"During this entire time, from start to finish, from the testing to the treatments to the posttreatments, I said, ‘This is not going to stop my company. I need to build it every day,’" he said. "I couldn’t go out in the shop and weld, grind, or cut, but I could do one thing a day for my company. I just had to figure out what it would be and get it done."

He ended up creating his website and optimizing it for search engines. He added more pictures to it to help customers understand what ProRanch Welding could do. He followed up with insurance companies, reached out to potential customers, and researched ways to reduce the cost of his business. A lot of that work later paid off when he got back to welding in late 2020.

3 ALWAYS NETWORK

As the early days on the parking garage job revealed, networking pays off. In fact, it helped him get back into the swing of things after getting strong enough to return to jobsites. Davis had work lined up and subcontractors on-site to help him should he struggle, which he inevitably did early on.

"I took a lot of time in networking with general contractors, with business owners, and just building relationships, which has paid off in a ridiculous way," he said. "I end up knowing people that know people and push hard to get meetings."

Davis said he has never thrown a business card away, and he always follows up with someone he meets. It might be just to check in, but he wants to keep those relationships alive. Someone who isn’t ready for his welding services today certainly might require them down the road.

4 MAKE YOURSELF KNOWN

"I don’t do it so much anymore because I can’t even keep up with the work I have, but I used to drive around, find any construction site wherever I was at, and introduce myself," Davis said. "I’ll show up, bring my card, and ask the first guy I see where the foreman or superintendent is.

"When I meet them, I give them a firm handshake, look them in the eye, tell them that I know that they are busy, and introduce myself right there."

Davis said that the foreman or superintendent consistently would respond with a job opportunity on the spot, such as repairing a bucket. After reassuring his new employer that he had the necessary tools, personal protective equipment, and certifications, Davis went to work.

"The fact that you showed up when others didn’t makes a difference," he added. "That’s why I liked to go out when it was nasty, cold, windy, and dark. On an 80-degree day, anyone could show up."

5 GROW AS YOU GO

When ProRanch Welding began, Davis saw it as more of a one-person operation. He liked it that way, as it reminded him of the welding process itself. If something was wrong, he only had to look in the mirror to find the culprit.

But if you do the jobs right, customers likely want you to do more. With more satisfied customers come more opportunities, and the way to keep those folks satisfied is to keep taking on the work. That’s when it might make sense to bring on the right people to join your business.

Today ProRanch Welding has six employees. They are handling more of the work while Davis acts as a liaison between customers and the welders.

"I’m not doing as much welding as I would like to because I love the welding process," he said, "but I’m more involved with fabrications, which can be fun."

6 NEVER STOP LEARNING

"I’m always interested in learning, and I’d like to keep learning about welding if money were no issue," Davis said.

Laser welding has caught his attention recently. A colleague aligned himself with a supplier of laser welding systems, and Davis was able to see how the technology works up close.

Even though the welds were really small, he discovered through some destructive tests of laser-welded coupons that the joints were acceptable. The laser provided deep penetration without the extra heat input that might come with more traditional welding processes and which typically leads to distortion.

7 LEARN TO LOVE IT

It’s 17 degrees F, and the wind is whipping. You’re 30 ft. up in a telescopic handler, trying to do a vertical up weld with the wind moving the bucket and the wall on which you’re welding. You’ve got leather gloves on, attempting to wipe the snow off your shield, but it only smudges it.

"At the end of a day like that, I feel like my welds frickin’ kicked. I feel fantastic, like I’m Superman," Davis said.

Some people might find shielded metal arc (stick) welding boring, but Davis said he’s learned to love it. He even likes the smell of his 6010 electrodes burning while welding because it reminds him of a campfire. It’s comforting and fun while also being a challenge.

8 STAND BEHIND THE WORK

From the very start, Davis has always guaranteed his work. He said customers are paying for a job well done, and he wants to deliver.

"If my welds break, I will go and redo the weld for free for life," he said.

He said customers expect wear and tear, such as on an excavator bucket. They can see it happening and plan for it. But if a weld repair on that same bucket fails, that general contractor can’t use the bucket until it’s repaired, leading to lost revenue.

9 LEARN TO USE VARIOUS TOOLS

"Welding gets you so far. Fabricating takes you a lot further," Davis said.

Customers like the idea of having one person work on their projects, he added. When a project involves multiple parties, the likelihood of delays and even quality issues increases.

Davis recommends learning to use press brakes or plate rollers. Tools like that can help a welder become a fabricator.

10 FIND THE RIGHT CUSTOMERS

One of the luxuries of being in business a while and establishing a solid foundation of familiar customers is being able to take on the work you want to do. That helps to keep you focused and satisfied with your work, according to Davis.

Every time Davis strikes an arc, he’s charging $300. His in-shop rate is $125 per hour, and the mobile rate is $150 per hour plus travel expenses.

Leave the $30 wheel repair jobs to a friend or relative. Pursue work so that you can build a portfolio of good customers and a solid business.

Davis is now cancer-free, and he doesn’t expect it to come back. His business is doing so well that he’s a regular contractor for one of the world’s largest companies, and he has a mobile office on its campus.

His discipline and love of welding have helped him to achieve a level of happiness and satisfaction that is 180 degrees from where he was when he rang that bell marking the end of his cancer treatment. The light at the end of the tunnel just happened to be an arc strike.

A mobile welding business operates better with a fully stocked truck capable of going where the work is.
