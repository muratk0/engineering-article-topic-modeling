hristian Crosby pointed to his well-worn copy of the Code of Standard Practice for Steel Buildings and Bridges, from the American Institute of Steel Construction (AISC). He was presenting at the 2024 NASCC: The Steel Conference, in a room within the convention center where attendees took notice of the wide span beams and trusses overhead—elements that, as Crosby pointed out, add to the cost of construction.

"When we start adding any complexity, outside simple beams and columns, the hours per ton can jump very quickly. When the complexity rises, the shop labor costs increase as well."

Crosby should know. He’s senior vice president of fabrication at Phoenix, Ariz.-based Schuff Steel, one of the largest structural fabricators in the country. His talk, titled "Connection Economy—Thoughts From a Steel Fabricator," focused on cost reduction in structural fabrication. He used "connection" in the title for a good reason. The cost of fabricating those connections turns out to be a primary cost driver in structural fabrication.

How Oversimplification Leads to Excess Fabrication Costs

To be sure, connections represent just one of many cost drivers in the fab shop. Material enters the equation too, including whether the fab shop can buy from the mill or needs to go through a service center. The number of beam sizes plays a role too (fewer is better to achieve minimum order quantities), as does the structure’s complexity.

Trusses and built-up shapes—that is, the fabrication of custom beam sizes—have an effect. Using stock beam sizes available from the mill is usually much more cost-effective than having a fabricator assemble and weld a custom beam size (see

Figure 1

). The use of welded reinforcements within a beam can also add costs. Sometimes the cost of those reinforcements is more than simply choosing a larger beam that doesn’t need reinforcement (see

Figure 2

).

"We over-weld a lot of our structures," Crosby said. "Many times, we don’t need all that excessive welding, and all of that extra labor adds up."

All this said, connection design represents a significant portion of overall fabrication costs. Much of this comes from the quest to standardize and simplify at the engineering and detailing stage; some of this effort actually pushes complexity to where it costs the most—on the shop floor and on the jobsite. A prime example is noting the percentage of uniformly distributed loads (UDLs) instead of the actual loads placed on a beam.

As Crosby asked, "In the design notes, why not use the actual loads? When you get into short beams, using that percentage of UDLs can lead to connections getting very large very quickly."

Similar thinking applies to standardizing to 100% column capacity with every column splice. "Some columns are indeed carrying significant load, so we need to develop [column splices] based on 100% of the column capacity. But there’s also a similar number of opportunities where we really don’t need to develop every column splice to account for 100% of the column capacity."

The issue again comes down to oversimplification in engineering and design leading to over-fabrication—and excess cost—on the structural fabrication shop floor.

Who Does the Connection Design?

All this leads to a poignant question Crosby posed at the conference:

Who should design connections?

Here’s where his well-worn AISC code of standard practice comes into the story. As to who should design connections, the code gives three answers: (1) the owner’s designated representative for design (ODRD), usually the structural engineer of record; (2) an ODRD-approved detailer; or (3) a licensed engineer working for the fabricator. He emphasized that plenty of caveats and further details exist in the standard, but broadly speaking, the construction industry works with those three basic options.

Crosby advocated for the third option—an engineer working for the structural fabricator, someone who can design for the actual (not UDL) loads and, not least, knows the fab shop’s strengths and weaknesses. The fabricator’s engineers certainly can work with certain critical connections designed by the ODRD. "But as for the rest of them, allow the fabricator to design the connections. Moreover, the design team needs to be open to value engineering. Fabricators have a lot of good ideas on how to save money on a structure."

Decisions, of course, need to be coordinated through the ODRD. The engineer of record can see when a fabricator’s idea doesn’t meet broadly accepted design standards. Regardless, having ideas come from engineers at the structural fabricator—mere steps away from the beam line itself—can create tremendous value.

"Fabricators know the skills their people have," Crosby said. "They also know the equipment they have. With this knowledge, they can select the connections they want and can help the fabrication of those connections be more efficient."

Different Fabricators, Different Strengths

Fabricators know their people and equipment best, and each fabricator has different strengths and weaknesses. For instance, a welded connection might work best for one fabricator, but a bolted connection might work best for another fab shop.

Equipment also varies tremendously. Some connections might be easier simply because of the type and style of a structural fabricator’s beam line, including its level of automation (see

Figure 3

).

"All fabricators have different skill sets," Crosby said, "and if you go across the country, those skill sets vary from location to location. Some might have a good pool of welders, while others might struggle to find welders but have a great pool of fitters."

Welding vs. Bolting

Consider a classic example: The engineer of record refers to a standard table for simple sheer connections using clip angles. Should these be welded or bolted onto the beam?

Any AISC-certified shop must have its quality management system audited annually, with implemented procedures in process control. This includes written procedures for both welding and bolting. "This doesn’t necessarily mean the fabricator is efficient in both welding and bolting," Crosby said. "It just means the shop has the processes and people to do them both."

The same thinking applies to other processes. For instance, an AISC shop might be certified to fabricate handrails, but walk the floor of a structural fabricator that focuses on heavy fabrication of beams, columns, trusses, and some built-up sections, and you probably won’t find many handrails being fabricated. Instead, the work usually is sourced to a specialist. A structural fabricator might be certified to fabricate handrails, but it probably doesn’t fabricate them efficiently.

"The same holds true for when you’re considering a bolted versus welded clip. A shop might have processes and procedures in place for a bolted clip, but if they don’t do it that often, they probably won’t be very efficient. This adds to the overall cost."

An old-school shop could rely heavily on mag drills and manual layout, complete with adjustable tape measure, T square, and soapstone, with every hole location drawn manually (see

Figure 4

). To be competitive, such a shop probably has an expansive welding department that can make welded connections efficiently. Some operations even have automated welding stations where robots fit, tack, and weld.

Of course, just because a shop can make welded connections very efficiently doesn’t mean it can magically make any welding job cost-effective. As Crosby explained, this again goes back to matching the beam selection with load requirements.

He described a job that called for a very large gusset plate that weighed over 50 lbs. welded into a column. The gusset plate was so heavy, in fact, that it tied up an overhead crane to lift and steady the piece during fitting. "Once you get over 50 lbs., now you have multiple people working on the connection and having to use overhead cranes," Crosby said. This adds to overall cost. Sizing the beam properly, to meet actual load requirements, could have simplified the fabrication of that connection significantly.

Costs can depend on the local market and fabrication technology available. The cost difference between shop and field welding might not be as pronounced in certain rural areas. On the other hand, automated welding at a specific structural fabricator might lower cost and widen that difference between shop and field welding costs.

Breaking Down the Costs

Manual welding dominates structural fabrication, and as Crosby cited, when you break down the typical job costs for welding, labor is by far the largest expense—at around 80%, depending on the job.

A fabricator that excels at bolting usually has beam lines with high-speed drills, which can make holes in a matter of seconds, and perhaps a robotic plasma system for holemaking in lighter structural members. Some even scribe layout marks and even the weld symbols. This makes welding more efficient, too—but often, the incredible holemaking productivity of a beam line can, at times, make those bolted connections a lot more cost-effective. As Crosby explained, holemaking might be automated, but the actual bolting can require a lot of shop labor. In fact, when you break down the costs of a bolted connection, 85% of it typically goes into labor.

Back to the clip example, should it be bolted or welded? Crosby described a scenario in which fitting and welding took 35% longer, while CNC automation creates holes quicky. Welders were paid 20% to 30% more than general labor, and those general laborers could fit, line up, and bolt those clips very quickly. Though they might be paid less than welders, general laborers performing bolted connections are likely part of a much larger team, which ticks up the overall labor cost.

Finally, material handling costs enter the fray, and this can vary greatly depending on how a particular shop floor is arranged. A beam coming off the drill line might flow straight to the bolting station, but a crane might need to take that same beam to an adjacent welding station. In this case, the labor costs might be slightly higher in bolting, due to the number of general laborers involved, but the increased material handling might make welding less attractive.

So, which is really best, bolting or welding? It boils down to communication in the construction supply chain. Engineers in construction should at least have an idea of what the true strengths of local fab shops are. They can’t know everything, though, which is why Crosby supports the idea of fabricators themselves designing most of the connections on a given project.

The Fabricator Knows

An old rule of thumb says that material, fabrication, and erection each comprise roughly a third of the total cost of steel construction. Still, increasing costs in fabrication might lower erection costs to the point where overall costs are lower—not to mention overall risk. More shop welding and bolting means more tasks occur under roof, in a controllable environment.

Structural fabrication shouldn’t occur in a vacuum. Decisions about connections in the shop can influence the kind of connections required in the field. As Crosby cited, welded field connections can cost upward of 40% more than connections welded in the shop.

Moreover, field welding is, on average, 35% less efficient than shop welding. Crosby described a column welded connection that didn’t look out of the ordinary— except for the cage hanging off a nearby truss. That’s where the welder needed to perch to access the weld. In this situation, even the best field welder in the world would probably be less efficient than a shop welder working in the flat position—even if a structural fabricator happens to be less efficient with welding compared to bolted connections. In this case, would it make sense to shop-weld the connection and keep most field connections in the bolted variety? Unlike welding, bolting isn’t affected by rain and wind.

Of course, this depends on the local market and fabrication technology available. The cost difference between shop and field welding might not be as pronounced in certain rural areas. On the other hand, automated welding at a specific structural fabricator might lower cost and widen that difference between shop and field welding costs. Alternatively, the structure complexity might introduce joint access issues and, hence, require manual welding.

The variables keep coming. Here, Crosby reiterated his opening message to engineers in the construction supply chain: Design to standard, widely available materials and beam sizes; consider actual loads; and know that myriad variables affect connection design.

"It’s impossible to know everything," Crosby concluded. "But don’t worry about it. Let the fabricator design their own connections."
