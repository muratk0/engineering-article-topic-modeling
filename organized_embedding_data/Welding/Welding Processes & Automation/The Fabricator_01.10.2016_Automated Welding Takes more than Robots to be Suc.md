# Automated Welding Takes more than Robots to be Successful

[TARİH: 01.10.2016 The Fabricator]

A father and a daughter shatter the myth that robotic welding is only for huge fabrication operations

By Dan Davis

Figure 1 TnL Welding, Sidney, Ohio, has been in operation since 2000. It has proven that a small shop can not only run a robotic welding cell successfully, but that it also can base its business model primarily on automated welding.

A

re you familiar with fabricating operations in which the welders are told to stay in the booth and stick to burning wire? Todd Whitt sure is. That’s the reason he owns his own shop that specializes in robotic welding.

TnL Welding LLC, Sidney, Ohio, is in business because Whitt’s former managers at a job shop told him to mind his own business. The company was struggling to make its new welding robots work as advertised, and they did not appreciate input from a run-of-the-mill production welder like Whitt, who just happened to be taking night classes at Lima Technical College (now known as Rhodes State College).

"One of the classes I was involved in was a robotics class. I snatched that right up. I was all about that," Whitt said. "I came back to work, but they still wanted me back in the weld booth."

It wasn’t until the fab shop had burned through all the patience of its technical staff, who now deemed the robots "junk," according to Whitt, that they were willing to take input from a nontraditional source, like from someone laying down production welds in a booth. Whitt said he jumped at the chance.

Knowing that an effective welding cell is more than just about a robot, Whitt examined part designs and welding fixtures more closely. He said he wasn’t afraid to play around with power source settings to dial in the correct parameters. Soon the robots were welding as they originally were intended.

Whitt’s success wasn’t winning him friends in the shop. As he was pulling more work from the production booths for the robotic weld cell, the welders were getting upset. When Whitt asked about a title change from welder to robot technician, he was reminded that he was only a production welder with a temporary side gig of working on robots.

He soon put that awkward working environment in his rear-view mirror in the fall of 2001 and found a job as a robot technician for a large aluminum fabricator. He also took the first steps toward eventually running his own business.

"When I went to that other company, I had actually purchased my first robot. It was sitting in my buddy’s warehouse," Whitt recalled. "This robot was mounted on the floor, and the tables were mounted on the floor. I made a cell out of it. I put everything on a pad so I could move it wherever I went."

The Small Automated Welding Shop

Working at the aluminum fabricator only reaffirmed Whitt’s belief that he could make a go of it as a small fabricating operation that specialized in robotic welding (see

Figure 1

). His evenings were spent working on his own robot and doing some occasional work. His days were spent trying to work with the welding department that knew welding, but nothing about robotics, and the maintenance staff that was responsible for the robots, but didn’t know how to fine-tune the units to deliver optimized welding results.

What he did take note of at the aluminum fabricating company was a new power source attached to a robot that came over from a sister manufacturing facility. The OTC Daihen power source worked very well with the aluminum material, Whitt said, clearly outpacing the other welding unit in the company’s original cell.

With knowledge that other large manufacturing companies might be struggling to implement automated welding or simply wanted to forgo the expense of buying a robotic welding cell and paying a technician to run it, Whitt launched his own robotic welding shop. TnL Welding was borne.

It also came around at the right time.

"Overflow from other companies helped," Whitt said. "Everyone was working overtime. Companies were farming work out. I wanted to be a part of that."

After working with several welding power sources, Whitt returned to the unit that worked so well at his previous place of employment.

"The ones that gave me the greatest ease of total arc control were the OTC units," he said. "They have more built-in variables, and they don’t restrict you to the kind of gas, guns, or wire you can use. That’s important to me because what sets us apart from our competitors is our ability to find the right combination of ingredients for a specific application."

Running the Shop Today

TnL Welding has had as many as three other employees, as work has ramped up and slowed down over the years. Currently Whitt and his daughter Kristen run the shop. Kristen moved back to Sidney after going to school for automotive diesel mechanics and working in graphic design. The two drag raced and worked on cars during Kristen’s early years, so they weren’t strangers to sharing the same workspace.

"When I moved back, I didn’t know anything," she said. "I came into the business as an operator running the robots. I got into welding at the tables. Since then I’ve moved on to help out with other things, such as advertising and sales. I dabble with everything now."

Whitt said his daughter picks up the finer points of welding and robotic programming very quickly.

"I can show her one time that this is what it looks like, and she can then figure it out on her own," he said.

Today TnL Welding has seven OTC power sources, three of which are linked to robots. The units have the ability to store as many as 100 welding programs, which can make a definite impact on a customer looking for a quick turnaround on a job.

As an example, Whitt described a past job involving long aluminum parts for a transportation application. If the shop were given that chance to run the job again and provided the fixture, Kristen said she would be able to bolt up the fixture on the modular table in 20 minutes and start the job. In six hours she could run 200 pieces.

"We didn’t have to reteach it," Whitt said about the robot. He added that larger fabricators lean on his expertise because frankly they often don’t have it within their own ranks. This is especially evident when working with aluminum (see

Figure 2

).

Whitt said he knows that the robot is going to deliver a consistent weld in the designated place and with consistent heat input. From there he can determine just what wire to use and just how much heat should be applied to the metal to avoid distortion. Again, the combination of welding expertise and knowledge of how best to use the robots and welding technology helps the shop to stand out.

One of Whitt’s favorite examples of his shop’s capabilities is a job it used to do for a major recreational vehicle manufacturer in Ohio, which had very high standards for its metal components. TnL turned out 600 aluminum roof bows, each of which required 60 1-in. welds, in one shift with one robot and one operator.

"Some might think that’s a lot of production for a shop of our size, but once we programmed the robot and integrated the OTC Welbee P-400 power supply, we turned out consistently smooth welds with excellent penetration and no spatter problems," Whitt said (see

Figure 3

).

Looking Forward to the Future

TnL Welding is not limited to robotic welding. It has the capability to tackle some manual gas metal arc and gas tungsten arc welding of aluminum, mild steel, and steel alloys. It also has a CNC plasma cutting table that can be converted to a routing table for plastic, wood, and other nonmetal materials.

Robotic welding, however, is still the heart of the operation (see

Figure 4

). Kristen understands this. Actually, she really likes it.

"Manual welding in a production environment can be boring and tiring," she said. "When you are running a robot, you don’t have the boredom. Nine times out of 10, you have something to keep you occupied during the cycle. Meanwhile, that robot goes to the same spot every time and does the same thing every time."

Figure 4 Todd and Kristen Whitt stand in front of their largest robotic welding cell. Each of the two tables in the cell can hold up to 1,600 lbs. and workpieces as long as 9.5 ft.

The Whitts now are looking for other customers willing to recognize their automated welding expertise and to lean on them for production efficiency that they might otherwise find hard to duplicate on their own shop floor. Sometimes it can be tough as some customers decide to do their project in-house, but TnL Welding keeps pushing forward.

The robots don’t quit. The Whitts don’t plan to quit either.
