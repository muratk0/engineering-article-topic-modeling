# Going large with lean

[TARİH: 01.08.2025 The Fabricator]

Part Flow

Adapting lean to high-mix, large-workpiece production

By

Tim Heston

Fabricating especially large workpieces comes with unique considerations, especially at the constraint process, often in welding. If talented welders spend most of their time moving large pieces into position, a fabricator probably isn’t getting as much out of its welding talent as it could.

Inzyx/iStock /Getty Images Plus

W

hen you study lean manufacturing, theory of constraints, and other continuous improvement methods, you find a lot of insight on high-quantity production. Precision sheet metal fabricators often adapt lean to achieve optimal flow from blanking to bending and beyond. The idea is to have all subcomponents come together at just the right time in welding and assembly, with minimal piles of work-in-process (WIP) in between.

That’s easier said than done, but it’s still doable. Fabricators dynamically nest multiple jobs for laser cutting, stage jobs for forming, and assemble kits just before welding or assembly. Quick changeover in blanking and forming make this possible. WIP buffers remain, but the flow is far superior to traditional batch-style production, when shops ran batch sizes in the thousands. Massive batches increased WIP and, hence, lead times.

But what about large pieces like rolled tank shells or massive vessels? Do the same principles apply? At their core they do, yet the approach is different. That’s according to Blayde Weekley at Longview, Texas-based Fabrication Solutions & Technologies. The sales and operations manager conducts workflow assessments at various metal fabricators, including many industrial fab shops that process extremely large workpieces.

"It’s not a high-production part, but it’s still a part," Weekley said, "so the same principles apply. We’re trying to limit movement and limit the required inputs that operators need to make."

Eliminating Trapped Knowledge

Weekley clarified that "limiting the inputs" does not mean reducing required skill on the shop floor. Skill does, however, need to be teachable and not remain trapped in a veteran operator’s head.

"I’ve been in a shop where I watched an operator run plate through an old three-roll system, manipulating two different cranes and two different foot pedals. The guy looked like he was playing drums for the Red Hot Chili Peppers. And he was the only one who knew how to run that machine throughout the whole facility. It required a lot of manual inputs that took a lot of time. When he goes on vacation, they’re not rolling anything for two weeks."

This, he said, is one reason shops upgrade machines and rely on more automation. Greater productivity is the icing on the cake, but the real improvement comes from reducing the reliance on a specific operator’s "feel" of a homegrown process.

"We need skilled operators, but how do we reduce dependency on one individual? What if one skilled person can make or break an operation?

That’s not an employee. That’s a handcuff."

Weekley added that no one is to blame here. The situation just reflects the reality of a decades-long skilled worker shortage. The idea is to think about the best way to make the most out of the talented people a fabricator employs.

A Value Stream of Steps

Any metal fabrication operation boils down to a series of steps performed by people with various skills. The

fewer steps

a job requires as it travels down the value stream or job routing, and the

more repeatable

each step becomes, the better.

"If a job has 1,000 steps, and you make a mistake on just 10 of them, that’s pretty remarkable," Weekley said. "The trick is, how do you reduce the number of steps you need to make?"

Measuring each step requires getting extremely granular, zooming in on every operation. Every movement matters, from twisting to retrieve a tool to making keystrokes on computers and machine controllers. The greater number of steps you have, the less repeatable the operation becomes.

Consider again the operator working with that old plate roll. Every push of a pedal, manipulation of the crane, and measurement of the workpiece matters. The sequence is so complicated, in fact, that it’s extremely challenging to document and difficult to teach—hence the reason why historically most plate rolling operators have learned the trade only after years of experience. The operation could entail dozens, hundreds, even thousands of "micro-steps" that, to the skilled person, eventually become intuitive and natural. He just "rolls it" like magic.

"This is where autonomous roll bending is really starting to make a difference," Weekley said.

Certain rolls today can "learn" like an experienced operator. Someone inputs the job specs (including workpiece diameter) and material parameters, including grade and thickness. The system then begins rolling, detecting the radius and adjusting on the fly. The next time the machine runs that material, it "remembers" the adjustments it made and tweaks the roll settings to streamline and perfect the process. This reduces those manual operator inputs and, hence, reduces the number of steps. Again, the fewer steps a job has, the more repeatable it becomes.

The Right Steps in the Right Order

The sequence of steps matter, too, and the most "efficient" order isn’t always obvious. Consider an assembly department at a high-product-mix custom fabricator. A shop might have disparate job routings all leading to one welding and assembly operation. The shop controls its batch sizes and the level of WIP it stages before assembly.

Fork trucks can add dozens of steps to a manufacturing process, including plenty of waiting. In large-workpiece fabrication, however, manual carts usually aren’t a material handling option. This is where focusing on steps, or the number of operator inputs, comes into play.

WangAnQi/iStock / Getty Images Plus.

All the parts arrive, the assembler starts—and then a problem emerges. Two or three pieces don’t fit. This creates the worst kind of waste: mistakes caught at the very end of a value stream. Every previous value-adding step in the job routing is now wasted effort, and the rework that ensues adds more variability to the entire system. Such waste can easily go unnoticed. After all, machine uptime looks great, until you realize that some of that uptime is spent producing parts that were already produced.

Fabricators rectify this by analyzing what exactly a specific manufacturing step needs to be successful. Weekley described one tank fabricator that rolled two tank shells, each of which met up with the tank head in the assembly department. The flow seemed intuitive. The individual shells and heads were easier to transport through the routing. Having them all "meet up" in final assembly just made sense.

Problem was, the rolled shells sometimes didn’t fit up correctly with the heads. The head installation

required

proper rolling of the two shells to be successful. "If that’s the case, why not assemble the head [just after rolling] and send two shells and the tank head down the job routing as a single unit?"

This strategy also made all the steps between rolling and assembly easier. Before, everyone who worked on the rolled shells had to make sure they maintained certain tolerances so that the head could mate properly in assembly. But with the head attached directly after rolling, those at subsequent operations didn’t worry; the head was already secured. Transporting the single piece took a little more time, but it was a small price to pay. If a head didn’t align properly, they could send the shells back to the rolls or, at the very worst, scrap the shell. But at least that occurred earlier in the routing before extra value-adding steps took place.

And yes, ideally, the rolling operation should be more repeatable. But nothing is perfect. Material could be more consistent, and tolerances could be looser. Regardless, an optimal sequence ensures a fabricator avoids extensive rework, which is arguably the worst kind of waste in metal fabrication.

Wasted Talent

Consider a welding department in which the welders themselves move large workpieces, like flipping a tank to orient it—a time-consuming task in large-workpiece production. At the same time, welding remains the shop’s constraint process. Why then is the welder moving material?

"Flipping a large shell could take 45 minutes or more," Weekley said.

Wasted talent

occurs when someone with a specific skill spends time doing other tasks. Sometimes this can’t be avoided. A cutting machine operator might denest a skeleton just to keep parts on the move.

But the initial cutting usually isn’t the constraint process. (If it is, a fabricator runs into trouble, since a constraint in cutting starves operations downstream of work.) Besides, the cutting machine is likely somewhat automated, so the operator has time to help move blanks to the next operation.

Manual welding, though, is different. More often than not, welding is the constraint process, and the less time a welder spends welding, the less throughput the overall operation achieves.

Forming can also fit into this category. Bending or rolling might or might not be the constraint, but the precision of that forming operation is critical. Elongation in bending affects proper blank size in cutting upstream and ease of welding fit-up downstream. Forming experts add the most value when they’re, well, actually

forming

. If forming jobs are in queue and operators are busy with other work, those forming and bending experts might not be adding as much value as they could be. This is why operations further along in the lean manufacturing journey often designate water spiders to move and stage tools and material for constraint and other key manufacturing processes.

Weekley added that this doesn’t mean people performing a key process—be it welding, rolling, or anything else—should just sit idle to wait for tools and materials to be delivered. People should be able to do what they need to do (fetch material, tools, etc.) to finish the job and move it downstream. Moreover, the constraint process in high-mix manufacturing can move. Welders shouldn’t weld just to further clog a constraint in final assembly. Ideally, though, shops should ensure enough material remains staged in front of the manufacturing constraint so the welder (or assembler or grinder) can comfortably and safely maximize throughput.

Wasted Motion

Weekley described a heavy fabricator that seemed to have a lot of wasted motion—at least at first glance. A crane operator transported large blanks from the burn table to plate rolls at the other end of the shop. The setup looked incredibly inefficient, but it really wasn’t. Space was limited, and as it turned out, that long crane pick ended up being the most efficient way to move the job between workstations.

"Believe me," Weekley said, "when a heavy fab shop gets busy, it’s almost always fighting for space."

Fabricators strive to minimize work-in-process (WIP). In large-workpiece operations, excessive WIP can consume a tremendous amount of space.

Available space was such that if the plate rolls had been placed closer to the burn table, the large blanks would have needed to move in a dogleg—requiring more inputs (button pushes) from the crane operator. Moving the cut blanks to the far side of the shop allowed the workpieces to move in a straight line, free of pedestrian traffic, with no stops to change direction.

You can’t change physics. Every time a crane operator stops, he or she needs to wait to ensure the load is stable and not swaying uncontrollably. To do this efficiently requires experience and finesse. Modern crane technology can help, and yes, a great crane operator could likely make the move very efficiently, but does every crane operator the shop employs share that level of skill? In this case, moving in a (long) straight line required less operator input, so it was far more repeatable. No matter who operated the crane, the transportation time remained about the same.

Weekley clarified that placing the burn table near the forming machines would have been ideal—if the crane move could remain in a straight line. Available floor space simply didn’t make this possible. And in this case, repeatability trumped speed. In large-workpiece fabrication, a mistakenly dropped plate takes time to recover from, and the cost of scrap and rework can be immense. An operation that focuses just on the distance between workstations is one that fails to see the forest for the trees.

Weekley added that similar examples abound, and many aren’t obvious. Casual observation isn’t enough. You need to pay attention to every operator input. In large-workpiece fabrication, the number of operator inputs usually matters more than the distance a person needs to walk. "Say you have a crane move that takes 21 button pushes to get from Point A to Point B. How can we get those down to fewer than seven? If we can, it will take only a third of the time to move it.

"When analyzing all this, I imagine myself as the part," Weekley continued. "In fact, I write a numbered list that starts with ‘I am the part.’"

Breaking out the operation this way gives a wider perspective, with the spotlight both on

worker movement

as well as

workpiece movement

. Weekley then gave the following example.

I am a half-pipe coil jacket. I call a forklift operator.

I wait for the forklift operator to get on the fork truck.

I wait for the forklift operator to check his surroundings before moving.

And so on. "I literally break this down to every movement. This all might take 300 steps or more, but it allows me to really see how much movement is being done. I then classify each move."

Categories include (among others) waiting, wasted motion, wasted talent, and overproduction. In heavy fab, "overproduction" usually isn’t producing too many of a single product (again, we’re talking thousand-pound parts here), but there can be redundant or otherwise unnecessary steps.

Categorizing helps prioritize each step and identify the big wins, especially in regard to wasted talent.

Why is the welder flipping the shell? Can someone else flip and stage the material? Even better, can we eliminate the need for flipping at the welding station by changing something upstream?

Weekley described a recent project involving a seemingly simple move of a half-pipe coil jacket. In truth, the process breakdown proved that the move took 45 steps. "We were able to eliminate 31 of those steps," he said, adding that savings came both from efficient movement and (even more important) greater

simplicity

. Again, the fewer steps any action takes, the fewer operator inputs it requires, which makes that action more repeatable.

Breaking down the steps reveals all sorts of low-hanging fruit. In one instance, an analysis showed work being transported from a welding station to a beveling machine, then back to welding. This required a fork truck that wasn’t always available immediately. The work spent a lot of time waiting.

Traditional approaches to lean manufacturing would have advocated creating a cell, with the beveler right next to the welding station. But this is high-mix, large-workpiece manufacturing. Manual carts aren’t a safe option. Workpiece size, shape, and quantities vary, and investing in a custom material handling device didn’t make business sense. Besides, the beveling machine is a shared resource, used for other products that flow through different bays.

Turns out, there was a simpler solution. The fabricator placed the beveling machine in an adjacent bay. This was still several dozen feet away, but in this position, employees could transport the work via an underutilized skyhook.

This exemplifies how the approach to lean manufacturing changes when workpiece size grows. A spaghetti diagram (a lean tool Weekley also uses) must be interpreted correctly and put in the right context. Distance matters, but the number of steps—button pushes, stopping the crane and waiting for the work to stop swaying, waiting for fork truck drivers, etc.—matters more.

The Culture Change

Weekley conceded that, when it comes to lean and other improvement methods, job security fears still pervade the industry. "We hear it a lot. People say, ‘You’re trying to take our job.’ We respond by saying, ‘We need you, now more than ever, because we need to be able to expand with the same workforce we have now. We can’t find new workers.’"

Improvement helps a fabricator grow the pie (sales) without adding resources, and it helps make the most out of new resources a shop does add. Weekley emphasized the need for technology. Properly maintained (and, hence, always available to produce) automation helps reduce the number of operator inputs. That makes everything more repeatable and a fabricator more competitive overall. But to get the most out of new technology, fabricators need to look at the big picture—including the hundreds, even thousands of steps that occur between the receiving and shipping dock.
