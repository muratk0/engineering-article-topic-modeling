# Educating, rather than just training, tomorrow’s workforce

[TARİH: 01.06.2024 The Fabricator]

Editor’s Corner

A traditional approach to vocational skills still holds up

Dan Davis

W

hile speaking with John Henderson of ESAB Welding & Cutting Products for this issue’s overview on oxyfuel cutting, I recounted a story of my first experience with an oxyfuel flame. It was at a "Welding for the Non-Welder" course at the Hobart Institute of Welding Technology in Troy, Ohio, in 2008. It was quite the education.

The first hands-on learning opportunity of the four-day course focused on oxyfuel welding. Never having done any sort of welding until that point, I had no idea what to expect. With 40 years of limited success in sports that value hand-eye coordination, I should have had a better idea of what to expect.

Let’s just say that it didn’t start off well. But with each subsequent coupon, I got a little better. I understood why people like to weld: Creating a bond that’s stronger than the source material is powerful.

Henderson commented that starting with oxyfuel as part of a welding education makes a lot of sense.

"Instructors will tell you the reason that they teach them oxyfuel welding is twofold. One, it’s a great technology to understand. It’s so versatile. Two, instructors know that a little bit further down the road, a student is going to learn the TIG application, and the muscle memory and the technique is identical to what you’re doing with oxyfuel."

As he described that scenario, I recalled how much easier I found gas tungsten arc welding (GTAW) during my introductory welding week. I got stuck with stick welding, and the gas metal arc welding (GMAW) process was frustrating. The GTAW process, on the other hand, went kind of smoothly, probably because it wasn’t totally foreign.

The reality, however, is that some welders don’t really have that introduction to oxyfuel welding. Maybe they are learning on the job or are part of an expedited program that focuses on GMAW. They don’t get the full welding process overview.

The focus on skills training for many companies is understandable. If they have a critical shortterm need, providing an employee with the practical skills necessary to take on a job in the most expedient way possible is the way to go.

Educating an employee, providing them with theoretical and historical knowledge, is more of a long-term play. It’s also a smart play for today’s demanding workforce.

Steve Benson, the owner of ASMA LLC and the lead instructor for FMA’s Precision Press Brake Certificate program, has been preaching the gospel on teaching operators the science behind forming for years. He argues that it’s worth it because they become more valuable employees. They can work that old brake and still make it somewhat productive. They can engage with programmers in the front office to let them know why a certain part can’t be bent on any of the press brakes on the shop floor.

"Why do some good employees stay over the long term? It’s often because they have been allowed to learn and apply their knowledge. When boredom pervades and interest in the trade fades, your best and brightest might start to walk away," warned Benson in a 2021 Bending Basics column.

Educating metal fabricating workers about their chosen field of work validates their career paths. Pedal pushers and button punchers might have been a fit for yesterday’s manufacturing environment, but for modern metal fabricating to be successful, even with all of its advanced machinery and software, it needs thoughtful contributors who can solve problems quickly and communicate with co-workers and customers.

A welder probably doesn’t need to know oxyfuel welding to be effective in a modern metal fabricating company, but he or she might be a more flexible employee. Think about instances of a power outage or a remote job where power access is not available; with the help of an oxyfuel kit, a welder can get right to work.

Educating tomorrow’s metal fabricators and welders is an investment in something that the U.S., frankly, turned its back on for the past 30 years. As parents looked to prepare their children for what they thought was going to be a brighter future and sent them off to college, local school districts dismantled their vocational programs in the latter part of the 20th century. It’s only recently that the tide seems to be turning, and school districts are investing in wood shops, machine shops, and welding booths. Parents and their children are questioning the price for a four-year college degree and seeing manufacturing careers as a smart move.

Educating metal fabricating workers about their chosen field of work validates their career paths. Pedal pushers and button punchers might have been a fit for yesterday’s manufacturing environment, but for modern metal fabricating to be successful, even with all of its advanced machinery and software, it needs thoughtful contributors who can solve problems quickly and communicate with co-workers and customers.

Knowledge of oxyfuel welding isn’t a sign that an employee is going to be successful. But the desire to learn about oxyfuel welding, where it makes sense nowadays and how it might be connected to modern metal fabricating, shows an intellectual curiosity that signals a real interest in learning. That’s typically a good sign for any new hire.
