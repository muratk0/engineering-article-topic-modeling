# A new start for the Hendrick Motorsports chassis team

[TARİH: 01.04.2025 The Fabricator]

Early returns on transition to contract manufacturing look good

By

Dan Davis

Hendrick Motorsports made a dramatic pivot from fabricating race car chassis to high-volume fabricating of chassis for troop transport vehicles used by the U.S. Army’s Airborne Divisions.

T

he world of motorsports hardly seems to have anything in common with contract manufacturing, but if there’s one thing that the skilled welders and fabricators working for a race team both know, it’s flexibility. That’s the one thing that keeps the vehicles on the track and helps to generate improvements in lap times. It also happens to be the one thing that’s enabled Hendrick Motorsports to replicate its winning ways on the track in the competitive world of defense-related contract manufacturing.

Hendrick Motorsports, founded by racing and automotive legend Rick Hendrick in 1984, is the winningest team in NASCAR Cup Series history, having captured more than 300 wins. Former drivers include famous names like Jeff Gordon, Jimmie Johnson, Darrell Waltrip, and Dale Earnhardt Jr.

One of the buildings at the team’s campus in Concord, N.C., was once used for fabricating chassis for Hendrick Motorsports’ NASCAR Cup Series entries. The 28,000-sq.-ft. facility could get a little crazy during racing season as crew chiefs from the four different NASCAR teams would have varied instructions for the nine welders and fabricators to tweak existing frames. "We need to try something different on the wheel base." "We want to camber the rear end a little different." "We need some new fixtures." The four teams had a variety of needs, but they had the same schedule: ASAP.

That all changed for the Hendrick Motorsports chassis-building team as the 2022 NASCAR race season approached. As part of its continuing push to encourage competitive racing and ensure driver safety, NASCAR sought to have one source provide all the chassis for the racing circuit’s teams.

The chassis crew still had work lined up for the Xfinity Series, the racing circuit just below the NASCAR Cup Series, as it looked forward to the 2022 season, but it was scaled back compared to the larger NASCAR commitment. That wasn’t going to keep the team busy enough through the year. Hendrick Motorsports needed a new focus if the welding and fabricating team were to stay together.

A New Focus on Defense

Fortunately, longtime partnerships can be the source of near-term opportunities. Around that time in 2021, GM Defense, a subsidiary of General Motors, was looking for assistance as it ramped up efforts to build the Infantry Squad Vehicle (ISV), a troop transport vehicle used by the U.S. Army’s Airborne Divisions. These lightweight ISVs are based on GM’s Chevrolet Colorado ZR2, with one of the main differences being the chrome-moly steel exoskeleton that acts as the vehicle’s rollover protection system.

Hendrick, or Mr. H as he’s known around the campus, has a long history with GM. In 1976, he purchased his first dealership in Bennettsville, S.C., becoming the youngest Chevrolet dealer in the U.S. Today, Hendrick Automotive Group has more than 100 franchises and 10,000 employees across 13 states. His race team also has been aligned with Chevrolet since the very beginning.

It made sense for GM Defense to reach out to Hendrick Motorsports, given the relationship and the chassis fabricating expertise. Early consultations eventually evolved into an effort to win the bid, and in 2022, Hendrick Motorsports accomplished its goal. It was no longer just building vehicle frames to protect drivers; it now was charged with building vehicles that would protect U.S. soldiers.

Today, this defense work is done under the umbrella of Hendrick Motorsports Technical Solutions (HMTS), a stand-alone advanced manufacturing company on the Hendrick campus in Concord, N.C. The manufacturing team moved into its new headquarters at the beginning of this year.

Using a workflow that was similar to the one used to make race car chassis, the welders work on smaller parts that are then grouped and placed on carts that then move to chassis assembly.

"The work is similar," said Chris Spaulding, HMTS production manager. "The vehicle that we’re building has a roll cage, and the race cars had a roll cage. It’s very similar tubing. We do use a different welding process than we did on the race cars for the cages, however."

It’s also much higher volume. Spaulding said that when the focus was on race car chassis, welders might be asked to build one per week. When they were completing three chassis per week, they thought they were doing really well, but the requests for more kept coming. They spent most of 2023 trying (successfully) to keep up with the demand. And now, having expanded to slightly more than 140 employees (adding about 50 in 2024 alone) and moved from the tight confines of the 28,000-sq.-ft. shop to a new 160,000-sq.-ft. facility, the HMTS team has made the successful changeover to a defense contractor.

New Welding Processes

The tubing used on the ISV is specified for its strength-to-weight ratio. The roll cages have to be strong enough to protect the passengers in the vehicle if a rollover occurs, but the ISV also has to be light enough to allow it to be air-lifted into a combat area.

With the new material came a new challenge for the HMTS welders. They needed to evolve from using gas metal arc welding (GMAW, or MIG) on steel DOM tubing to making gas tungsten arc welding (GTAW, or TIG) their main focus. The military specification provided no flexibility on what welding process to use, regardless of what made the most sense from the contractor’s position.

The transition might be seen as a major obstacle for a company looking to scale in a hurry, but HMTS has the advantage of being in the cradle of auto racing, home to a lot of talented welders and fabricators, and standing out as one of the preferred employers. Hendrick has a reputation for taking care of his employees, something that is well known outside the Concord campus.

"As teams have gone through these types of transitions with the new [NASCAR] cars, there’s been kind of a change in the skill set needed to complete and build those cars. The area now had a lot of experienced welders that weren’t really needed anymore," said Andy Weyenberg, who is involved with motorsports marketing at Miller Electric Mfg. Co. and is host of the YouTube series "Real Garage with Andy."

With talented welders joining the cause, HMTS had to provide these craftsmen with the right tools. The team decided to standardize on the Miller Dynasty 300 as its TIG workhorse.

The power sources gave the welders enough flexibility to tackle thick and thin materials. While lighter-duty machines might have sufficed for some of the work being done in the shop, HMTS didn’t want to be taxing these types of machines for long periods of time. The Dynasty 300s gave the team peace of mind that the machines would be able to stand up to whatever was needed during the ISV production process.

"In these applications with high-strength, low-alloy steels, they determined that pulsing was the best way to go," Weyenberg said. "The pulse parameters of the Dynasty are quite expanded. They go up pretty high." Weyenberg said that the Dynasty offers up to almost three times the pulses per second compared to some of the other TIG machines on the market. That’s one of the reasons why the Dynasty series is often the choice for aerospace manufacturers as well.

"This is equipment for people that know what they’re doing," he said.

The standardization also helps with servicing the equipment. Because all of the 65 Dynasty power sources are of similar technology and age, HMTS only requires one type of technician. They don’t need an expert with knowledge of three or four different welding machines.

HMTS also maintains about a dozen Multimatic 215 multiprocess welding power sources, primarily for general MIG welding. Weyenberg said that the team’s main applications for the pulsed MIG process are the longer frame runs, which are performed by Millermatic 255 machines.

The MIG machines have digital meters, which help get the welders to adhere to weld procedure specifications. Following the job specs is mandatory when doing this type of defense work.

New Working Relationships

Change was a normal part of fabricating race car chassis. If a crew chief wanted a tweak to a chassis, the team was on it.

With defense work, change happens slowly. Welders who used to roll up their sleeves and jump at change requests now must follow a process filled with documented suggestions and attend meetings to determine the validity of the change requests. Any approved changes won’t happen for a week at the earliest.

"We’re not used to being patient," said lead welder Kenton Freshcorn. "We’re not very patient people."

The lack of change, arguably, has been one of the biggest changes for HMTS. Everything is specified in terms of welding process and parameters. Everything also is documented.

"It’s similar standards [to the race car industry], but it’s a lot more documented," Spaulding said. For example, the Hendrick Motorsports team has to be able to trace any material that is part of these military vehicles back to the mill. That simply wasn’t a concern when building race car chassis.

HMTS now is actively involved in production part approval processes in which the job specifications are spelled out, agreed upon, and carefully monitored during manufacturing. This involves inspecting parts before, during, and after a production run.

To assist with this new quality effort, the crew has added three certified welding inspectors to the roster. These qualified eyes pay close attention to the results of many weld assessments, including etch and bend tests. They also help to certify all welders to certain joints and processes. There’s no room for ambiguity in this type of work.

New Workflow

If the HMTS team has perfected anything, it’s fabricating, welding, and assembling a chassis in a small space without the need of a lift truck. In its old confines, where space was at a premium, materials and chassis were moved on carts from one station to another. The lift truck often was outside the shop, used primarily for moving raw material off delivery trucks.

The race car chassis were built by fabricating subassemblies and bringing them together on carts for final assembly. Front and rear "clips," as the front and rear ends of the vehicle are described, were fabricated in separate areas, then brought to join the center section, which contained the roll cage.

This assembly method not only made sense from a material movement standpoint, but it also helped with welding. The heat input incurred during the welding of these smaller subassemblies leads to much less warpage than if welding were done on a large chassis assembly. Tolerances could be held much more effectively by bringing the subassemblies together, according to Weyenberg.

When Hendrick Motorsports began doing the defense work, the definition of high volume changed pretty quickly. Initial volumes called for the team to triple its output.

"At the time, we thought high volume was three chassis per week," Spaulding said.

Relying on similar workflows, the team went from needing about 16 hours for a complete build to requiring six hours at the most for one. Spaulding said the production improvements were especially noteworthy because the team wasn’t working in any sort of linear format; the production flow was more like a cobweb, given the tight confines.

Like a race car, the ISV chassis is put together using the carts. Larger subassemblies, like the rear bumper, are fabricated and welded and then sent to the main assembly area, where everything is brought together. This also goes for smaller carts that contain small subassemblies that also are affixed to the chassis when final assembly takes place.

"We try to keep things rolling by having carts of subassembly parts ready to go. It helps us to adapt if people are taking PTO or sick [time]," Spaulding said.

The new 160,000-sq.-ft. facility has changed the game for HMTS. The material and production flow is much more direct. The days of serpentine routes through the shop are gone.

Raw material is delivered at one end of the building and sent to blanking, where laser cutting machines cut the parts to size. (The team used to go to another facility for laser-cut parts and then delivered the parts back to chassis assembly.) From there, the blanked parts go to forming and then sanding before making their way to the parts room, where they are organized on carts. Those kits are then wheeled over to the welders who fabricate the different subassemblies that ultimately make their way to the final build.

New Horizons

Even with the expected improvements in production flow and output, HMTS is still committed to looking for efficiencies. Given workers’ backgrounds in the racing industry, they might be addicted to the constant push to shave time off chassis builds.

Automation is definitely an area of interest for the team. The welders currently are using a Miller Copilot cobot to make fender parts for the ISV chassis. As the team becomes more experienced with the new workflow, HMTS likely will incorporate more automation into the production process.

Ross Fleischmann, marketing manager, Miller’s Automation group, said he has seen plenty of shops make the transition from basically a manual operation to one using more robotic welding cells and cobots. He thinks HMTS is in a particularly good place to gracefully make a similar type of transition.

"It’s obvious that Hendrick really values the welding talent that it has underneath [its] roof," Fleischmann said. "Some organizations just look at the numbers when it comes to making these types of transitions. I think that’s a mistake.

"You need to keep the welding knowledge and talent if you want to be good. That’s what makes for a good welding company."

The HMTS chassis team knows what it takes to protect drivers. The mission remains the same, even if the drivers are wearing camouflage instead of racing uniforms, and delivering the best chassis requires the best welders.
