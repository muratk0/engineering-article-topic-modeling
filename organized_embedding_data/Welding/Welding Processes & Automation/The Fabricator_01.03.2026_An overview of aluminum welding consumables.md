Some of the main drivers behind this shift are the material’s easy machinability, its corrosion resistance that comes from the naturally forming surface oxide, and the lightweight nature of the material.

Technologies such as computer-aided design also are helping drive adoption, as engineers and architects can easily model parts with complex geometries to better use the base metal’s strength.

THE FABRICATION WORLD

With this shift in base metal alloys comes new considerations for the welding and fabrication community. Most welders get their experience welding on regular carbon steel, so the thought of welding aluminum may seem daunting—the equipment, weld preparation, technique, and filler metal are all different. Today, we will focus on the filler metal, as it is often overlooked.

Regular carbon steel welding is dominated by the standard ER70S wire. Since it’s the primary option, filler metal selection is simple. When transitioning to welding on aluminum and its alloys, however, choosing a filler metal is not as straightforward. It requires a more intentional filler selection approach than steel.

This intentional approach involves determining two fundamental elements: the filler alloy needed and the appropriate size filler metal for the process and application.

ALUMINUM FILLER METAL SELECTION

Choosing the wrong filler metal alloy could lead to issues such as cracking during solidification of the weld pool; parts failing in service due to insufficient strength; or even issues in postweld processing, such as anodizing. So selecting the one that best matches your application is key.

AWS A5.10 requires filler metals to be tested for quality assurance and identified so they can be distinguished from one another. The Aluminum Association and reputable manufacturers, such as Hobart, publish aluminum filler metal selection charts to help welders make informed decisions.

Using the header and the left column of a selection chart, cross reference to the associated square. The square indicates which alloys are compatible without cracking, but it also requires you to consider other factors:

Strength in the as-welded condition

Ductility of the weld metal if forming or bending operations are to be completed downstream

Corrosion resistance in fresh- and saltwater environments

Reaction of the material at sustained elevated temperatures of 150 to 350 degrees F

Color matching of parts that will be anodized

Ability of the material to respond to postweld heat treatments

Toughness, or the ability to bend without cracks propagating

For example, when welding a common billet alloy such as 6061, you may choose a 4943 filler metal because of the higher resistance to hot cracking. However, if you plan on anodizing the part, this would not be a good choice. The 4xxx series alloys will not color-match when anodizing, so in this case, the 5356 would be a better choice. Simply stated, selecting the correct alloy is a balance of crack resistance, strength, and downstream finishing needs.

FILLER METAL SIZING

Once you know the desired alloy, you can determine which diameter you need. While the process of picking the diameter is slightly less scientific than picking the alloy, it can play a critical role in the success of the process. Choosing the correct diameter is essential for weld quality, ease of operation, and overall process consistency.

If you are using a wire feed process such as gas metal arc welding (GMAW, or MIG), you will be making a slightly different choice than with gas tungsten arc welding (GTAW, or TIG) and hand-held laser welding.

MIG

. In this process, the electricity flows through the filler wire and generates an arc between the wire and the part. Eventually enough heat is generated to melt the wire, which then is deposited on the part being welded.

Smaller wires such as 0.035 in. take less heat to melt, so they are commonly used for thin applications. Larger-diameter wires such as 3/64 or 1/16 in. require a significantly higher amount of energy to melt. As a result, they are found more commonly in applications that require significant weld deposition on parts with sufficient mass to dissipate the heat without blowing through or losing control of the weld puddle.

TIG

. With the TIG process, heat is generated separately from the filler metal. The torch is used to establish a puddle on the base metal before the addition of filler metal. The filler metal then is melted with the puddle— not the arc. This deposition not only cools the weld puddle, but it also creates the commonly desired "stack of dimes" appearance.

Using a filler metal that’s too large in diameter causes the puddle to close completely, while a filler metal that is too small results in insufficient cooling of the weld puddle, making it difficult to control the process. A good rule of thumb is to choose a filler metal that is slightly smaller in diameter than the thickness of the base metal and never larger than the base metal thickness. For example, on 1/8-in. aluminum sheet, you might opt for a 3/32-in. diameter filler rod.

Hand-Held Laser

. This welding process is similar to TIG in that the wire is absorbing heat from the weld puddle and will limit the weld penetration as the filler diameter increases. However, with a larger diameter, you can expect better feed characteristics, more tactile feedback in the travel speed control, and an improved ability to bridge poor part fitup. Most installations use 1/16-in.-dia. wire—with the process generally requiring gaps no larger than the diameter of the wire. Armed with the proper knowledge about filler metal selection, you can weld aluminum and aluminum alloys confidently and mitigate fears of running into problems with the metal’s mechanical properties and weldability.
