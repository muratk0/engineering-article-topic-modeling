Wisconsin teacher publishes welding picture book

Rafael Guerrero

P

icture books capture the attention of children, taking them to places they never considered, to meet new people and creatures and to see things they’ve never seen.

Joseph Kluge, a welding instructor from Janesville, Wis., asked himself this question: Shouldn’t there be a children’s picture book on welding that does all of that?

"I saw a lot of kids books out there" about other professions and hobbies, like drag racing, Kluge said. "And I thought, ‘Wow, that’s a way that you can really start getting to kids.’

"It was just a cool idea, I thought. So, I started looking for a welding one, but I couldn’t find anything," he added.

Late last year, Kluge took a crack at answering that question when he published his first book, "My First Welding Book." The 30-page work follows Miguel, Rosie, and Sparkz the dog as they learn about welding jobs and opportunities in the world, primarily through colorful illustrations.

On an Adventure

Flip through the book, and you’ll see the three characters interacting with welders at work. In one example, the three visit the ocean and an underwater welder. And in another, they visit a metal artist and their sculpture.

The illustrations—drawn by Kluge’s daughter, Kyla—dominate each page.

"The cool part is it doesn’t have a ton of words in it," Kluge said of his choice to keep the words to a minimum. "What it’s meant to do is open up [discussions between children and parents]; you see someone welding underwater and hopefully you get a conversation going … it’s hard to create a story when the story is all laid out."

Kluge is a high school and technical college welding instructor in southern Wisconsin.

Kluge echoes this sentiment in the book’s description, which can be found on the book’s Amazon and BookBaby pages.

"The book is a means to get kids interested in the field of welding and have some fun along the way. It can create stories with them and hopefully inspire them to dream big," the description reads. "They can see what it is like to work as a welder in a career and show them all the cool things that welders get to work on every day."

As a child, Kluge didn’t like reading. The PBS show "Reading Rainbow" helped motivate him to do so, he said. The show’s approach to engaging children and getting them to read stuck with him all these years later as he brainstormed his first picture book.

"What if I did one of these that could have been on this show? This idea kind of generated from that," he said.

It Sells Itself

In the past, Kluge worked in production, job shops, and as a pipe welder, and he has earned CWI/CWE certifications—a humbling experience that taught him the value of welding education.

Nowadays, he is a welding instructor at Blackhawk Technical College in Janesville and a technology education teacher at Beloit Memorial High School in neighboring Beloit, Wis.

Kluge, 45, is no stranger to advocating for the welding profession. Teaching has given him a chance to give teenagers and others more exposure to welding and to share with them why he first fell for welding more than two decades ago.

"When I was growing up, trades were not nearly as popular or as pushed as they are today," he said. "It speaks to our work; we still need to push that aspect further."

So far, so good for Kluge. This school year, he teaches about 220 students at the high school. And for the first time, there is a waiting list to get into the welding classes.

"I’m not out trying to sell it to anybody, I think it sells itself," he said about the popularity of the school’s welding classes.

Ideas in the Works

"My First Welding Book" has received positive reviews since it was published in November 2023, Kluge said.

And with one published title under his belt, Kluge has ideas for more.

He’d like to take this concept to an older audience—middle school, perhaps. After all, "My First Welding Book" has a targeted audience of 12 and under; preteens and teens might not find a picture book appealing.

"We can start talking more about machines, techniques, things like that, but make it more understandable," Kluge said.

"I’m not sure how exactly that will lay out … I’ve got a few ideas in the works."
