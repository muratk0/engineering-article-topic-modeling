# Welder Underground expands mission to train young people

State grant gives organization boost, ability to train more interns

By

Lincoln Brunner

O

cean White entered the welding world through the back door—a door behind the back door, in fact.

But that’s probably why the dancer, yoga instructor, and now welder fits in so nicely at Welder Underground: It’s a beacon for aspiring welders with no experience but plenty of the chutzpah that drove White to expand herself beyond a career in fire dancing and circus performance.

NOTICING THE SKILLS

White came to New York to work in modern contemporary dance and found her way to the circus scene about 10 years ago. As a solo dance artist, she discovered fire dancing, a specialty that naturally involves a lot of pyrotechnics—notably metal costume and sculptural elements that light on fire.

"That’s what brought me to welding," said White, 34, who still performs in circuses on weekends. "As I’ve been traveling on stages, I’ve just been seeing the big sculpture art and what you can do with pyrotechnics. And I would talk to the pyros at festivals, and they were all welders, they were all metalworkers, they were all sculptors.

"So, I’m like, ‘OK, that’s the start. If I want to get into bigger pyrotechnics, that’s where I start—I learn to weld, and I learn metalworking.’ So, that’s what got me super passionate about wanting to weld."

A knee injury finally convinced her to make a move. So, wondering how she could learn welding inexpensively while working multiple jobs, White browsed the internet for possibilities and found Welder Underground’s Instagram page through a friend. She applied for, and landed, an internship with the organization’s first-ever group of interns (or cohort, as the company calls them) about a year and a half ago.

Ocean White performs a TIG weld on a bus window frame at Welder Underground’s shop in Brooklyn, N.Y.

Welder Underground

Now that White has completed that internship, Welder Underground has hired her as a full-time welding apprentice mentor—a sort of intermediary between the instructors and the 12 new apprentices that just started the latest cohort in March.

"It was a very quick and excited ‘yes,’" White recalled about taking the job offer. "I’m someone with fresh eyes, fresh out of the program. I really look forward to learning from these instructors that we have and learning from the apprentices coming in—just making sure if there’s anyone falling behind, I’m there to help them. If there’s anyone a little ahead of the game, I’m there to challenge them.

"Teaching is my favorite way to learn," she added. "I’m working on honing my skills and also teaching whatever I know."

EXPANSION PLANS GET FUNDING

In February, Welder Underground was awarded a $740,000 grant from The New York Power Authority—the entire amount it applied for. Welder Underground founder Marc Levin said that while the grant doesn’t pay all the freight, it does allow the organization to add two more apprenticeship cohorts, the first one 12 students (which White will help lead) and the second 14.

Of course, the ostensible point of the fourmonth program is to teach stick and TIG welding skills through instruction, practice, and job shadowing—skills that make graduates attractive hires for New York-area employers like Con Edison, NineDot Energy, Haugland, and various trade unions. But the thrust of the course is a 10-point plan that teaches interns (who are paid $600 a week) how to earn and keep employers’ respect—things like showing up for work half an hour early so they’re ready to go when the shift begins.

It’s all added up to 100% job placement thus far for the program’s 16 graduates—a stat about which Levin is smilingly proud.

When she’s not welding or showing new interns how to do it, Welder Underground graduate Ocean White still finds time to perform in fire dancing—the pursuit that led her to welding in the first place.

Welder Underground

"They’ve got jobs. They’re doing precisely what they had in mind to do when they walked into Welder Underground," Levin said. "None of our apprentices have any experience coming in. We provide subway cards, work boots, all the PPE, welding hoods. We provide everything—all we ask of them is to buy their own tape measures and put their initials on them because we want them to carry that tape measure around at all times. It’s a basic tenet—if you can’t measure, you can’t work."

Levin noted that whether it’s a job in the clean energy sector that this latest grant supports or with another employer, interns coming out of Welder Underground have the basic skills to thrive anywhere.

"Nobody’s coming out of this program after four months as a journeyman welder," Levin joked. "But they’re going to be trained to be a really good assistant. They’re all really good welders when they leave.

"We see a huge need for welders, which is across the board. Everybody’s already identified that there’s this massive need. We want to give young people who don’t typically have these opportunities, who can’t afford these opportunities, whose parents can’t afford these opportunities, a really good foundation to get in the trade and have family-sustaining careers. We think it’s achievable, and this is how we do it."
