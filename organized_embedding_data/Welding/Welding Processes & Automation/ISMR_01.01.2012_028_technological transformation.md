The company manufactures a range of equipment for partner companies and international clients, including deck equipment, rescue equipment, cranes and oil and gas supply systems for gas turbine power plants, along with their hydraulic systems and switching stations. Some 90% of the components are welded, which calls for reliability, cost-effectiveness and quality.

Hische has found it much easier to meet these criteria since it introduced the latest inverter welding power sources from welding technology company, EWM Hightec Welding of Mündersbach. The innovative coldArc® and forceArc® joining processes played a key role. The forceArc joining process has even enabled the company to make a competitive entry into the large crane industry.

## Efficiency leads to increased reliability

The technological transformation at Hische got underway when the company's welding engineer and quality officer, Ralf Nitsche, began to look for more effective ways to weld the numerous circumferential seams in pipe components for gas turbine power plants, including filter systems, measuring units and pressure regulating stations (Fig. 2).

The parent metals are steels that meet ASTM (American Society for Testing and Materials) specifications e.g. in accordance with EN P235GH-TC1 (DIN ST 35.8), (ASTM, A 369) or low-temperature tough steels for cryogenics. The pipe diameters range from DN200 (wall thickness 8.2 millimetres) to DN800 (wall thickness up to 22 millimetres). See Fig. 3 overleaf.

All components must meet stringent safety requirements so monitoring is strict – each individual weld undergoes an X-ray inspection.

"For a weld to be reliable, it needs to be 100% perfect," said Ralf Nitsche. "In the past, we used tungsten inert gas (TIG) welding processes to achieve reliable weld seams. But, for the last two years, we have been using the coldArc cold short arc MIG/MAG process to weld the weld root more effectively – with amazing results. Two highly dynamic Phoenix 451 inverter power sources are used in the welding process." (Note: since the latest range of EWM equipment was introduced, the alpha Q welding machine has been equipped with coldArc joining technology.)

The welding engineer continues: "This process allows us to achieve renowned TIG quality at MAG speed, meaning we weld the root almost 40% faster."

## A fruitful partnership

Fig. 5: Successful team: management board member Fritz Hische (centre) and welding engineer Ralf Nitsche (left) received strong support from Harald Teping (right) Photo: EWM

The competent support provided by Harald Teping, technical advisor at EWM specialist dealer P+M Schweisstechnik GmbH of Holdorf, had a major bearing on Hische's decision to switch to the new power sources (Fig. 4).

A good partnership is always fruitful, and this fact was underlined when both companies worked together to develop more applications using the Phoenix power source. Other properties are required for the steel components of deck appliances, crane systems, winches, davits (Fig. 5) freefall rescue systems for cargo vessels and cruise ships, yachts and heavy-duty naval cranes in particular. These properties include precise, targeted arcs; safe, deep penetration; good sidewall fusion, no lack of fusion, and spatter-free welding – properties like those offered by the Phoenix and forceArc process.

The forceArc joining process has even enabled the company to make a competitive entry into the large crane industry.

Nitsche regards this process as suitable for welding the box sections and columns of his crane booms. "We tested the characteristics of the powerful arc. The results were impressive right from the start. It's the ideal welding process for our materials, which range from sheets of S355JR structural steel of between 5 and 40 millimetres thickness to high tensile, finegrained S690QL1 steel."

The company is now using two more Phoenix power sources (Type 521) for these components.

## An eye on economy

Ralf Nitsche thinks the technological advantages and cost-effective benefits of the process are inseparable.

"To give an example – when we used the old conventional MIG/MAG welding machines for joining, we had to lay a rail on every side of our approximately three-metre-long box section for a reliable welding result; we cannot weld from the inside. Not only did these rails obstruct the later assembly of the crane, it was also uneconomical in many ways when compared with the forceArc process.

"Nowadays, we weld 50-degree single bevel welds partly as a full penetration weld from one side and automatically obtain the backing run on the reverse side. That means we save on the material for the four rails and on the time needed for their preproduction as well as for tack welding. It is tensioned and then welded with full penetration – saving around two hours per boom. We also save on gas and welding consumables."

A welding procedure test for forceArc welding with a reduced included angle of 30 degrees is currently underway in the company. If the test is successful, it will result in even greater potential for savings – in welding time, welding consumables, energy used, weld preparation and finishing work (Fig. 7).

## Finding the right setting

The medium-sized Limited Company has seven welders with EN 287/ISO 9606 certification, all of whom found the new processes easy to operate right from the start. Nitsche attributes this to the straightforward and convenient way the new machines operate: "Our welders select the JOB number from the preset JOBs (welding tasks) with optimised characteristics by reading the values for the material, the diameter of the filler wire and the type of shielding gas, and then type this in – the operating point can be set as the wire feed speed or panel thickness using the Synergic one-knob operation. Then they can start working straight away."

In addition, the welder can set up to 16 welding sequences per JOB on the wire feeder or access them at the touch of a button directly on the welding torch.

## New markets for joining technology

This new joining technology is also opening up new markets for Hische: the two largest cranes it ever built for marine service vessels left the factory in 2011. The company's entry into marine engineering will be followed by a move into crane construction for offshore platforms. This industry has especially high standards for product quality and reliability. ■
