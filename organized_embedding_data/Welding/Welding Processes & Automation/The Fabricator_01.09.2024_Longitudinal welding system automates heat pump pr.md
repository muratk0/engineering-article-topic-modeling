S

aving energy is always a goal for consumers, whether to decrease household costs or to be more environmentally conscious. One result is rising demand for energy-efficient heat pumps, which run on electricity instead of fossil fuel and serve as an alternative to furnaces and air conditioners.

In fact, according to a February 2023 article published by clean energy news source Canary Media, Americans bought more than 4 million heat pumps in 2022, the highest number ever. Demand is up in Europe, too, as consumers look for ways to reduce fossil fuel consumption.

How Heat Pumps Work

In a heat pump, a liquid stores heat from the ambient air or the ground, transports it to a heat exchanger, and transfers it to another circuit with refrigerant, which then evaporates. The vapor is then compressed, causing its temperature to rise. The hot, pressurized refrigerant expands and transfers its heat to water for the heating circuit or direct use from a faucet. In this closed-loop system, the refrigerant then begins the cycle again.

In this efficient, climate-friendly process, 1 kW of electricity becomes 2.5 to 5 kW of thermal energy, and if the electricity comes from renewable sources, emissions tend toward zero.

Typically, heat pump diameters vary from 11 to 24 in. with heights from 11 to 80 in., depending on volume. Versions made of corrosion-resistant stainless steel have wall thicknesses of 0.003 to 0.06 in. Carbon steel vessels have walls up to 0.11 in. thick with a protective enamel layer that is hardened at high temperatures.

As demand rises for heat pumps and complementary goods such as boilers, water heaters, and storage tanks, there is a need for efficient, financially viable production technology. Because these pressure vessels are available in many variants with different dimensions and wall thicknesses, fabrication processes must meet conflicting goals: a high degree of automation for cost efficiency and reproducible quality versus flexibility for special sizes with a batch size of one.

A Process Chain for Pressure Vessel Production

Weil Technology has taken up this challenge, developing a flexible process chain for the fully automated production of pressure vessels for boilers and buffer tanks made of stainless steel and carbon steel.

With the Variostar longitudinal seam laser welding station, the boilers and tanks can be produced at cycle speeds of one vessel every 60 seconds. The process starts with a blank or cutting a steel sheet from a coil. The blank is then laser-cut with holes and cutouts for inlets and outlets, sensors, and other connections. After laser cutting, it’s formed into a cylindrical shell, and the longitudinal seam is welded. In the final step, the resulting cylinder is welded with a top and bottom. Let’s take a closer look at each step.

Step 1—Cutting to Length

. Cutting hard sheet metal is a high-wear process using mechanical means, so laser cutting has proven effective and economically justifiable. In the cut-to-length line, pressure vessel diameter is defined by the width of the coil, and height is defined by the blank length. This process is flexible to accommodate reel changes during production, as well as the manufacture of special sizes.

Step 2—Shell Production

. After cutting to length, the blank is moved to a 2D cutting table where a laser cuts the holes required for the connections. If necessary, it also cuts the weld edges to help ensure perfect edge quality.

After this, the blank goes to the longitudinal welding station. Here, stainless steel sheets up to 0.06 in. thick and carbon steel plates up to 0.14 in. thick are roll formed into a shell with high precision, held in this shape, and passed under a stationary laser for welding at speeds of 9.84 to 14.76 ft./min.

Twist-free forming is essential, as only when the sheet metal lies neatly edge to edge with a minimal gap can the welded seams be produced to the required quality. Any transverse offsets during roll forming will cause leaks at the T-joints.

One challenge with the longitudinal laser welding process is welding different materials and sheet thicknesses on a single system. To meet this challenge, the process uses various welding processes. For joining stainless steel sheets, a laser/TIG hybrid welding process without wire feed is used. For carbon steel blanks with wall thicknesses up to 0.14 in., a laser/MIG process joins the deep weld seam without inclusion at 14.76 ft./min. to meet the target of less than 60 sec. per finished shell.

The flexible system thus produces cylindrical shells with material thicknesses between 0.3 and 0.14 in. and variable heights and diameters. Diameters are adjusted without tool changes or retooling in the automated process, enabling the system to produce one-off pressure vessels directly from the roll or from a blank stack.

Step 3—Attachment of Shell to Top and Bottom.

Deep, precise welding seams are required to join the tops and bottoms to the shell permanently and within tolerances. This prevents blistering and bead formation, which can cause defects during subsequent enameling and high-temperature curing.

While the Variostar is the heart of the process chain, Weil’s MBF 300 and 600 circumferential welding stations are available to form the connection between the shells and their tops and bottoms. Designed for circumferential welds up to 12 in. and 31.5 in. dia. respectively, both stations offer the hybrid laser/TIG and laser/MIG processes, so they are equally suitable for producing stainless steel and carbon steel pressure vessels.

This attachment process keeps pace with the longitudinal seam welder’s 60-sec. cycle. Robots in the process chain ensure the precise alignment of the shells (filled with inert gas in advance), tops, and bottoms, while the Variostar’s continuous welding process produces precise longitudinal seams of up to 61 in., even on sheet metal shells up to 0.14 in. thick.

Increasing Output to Meet Increasing Demand

Since a decision in favor of one material is not yet apparent in the storage tank market, manufacturers need flexible, efficient manufacturing options for all materials, material thicknesses, and variants in use. The Variostar longitudinal welding system and development of the continuous welding process for sheets up to 3.5 mm thick will help manufacturers of heat pumps and the associated buffer storage tanks to tap new productivity potential.

This continuous process chain is helping to scale the production of heat pumps and the associated boilers and buffer tanks. With cycle times of 60 sec. per pressure vessel and the option to manufacture different product variants in a single, fully automated production line, the system paves the way for a rapid increase in flexible production as heat pump demand increases worldwide.
