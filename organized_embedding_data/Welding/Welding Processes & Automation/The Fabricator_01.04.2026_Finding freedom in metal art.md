How welder Adam Stempka discovered a creative career as an artist

W

ith three generations of welders coming before him, Adam Stempka had a set career path, even if he didn’t realize it as a 12-year-old watching his grandfather weld parts in the garage.

He said he enjoyed his art classes in school and always had a creative streak, but he didn’t see a future in that. In Erie, Pa., which was once known as the boiler and engine capital of the world, metal art wasn’t necessarily celebrated. Metal components for things like locomotives and construction equipment were more prized.

So that’s what he did. He worked for the family welding business and got his certifications. He later tried working in different settings, like repairing barges, building tugboats, and welding parts for locomotives, but he didn’t feel content.

"I love welding. I do and still do to this day," Stempka said, "but I was just missing something."

He said his "aha moment" came when he saw the realistic work of metal sculptor David Madero. He knew that’s what he wanted to do with his life.

Around this time, he got a request for a metal deer skull mount. Upon completion of that project, the same person requested a metal replica of a battle-worn, tattered American flag. He found the work gratifying, but he also saw it as a pathway to another career.

Stempka created The Seeker during a one-month invited residency at the Buffalo Creek Art Center in Gardnerville, Nev. The 15-ft.-tall sculpture is composed of hand-formed 0.5-in. steel rods, each individually shaped, placed, and welded to build the figure’s structure. Stempka said the sculpture reflects a culmination of four years of personal thoughts, emotions, and spiritual experiences. Installed in August 2025, The Seeker now resides permanently within the Buffalo Creek Art Center Sculpture Park.

Adam Stempka’s The Lion of Judah, a distinctive sculpture comprising thousands of recycled nuts and copper, earned a spot in an art show in Lisbon, Portugal, in March. Stempka carefully selected and positioned each nut to form the intricate details of the lion’s mane and body, while using copper to add a warm, golden hue.

Images: Stempka Designs

"I started thinking that I could make these flags and make a business out of it," he said. The founding of Metalheads, an online venture where he sells the handcrafted flags, soon followed.

Making those flags on the side provided Stempka with additional income, but it didn’t totally scratch that creative itch he had. Then came a request for a commissioned work, a cello sculpture, in 2021.

Stempka used steel, brass, and copper in the Mixed Metal Cello sculpture. He removed some of the exterior to reveal the hollow interior and put internal lighting inside to illuminate it. A patina was applied to mimic an old wooden finish. "Taking that to him and seeing how proud of it he was and how proud I was of making it, it was like, ‘Adam, you have to do this sculpture thing and just go all in,’" Stempka said.

He followed up the cello sculpture with a series of guitar sculptures, one of which ended up in the country star Luke Bryan’s possession. He worked on animal shapes. He’s also done a public sculpture for Titusville, Pa., home to the first oil well in the U.S.; brass palm trees for a lounge in Fresno, Calif.; and a relief of the Nittany Lion for a new hall on the Penn State Behrend campus in Erie. His portfolio keeps growing as more people learn about his work via social media.

Even with his newfound success, Stempka doesn’t consider his previous life as a full-time welder in manufacturing environments as being wasted. That’s where he sharpened his skills to manipulate metal.

"The way you heat metal and work with it and the different textures that you can pull, all of that came from experience," he said.

Stempka wants to continue building both the Metalheads and Stempka Designs brands. Metalheads is producing official merchandise marking the 250th anniversary of the signing of the Declaration of Independence, and Stempka Designs continues to seek opportunities to work with businesses, public institutions, and art enthusiasts. Meanwhile, Stempka is still working on his craft.

"There are so many things that I’m learning and I’m getting better at," he said. "I don’t think I’m the best that’s ever been, so I’m constantly pushing myself. I enjoy the challenge."

He’s also interested in sharing his story of personal growth. Describing his life as a little bit of a "mess" as a young person, Stempka said he committed himself to "doing the right thing" and "cleaning up his act." That involved a spiritual journey as well, in which prayer played almost as important a role as welding.

Metal art not only provided Stempka the freedom to pursue his true passion; it gave him an outlet that’s a positive retreat from the world around him. He feels there will come a time when he will have a much larger stage to share his story of faith, but right now his art is sharing that message.

This cello sculpture, which weighs just over 500 lbs., was one of Stempka’s first commissioned pieces. This opened the door to other opportunities, including fabricating a custom metal guitar sculpture for country star Luke Bryan.
