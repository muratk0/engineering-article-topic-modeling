hen starting a new aluminum welding project, a welder has many things to consider. Among those factors are the material being welded—whether it’s stainless steel, aluminum, titanium, or some other metal—and the welding process selected for the application, such as arc welding or the more recently developed option of hand-held laser welding. Additional considerations include whether filler metal is required, which alloy is best suited for the job, and how the weld will be shielded (both gas selection and delivery). Because the gases used in these welding processes are often underemphasized, it’s important to explore this topic and explain some basics, myths, and best practices.

TIG WELDING

Aluminum welding is often associated with gas tungsten arc welding (GTAW), more commonly known as TIG welding, because of its highly aesthetic appearance and the distinct stacked dimes look that is often showcased on social media. Old-school welders might refer to this process as heliarc, which comes from the legacy process that used helium shielding gas. Today, argon, an inert gas that prevents oxygen from contaminating a molten weld pool, is the more common shielding gas used for TIG welding aluminum, mostly because it is notably less expensive than helium and largely universal when TIG welding all metal types.

Helium is sometimes still used because it has a slightly higher heat capacity, which results in a hotter and more fluid weld puddle and can increase penetration. Welders will sometimes opt for the extra heat when they have their amperage setting maxed out, but since it also has a higher ionization potential, the tradeoff is an arc that is harder to start and maintain. Most gas suppliers offer both argon and helium in pure form along with blends of the two, allowing welders a spectrum of benefits and tradeoffs.

Welders have a few options for gas delivery as well. A common collet body directs the gas stream inside the shielding gas cup or nozzle, but it results in a more turbulent flow. Using a gas lens, which acts as an equalizer plenum, gives the welder a more uniform, fully developed flow. This means that the welder can effectively extend the tungsten farther out of the cup and still achieve consistent gas coverage.

One major misconception about this process is that more gas is better. While exceptions to the rule exist, studies actually have found that moderate to lower settings can result in less turbulent gas flow, meaning it is less likely to draw contamination from the surrounding atmosphere into the shielding column. Although counterintuitive, this can improve weld quality.

Using a lower gas flow rate also reduces the overall consumable cost of the weld and makes the operation more cost-effective.

LASER WELDING

A more modern alternative to arc welding is the hand-held laser welding process. This process enables even beginner welders to make consistent welds in previously challenging applications. The appeal of creating welds with low distortion and making precise welds on thin materials is driving companies to learn more about the process.

While MIG and TIG require the correct shielding gas to not only shield the weld puddle but also generate a stable plasma column, laser welding performs effectively with either of the two primary gases of nitrogen and argon during the actual welding process. However, the resulting weld is heavily dependent on the correct selection. If a reactive gas such as nitrogen is used, the surface of the weld can look comparable to an argon-shielded weld, even though radiographic inspection would reveal higher levels of internal porosity. Keeping all other factors the same and switching to argon typically will result in welds with significantly improved internal quality. As with other aluminum welding processes, argon remains the most cost-effective choice for achieving consistent results.

IMPLEMENTING THE KNOWLEDGE

A little knowledge about gas selection and delivery will get a welder started in the right direction. For TIG, MIG, and laser welding, argon remains the most reliable starting point because it is affordable, widely available, and effective.

From there, the details matter. A gas lens and moderate flow rates can improve TIG weld quality. CO

2

-based gas mixes should be avoided entirely for MIG welding of aluminum. Gas choice for laser welding can impact internal weld quality even when the surface looks acceptable. Keeping these fundamentals in mind can help reduce rework, save time, and produce stronger, more consistent aluminum welds.
