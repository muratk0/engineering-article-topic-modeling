# How the right equipment can help with ergonomics In the welding department

Welding

A welder that can keep comfortable doing the job is likely to avoid unnecessary strains

By

Dan Davis

Allowing a welder to work without extensive reaching or leaning is one way to minimize muscle strains in the welding department.

t’s no surprise that as welders reach their 30s, they are not as flexible and limber as they were when they were just starting out in the metal fabricating business. What is surprising is that most companies don’t take that into account, putting these valuable craftsmen at risk of possibly developing sprains and strains that make them unable to work.

These types of ergonomic injuries, where awkward positions and repetitive activities result in pain and damage to muscles, tendons, and ligaments, are a real factor in the manufacturing industry. According to the Bureau of Labor Statistics, 38,640 cases of these types of musculoskeletal disorders were reported in the private sector in 2018, the latest date where this type of data was collected. That ranks just behind workers in health care and social assistance (56,360 cases) and retail (41,070 cases). The BLS also reported that the median days away from work because of these injuries was 12.

Those types of injuries don’t jump out as major issues when only a few days of work are missed. After all, tweaking a back muscle can be treated with some ibuprofen and ice, right? But sometimes those types of strains can compound over the years and result in much larger issues—with much larger price tags and increased time away from work.

To reduce the risk of sprains and strains in the welding department, a metal fabricator can ensure that the welders are working comfortably and with the right equipment.

Avoid Awkward Positions and Poor Posture

Back and shoulder injuries are common with welders because they often find themselves in awkward positions as they tackle their work. The back injuries often stem from leaning over a workpiece for extended periods of time. The shoulder injuries are related to holding arms away from the body or above the shoulder for a long time periods.

That’s why a welder should assume a "neutral" position when working, according to Andy Monk, product manager, Bernard. Think of it as being a natural position for the body, like if it were at a period of rest in a standing or seated position.

"Work should be somewhere between the waist and your shoulders," Monk said. "You want to keep the elbows and hands in as close to a neutral position as possible."

The design of gas metal arc welding (GMAW) guns actually can help address some of these ergonomic concerns. The curve of the handle, the shape of the neck, and the gun can help put the welder in a much more comfortable position when working.

"Of course, everything is based on the application, but you can work to put yourself in a good place so that your body is in that neutral position," Monk said.

For example, in some heavy-duty welding applications, a welder might choose to use a straight-handle GMAW gun, draping the power cable over his or her shoulder while activating a top-mounted trigger. The shoulders help to support the weight of the power cable during the joining process, taking some of the stress that normally would be on the arms and the back, and make it easier to move around the larger workpiece.

Monk added that most people working with a straight handle choose a 60-degree neck because that puts the nozzle right into the weld pool. People who elect to go with a curved handle might select a 45-degree neck because it complements the curvature of the handle, helping the welder achieve an optimal welding position.

Whether the handle is curved or straight, the goal is for the welding gun to be lightweight and comfortable in the welder’s hands. Monk said that in general, a smaller handle can more easily be maneuvered than a larger handle.

Again, the application really drives the gun selection. If a welding task calls for a low-amperage gun, the welder is going to be able to use a gun with a smaller handle and accompanying cable, making life easier for the person under the hood. Obviously, a job calling for higher amperage is going to call for a gun with a large handle size and cable.

"In really high-amperage, heavy-duty applications, people migrate to a longer neck," Monk said. "That gets your hands farther away from the ambient heat of the weld and also the hot parts being worked on."

A welding gun expert can help walk a welder through the options available for a challenging welding application. Might a job be done with a lower-amperage gun, which will be easier to maneuver? Possibly, but duty cycle, shielding gas, and transfer mode should be considered. Will the gun run too hot because the power cable has less copper than would be needed for a higher-duty-cycle or pulse application? If it can’t be done with the lower-amperage gun, what can be done to reduce the muscle stress on the welder?

Speaking of power cables, smaller and short power cables are easier to move around, helping to reduce operator fatigue. Short cables also are less of a tripping hazard than longer versions that might be coiled up on the floor and generally in the way. Monk said that a common choice for cable length is 15 ft., although 10-ft. lengths are available from most welding gun manufacturers if a welder wants something shorter.

Another aspect of welding guns that can help welders stay out of awkward positions is the ability to adjust the neck. This includes rotatable and flexible necks.

Rotatable necks are good for out-of-position jobs, like welding overhead.

"If a welder is working an awkward position, the neck can be loosened and positioned to best reach the weld joint," Monk said. "After tightening it down, the welder is better able to keep the elbows and hands in as close to a neutral position as possible."

Flexible necks can be adjusted to fit the application. If a joint is difficult to reach, an altered neck position might do the trick.

Adjustable-height tables and seats that fit a welding application also can help welders avoid excessive strain when working. The adjustable tables make a lot of sense given the differing heights of welders in the department; they can set the table height that puts the work within that neutral position. When it comes to seats, most people think only about the standard round seat with three wheels, but workshop chairs have really evolved. They are now designed with back rests, and some are even available in special positions, such as a lawn chair style.

A torch’s curved handle and neck help a welder more comfortably handle the gun while simultaneously getting the nozzle into the joint.

Minimize Highly Repetitive Tasks

The job of most welders is not filled with a lot of repetition. Those jobs often find their way to a robotic welding cell.

But a lot of welders who use GMAW guns have to repeatedly pull the trigger all shift, day after day. That can result in a muscle strain that might be minor in scope but a large pain for a welder that needs the fingers to be working.

A locking trigger is designed to address that repetitive movement. It can provide relief for the finger that might be in a pressed position for most of a shift.

"We can do it with a gun, and a lot of feeders have it now as well," Monk said. "But as it relates to the gun, in one example, you can pull the trigger, push the secondary button that holds the trigger in place, and get to welding. To stop welding, you just tap the trigger.

"It’s not something that everyone would use, but if you have really long welds, it can be beneficial and keep you from having your trigger finger depressed all day."

For those welders working for a contract manufacturer or an OEM where a repetitive weld might be part of the job description, they need to take time to break away from being in that one position for a long period of time. Taking a break to perform cleanup or some other task gives muscles a chance to relax before the body needs to re-engage with the same positioning.

Make Smart Lifting Decisions

Not all shops have central feeds for welding gases, which means that welders have to deal with individual gas cylinders. This isn’t a big deal, but a welder should still use common sense when moving these containers around.

The Canadian Centre for Occupational Health and Safety offered up these tips for welders:

• Use a lifting aid or ask for help when lifting full or partially full cylinders.

• Before moving, ensure that the cylinder cap is secured.

• Tilting and rolling a cylinder slightly on its edge is good for moving cylinders short distances, but for longer trips, use a cylinder trolley. Also, secure the cylinder to the trolley before moving.

• If a cylinder has to be lifted by one person, place the forward foot around the cylinder, lower the cylinder across the thigh by pressing down with the rear hand while holding the cylinder with the other hand, and push the cylinder forward onto a flat surface with the rear hand.

These tips are not a guarantee that welders will avoid any sort of muscle sprain or strain, but it does minimize the risk. In the business of manufacturing, that’s the type of continuous improvement plan that makes a lot of sense, particularly in the face of organizations claiming that they can’t find people to step into open welding positions.
