O

n Oct. 25, Brampton, Ontario-based SORSYS Technologies hosted a demonstration of FuseRing’s induction-assisted friction welding technology on tube and rod materials. Attendees included representatives of the Canadian federal government, local associations, colleges, the nuclear industry, and the aerospace industry. Adrian Gerlich, professor in the department of mechanical and mechatronics engineering at the University of Waterloo, Waterloo, Ontario, and director of the university’s Centre for Advanced Materials Joining, presented results from the studies his team has performed on parts that were joined using the technology. (He also presented these findings at FABTECH in Orlando, Fla., in mid-October).

It was an important moment for the technology—although the theory behind it has been recognized as plausible by many, there was no proof of concept available to see in person until now.

Solid-State Fusion

Paul Cheng, principal of FuseRing, partnered with SORSYS Technologies in 2022 to build demonstration models of the technology. SORSYS was founded by Saman Sahraei, Saeid Khosravani, and Peyman Eskandari, engineers who decided to start a company focused on creating automated systems for specific industrial issues that may affect a company’s business growth trajectory.

This demonstration of the FuseRing technology showed attendees a model that could heat the material in 2 seconds, but also could have its temperature settings changed, as well as the pressure and angle of the material.

The FuseRing concept uses solid-state fusion to join sections of pipe. The inventor of the welding process, Canadian David Lingnau, described it as a "spinduction" process—combining induction heating and kinetic energy to join two workpieces without the filler metals or solid-to-liquid phase transformation. Essentially, the process involves using an induction heating coil to preheat the ends of two tubes or pipes. The operator then retracts the coil and compresses the ends, and then rotates one of the two pipes.

The traditional solid-state fusion process has one inherent restriction—one part has to rotate, thus limiting the joining of pipelines to valves or any segment that is nonlinear, or really any part that is difficult to rotate in the field. This is where the FuseRing concept provides value.

The technology serves as an intermediary between two nonrotatable objects; a coupler ring of the same material as the host pipe being joined is placed between two sections. The device is designed to heat this coupler and turn it, "squeezing" it between the two nonrotatable sections. This enables the entire pipe segment to be joined using one fusion system.

The technique uses no filler and produces no fumes or particulates.

"The whole process takes less than 60 seconds," said Cheng, who has been promoting the concept at conferences in Canada and the U.S. for several years.

Pipe Demonstration Results

The demonstration machine SORSYS has constructed for pipe applications can join pipe between 4 and 6 in. OD. The demonstration in October was of a 4.5-in.-OD carbon steel pipe.

Gerlich noted that unlike standard friction welding, this technology only requires a slight rotation of the material and less force, which means that deformation of the profile is less pronounced and could potentially be reduced even further with the introduction of a bevel on the pipes. It also avoids the flash associated with friction welding.

More importantly, he noted that the deformation that takes place in the process actually refined the grain structure where the sections met due to the well-controlled reinforcement of the process.

"There was no evidence of martensite in that zone," he said. "Hardness was well within the allowed range, and tensile strength tests demonstrated that the pipe fractured in the base material, demonstrating the strength of the resultant joint."

Fuel Rod Assembly Application

A second application of the technology joined a tube to a rod material—a process that would be ideal for nuclear fuel rod assemblies. In this case, the induction preheat is on the rod, which is then plunged into the tube. The tubing and rods used in the demonstration were a zirconium alloy (Zr4), and the tubing had an OD of 12 mm and a 0.5-mm wall thickness. The rod, or plunger, is rotated to shear and join with the tube, with the joining happening around the circumference.

The completed joint from the demonstration is shown.

A second application of the technology involved the joining of a tube to a rod material—a process that would be ideal for nuclear fuel rod assemblies. In the demonstration, Zr4 tube and rod were joined. The tube had an OD of 12 mm.

SORSYS ran approximately 100 different weld trials to find the ideal setting for this process, according to Gerlich.

"Fractures all occurred in the base metal," Gerlich said. "And the plunger had an interesting effect on the weld. The peak stress shifted to the tube material itself. In the bonded region, deformation pushed out from the rod material, and residual oxide ended up outside the tube material so can be cut away. In the heat-affected zone, quite fine-grained interlock needles were found and the microhardness distribution was quite even."

Target Markets

Cheng noted that a wide variety of materials are suitable for this form of welding, and his focus has been on promoting it to industry sectors that struggle with time constraints and safety concerns, such as pipeline, refinery, nuclear, shipbuilding, and submarine.

While the event in late October was the first demonstration for the Canadian market, Cheng is planning a second for international customers.

"The important thing about this technology is that it doesn’t require postweld treatment," said Cheng. "If you’ve got a spent fuel container for the nuclear industry, how are you going to treat the inside of that weld? It’s an answer to that type of issue. The heat in this welding process only extends a few millimeters, so the interior of the container remains almost at room temperature."

Testing will continue on materials, such as aluminum to aluminum, as they are made available.
