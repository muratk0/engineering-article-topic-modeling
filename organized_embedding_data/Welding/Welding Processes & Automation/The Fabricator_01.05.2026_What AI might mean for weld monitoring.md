# What AI might mean for weld monitoring

[TARİH: 01.05.2026 The Fabricator]

Weld Monitoring

The technology holds promise, but is it ready for widespread application?

By

Dan Davis

Sonibel Instruments has developed AI software that can identify weld inconsistencies in semiautomatic MIG welds before they get to the inspection process.

Sonibel Instruments

A

rtificial intelligence holds great promise as a tool for boosting quality in the welding department of metal fabricating companies. It’s going to be a while before AI can alert a welder in real time that a mistake just occurred, but the industry is heading in that direction.

When most people think of AI today, they think of its ability to answer a difficult question, rewrite a resume tailored to specific job openings, or perhaps create a meme to share on social media. People are aware of its potential, but for the most part, AI has yet to make a real impact on their everyday activities.

That’s slowly changing. It just takes some time.

Consider "Are we close to having humanoid robots in the shop?" in the April 2026 issue of The Fabricator. The feature shared how SSE Steel Fabrication, a structural steel fabricator in St. Bernard Parish, La., opened its shop floor to Persona AI so that its humanoid robots could gather data in a functioning fab shop with only 20,000 sq. ft. of manufacturing space. "It’s an incredible proving ground and engineering test bed as we then also get into production-oriented activities," said Nic Radford, founder of Persona AI.

While those Persona AI robots are learning at SSE Steel, other humanoid robots from the same company are learning in the shipyards of South Korea. And all of the experiences are being fed into an AI system that then shares that information with other robots. As AI grows more experienced and sophisticated, robots are able to retain more knowledge and grow in their abilities. In the case of Persona AI, it moved beyond a memorandum of understanding with HD Korea Shipbuilding & Offshore Engineering and HD Hyundai Robotics last May and entered a joint development agreement with those parties to commercialize the robots for work in the shipyards. Prototypes have reportedly demonstrated technical feasibility, which likely will continue with AI helping the robots to learn at an ever-increasing pace.

With technological jumps now being measured in weeks, how long does the metal fabricating industry have to wait for AI to make its mark on weld monitoring? It won’t happen right away for most small and midsize shops, but it might not be that far away either.

THE EXPENSE OF IT ALL

The most helpful AI-enhanced tool for a welder would be a power source that can run AI directly. That would enable the unit to take in all of the parameters of the weld as it occurs and notify the welder of errors in real time to minimize the chance of an unacceptable weld.

As Steve Zatezalo, the head of the Welding Business Academy at Fronius USA, wrote in "Implementing AI in Welding" in the November 2025 issue of the American Welding Society’s Welding Journal, that type of equipment is not feasible given the price tag that would come with it. That’s a lot of computing power that needs to be added, which drives up costs and increases energy demands.

If the AI is not built into the machine, then perhaps a cloud-based AI engine can collect data and process it for a shop. But that entails the installation of sensors and even cameras, which adds more expense and complexity to welding operations.

That’s why AI that detects weld defects will likely be pioneered by large organizations that can afford the investments necessary to produce results that ensure significant return on investment. This is already occurring in high-volume, low-mix industries, like automotive manufacturing.

In 2023, IBM disclosed how its Smart Edge for Welding on Amazon Web Services was being used in such a setting. Visual and audio recordings are taken during an active weld, and AI and machine-learning models are used to analyze the quality of the weld in real time. If the quality does not meet pre-established standards, alerts are sent, allowing a welding technician overseeing an automated welding cell to stop the process, fix the weld, and reconfigure the parameters to avoid making the mistake again.

Data collection for IBM Research’s patented multimodal AI is thorough. Xiris Automation’s weld cameras are used to capture high-resolution, in-process videos of the weld pool, wire, and workpiece, and the company’s thermal cameras provide a visual into the heating and cooling behavior of a weld as it is being produced. A Xiris WeldMic is used to listen to the arc sound in real time.

These measurement devices allow the manufacturer to keep tabs on many variables that can determine overall weld quality:

• The optical video provides insight into the weld pool’s size and shape, how it solidifies as it cools, how the wire deposits into the joint, weld spatter, turbulence in the shielding gas, and the appearance of holes.

• The thermal video tracks thermal zones through color coding, trail uniformity, and the size and purity of the weld pool. Annotations also are created by AI models to track the possible appearance of porosity as it occurs.

• Capturing audio helps the AI to determine patterns of normal and abnormal behavior and classify abnormalities to specific failure modes.

All of that information then feeds the AI engine, which produces real-time feedback on weld quality. A weld technician can see this information in the form of a dashboard that indicates whether a weld is pass or fail, with an explanation of the defect if the weld does not meet specifications. A process engineer can use the collected information to see patterns for the weld cell over shifts, weeks, and months, and for certain weld programs and specific materials.

In one project involving Xiris Automation, a vehicle manufacturer found that the AI successfully identified weld defects a majority of the time. For example, the AI identified defects that were linked to bad gas or spent nozzles.

Larger companies involved in high-volume, low-mix manufacturing are finding the use of cameras and microphones to identify real-time weld defects is worth the investment. In particular, it helps to avoid product recalls related to poor welds.

Xiris Automation

"They started to understand that there was a sound signature," Xavier Jauregui, Xiris Automation’s COO, told The Fabricator last year. "And the correlation with inspected welds was more than 80% of the time."

Of course, this improves as AI is exposed to more production data and applications.

The impact of such technology can be huge for a large manufacturer. Quality welds help an organization avoid warranty costs and possible recalls. They also help maintain a company’s brand, which can suffer if quality issues become commonplace.

SOUNDS LIKE PROMISE

Technological advancements take some time to work their way down to small and medium-sized manufacturers, but that can be hastened by startups that recognize an opportunity where others don’t.

Sonibel Instruments is launching a real-time weld detection system that captures sound signatures of the weld arc and uses AI to identify the sound pattern and link that to welding quality. Video is not part of the system. An aluminum-cased sensor mounted on the welding torch focuses on how the droplets of molten metal are vibrating when they hit the weld puddle, Sonibel’s Chief Technical Officer George Hollo told The Fabricator earlier this year. The frequency variations in those vibrations indicate the weld quality. The system’s software processes the audio feedback and shows the general location of the defect in the weld bead.

"Sometimes it’s very clear that this is a terrible, porosity-filled weld," Sonibel CEO Sophia Millar said. "We can just label that right away. But other times, it’s subsurface, and that’s where our tool would help most."

Any veteran of a metal fabricating shop floor likely has an ear for a weld that doesn’t sound right. The Sonibel tool and its AI brains are taking that type of institutional knowledge and making it universally accessible.

But it’s not quite there yet for all types of welding processes. The developers claim to have optimized weld detection for semiautomatic gas metal and flux-cored arc welding to the point where porosity shortcomings and lack of fusion defects can be identified. For manufacturers seeking similar monitoring for other arc processes or laser welding in semiautomatic and automatic applications, Sonibel just needs to add to its ever-growing database from which its AI software can deliver accurate quality assessments.

That additional information and smarter AI will translate into a future iteration that will not only be able to indicate a weld defect but also identify it, according to Hollo. The welder or weld cell technician won’t have to investigate whether the defect is related to porosity, lack of penetration, or something else.

SOON TO BE EVERYWHERE

So, if you look hard enough, you’ll notice that AI is making an impact in welding. It’s just not a widespread phenomenon.

But change in the digital age occurs at a rapid pace. AI has an insatiable thirst for knowledge, and as long as the power stays on and AI is being fed trustworthy data on which it can make quick and accurate decisions, manufacturing, and welding more specifically, is going to experience dramatic change.
