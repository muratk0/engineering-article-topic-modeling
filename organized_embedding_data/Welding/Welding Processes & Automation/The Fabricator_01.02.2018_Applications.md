Weld assembly equipment provider aims for lights-out manufacturing with mobile manipulation system

SITUATION

Hirotec America, part of the global Hirotec Group, provides weld assembly equipment for automakers including GM, Fiat Chrysler Automobiles, Ford, Toyota, and BMW. The company strives to be a lights-out manufacturer by 2025, using automation almost everywhere within its facility and placing spare-parts operators into high-value positions that require complex problem-solving or attention to detail that cannot be paralleled by machines.

The company chose to automate the black oxide process first because of the inefficiencies of performing this task manually. It involves moving parts from bucket to bucket in a sequence, with delays in between while the parts become oxidized. The final parts are coated with a thin, corrosion-resistant surface that must be consistent to ensure that the parts don’t rust.

Hirotec operators previously completed this task manually, interrupting their tasks at the machine shop to transport parts to the process line and run them through the dipping procedure. Whichever operator happened to be available from the shop floor would complete the task—there was not a set operator on the black oxide line.

The parts needed to be left in buckets for long periods of time, interrupting the flow of other processes around the facility as operators waited for the oxide line to be completed. Oft en the black oxide had to be repeated multiple times on the same parts–if operators did not dip them with perfect timing, results were inconsistent.

The company began looking for a platform that could make a dual-arm manipulator into a fully mobile manipulation system.

RESOLUTION

Hirotec integrated a Yaskawa Motoman dual-arm manipulator with an Otto 1500 self-driving vehicle to pick up and dip parts in black oxide for corrosion protection.

"[The system] was up and running around our facility within a day. In less than a week we had it running around between our different black oxide stations, and it’s providing much more consistent parts than what we saw with our manual process. We don’t have to run the parts through multiple times anymore, which has improved our productivity in spare-parts production," said Peter Mourelatos, R&D project engineer. "Operators can now remain focused on the valuable tasks in the shop and not be distracted with secondary jobs like black oxide dipping."

Hirotec didn’t have to change any of its facility’s infrastructure to get the system to work; there are no hard-set paths for the vehicle to follow. And rather than working behind a fence, the vehicle can work in human-dense environments and travel safely around forklifts and human operators.

Automating the black oxide dipping process is just the first step in the company’s lights-out vision. "We’ve successfully implemented mobile manipulation at our site," explained Mourelatos. "It’s just the beginning for us as we work towards our goal of lights-out manufacturing with zero operators for spare-parts production."

Ladder manufacturer uses automated positioners to eliminate inventory concerns

SITUATION

Cotterman Co. is a manufacturer of rolling steel ladders based in Croswell, Mich. Its towering industrial ladders and maxi lift platforms, similar to rolling staircases, are designed to give workers simple, safe access to out-of-reach items in commercial and industrial environments. The company also produces office ladders, aluminum railings, and various track ladders.

In true lean manufacturing form, every ladder is built to order. "Cotterman’s main goal is to get a quality product to the customer in a timely manner. If it’s ordered today, it’s shipped tomorrow, and we don’t stock any ladders," explained Larry Higgins, plant manager.

But refining the manufacturing processes so the company could build ladders to order proficiently didn’t happen overnight.

Rob Stimpson, project manager at Cotterman, described the company’s previous inventory environment: "We would build large batches of items. We would set up our manual stops on a bender or steel cold saw, cut a million of them [parts], put them in a big bin, and they would sit there. We would basically analyze our inventory by how much dust was on it."

Manufacturing Engineer Manager Mark Desjardins was wrestling with how to combat the company’s immense amount of in-process inventory without adding a great deal of complexity to his operators’ work loads. The company would stock inventory for products that would be built only a handful of times per year, taking up factory floor and storage space and costing the company significant dollars. Certain products requiring the use of the bending machine would have to be stocked as well, because it was so costly and time-consuming to set up the bender.

RESOLUTION

To combat these setup time and inventory challenges, Desjardins purchased a TigerStop automated positioner that could attach to the existing machinery and quickly cut parts to length. Being able to cut specific jobs accurately and efficiently eliminated the need for stocking inventory and setting up manual fences on machinery. Operators would type in the desired part length, or pull up a presaved cut list, and press start.

The company installed its first automated positioner on its steel cold saw to cut bundles of tubing for use in the construction of rolling warehouse ladders. Multiple tubes can be cut quickly to the desired specification. The company then added another automated positioner to its bender.

"The setup time was killing us," said Desjardins. "Now it’s so easy. We pull the number we want to run and run it without any setup. Just plug it in and go."

The payback was so substantial on the bender that the company continued automating other workstations with positioners, including additional saws and punch presses. And the learning curve was short.

"We have an operator that has been with the company for over 20 years, but has had almost no computer experience," said Desjardins. "He was operating that machine within a few hours, and even though there were some concerns that he wouldn’t adapt to the technology, now he would never go back."

Inventory is no longer a concern, and labor costs have decreased along with setup time. Before implementing the automated positioners, the company stocked subassemblies of nearly every SKU of its very popular items. "Today, what we give to our customers is product consistency. We went from a 10-day lead time to 24 hours," Stimpson said.

"The steel warehouse was full of steel and now it’s maybe half full, and yet our throughput is up probably 50 percent," explained Desjardins. "Using the [positioner], we save probably 50 percent of labor costs. It used to take forever to set up a job, and now it’s down to seconds. We used to always say plus or minus ⅛ inch was pretty close for us, and now we’re cutting parts ten thousandths of an inch and it’s holding all day long."

Zero warehoused inventory through just-in-time manufacturing practices has reduced the required square footage of facility space needed to manufacture and store finished goods. The company can deliver products on-the-fly with a 24-hour drop-ship turnaround, helping it to lower production costs, increase sales, and open additional facilities throughout the country.
