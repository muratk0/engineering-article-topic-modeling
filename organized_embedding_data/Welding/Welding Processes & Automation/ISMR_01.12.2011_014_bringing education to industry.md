# BRINGING EDUCATION TO INDUSTRY

[TARİH: 01.12.2011 ISMR]

ISMR interviews Adrian Hawkins, Chairman of the Association of Welding Distribution (AWD), who has kick started a major training programme for UK welders.

Adrian Hawkins was elected Chairman of the Association of Welding Distribution (AWD) in March this year. He was closely involved in the welding distribution industry at BOC and as the current managing director of Weldability-Sif.

Weldability-Sif is a multi-million pound company operating from purpose-built facilities in Letchworth Garden City, Hertfordshire (UK). It supplies MIG, TIG, MMA, spot and oxy/fuel welding and plasma cutting machines, torches, accessories, consumables and personal protective equipment to the UK distributor market and exports to a number of countries.

Its UK facility enables distributors to single source over 2,000 different products and the company to maintain its stock of high volume consumables including the distribution of over 4,000 tons of mild steel MIG welding wire per annum to the market. Hawkins has achieved 15 per cent growth this year, despite difficult trading conditions, and has focused on growing overseas markets, such as Brazil and Saudi Arabia, for future export opportunities.

ATTRACTING MORE WELDERS 
“Business develops from ideas, contacts, opportunity and value,” he told ISMR. “This, not the free market economy, governments or banks, creates the environment in which we all live and trade. We need to replace our consumer-spending dependency with something far more fundamental and place the focus on developing the infrastructure and our energy industry in the UK, hopefully reducing our dependency on financial services.

“This will be the kick start needed for a manufacturing-based economy and will be the single most important development in the UK welding industry in the past 35 years. We must consider the urgent necessity the industry has for some 20-30,000 additional competent welders and develop ways in which this challenge can be met.”

Hawkins stresses the urgent need for skilled welders, which is becoming more pressing in the industry.

“We are losing skilled welders. Around 18,000 welders have been lost in the last five years in the UK. That, coupled with the expected energy boom which will require around 20,000 welders, leaves us about 35,000 welders short. As an industry, we need to organise around promoting welding as a profession. Some companies are paying excellent salaries to welders to attract the best quality. In terms of welding processes, we now have easier-to-use technology, great electronics in welding machines and superb variation and diversity of consumables and gases that can be used in welding. This requires a very sound knowledge base.”

The AWD is heavily involved in the promotion of welding and, as a result, BOC is running the UK Apprenticeship Skills Award Scheme. Other AWD members supported the World Skills 2011 event held in London in October whilst SkillWeld 2012 will identify some of the best young skilled welders in the industry. The Wilkinson Welding Academy and the Weldability-Sif foundation also provide vital training support for welders.

TRAINING PATH FOR WELDERS 
“Previously, there were 60/70 different qualifications focused on welder training. As the AWD, we tried to give focus on what we felt was necessary for people to qualify. The industry needs to be telling colleges which skills and qualifications should be needed for training. We networked with the Welding Institute (TWI), Cambridge to discuss the qualifications needed – it had already started work on three EAL certifications.

I started visiting colleges and asking questions. I found that EAL skill level one was not attractive for colleges. It required amending as we needed to get people started using the welding process and most schools do not offer access to any form of welding for health and safety issues,” Hawkins told ISMR.

“Because of this, I felt that the AWD’s mission was to ‘start connections’ and give the trainee industry a proper career route to follow. We defined the career pathway and then decided that basic training skills should be established in colleges around the country. We set up the Weldability-Sif foundation as a charity to encourage colleges to train in a certain direction. We offer bursaries to colleges to train in a certain way. That principally led me to organise the launch event at North Hertfordshire College in Stevenage recently where we showcased our sponsored classroom and welding bays.”

At the time, the college was hoping for two days per week on training opportunities. It is currently running welder training courses 4.5 days per week and is examining the option of evening courses. 300 learners will complete the Weldability-Sif MIG course in the next six months.

“There are five courses that EAL has provided for us: MIG, TIG, oxyacetylene, MMA welding and a general knowledge of welding version. All five courses have also recently been supported by the Unemployment Fund so that colleges will also receive additional funding if they offer any one of those five courses to anyone who is currently unemployed. So these courses are being supported by government, the Weldability-Sif Foundation and the AWD.

“We also designed a separate section to help people going down the sales and distribution route – they can all start with a foundation course and go into the AWD Professional Sales diploma and then onto a TWI qualification. So, in essence, we have identified two routes: one for real welders going on into industry and welding up nuclear power stations etc. and another one to encourage people into the distribution industry,” Hawkins explained.

This route, which has been agreed with TWI and AWD, is effectively delivering further management and training opportunities into the Welding Institute. There is a point where, after Skills Stage 3, the Welding Institute can offer a Passport to Practice enabling any welder to weld around the world using this transferable qualification. The Foundation welder training courses are all 60 hours and Weldability-Sif has also created online learning for welders to assist colleges with delivery and support their guided learning hours.

“We have really facilitated and brought together a lot of organisations within the industry to create this pathway to help colleges deliver courses efficiently. We are also working on a system where we create tutors for tomorrow - effectively, Train the Trainers. So Stevenage is the blueprint with the aim of bringing in other colleges across the UK. We have had a great response from many other colleges with interest in the Foundation scheme, with the long term intention to work with about 40 colleges,” he explained.

“We set up the Weldability-Sif Foundation Charity to encourage the development of new welder training facilities across the UK and the North Hertfordshire College is the first to inaugurate a specialist foundation level training facility. As National Chairman of the AWD, my vision is of investment in education through government and charitable funding for Skills Centres and the new Studio Schools. With a growing number of unemployed people and a high proportion of those being aged 18 to 25, the availability of these courses may seriously help with their employment opportunities. Vocational skills are back on the educational agenda and this certainly helps our cause.”

The Weldability-Sif Foundation has injected funds of approximately £40K per welding unit plus ongoing costs of consumable supply etc. It is open to other companies joining the charity and investing in welder skills training.

“Manufacturers are crying out for this skillset so it is a worthwhile cause aimed at developing our long term future,” say Hawkins. “It ticks all the boxes – there is a demand for it, there’s a facility for it, there are organisations who wish to support it and a ready supply of youngsters wanting to get involved in it. We are basically adding that extra bursary to encourage colleges to do the training and we need them to achieve certain numbers (hopefully 5 – 8000 people trained every year to replace the 35,000 welders) so there is an entrepreneurial aspect to bringing education to industry.”

WELDING SKILLS IN FOCUS Weldability-Sif has been working in close co-operation with The North Hertfordshire College in Stevenage to set up a new welding skills training initiative and centre at the college. It offers bursaries to colleges to train in a certain way.

This new facility was officially opened by John Hayes MP, Minister of State for Education, Skills and Lifelong Learning on 13 October 2011. A classroom and six welding bays were sponsored by the Weldability-Sif Foundation.

“On 15 June, I set out my vision for further education. A future that is characterised by innovation, vocational excellence and a renewed sense of enthusiasm, for and pride in, skills. This partnership between Weldability-Sif, The Welding Institute, Cambridge and Stevenage Skills Centre, part of North Hertfordshire College, to deliver training to meet demand and to overcome the current skills gaps in the UK welding industry is a great example of making this vision a reality," said John Hayes, MP.

## AWD IN FOCUS

The AWD was formed 37 years ago by a group of welding distributors and then attracted a number of welding suppliers, manufacturers and importers as members. In the last three or four years, there has been a redefinition of the organisation to more accurately reflect the membership which now numbers anyone associated with the handling of welding products – manufacturers, distributors and end users.

Members now include gas manufacturers (for welding operations), welding consumable manufacturers (rods, wires etc.) and cutting machine manufacturers, suppliers safety products (PPE and fume extraction) and other ancillary products found in manufacturing where welding is used ie grinders, bevellers and other tools, plus companies involved in welder training.

“We also see an opportunity to service end users – those who need advice and support – by bringing them into contact with our members. We have been trying to focus our efforts over the last few years in correctly representing the welding industry. Welding is an international process,” said Hawkins.

AWD has its own magazine (WeldingWorld) and supports members by holding exhibitions on a regional basis. In November, the AWD worked alongside the UK Health and Safety Executive to promote ways to use welding safely. It has a Fume Focus group which examines how to control the fumes that come off welding, how to exhaust it and how to protect the welder. Next year, it will support the MACH 2012 manufacturing exhibition by presenting the Welding World village, with member stands, a seminar facility within the village and its own lounge.

“One of the things I have done since becoming chairman is to create three focus groups. The first is around product safety and quality and we have managed to warn against a couple of products imported into the UK that may be unsafe. We also have a member benefit committee to focus on delivering something back to members. The remaining group is focused on Education & Training. There are no longer big employers out there for large groups of apprentices (ie employing 50 or 100) but there are lots of companies that can take 1 or 2 apprentices so if we can concentrate on a training scheme that fits these needs, we know that the skill is needed. That is a concept for the third group, the education committee, to develop.”

Since announcing the need to form three new Working Groups at the AWD conference in April 2011, Hawkins formed the groups and the first meeting of the PSQ (Product Safety and Quality) committee took place on 4 July 2011. The new education committee group supports and promotes a new training pathway for welders. The next step for Hawkins now is to create an apprenticeship scheme where the AWD supports and employs apprentices and finds them posts within industry etc.

“Projects within the UK’s renewable energy sector strategy and structure will remain central to the future of the welding industry in the UK and provide us with many new opportunities for investment, innovation and profit. The AWD is aligning itself with many of these projects to assist members in developing local contracts to supply.” ■

With the increasing polarisation of the new car market, global market growth over the next decade will be driven mostly by the budget and premium car segments leaving the mass volume OEMs to fight for market share in the middle, says TNS Automotive (an automotive market researcher).

OEMs are tapping into growth opportunities at both ends of the market. In fast-growing emerging markets, the rising number of new affluent consumers who aspire to exclusive and luxury models is encouraging car manufacturers to develop their high-end product portfolio. At the current pace, the premium car segment could well reach up to 8 percent of the global car market by 2020, according to TNS.

At the other end of the spectrum, growth in the low-cost car segment is forecast to accelerate, reaching 20 per cent of the global car market by 2020. This increasing demand for budget cars is fuelled by a combination of factors: the rise of middle-class consumers from BRIC and Central Europe with a limited budget (US$ 10k) for their new car purchase, the long-term market trend towards small, environmentally-friendly cars and the rising demand in Western markets for basic, no-frills cars as consumers choose to divert their spending from automobiles to alternative categories that better reflect their priorities.

Low-cost cars are no longer synonymous with poor quality, obsolete technology or cheap imitations. Instead, traditional car manufacturers and newcomers from China and India are increasingly investing in highly innovative and cost-effective processes to develop sturdy and reliable models that meet consumers’ specific functional needs.

“One thing is clear: traditional OEMs need to re-think their marketing strategies quickly and find innovative ways to reposition themselves in this increasingly dynamic market if they don’t want to see their market share being squeezed further by the low-cost car phenomenon,” says Rémy Pothet, Global Sector Head at TNS Automotive.

## AN EYE ON CHINA

Could China reinvent the global automotive industry model in the next 20 years?

China has successfully established itself as a leading producer and buyer in the automotive market worldwide, transforming the automotive leader board in less than 20 years. China’s automotive industry, however, is still in its infancy. The rate of car ownership in China is less than 25 cars per 1000 inhabitants, compared to 500-600 in mature markets. 18 million vehicles were sold in 2010, of which 70 per cent were first time buyers. There is still huge potential for further growth as those living in lower-tiered cities all aspire to buying a car as quickly as possible.

“It is incredible to think that China is already market leader globally although its domestic manufacturers haven’t yet broken into other continents. Of course, there are joint ventures that are getting stronger and stronger (the likes of SAIC, FAW, SAW or ambitious 100 per cent Chinese companies like Geely, BYD or Chery) but none of them have become world players – yet. It’s only a matter of time. But, for now, the Chinese automotive industry remains very fragmented and dependent on ‘foreign’ companies,” says Pothet.

Challenges such as the rise in fuel prices, congestion in major cities and increasing awareness of pollution will add extra pressure to reinvent the model and leave China with no choice but to take the lead in the development of clean alternative fuel vehicles.

## OUR FRIENDS ELECTRIC

The escalation in demand for clean and green energy storage has motivated industrial and research communities to invest in and employ fuel cells for automotive applications. The demand for energy storage is expected to witness an upswing with electric vehicles (EVs) and hybrid electric vehicles (HEVs) gaining increasing traction. Fuel cells have an edge over conventional batteries as they exhibit appreciable characteristics such as higher energy delivery per volume weight.

To achieve high uptake of fuel cells in the automotive sector, certain requirements are necessary. These include noiseless operation, lower heat signature, better fuel efficiency, the possibility of superior acceleration compared to similar diesel or gasoline-powered vehicles and on-board electric power generation.

New analysis from Frost & Sullivan, ‘Energy Storage Technologies for Automotive Applications – Strategic Portfolio Management’, finds that fuel cells hold great potential because of their favourable thermo-physical properties. Hydrogen and fuel cell products are being tested and commercially deployed in materials handling, fuelling infrastructure and transit bus applications.

“Mass market adoption of fuel cells is expected to depend heavily on the wide availability of hydrogen and governments worldwide have been revamping the hydrogen infrastructure,” notes Technical Insights senior research analyst, Avinash Iyer. “Thus, in the next five to seven years, many companies are expected to produce hydrogen and thus mutually benefit from research and development efforts on fuel cell technologies.”

Although the prospects for the market are upbeat, there are some challenges curtailing market progression. Efficiency of the fuel cells, poor chemical reaction in the fuel cells, hydrogen storage and water management are some of the key challenges in the industrial sector. The efficiency of a fuel cell is generally a major issue because low efficiency results in high cost of production.

Ongoing research activities on efficiency improvement will aid in reducing the cost of fuel cells. For instance, use of nanocatalysts enhances fuel cell efficiency. Heat management is another criterion that will affect the performance of fuel cells. The utilisation of alumina coatings to provide strong insulation and protect sensitive electronics could resolve this issue. However, it would take about five to seven years to identify a suitable insulation material for such fuel cells.

## COMBATING OIL DEPENDENCE

A new report from TrendTracker questions whether electric cars can effectively combat oil dependence. Its latest report, ‘Electric Vehicles: Energy, Infrastructure and the Mobility Market in the Real World’ suggests that this year’s ‘Dawn of the Age of Electric Motoring’ heralded by the launch of the Nissan Leaf and Mitsubishi iMiEV may prove a false dawn. The global car population is expected to more than double to over two billion as China and other BRIC countries become motorised, but the proportion for which electric cars will account is much less clear.

This 242-page report suggests that moving from oil to electricity could represent one of the greatest challenges humanity will have ever faced. Yet little thinking to date makes sense of the enormous challenges ahead if electric vehicles (“EVs”) are to fulfil their promise and break out of a tiny niche of high-income suburban commuters with home charging facilities.

With radically reduced parts consumption and probable high depreciation, EVs represent a strategic threat to the standard automotive industry business model. Meanwhile, alternative new powertrain technologies are fragmenting vehicle production and markets. The report’s lead author, Toby Procter, says that these challenges must be met or, given the longevity of cars, the swelling global car population will remain oil-dependent after oil has become too scarce and/or too expensive to fuel it.
