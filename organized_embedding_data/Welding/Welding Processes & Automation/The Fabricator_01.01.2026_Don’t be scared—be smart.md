Laser Welding Safety

Understanding the safety challenges of hand-held laser welding

By

Dr. Fanrong Kong

baona/iStock/Getty Images Plus

s laser welding continues to gain traction within tube fabrication shops and manufacturing plants as an efficient, high-quality welding process, safety considerations are becoming critical.

Traditionally, laser welding has been confined to automated systems within enclosures that have doors and tinted windows to protect personnel. Now, with the innovation of high-powered hand-held systems, laser welding has become mobile, and that requires additional considerations for safeguarding operators and other personnel in the work area.

Hand-held laser welding offers speed, precision, and reduced distortion on material up to about ¼ in. thick. It also can help less-experienced welders provide higher-quality welds than they could before. With welders in high demand, this is a big advantage; manufacturing production lines can’t wait for an operator to become a seasoned welder.

But this "quick" solution presents unique safety hazards that demand specialized training, personal protective equipment (PPE), and a proper facility and floor plan. The good news is that these all are achievable, and with them, the risks of laser welding can be easily mitigated.

UNDERSTANDING LASER HAZARDS

International standards classify lasers according to output power and associated hazards. Laser welding systems carry immense optical power density, measured in watts per square millimeter, that can melt metal. Because of this power, hand-held laser welding machines are universally rated as ANSI Class IV, the highest danger category, which requires strict engineering controls, certified PPE, and operator training before use.

Laser light stays tightly focused over longer distances than normal white light. Reflected laser light retains more than 90% of its original intensity, so it can harm not only the welder but also co-workers and nearby equipment (see

). Smooth or metallic surfaces create mirror-like reflections (specular), while rougher materials cause reflections that scatter in multiple directions (diffuse) (see

). Both are dangerous.

When a laser beam focuses on a human eye, it can instantly burn retinal tissue. In addition, direct or reflected laser welding light can re- sult in severe skin injury, including third-degree burns on any exposed area. Laser light also can present fire and electrical hazards, particularly from concentrated reflections striking nearby flammable materials.

SAFETY RECOMMENDATIONS FOR LASER WELDING OPERATIONS

Because laser light is a different frequency from arc welding light, companies must use safety equipment designed specifically for laser welding. Standard welding helmets and welding curtains do not provide protection against laser light.

Figure 3

shows a traditional welding helmet severely damaged after less than 10 seconds of exposure to a reflected laser beam. Even brief, indirect contact can wreck protective gear that is not certified for laser welding and cause permanent injury. Following are some safety equipment recommendations for hand-held laser welding operations.

Welding Helmets and Filter Lenses.

Helmets should meet ANSI Z136.1-2022 or EN 207 D LB6 standards for safe use of lasers (see

Figure 4

). These ratings ensure that both the filter lens and helmet can withstand the specific wavelength (1080 nm, for example) and intensity of a Class IV laser. It is a good idea to check OSHA standards as well.

Zero-standard welding helmets also work, but only a few welding helmet shells currently meet this certification.

Interestingly, the filter lens on a laser-certified helmet cannot be removed or replaced. It is installed permanently so that an operator can’t accidentally replace the lens with a noncertified one.

Laser-Specific Safety Glasses.

Safety eye-wear is a critical component in any industrial environment, and it is required for all welding activities. However, eye protection for laser use is a more complex issue.

Traditionally, laser use was mostly restricted to lab or medical environments, and laser eyewear was designed to protect the eyes from very specific light radiation wavelengths. Lasers used in tube fabrication, especially hand-held lasers, are generally more powerful, and the reflectiveness of many metals increases exposure hazards for the welder and those nearby.

It is nearly impossible to predict how laser light will reflect off the material during hand-held laser welding, so the welder needs more than just eye protection. Laser-specific protective eyewear is only effective when used in combination with EN 207 or ANSI Z136.1 safety devices, such as enclosures, face shields, and welding helmets.

Protective Clothing.

Operators must wear leather gloves. Flame-resistant apparel typically used for arc welding may be suitable, but leather body protection is best and highly recommended. This is because standard arc welding jackets may not resist laser energy or reflected radiation.

Note that short sleeves, open V-neck shirts, and other skin-exposing apparel is strictly forbidden for laser welding. Cover all skin and remember that reflected laser light remains highly dangerous.

Enclosures, Screens, and Partitions.

Workspace design plays as critical a role as PPE in hand-held laser welding safety. Laser welding ideally is performed inside an ANSI Z136.1- or EN 207-certified enclosure with fume extraction, at least four walls, and a roof to prevent stray beams from escaping.

Further, the door of the enclosure must be connected to an interlock switch on the laser that disables the laser when the door is open. This prevents other operators from exposure if they enter the enclosure. Posting a "Laser in Use" sign outside the enclosure whenever the system is active adds another measure of safety.

In addition to an enclosure, companies may choose to provide further protection against diffuse light for those who work outside of the controlled laser area by deploying laser curtains and barrier panels. Note that traditional welding screens cannot provide the required level of protection.

CREATING A CULTURE OF LASER SAFETY

Hand-held lasers are a major advancement in welding technology, but they also require a shift in how the industry must think about safety. To build a strong safety culture:

Assign a qualified laser safety officer to oversee compliance and emergency procedures.

Conduct laser hazard assessments before introducing new systems.

Train all personnel on the unique hazards of optical radiation.

Use only certified equipment—no exceptions for helmets, filters, or enclosures.

Audit regularly to verify continued compliance with ANSI Z13.6, EN 207, and local regulations.

Hand-held laser welding is redefining tube fabrication with precision, speed, and portability, but its potential can be realized safely only through disciplined and smart adherence to laser radiation safety standards. By combining certified PPE, controlled environments, and trained oversight, manufacturers can fully leverage the productivity of hand-held laser welding while ensuring a safe workplace for every employee.
