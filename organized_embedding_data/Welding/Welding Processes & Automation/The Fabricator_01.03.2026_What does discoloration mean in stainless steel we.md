# What does discoloration mean in stainless steel welding?

I

have a small welding repair and fabrication business, and I do some stainless steel work. I’ve always thought the goal with TIG welding was to have a weld bead with nice blue and purple color to it, as that means you have good weld penetration. I have seen some comments online stating that is inaccurate, and I am a bit confused to what is correct. Can you provide some clarity on this topic?

Yes, we certainly can. That is a good question, as many people have a misunderstanding of what the different colors in a stainless steel weld mean.

Discoloration happens in many different welding processes, but we will focus on the gas tungsten arc welding (GTAW, or TIG) process. For GTAW of stainless steel, the shielding gas is 100% argon. Argon is an inert gas that does not react with anything, and its primary duty is to displace the atmospheric air to prevent porosity. Argon is heavier than air, so it sinks in the local vicinity, which allows the weld pool to solidify before it disperses. If you ever experienced weld porosity, that’s a good sign you welded through some impurities in the weld joint, or the shielding gas coverage allowed oxygen in, which contaminated your molten weld pool.

In stainless steel welding, discoloration next to the weld or in the weld bead itself is an indicator of the amount of oxygen present and the temperature at which the material was exposed to the oxygen. It can be caused by either oxygen contamination or travel speed and heat input.

As the temperature or the oxygen content at exposure increases, the discoloration progresses from a light straw color to a dark purple or blue. AWS D18.2 is an excellent reference to compare the relative discoloration level to the oxygen content present in the surrounding atmosphere.

Discoloration taken to the extreme, such as burn-through on thin material, often is referred to as "sugaring." Because the molten material has no shielding and has a blackened, coarse, grainy appearance, it looks like sugar has been poured into the weld. Back purging with argon or, more cost-effectively, nitrogen can protect thin sections from sugaring.

Regardless of the level or amount of discoloration, once it is evident, you can be sure that the corrosion resistance of the weldment has been affected. Any discoloration reveals the presence of a nonprotective oxide, which differs from the ultrathin passive chromium oxide layer that gives stainless steel its corrosion resistance. Instead, the welded surface becomes covered by a thicker, mixed oxide that does not effectively protect the metal from corrosive attack.

A chromium-depleted area can form where the available chromium content falls below the level needed to maintain passivity, leaving the subsurface metal unable to re-form its protective film. This area then is susceptible to localized corrosion, in the form of pitting or crevices, especially in aggressive environments like chloride-containing solutions.

Welding procedure control and postweld processing are the best ways to address weld discoloration. Good shielding gas coverage and proper back purging on tubing can minimize oxygen exposure during welding, thus reducing oxidation. A gas lens on your torch provides laminar flow, preventing turbulence that can stir in atmospheric contaminants. Controlling heat input by adjusting travel speed and amperage also limits the extent of the heat-affected zone (HAZ) and resultant oxidation by allowing the metal to cool within a protective atmosphere. A trailing-gas nozzle can be used to help provide protection without the negative impact on travel speed.

Where discoloration does occur, mechanical methods (brushing, grinding, and abrasive blasting), chemical techniques (acid pickling), and electrochemical processes (electropolishing or electrolytic cleaning) can be used to remove the oxidized layer. This restores corrosion resistance by allowing the chromium oxide to form properly.

Even lighter straw-colored tints reflect some loss of passivity, and industry guidance often recommends removing all heat tint beyond the very lightest color to ensure full corrosion resistance is restored. These practices are especially important in applications like food processing, medical equipment, and marine environments where corrosion resistance is critical.

To review, discoloration has no impact on the joint if the weld was performed correctly. Proper shielding of the weld, HAZ, and potentially the back side of the weld area is necessary to produce a successful weldment. Postweld cleaning is recommended to achieve the corrosion resistance that the stainless steel was intended to provide.

Ultimately, controlling and removing welding discoloration are essential to preserving the inherent corrosion resistance of stainless steel.
