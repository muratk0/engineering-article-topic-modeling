n his role as senior director for national advanced fabrication at Airgas, Bill Pharmer has been in a lot of welding shops in recent years, and he constantly sees opportunities for productivity improvement. In certain cases, welder performance is not keeping pace with the sophistication of new technology.

Airgas has conducted more than 2,000 studies of welding operations in recent years through its Unlocking the Hidden Cost of Welding program. By looking at several key performance indicators, Airgas experts are able to determine how efficient welding activities are at a shop and identify hidden costs, leading to savings and increased throughput. The industry calls this benchmark the "operator factor," basically arc-on time divided by total time, then expressed as a percentage.

"Five years ago, the operator factor for most shops was about 15%, but the more studies we do now, we’re seeing it creep down to 12% to 10%," Pharmer said. At 15%, a welder is only filling joints about nine minutes out of every hour.

What’s taking them away from actually welding? A lot of it has to do with consumable choices. By making smarter choices, a shop can get its welders back to what they are getting paid to do—and perhaps simultaneously save some money as well.

ABOUT THOSE GAS CYLINDERS

Want to know how a welder ends up spending more time away from the welding table during a typical shift than under a hood? Let’s consider what happens when a gas cylinder needs to be replaced.

Before welders can take the empty cylinder to storage, they need to find the crescent wrench to take off the regulator. After that’s done, they have to find the cylinder cap and screw that on. Then it’s time to deliver the empty cylinder to storage, which is across the shop. But before that can happen, they need to find the hand truck.

After delivering the cylinder to storage and picking up a full one, they head back to the welding area but see a co-worker. They discuss last night’s game and catch each other up about family news.

Upon returning to the table, welders have to get the cylinder set up, return the hand truck, and hopefully replace the crescent wrench and cylinder cap where they can be easily found. A five-minute task becomes a 20-minute quest. That’s more common than most shop supervisors would want to admit.

Pharmer offered up a couple of suggestions to remedy this type of situation. The first consideration is bulk gas delivery. If a shop requires gas flow that exceeds 10,000 standard cubic feet or more per month, then the installation of a bulk tank or banked cylinders, as well as plumbing the shop for delivery of the gas to welding stations, might make sense. Having gases like argon, carbon dioxide, hydrogen, nitrogen, and oxygen constantly available keeps the welder closer to the table and more engaged with actually laying down metal.

Of course, not all shops are large enough to make bulk gas a feasible option. But that doesn’t mean they can’t save time and money based on their gas cylinder selection.

Pharmer said that more advanced cylinders come with built-in, quick-connect regulators to simplify setup, and they also include enhanced safety features. The Airgas ARCAL line of shielding gases, for example, comes with options for wrench-free regulator changeout and easy on-off levers for quick openings and shutoff.

Additionally, reverse-pressure valves on modern gas cylinders ensure that the gas inside does not get contaminated and that most of the gas ends up getting used. When welders leave valves open on empty cylinders for extended periods, gas contamination can occur. Reverse-pressure valves—typically set between 60 and 80 PSI—keep the backflow contamination from happening.

"If you watch some people, they leave a tank open in storage. In places like Florida and Houston, that tank can get full of moisture," Pharmer said. "That 60 PSI keeps all of that out of the tank."

WHAT ABOUT GAS FLOWMETERS?

They might have been instructed otherwise, but many welders still hold onto the idea that more gas flow is better for the weld. It doesn’t matter what that welding procedure specification says.

Gas flowmeters can be preset to maintain a certain flow rate, but even a device like that can’t prevent welders from ramping up the gas flow if they want to see it higher.

Pharmer said that the real way to prevent the excessive use of assist gases during welding is training. If a shop can demonstrate why a TIG weld with an assist gas flow rate of 25 CFH works, welders are more likely to trust that what they have seen work and be less inclined to create defects in their welds.

LET’S TALK ABOUT WIRE

The same conversation about individual gas cylinders applies to wire setups as well. A welder working with 44-lb. boxes of wire is obviously going to be spending more time switching out wire than if that same welder were working with a 600-lb. spool of wire. Again, less downtime making these changeovers means more time under the hood.

But not every shop processes enough work for such a large drum of wire. Wire just sitting around waiting to get used can end up becoming contaminated or even rusting if it’s steel wire.

Another thing to consider about wire is that solid wire might be a suitable replacement for flux-cored wire in many instances. Working with solid wire eliminates the need for cleaning the weld, and it also means more actual metal is going into the joint.

"I can only think of one reason why flux-cored wire exists, and that’s welding out of position," Pharmer said. "When I need to go uphill, E71T flux-cored is designed to hold that weld with that slag and flux system."

But when flux-cored wire is used on flat and horizontal applications, its deposition rate is not as efficient as solid wire. Pharmer estimates about 15% of the flux-cored weld falls off when the slag and flux are cleared.

"The problem is that I can’t go out of position very well with solid wire," he added. "I either have to short-circuit-transfer that, which is a cold, low-deposition-rate process, or I have to pulse it, which can be done but you need some pretty heavy-duty capital equipment to do it."

If a shop wants to make the most of its flux-cored and solid wire use, Pharmer suggests one of two things: a positioner to counteract gravity or a dual-wire feeder so a welder can have the best of both wires.

Welders might be surprised to learn that they can use thicker wire than is typically thought possible for some applications. Conventional wisdom in the welding industry suggests that larger-diameter wire is for heavy-duty applications, but these thicker wires have versatility.

"You often can go up a wire diameter and do a lot more than you think you can do, particularly with aluminum," Pharmer said. "There are a lot of companies welding 3/64 [in.]-diameter wire that could change to 1/16 [in.]-diameter wire and still do the same job and probably do it at a higher deposition rate more efficiently."

Pharmer said that using larger-diameter wire in thin sheet metal applications is "counterintuitive," but it might be worth a discussion about other applications, particularly if it lets welders continue to work without worrying about changing over to a new wire size.

WHEN WELDING SPEED OUTPACES WORKFLOW

Any discussion about maximizing a welder’s time under the hood must begin with a modern reality: Technology has made the actual act of welding faster than ever. Pharmer said that with sophisticated wave form power sources, advanced gas blends, and high-deposition wires, welders are completing joints in record time.

However, the operator factor often declines because manufacturers haven’t updated their infrastructure to keep up. When the welding is done, the time gained often is lost to manual material handling, poor fit-up, or a lack of automation in fabrication. The bottleneck isn’t the welder; it’s the workflow surrounding the welder.

For example, Pharmer shares that equipment manufacturers often tout high deposition rates, boasting power sources and wires capable of dropping 12, 15, or even 20 lbs. of metal per hour. But these metrics ignore the human constraint: Welders can only consistently and accurately weld at about 15 to 20 IPM. For a manual welder to maintain a standard 15 to 20 IPM travel speed, a 3/16-in. fillet weld only requires a deposition rate of roughly 6 lbs./hr. To use a 20 lb./hr. rate on that same joint, a welder would need to move at a much faster speed, which is virtually impossible for a human to sustain with any degree of quality. When the industry pushes deposition rates beyond the physical limits of human travel speed, welders don’t gain efficiency, but they end up overwelding.

"I haven’t done the formal calculations, but at that point, you’re probably pushing 30 in./min. of travel speed for the machine. A human welder can’t weld that fast," Pharmer said.

Despite some of these gaps as a result of technology advancements, every consumable choice is an opportunity to reclaim lost minutes, from simplifying gas changeovers with quick-connect regulators to selecting the right wire diameter and shielding gas for the application. It is just one more reason that a weld shop or a welding department for a large metal fabricating company should consider letting an expert come in and take a look at operations. There’s always room for improvement.
