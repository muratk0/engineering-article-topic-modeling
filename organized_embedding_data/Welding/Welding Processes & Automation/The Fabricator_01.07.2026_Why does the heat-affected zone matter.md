A

s a weld shop supervisor, I’m often in conversations with the quality and engineering departments about various projects. Most of the work we do is with different types and grades of carbon steel, and the discussions center on maintaining control over the maximum heat input from welding. I understand it has something to do with the metallurgy of the steel and the heat-affected zone (HAZ), but can you explain it in more detail at a level a non-engineer would understand?

Yes, this is a great opportunity to discuss and simplify the importance of adhering to your welding procedure specifications and controlling your maximum heat input.

Let’s recap the fundamentals of a welding procedure specification (WPS). A WPS is the recipe that a welder should follow during any welding operation while performing work on a component that is governed by a specified welding code. Manufacturers in industries such as pressure vessel fabrication and welding, structural/bridge fabrication and welding, shipbuilding, and others are legally bound to adhere to the guidelines set forth in the applicable welding code when producing welded structures. These may include prequalified welding procedures or a WPS derived from a completed procedure qualification record test.

Keep in mind that plenty of general fabrication shops or industries are not legally bound to follow a welding code; however, their quality and consistency would greatly improve if they chose to follow a welding code book.

The control of heat input is one of several critical variables that must be adhered to. Heat input is volts multiplied by amps, multiplied by 60, then divided by travel speed (V x A x 60 / IPM), and the result is typically expressed in joules per inch. Therefore, in a WPS, the maximum allowable voltage and amperage combined with the minimum allowable travel speed set the upper limit for heat input.

Many types and grades of steel have varying tolerance to heat input and the ability to maintain toughness in the HAZ. Toughness is the ability to resist fracture at exceedingly cold temperatures. In the metallurgy and engineering world, it is well known that steel becomes brittle at increasingly low temperatures, depending on the type and grade of steel. With that being said, it would make sense that we understand the limit of that particular steel.

So what does that have to do with the HAZ, you ask? The HAZ is the adjacent base material to the weld deposit that has not experienced melting during the welding operation. The dark area next to the weld deposit, as shown in

, is the HAZ. This area experiences extreme heat from the welding arc and molten weld metal.

Because of to the extreme temperatures experienced in the HAZ, a microstructure change may occur in this zone. Base materials that undergo special processing, such as quench and tempering, or that contain small amounts of alloy, as in the case of high-strength, low-alloy steels, have a greater likelihood of a microstructure change.

The primary microstructure we are trying to avoid is martensite, a very hard and brittle structure. The reason for avoiding martensite is that if it fails or breaks, a welder has no warning sign, which is not ideal. If a welded structure is going to fail, we prefer to see a warning sign, typically in the form of deformation, such as bending or distortion. This type of warning sign requires a ductile microstructure that allows it to be identified and repaired before complete failure.

How does martensite form in the HAZ? Depending on the chemistry of the base material, metallurgists use diagrams called time-temperature-transformation diagrams. A TTT diagram considers the chemical composition of the base material and predicts microstructural changes at various temperatures during cooling. In many cases with specialty steels, if the cooling rate is too rapid, martensite forms. To prevent the formation of martensite, the cooling rate must be constrained or slowed to allow other favorable ductile microstructures to form.

Many of the steels used in various industries have well-known maximum allowable heat input limits to maintain a ductile microstructure and avoid martensite formation in the HAZ. For steel that is more prone to martensite formation, preheating is recommended or required. Preheating the weld joint before welding slows cooling, allowing more desirable ductile microstructures to form rather than martensite.

The key takeaway here is that if you are bound to a welding code, the welders shall follow all instructions contained within the WPS. (Shall is AWS terminology for required/nonnegotiable). They are required to stay within the specified preheat range, and if preheating is required, they may not skip this operation. By following the WPS, a welder is very unlikely to produce a subpar weld deposit or a weld that results in an undesirable microstructure.
