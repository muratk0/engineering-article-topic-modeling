# The path to becoming a welding supervisior

[TARİH: 01.11.2025 The Fabricator]

ARC Welding

Learn how an underrated AWS accreditation can change welding department dynamics

By

Dan Davis

A welding supervisor role provides not only management experience but also a chance to alter behaviors in the welding department, which can lead to more productivity and possible cost savings for the company.

Phynart Studio/E+

I

n late summer this year, Justin Mixon posted a copy of his Certified Welding Supervisor certificate from the American Welding Society on his LinkedIn feed with the following comment: "Pursuing the AWS Certified Welding Supervisor certification has been one of the most valuable endeavors I have undertaken to this point in my career and exposed me to the true cost of overwelding. I have shown the charts, graphs, and pictures to welders, CEOs, and welding students for years now. Everyone across the board appeared as shocked as I am."

The CWS certification is not as well known as the AWS Certified Welding Inspector certification, which Mixon owns as well, but maybe it should be. The Fabricator spoke with Mixon about his welding career, what being a certified welding supervisor means to him, and what sort of impact that kind of position can have on a welding department.

The Fabricator:

Did you start out in the industry with the idea that you might one day become a supervisor of a welding department, or did it just happen as you progressed through your career?

Justin Mixon:

I’ve learned about it as I went along. We had Airgas representatives come in and do some training at the shop that I was in when I was just starting in welding. I didn’t know anything about those certifications really.

One of the guys that was doing the training was a CWS. So that was the first time that I heard about it.

But, at that time, it was kind of like the company called in Delta Force to save us. They were there to break down our issues, determine what the root causes were, and try to eliminate them.

Seeing that, I got [a] CWI (Certified Welding Inspector certification), and a couple years later I went after the CWS.

The Fabricator:

Did meeting those consultants really alter your career path?

Mixon:

I didn’t learn to weld at a trade school. I worked my way up from doing material prep and eventually into welding. I kind of learned from an older gentleman. But I didn’t learn to set the machine up; I had somebody who would set the machine up for me. But over time I learned how to adjust the machine to make it do what I wanted to do.

Around that time, I really didn’t know what was causing my welds to act up. For all I knew, it could have been an act of God.

But the CWS is based off of a book that everybody knows as the Big Orange Book. It’s the "Certified Welding Supervisor Manual for Quality and Productivity Improvement," and it breaks down each process into essential variables. So, it is not a million things that could be going wrong that’s causing this problem. It’s one of five, six, or maybe seven different things that you have to control to make an efficient and cost-effective weld.

It really narrowed it down for me and made it so that I knew where my focus should be to correct quality issues. And I developed my training using these books.

The Fabricator:

How did the CWS manual influence your welding training methods?

Mixon:

It’s like what Stephen Covey said: "Begin with the end in mind." I try to give people I’m training for welding basic visual inspection criteria an idea of what they’re learning to create and to make the right weld. The idea is by the time that they’re ready to go off on their own, they can visually inspect their own work. They know that they’re creating a weld that’s going to be accepted. They’ll have inspectors just coming along to verify what they already know.

The Fabricator:

Do you still practice this training philosophy?

Mixon:

I don’t really do it as much now as I did, now that I’m in management. But I do instruct the inspectors on what to look for.

The Fabricator:

Do you see your path in quality as being a natural extension of what you’ve done in the welding area?

Mixon:

Yes, sir. Because in my mind, fast and efficient doesn’t kick in until you’ve done right.

Someone who has attained a Certified Welding Inspector certification might have a head start in attaining a Certified Welding Supervisor certification because of the similarities in the fundamentals portion of each test.

Funtay/iStock/Getty Images Plus

When you are making a mistake really fast, you’re not doing what you agreed to whenever the job was bid. You need to meet these minimum requirements to meet the specifications of the job. Quality, efficiency, and integrity all tie together for me with the job.

The Fabricator:

Have you met other people like yourself who’ve come up from the welding ranks to be in quality?

Mixon:

Not many. It is kind of skipping over a step. When I took my CWS seminar, there were about 150 people in the room. It was me and another guy that came from welding, at least from structural steel.

When you look at that many people, people might normally spend time as a fitter and then maybe a shop superintendent or a foreman before moving into quality, or something like that. They might have some idea of the quality requirements other than the size, length, and location.

That has made it kind of awkward sometimes. I was never a structural fitter. I worked in a miscellaneous shop. I started welding and continued on in welding. Later on, the stars aligned, and I had the opportunity to take my CWI. I got the CWI and went straight from there to management. In the span of a couple of months, I went from being a welder on the shop floor to having inspectors that I’m directing.

The Fabricator:

What was the most rewarding part of the job when you were acting in a welding supervisory role?

Mixon:

Seeing your group working as a team and the work is flowing are two things I liked to see. And if there was a challenge and people had to perform work to a certain specification, seeing 10 to 20 people adapt and meet the new requirement is rewarding. You can see the morale start to change. That is the most fulfilling part to me.

The Fabricator:

What were some of the most challenging things?

Mixon:

The honest answer would be whenever there’s a communication breakdown somewhere, and we find out later that there’s a requirement when the job is being fabricated. For example, when you have the first pieces getting loaded for shipment, but you find out the job was supposed to be galvanized, and all the welds were supposed to be wrapped.

The Fabricator:

When you determined that you wanted to pursue the CWS certification, what were your first steps?

Mixon:

I got the book and went through it. The CWS certification test has a lot of trigonometry and higher-level math. I didn’t finish high school, but I went back and got my GED. So this was a lot of stuff that I’d never seen before.

I also took the AWS online learning courses. For CWS, the fundamentals portion of the exam is pretty close to the CWI, so I really didn’t focus on the fundamentals portion that much. I just focused all on the math and the AWS online courses. I also used Khan Academy.

The Fabricator:

Do you see the higher-level math being an obstacle to people possibly pursing CWS certification?

Mixon:

Yes, because so many people have similar situations like myself. Either they didn’t complete high school, or they just have a high school degree. It can be scary not knowing where the Greek letters come from all of a sudden.

In fact, I know people who have turned down the opportunity to pursue CWS certification because they were scared to mess with the math.

The Fabricator:

Can you provide an example of how your CWS training has made an impact?

Mixon:

There was a chart from the CWS book that I used to share that provides an accurate example of what can happen. It was a part of my welder training program that I used at my previous employer and when I taught for a college.

The chart covered a weld that called for a 3/16 fillet, but one of the 3/16 fillet is a ¼ in. That is 33% additional weld volume, and it’s one leg being 1/16 longer than it’s supposed to be. If it’s ⅛ in. longer, it’s 67% more.

And it just doesn’t seem that way whenever you’re looking at it, but the majority of fillet welds have a horizontal leg that is going to be longer, at least with wire-fed processes, because you have gravity helping you—or hurting you—depending on how you look at it.

Correcting that could mean that the welder could save like $12,000 per station. Of course, this was back in the 1990s, so that might have changed.

The Fabricator:

How are you able to drive home a point like that to a welder?

Mixon:

The only way that I know to get them to see what I’m talking about is to literally have them look at the pictures. It’ll show the equal leg triangle and then show the one with the longer leg overlaying it, so you can see the difference in volume. It resolves the disconnect between the thinking about the number and seeing how much additional weld volume it is.

The Fabricator:

If you had the ability to change the way students are taught welding in schools, what kind of recommendations would you make?

Mixon:

I would emphasize visual welding inspection, making sure that the welder is able to identify common rejectable conditions. That’s going to eliminate a lot of potential pitfalls in their careers because they will learn what is not acceptable work.

If they can’t identify those rejectable conditions, then you end up getting QC involved and consuming valuable time in evaluating the welds. It’s going to involve a lot of mental energy. And then the welder has to repair it and do it again until it’s right. It’s a negative feedback loop.

The Fabricator:

What kind of advice would you have for a welder who might want to pursue a supervisory or management role in a shop?

Mixon:

I think having the right attitude helps because it’s not just about your own work. You’re going to be responsible for everybody else’s work. You’re going to have to be able to hold everyone accountable.

Some people just want to be in charge of something. Their egos need to feel that they can exert control and force their will on others. Well, that’s going to make people miserable, and nobody wants that.

You need to look at this type of role from the perspective of a shepherd. You’re guiding these people in the most profitable direction that they can go.
