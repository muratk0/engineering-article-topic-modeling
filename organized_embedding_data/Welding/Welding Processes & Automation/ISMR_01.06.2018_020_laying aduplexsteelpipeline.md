# LAYING ADUPLEXSTEELPIPELINE

[TARİH: 01.06.2018 ISMR]

"Exxon Mobil Production Deutschland GmbH awarded a pipeline installation contract in Germany to PPS Pipeline Systems GmbH."

Moritz Fankhaenel (on-site welding manager) and Jan Holtvlüwer (project manager), of PPS Pipeline Systems, and Jürgen Krüger of Polysoude, describe an innovative welding project in north Germany.

A project to add a manifold to a pumpstation for storage water facility at Rühlermoor in Lower Saxony (Germany) and to connect it to the Adorf station, by a 10.7km long field pipeline, was completed in 2017 after a long period of complex preparation. As suggested by the name 'Rühlermoor', the area consists of moorland with swampy ground, ill-suited to moving heavy equipment.

The pipeline had to cross a nature reserve, which hindered authorisation proceedings. In addition to the lengthy and complicated approval processes for this kind of construction work, specific licensing procedures and environmental impact assessments had to be carried out. Consequently, the time span allocated for pipeline installation in the field was extremely short.

The project's client, Exxon Mobil Production Deutschland GmbH, awarded the pipeline installation contract (including the tie-in to two related stations) to PPS Pipeline Systems GmbH. Applied welding technology was key in this installation as the short delivery time required rapid welding. The delicate Duplex steel in the pipeline will only retain its mechanical characteristics and corrosion resistance when treated in accordance with specific instructions. The material properties were such that any subsequent repair welding on the pipeline was ruled out. Above all, it was clear from the beginning that any impact on joint quality would not be acceptable.

PPS owns and operates modern welding equipment, which enables improved productivity when used in conjunction with Orbital TIG Welding to join pipes whilst maintaining high joint quality. PPS had already gathered a considerable degree of expertise in the mechanised welding of Duplex steel pipes in the field; a similar project for Exxon had been concluded successfully in 2015.

## Main pipeline features

The pipeline was composed of pipes DN-200 (8"), PN 40, made of Duplex steel mat. 1.4462, heat insulated with PE casing pipe OD 315 x 12.00. It was designed for working temperatures of between -10 °C and +80 °C and a working pressure of 40 bar.

The longitudinally welded pipes, with an OD of 219.1mm and a wall thickness of 4mm, have a length of 12m each. For zones with higher stability (e.g. street crossings), seamless tubes with a wall thickness of 5.6mm and lengths of between 6 and 8m were used (the overall length of these tubes was 2,000m).

Duplex stainless steel takes its name from a two-phase microstructure, in which small islands of austenite are embedded in a ferritic matrix, both phases at a ratio of roughly 50:50. This material is characterised by excellent strength, shows good toughness and ductility and, depending upon the grade, provides resistance against a wide range of corrosive media.

Welding Duplex steel is generally possible, but the extraordinary properties of the material can be badly affected by inexpert processing. In particular, the specific heat input and interpass temperature are rigorously controlled and, if exceeded, the resulting changes in material composition and the formation of coarse grain structures can cause disastrous consequences for the mechanical characteristics and corrosion resistance of the joints.

## Pipeline installation

Welding activities need to be protected against sand, dust, draught and humidity. In view of the unfavourable weather conditions in the north of Germany, instead of the commonly installed tents for pipeline laying, a container had been selected and prepared as a welding booth.

Welding activities need to be protected against sand, dust, draught and humidity.

The pipes were stored behind the welding booth (Fig. 2, left of the blue container). One after another, they were introduced through a hole in the back and aligned to the end of the last pipe of the already finished segment, which exited through an opposite hole in front of the booth. The extremities of the pipes were butt-welded together, then the segment was pulled out until the second end of the recently-joined pipe settled in the correct position and the entire procedure could be repeated.

The finished segment of the pipeline was supported by roller blocks (Fig. 2), as the insulation still had to be completed before the structure could be lowered to its final position.

During preparation of the pipeline path, the fertile surface soil had been stocked separately (Fig. 2, right of the finished pipeline segment) from the sand generated when the pipeline trench was dug. After laying, the pipeline had to be covered by at least a 1.2m thick layer of sand before the post-construction restoration could be started.

## Welding technique

Achievable progress throughout the whole pipeline-laying process depends exclusively upon the efficiency of the joining technique. The most important goal of an operation of this type is to establish a rapid and reliable process which, when applied on-site, is not susceptible to failure.

The approval procedure is a very important step to ensure the expected productivity of welding operations on-site. A realistic simulation of the working conditions allows operators and welding personnel to become familiar with the specific requirements of their mission.

Welded coupons are visually inspected for the appearance of the welding seam, weld geometry and colouration. Later, the joints are examined for adequate mechanical properties and sufficient corrosion resistance. If the results meet stipulated targets, the welding parameters can be optimised for the highest efficiency.

Although, at this stage, it is often a priority to increase the welding speed as much as possible, it should be taken into consideration that, ultimately, a stable welding process is the key to enhanced productivity. Even slightly reduced welding speeds are acceptable if unintended interruptions of the weld cycles and time-consuming reworking can be eliminated.

The approval procedure is generally accompanied by the final release of the qualified welding procedures for production, given by an independent supervisory body. The outcome is a document with exact and complete instructions as to how to carry out the welds; this WPS (Welding Procedure Specification) must be applied with binding effect by everyone involved in the joining process.

## Welding equipment

Besides the experience of the engineering department, the competence of the welding personnel and the skills of the operators, suitability of the welding equipment plays a significant role during approval and production. For the Rühlermoor project, PPS had selected an Orbital TIG welding installation which had been designed and manufactured by French company Polysoude, a global specialist in automated TIG welding and cladding technologies.

Fig. 5: Orbital TIG welding head in action with pulsed current

For easy communication, the power sources of the P6 series (Fig. 3) provide an intelligent graphic user interface (Fig. 4). Empowering search functions help to find appropriate weld cycles with proven parameters in the integrated database or, as in the Rühlermoor project, well-tried welding programs from former applications can be modified to fit the actual conditions.

To accelerate the development phase, the entire range of welding parameters is presented in a clear and comprehensive manner, all are easily accessible and, within the limits of the installation, can be modified as required. During a welding cycle, the incorporated real-time data acquisition system indicates the relevant welding parameters on the screen, modifications are monitored immediately and their effects on the welding result can be evaluated by experts.

In production mode, the real-time welding parameters are also monitored, but only a few selected values can be modified by the operator. So, in case of an incident, he can react on his remote-control pendant and try to avoid an interruption of the welding cycle in progress but will always remain within the permissible limits specified in the WPS.

The orbital welding head (Fig. 5) is a device which is fixed on the pipe by a clamping system to guide the welding torch along its circumference. The name "orbital welding" stems from the circular movement of the welding tool on an orbit around the workpiece.

Successful orbital welding depends upon reliable control of the bath of molten metal during the entire weld cycle, taking into account the influence of the continuously changing position of the torch and the increasing temperature of the pipes. The most effective measure to keep control of all weld positions during the orbital welding cycle is to use pulsed welding current, where the current intensity switches periodically between two different levels.

During a specified time period, the welding current remains at a high level (pulse current), the volume of the weld pool increases to its maximum and the desired penetration level can be achieved (Fig. 5). Afterwards, the current intensity is reduced to a lower level (background current), the weld pool cools down and its volume decreases to a minimum, so that any adverse gravitational effects can be mitigated. (Fig. 6).

## Joint quality

A great deal of attention must be paid to producing an excellent root pass, as this not only serves as the foundation for subsequent filler passes but is also in direct and continuous contact with any aggressive medium passing through it. The recommended pipe end preparation for Orbital welding is a J-Preparation (Fig. 7).

The geometry of the J-preparation can be adapted to the requirements of the process, the thickness T of the lip is chosen to allow full, but not excessive, penetration of the weld in all positions. By using mobile cutting machines, the pipe-end preparation is carried out on-site just before the welding operation begins (Fig. 8), so that any subsequent contamination of the welding gap can be excluded.

For stainless steel, heat tint of the welds is a serious concern as it can considerably lower the corrosion resistance of the material. Colouration of the Heat-Affected Zone (HAZ) at both sides of the weld seam (Fig. 6) on the outside of the pipeline is tolerated, as it can be removed by a specially designated wire brush.

However, inside the pipeline, the occurrence of any colouration or heat tint is not acceptable. The welds must present a blank metallic surface, in accordance with the rest of the pipes. Therefore, a backing gas chamber is installed inside the pipes, the entire area of which is heated up during the welding and protected by a flow of pure argon gas. The resulting smooth surface of each joint welded is documented by video (Fig. 9).

After the removal of the backing gas chamber, there is no further gas protection inside the pipeline. For this reason, the need for any subsequent repair welding is eliminated, as was the case at Rühlermoor.

## Productivity

For the entire Rühlermoor project, 1,035 welded joints had to be made, 877 of which were produced by mechanised welding. The joints of the pipes with a wall thickness of 4mm were composed of the root pass, three filler passes and a cap pass. For the filler passes and the cap pass, torch oscillation had been applied. Besides visual controls, a 100% video documentation of the inside of the roots and a 100% x-ray examination were carried out.

The planning period of 13 months was followed by eight months of work on-site. The pipeline installation started on 14 March 2017 and finished at the end of October the same year.

To carry out the welds, two welding teams were employed with two Polysoude power sources (type P6) and two MU-type Orbital welding heads (as shown in Fig. 5). The arctime to complete a joint between the pipes with a wall thickness of 4mm took one hour approximately; generally, during an 8-hour shift, six welds were produced.

## Conclusion

In most cases, pipeline installation has to be carried out under tremendous time pressure. Mechanised welding is not dependent upon the availability of skilled welders, so a more accurate plan of work progress is possible.

Orbital TIG welding is well known for outstanding quality and high reliability, even under harsh environmental conditions. Thanks to the achieved result of 100% defect-free welded joints, unintended interruption of the work and time-consuming reworking could be eliminated.

The final success of the Rühlermoor project is a testament to this.
