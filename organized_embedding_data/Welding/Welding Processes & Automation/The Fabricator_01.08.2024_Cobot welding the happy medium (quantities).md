s a $500 million-plus contract metal fabricator, Mayville Engineering Co. (MEC) has years of experience with robotic welding. Anything with sufficient volume flows to the robot cells, many of which have hydraulic fixturing to ease changeover and make setup more consistent. Yes, adding hydraulic components to a welding cell bears an upfront cost, but the resulting consistency and quality pays for itself and then some. After all, an operator failing to close just one toggle clamp can be a costly error.

MEC stands apart from the average fab shop, considering all the plants the Milwaukee-based custom fabricator manages throughout the eastern half of U.S. But even with thousands of employees and the ability to scale like few in this industry can, the fabricator still processes a range of part quantities—and therein lies the challenge with welding automation.

MEC has long perfected its approach to welding for quantities on both ends of the spectrum. Low-quantity work goes to the manual weld stations while high-quantity work goes to the robots. But what about all the jobs in the "middle" of MEC’s job-quantity spectrum?

To dive deeper into MEC’s welding automation strategy, The Fabricator spoke with David Higgs, vice president of MEC Business Excellence. According to Higgs, MEC is testing one welding technology to help bridge the gap between high and low volume, and the organization could soon be using the technology in a big way. Even at a fabricator as large as MEC—a company that has ranked No. 1 on The Fabricator’s FAB 40 for more than a decade—the welding cobot is finding a home.

The Medium-Volume Conundrum

"We have hundreds of pieces of welding equipment throughout the company," Higgs said. "In robotic welding, we have a depth and breadth that would rival anybody in our business. But we’re at a point now, being such a high-product-mix fabricator, where we’re asking ourselves, ‘How are we going to integrate cobots into our overall strategy?’ So now, we’re evaluating various equipment and applications. We’re deep in the middle of the evaluation process."

Traditional welding robot cells require extensive programming. Various options exist to make programming easier, beyond the traditional teach pendant (many of which have been covered in this magazine). Still, traditional articulating arm robots require space for guarding and infrastructure.

Cobots do require guarding. A power- and force-limited robot wielding a welding gun doesn’t eliminate the need for eye protection and fume mitigation. But they do offer greater flexibility, especially considering the user-friendly programming methods they offer.

"It’s much easier for an operator to program using a cobot, at least from our experience," Higgs said, "mainly thanks to the user interface and the ability to manipulate the equipment to set points in the weld program."

Volume isn’t the only factor MEC considers. Part complexity and worker ergonomics play big roles as well, potentially extending the work life of MEC’s welders. If a part has joints that would require a significant amount of awkward welder stances (like overhead and other out-of-position welding), programming a lower-volume part on a traditional welding robot makes sense. But for a large swath of MEC’s parts, the question of whether to automate isn’t so obvious.

This is where cobots will soon play a major role at MEC, effectively augmenting the capacity of the fabricator’s manual welding capacity. In fact, Higgs sees the technology changing the welder’s workday in some dramatic ways. "They’re not going to be welding longer runs or be ergonomically challenged. We really see some safety, ergonomic, and productivity improvements."

As the cobot welds in loop mode, the operators will complete material handling and reloading of the adjacent table using simple toggle-clamp fixturing, allowing the cobot to weld continuously. One person can handle the fixturing, tacking, and programming. That’s very different from many of MEC’s robot welding cells, where one person might program while another runs the cell.

Higgs emphasized that cobot welding really can’t touch the speed of traditional robot cells, which will always have a place at MEC (and most other fab shops, for that matter). But for lower-volume parts, flexibility matters—both in programming and the ability to reconfigure and move equipment where needed. Quantities change, customers change, delivery demands change, and MEC’s fabrication equipment needs to navigate through all of it.

Cellular Fabrication and the Cobot

Excessive handling remains one of the greatest wastes in metal fabrication. High-product-mix fabrication usually occurs either in a department- and processed-based layout—for good reason. Automated machines that cut parts out of nests, be they punches or lasers or plasma cutters, benefit from batching and grouping jobs together to maximize material yield and get the most out of the automation. These flow downstream to other process-based departments, including automated bending and welding, where technicians work to make each process as repeatable as possible—hence the reason for those hydraulic clamps at the welding robot fixture.

Low-quantity parts fit easily in this picture. An automated laser or punch might process the "long runner" jobs overnight, unattended, then run a disparate series of dynamically nested parts throughout the day. Those parts then head to manual bending, welding, and coating—the traditional job shop flow.

For the past 18 months, MEC has implemented several multiprocess cells, with scheduling that follows a version of the product wheel methodology, where every wedge represents a job and every space a changeover. Each cell might process a variety of parts for a variety of customers, though all parts in the cell do share similar or identical job routings.

Product Wheel Scheduling

But what about those medium-volume jobs? To streamline these, MEC has turned to cellular manufacturing, a big part of the organization’s MEC Business Excellence (MBX) initiative. Over the past 18 months, the company has implemented various multiprocess cells dedicated to specific product families. Blanks still come from a shared resource (like the laser), and some manufacturing steps, like powder coating, are "manufacturing monuments" by nature and require a certain amount of batching. But in between, jobs flow through multiple machines in a cell—like from the press brake to a hardware press or spot welding machine and on to a subassembly station.

As Higgs explained, these cells have become a major part of the company’s manufacturing cycle time improvement effort. They’re called "high-volume cells," but they’re only "high volume" in the context of a high-mix, high-complexity environment. The cells still process a high mix of parts, but all of them share similar routings and have similar requirements—like press brakes with certain bed lengths, tonnage capability, and tooling; hardware insertion; spot welding; or any number of secondary processes.

The multiprocess cells aren’t designed to achieve single-piece flow. Instead, operators abide by the

product wheel

(or product planning wheel) scheduling methodology. Developed by Peter and Jennifer King of Lean Dynamics LLC, the wheel helps develop standard production sequences to streamline throughput. Each "wedge" on the wheel represents the time it takes for the fabrication of a part, including setup and production time, and the spaces between represent changeovers. After the wheel makes a full rotation, the production sequence begins again.

As MEC has found, the cells and product planning wheels fit the fabricator’s production model extraordinarily well. Jobs from different customers are grouped together and run through the same multiprocess cells simply because they have similar job routings and production requirements. And, of course, the larger number of products a fabricator has, the more potential there is to find common job routings—and few custom fabricators process as many different jobs as MEC.

"It’s all about the sequence and finding common tooling," Higgs said. "And we can even modify tooling to make it easier to fit within the product mix that flows through each of these cells."

Certain sequences might optimize common or similar setups, but everything is coordinated to increase the flow velocity of disparate parts through a cell. The idea behind the product wheel is to find a good balance, with sequences and batch sizes dialed in to maximize throughput of the cell and the overall manufacturing cycle, from raw stock to the shipping dock.

As Higgs explained, the cells also allow room for hot jobs. If a customer, for whatever reason, needs a subassembly of a certain quantity very quickly, the operators within the cells can process it, then move seamlessly back to regular production. "We’re able to respond within hours now, where it used to take us days," Higgs said. "These cells have made a huge impact on our ability to respond."

Some of these cells already have spot welding stations, but MEC is hoping to implement a cobot welding station within these cells soon, once it completes its product evaluation. "Linking cobot welding to this might be a natural fit," Higgs said. "It’s a logical next step for us."

The exact setup of the welding cobots will depend on the product mix, but Higgs did describe a hypothetical scenario with two cobot stations, each with simplified fixturing and programming, positioned in a multiprocess cell adjacent to a part tacking station.

"As one robot welds a job, the operator can be processing a different part on the other cobot," Higgs said.

A traditional robot would be difficult to integrate, Higgs said, due to space constraints and layout. Within these cells, flexibility is key. Should a product mix or even the sequence shift in the product wheel, operators can move cobots to the optimal place within the cell. When the product mix changes, the equipment needs to move to accommodate.

Higgs emphasized that, regarding cobot welding, MEC still is in the evaluation phase. At this writing, the fabricator is considering several companies selling products with various platforms.

"Whatever platform we choose, we’re making the move because we like the flexibility and, especially, empowering the operator to be able to do everything." This could entail the tacking, fixturing, and programming, all performed in coordination with other workers in the cell. "There’s real power in that."

Flexible Scaling

Cobot technology will allow MEC to scale without the need to find an army of skilled welders—which, with the persistent labor shortage, have been increasingly hard to find. Most importantly, Higgs said, the cobots will allow each MEC employee to add more value.

Rather than tying up skilled welders on medium-volume parts, in which they’re laying down one straightforward weld after another, they’re wielding a MIG gun or TIG torch for applications that demand serious welding aptitude. Meanwhile, multiprocess cells (and other areas of MEC) can employ cross-trained workers who could tack, form, operate a hardware press, fixture parts in a cobot cell, and more.

Higgs emphasized that MEC will always have a place for traditional automation, from lasers and punches to tube benders, press brakes, and, of course, weld cells using traditional articulating-arm robots. The fabricator’s ability to scale is, after all, one of its key value propositions. That said, the last thing MEC wants is a highly qualified welder (or press brake operator or anyone else) spending monotonous days on simple, medium-quantity work.

As the custom fabrication giant looks to streamline its growing "middle" of the product-quantity mix, flexible manufacturing cells and welding cobots will play increasingly important roles. As most in custom fabrication know, speed is the name of the game, and that can’t come without flexibility.
