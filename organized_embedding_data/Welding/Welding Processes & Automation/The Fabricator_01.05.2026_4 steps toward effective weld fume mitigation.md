T

his isn’t your father’s manufacturing. It’s no longer dark, dirty, or dangerous. That might be a reason some recent technical school grads decided to give the trades a shot in the first place. Then they visit a weld shop. Their eyes water as they see the blue haze near the ceiling.

Looks dark and dirty to me

, they think.

Investing in weld fume mitigation might seem like a no-brainer, but finding the right system isn’t always straightforward. For insight, The Welder spoke with Travis Haynam, vice president at Nederman, Extraction and Filtration Division, about weld fume mitigation, including the available technologies and how to implement them.

For shops looking to step up their weld fume mitigation efforts, Haynam outlined a four-step process—one that, like so much else in metal fabrication—starts with knowing the technology and ends with knowing the people on the shop floor.

1. KNOW THE OPTIONS, AND REALIZE THAT NOTHING IS PERFECT

Besides ambient airflow and filtration options, hoods, and weld helmet respirators, shops have four basic alternatives for local fume extraction: a fume extraction arm that connects to a central filtration system; an arm that connects to a mobile filtration system; a downdraft table; and an on-torch fume capture unit.

Central systems usually become practical when an operation has three or more extraction points, but central systems also offer less mobility, so the best approach depends on what each welding workstation needs. Established, stationary workstations might benefit from a central system.

"The part size and geometry come into play more around the capture technique—that is, an extraction arm versus the on-torch [approach]," Haynam said.

Fume extraction arms can be designed for quick repositioning as welders make their way around a large workpiece.

Today’s on-torch fume capture systems use airflow to shroud the weld and assist gas.

An on-torch system can work in areas where the extraction arm really isn’t effective on its own, or for large workpieces where local fume extraction just isn’t practical. As Haynam explained, "When used properly, [on-torch systems] can capture effectively without impacting shielding gas flow and overall weld quality."

Downdraft tables can be an alternative, but according to Haynam, how effective they are depends on the part geometry and the effectiveness of the downdraft table. Fume wants to rise, and if welds are a certain distance above the table, a good portion of that fume might resist being sucked downward and continue floating upward.

As Haynam explained, fume capture effectiveness at a specific location (relative to the downdraft table surface) varies. Even so, if you have "taller parts or welds high off the surface of the table, fume will be difficult to capture."

2. KNOW THE WELD PROCESSES USED

Every system has its pros and cons, and some applications might call for a combination of fume extraction technologies: an on-torch system combined with ambient air filtration, for instance.

The weld processes used, including the wire diameter, provide the baseline. "Studies have shown that the vast majority of fume actually comes from wire consumption," Haynam said. "So, if you change to a larger-diameter wire, you’ll generate more fume. And if the wire’s a different material, it could generate more, and the toxicity of the fume could change."

Gas tungsten arc welding produces fumes different from shielded metal arc welding, both of which are different from gas metal arc welding. Add flux-cored wire, and fume capture considerations change. "Flux-cored processes can generate a tremendous amount of fume," Haynam said, adding that this might rule out certain fume extraction approaches, depending on what the fume measurements are, what the risk assessment reveals, and how likely welders will use fume extraction consistently and correctly (more on this in No. 3).

Also, just because you don’t see the fume doesn’t mean it’s not there. "Consider hand-held laser welding," Haynam said. "Those fiber lasers weld so hot, the particulate they produce are submicronic. A traditional wire-welding process produces larger particles that refract more of the light. Laser weld fume refracts light, too, but the [particulate] is much smaller, so it’s much harder to see. A lot of GMAW and FCAW fume particles are submicronic as well. … Still, laser welding’s submicronic fumes can present a significant hazard because they’re very respirable."

Mobile fume extraction systems can be moved where welders need them.

3. KNOW WHAT WELDERS NEED

Knowing exactly how welders perform their jobs is critical. Do they work on one product or a wide mix? Do products always fit on a weld table? Are workpieces large, requiring welders to crouch, weld overhead, perhaps move the torch into a tight corner that forces the fumes to move directly toward them? Are they in a confined space? What’s the longest bead that welders lay down during a typical shift?

Also, arc-on time can vary from one welder to the next, and not just because they’re welding different products with different processes and in different positions. Some welders have different work habits, and those alone can change their exposure levels measured over an eighthour shift. All this spurs detailed conversation that can lead to insight into what weld fume mitigation method would work best.

And as Haynam explained, this is especially true because of one immutable fact: "The worker exposure to fume is likely more related to the quality of capture than from the quality of air being returned."

A better filter won’t improve weld fume mitigation on its own. Many ask Haynam about putting HEPA filters on their fume capture systems. But if you just put a HEPA filter on a mobile fume extractor that the welder doesn’t use, or doesn’t use properly, then you’re not changing much since you’re not capturing the fume in the first place.

He added that filtration technology can be critical in certain circumstances. "But typical filters, like MERV 15 nanofiber filters, are already very effective. They’re usually capable of removing weld [fumes] from air, but again, only if the fume capture device is being used properly and consistently."

Ideally, the welding strategy and fume filtration strategy are built together. Say you have a long weld that would require a welder to reposition a local fume extraction device. Would there be a way to rework the weld sequence or procedures to allow for either shorter welds or the time it takes to reposition that fume extraction unit? Or, would an on-torch system be viable?

"If you could have multiple extraction arms and mobile units taking up floor space and getting in the way," Haynam said, "all that could make the welder’s job more difficult. That’s a critical consideration.

"Let’s say you’re working on a server rack where it’s difficult to get an extraction arm positioned within the geometry and in the best position for fume capture," he continued. "In that case, you might choose an on-torch system."

He added, however, that on-torch systems’ capture rates can vary, depending on the specific system used and the application. "The system works by creating a uniform airflow pattern around the neck of the welding torch. If a part geometry impedes that, you’ll start seeing varying degrees of performance."

Also know that when it comes to changes in ontorch fume capture performance, part geometry isn’t the only culprit. "The weld angle and technique, as well as the flow and pressure capabilities of the fume extractor, can vary," Haynam said, "all of which can affect fume capture effectiveness."

Actual measurements might reveal local fume extraction arms reduce fume exposure the best. That said, if an on-torch system is more likely to be used consistently and correctly—and doing so doesn’t negatively impact weld quality—then that might be the way to go.

Another important consideration is to ensure the filtration system design fits the welding application, especially when multiple ducts connect to a centralized collection unit. If application needs change, certain on-the-floor tweaks meant to improve a filtration system might actually make it less effective. Haynam said that dampers are a prime example. They often are installed to try to balance or control the airflow, but plugging one arm doesn’t necessarily make the other extraction arms more effective.

"We sometimes see dampers installed at certain points in a centralized fume extraction system. If you have a five-arm fume extraction system and damper one arm, doing so doesn’t evenly distribute the airflow among the four other arms. It actually causes the entire system to work in a way that it wasn’t intended, so you’re not getting the consistent airflow you need."

When implementing a fume extraction strategy, permissible exposure limits (PELs) provide the baseline. But as Haynam explained, once exposure levels are below those limits, ease of use should be considered as well. A less effective fume capture method that’s used consistently is better than an incredibly effective fume capture device that’s never used.

4. DRIVE THE CULTURE TOWARD SAFETY

To anyone outside the welding industry, the need for weld fume mitigation seems painfully obvious. "There’s been study after study after study," Haynam said. "The things you can’t see, including those submicronic fume particles, are harming you. Companies need to communicate this and ideally instill this early in a welder’s career."

Safety buy-in starts with company leaders and owners. Unlike typical capital investments, fume mitigation lacks an easily measurable return on investment, especially if a weld shop has no fume extraction whatsoever. Upgrading an existing filter system could provide energy savings, extended filter life, and reduced maintenance, but there’s no getting around the fact that starting a weld fume mitigation program from scratch costs money.

The real ROI is the long-term, indirect payback—and this includes going beyond compliance with PELs and clearing the air (literally and figuratively) to attract the best people. "I’ve been in places in which I leave and feel like I smoked five cigars," Haynam said. "You can’t wait to get out of there. Just imagine being a prospective employee walking in for an interview."

How do owners, managers, and shop leaders "buy in," exactly? As Haynam explained, loosening the purse strings and investing in fume mitigation equipment should be the final step. The first step should be talking with welders, asking about what they need and how they approach their work, then developing weld procedures in concert with the fume mitigation strategy.

Admittedly, the culture change might not be immediate. As Haynam put it, "If you have a veteran welder who’s inhaled weld fumes for 20 years and he feels just fine, you’re going to have a harder time convincing him that weld fume mitigation is important." He added that over time, with welders and other frontline personnel continually involved, attitudes and behaviors will change, which in turn will help sustain a culture that cares about safety.

A first-time visitor, perhaps a student who’s never stepped foot in a manufacturing plant before, might smell weld fumes, see blue haze hanging in the rafters, and then think,

I’m never working at this place

.

Eventually, when fume is mitigated and the air is cleared, current employees will start thinking the same way. They’ll start expecting clean air and weld fume strategies that don’t hinder their job performance. That perception will help not only a weld shop attract the best talent but also help the industry at large.
