# A welder discovers what AI can offer to a small repair shop

[TARİH: 01.06.2026 The Fabricator]

Welding

Prompts and pictures help, but in-depth expertise still resides with experienced welders

By

Jo Mackiewicz

Artificial intelligence has the potential to revolutionize many industries, but it might need more learning before it can offer suitable guidance to an experienced welder.

Dimensions/E+

A

quick Google search for "AI metal fabrication" will point you toward articles that have reported the ways in which AI has boosted production quality and improved process control. Others, such as a November 2025 article on the FABTECH site, have described some of the challenges of implementing AI in welding, such as expensive sensors and increased power usage, but pointed out some other applications that require fewer resources in real time: "Systems can monitor consumable wear and predict when a contact tip or nozzle needs to be replaced to prevent downtime or bad welds before they happen. Machine vision can inspect bead geometry or joint penetration depth more quickly and consistently than the human eye, speeding up quality assurance. Training simulators already use AI to help new welders learn faster by analyzing their hand motions and arc behavior then giving targeted feedback."

The authors of such articles have, not surprisingly, tended to address AI’s potential to facilitate large-scale production. But what about metal fabrication at the level of a shop specializing in repair and one-off fabrication?

ASKING AI

Curious to find out how AI might help assess completed repairs at the shop where I work, Howe’s Welding and Metal Fabrication, I asked three LLMs—ChatGPT (GPT 5.3 Instant), Claude (Sonnet 4.6), and Grok (Grok 4)—about a metal repair project. The repair project involved cracked, thin (about 16-ga.) metal tubing. When I posed my questions, I had already repaired the crack, using a series of tack welds to join the sides of the crack together again. But after I went home that evening, I thought about the client and the chance for my repair to fail.

The client owns a party supply business. Besides the repair of the cracked tubing, he had asked us to waterjet four 8-in. circles. He’d make these circles into targets for his dunk tank. Knowing that he had a dunk tank under repair, I imagined that the thin tubing I’d just repaired was the dunk-tank arm—destined to receive impact after impact from some ball-variety projectile once the target was attached. So I wondered: Will my repair hold?

That’s when I decided to ask an LLM like ChatGPT this question. In the end, I asked three LLMs for their assessments, giving each the same initial prompt plus two images (see

Today I MIG welded thin square tubing that had broken on its welds back together. First, I cut it apart, ground the old welds down, and then MIG welded it again. It was difficult because sometimes I’d blow a hole in the tubing. Do you think my welds will hold? I’ll upload a picture.

In retrospect, my wording could have been clearer. But nevertheless, each of the LLMs answered my question with a lengthy response.

Interestingly, ChatGPT and Claude began their replies with a statement that they intended to be "honest" and "straight" with me, as if I might have expected otherwise. Such assertions have generated conversations about LLM sycophancy—the tendency to prop up their users with hollow praise. These LLMs outputted reassurance: I would receive an objective assessment—even if it hurt my feelings.

Even so, two of the three—ChatGPT and Grok—started their assessments with praise. ChatGPT told me that I’d done "nice work" in tackling the thin-walled tubing. Grok said I’d performed "a solid repair job," and then noted that I had not generated "obvious large craters, excessive spatter, or unfilled blowholes." These preliminary assessments reminded me of how those of us who teach or tutor students in writing make comments on students’ work. It’s common practice to begin with some good news—encouragement that buoys student writers and shores them up for the discussion of revision strategies to come.

Indeed, all three LLMs listed positive assessments before enumerating the potential problems. However, at least one of these positive assessments was quite facile: ChatGPT reported, "You prepped it properly." All three noted that I’d achieved fusion of the two sides of the crack.

SOME TREPIDATION

In providing critique, both Claude and ChatGPT assessed my work based on what they could perceive from my prompt and my images rather than making unsubstantiated claims. But even so, I received confusing feedback. ChatGPT said my repair could suffer from "lack of penetration in spots," given that I’d stated that I’d had a problem with blowing holes. But then it seemed to conflate penetration with fusion: "When you compensate for burn-through by moving faster or lowering heat too much, you can end up with areas that don’t fully fuse." This jumbling made me wary of its counsel.

Perhaps worse, though, was Grok’s unsubstantiated claim about weld penetration and its subsequent self-contradiction. In assessing whether my welds would hold, Grok stated that I’d "achieved decent penetration into the base metal"—something it couldn’t know from the images I’d appended to my first prompt. Then later, in listing "potential weak spots," it noted that my welds might suffer from "incomplete penetration on the inside of the tube" and parenthetically noted that this penetration (or its absence) is "hard to see/access." Grok’s contradiction highlights the reality of LLMs: They output words linearly—one after the other—based on their training. Within-text coherence is something altogether different.

All three LLMs answered my question—"Will it hold?"—positively, but with important qualifications. They all pointed out that vibration and load play critical roles in how well a weld holds. My repair, according to ChatGPT and Claude, would be "fine" for light-duty purposes. Grok said the same without employing the adjective "fine." Interestingly, Grok provided some ways to test my welds, including a load test. (It listed an X-ray test, too, but prudently pointed out that would be "overkill.")

Both ChatGPT and Grok delineated suggestions for improvement the next time I need to make such a repair, but these recommendations seemed rather basic to me: turning the voltage and wire speed down, tightening the fit-up, keeping a short stick-out. Then again, I didn’t say that I’d been working as a welder/fabricator for nearly four years and thus already knew these strategies, so perhaps it’s unfair to knock this advice for its failure to recognize its user’s level of expertise.

PROVIDING MORE INFORMATION

At the end of their responses, both Claude and ChatGPT asked me for more information so that they could better answer the question of how well the repair would hold. Grok didn’t ask for more information, but I provided it with the same response I gave to the other two in order to be fair:

I think it’s an arm for a dunk tank. I think it’s going to have a target attached to it. People throw balls at the target.

As it turns out, I was wrong about this. Worried about whether my weld would hold as an arm for a dunk-tank target, I called the client and asked him about the purpose of the tubing. He told me that what I’d repaired doesn’t hold the target; rather, it does light-duty work on another part of the dunk-tank setup. But I didn’t know that at this point in my conversation with the LLMs.

Both ChatGPT and Claude responded to my (incorrect) postulation about the function of the tubing with concern. ChatGPT told me that because my repair is on a "stress concentration joint," it will "probably hold for a while." It continued, "I wouldn’t trust it long-term under repeated hits." ChatGPT then recommended that I gusset the corner to take stress off my welds. It also asked me if I’d like to see a "sketch" of what that gusset might look like. I received a reworked version of my own photo (see

ChatGPT had added a gusset to my image, yet it had also used yellow lines to indicate where I could add a gusset. Further, it had added what look somewhat like welds, oddly placed on the corners of the square tubing. The image that ChatGPT produced suggests it needs more training on images of real repairs.

Claude articulated even greater concern with my repair upon learning that the tubing might work as the arm for a dunk-tank target, exclaiming, "That’s a pretty important safety context!" Claude then explained its worry: The dunk-tank arm not only held the target, it also held a person over the water. That is, Claude’s training apparently had not included sufficient texts related to dunk-tank mechanisms, which really isn’t too surprising. It, too, recommended a gusset to reinforce the point of stress. It also suggested that I get a second opinion "from an experienced welder in person."

In contrast, Grok was far more optimistic about my repair’s ability to withstand repeated blows. It noted that these impacts "add some cyclic loading, but nothing like heavy structural or vibrating machinery." It also told me that many commercial and homemade dunk tanks use similar square tubing. And, finally, it told me that my continuous fillet weld on prepped steel would be an upgrade from "the original factory tacks or light welds that broke." This last would have been good news if I’d actually repaired the crack with a continuous bead, but instead I used a series of tack welds, sometimes sidetracking to fill in holes that blew through the tubing.

AN HONEST OPINION

So what’s my overall evaluation of these repair assessments? In truth, I was impressed with the ability of these LLMs to analyze the objects in my pictures and juxtapose those analyses against my prompts to provide feedback on my repair’s chance of success. Each LLM generated text seemingly aimed at bolstering my confidence, yet each also delineated potential problems with my repair. Each presented the contingencies of success, such as load and vibration. Each offered suggestions for improving the repair and recommendations for repairing the next dunk-tank arm that comes my way.

And each asked me whether I wanted more feedback and asked me to provide more input. Grok, for example, asked me, "Would you mind sharing a wider photo of the full arm assembly or the pivot/trigger mechanism? Or the approximate size of the square tubing (e.g., 1 in. by 1 in., 1.5 in. by 1.5 in., and wall thickness if you know)?" Such tables-turned prompting can generate input that will further the LLM’s assessment. But it can also simply take the conversation off track: Claude, seemingly nervous about the human being who would suspend above the tank on the strength of my welds, asked "How tall is the dunk-tank seat above the water?" Such questions—common in LLM output—extend the interaction by keeping users engaged.

To end, I’ll point out that I asked these LLMs to assess a repair I’d already completed (although, to their credit, they suggested ways to augment what I’d done). An employee at Howe’s or at a similar small shop would be more likely to ask an LLM to assess an as-yet unaddressed problem and then to describe and to illustrate potential solutions, including the drawbacks and benefits of each. That’s a topic I’d like to explore in a follow-up article, using different LLMs as test subjects. This first attempt at putting LLMs to work in repair suggests to me that these interactions might suit novice welder/fabricators best and that more than two rounds of prompts are necessary to generate feedback useful for applications in the metal-physical world.
