ISMR: What are your plans and targets for 2010 and how do you intend to achieve them? What is your overall vision/strategy for Allsops over the next five years?

SF: We are targeting volume work with the right type of customer, actively seeking partnership style projects on long term contracts including potential partnerships from abroad. New investment in a new building and machinery will also add to our capabilities in being able to supply our customers with the full range of services from design and manufacture right through to assembly.

In May this year, we will be exhibiting the colour/temperature monitor at an exhibition in Frankfurt and it is still hoped that the ultimate goal for Allsops is to eventually have its own product in this line/suite of equipment. This will add an extra regular revenue stream to our activities.

Around 20-25 regular customers provide around 90% of our income. Ultimately, I would like to look at reducing the number of customers that we service and increase the volume of work going through. That means finding new customers who can give us more volume work. I also aim to have our lasers, punch press and paint line working 24/7, lights-out manufacturing. I have a shift pattern on the back burner that will accommodate that pattern of working.

An area where companies are currently looking at making savings is in their estates, so space is at a premium now. It is no surprise that we are increasingly being asked by our customers to provide more assembly and storage services/facilities (hence our latest investment in an additional assembly area). We are also actively looking at selling some of our laser and punching capacity to companies abroad who may need a manufacturing base in the UK.

Increasing our exports is also a key priority to diversify our client base as well as forging new partnerships/alliances and strengthening existing relationships.

ISMR: What impact has the global economic downturn had on your business? What do you forecast, in terms of the UK market and economy, for the rest of this year? What are your thoughts on international economies (for exports)?

SF: Being subcontractors, we have serviced upwards of 700 customers down the years across various trade sectors and it was very noticeable that it was the shop fitting trade that slowed down first, especially in the sports shop/leisure industries. During the last month or so, however, we have seen things picking up in this area.

## FACE TO FACE

We have targeted countries within Europe (especially France and Sweden) where the economic downturn was less noticeable with a view to working in partnership with the right types of businesses. Exporting to Sweden has always been difficult so we have offered Swedish companies the opportunity (if they require a manufacturing base in the UK) to tap into our facilities/spare capacity on a partnership basis.

Ironically, although we made our major capital investments at the beginning of 2008 which coincided with the start of the global economic downturn, that machinery made us more competitive (price-wise) because we could work lights-out manufacturing and reduce our prices accordingly.

The economic downturn was also an opportunity for us to have a good look at our operation ie procedures, personnel, roles and responsibilities within the company and work at making ourselves a more dynamic, slick enterprise. We have made a conscious effort to get the right sort of work and bring back the volume jobs.

ISMR: How is your business split (ie subcontract, products etc.)? Please give percentage splits. Who are your customers (ie what sectors), where are they based and how are they split?

SF: We are currently 100% subcontractors servicing customers across a broad spectrum of trade sectors (defence, water industry, supermarket, shop fitting, kiosks, lighting companies etc.) in the UK, France and Ireland.

Our investments in the latest laser and punching equipment has enabled us to win orders from very high profile customers - one such order from a well known high street retailer is for an interactive 'pay as you go kiosk' which eliminates the need for the customer to queue and pay for their items. They can simply insert their credit card into the machine. The units are laser cut due to the large number of cut-outs and irregular shapes. In contrast, an order for an electrical cabinet is produced on the punching machine due to the large number of perforations and formed louvres.

ISMR: How do you view the development of sheet metal markets in the UK over the next five years?

SF: As with most industries, the barometer/bench mark is usually set by the construction industry and, as that recovers, the knock-on from that impacts on sheet metal markets. The 2012 Olympics are supposed to generate more work and we have registered on the Compete For 2012 Website but to date have been unsuccessful in securing any work from this source.

Having said that, I am quite optimistic for our prospects in 2010. We have just landed a substantial military order for a pontoon (floating dock) through Northern Defence Industries and we can see future potential for work in this area. However, with a General Election approaching in the UK, we need to keep an eye on how military projects are affected in the wake of any potential government changes.

ISMR: What do you feel are the greatest challenges facing sheet metal manufacturers/subcontractors at present?

SF: Competition is very, very fierce now with margins being squeezed very tightly.

There is also the threat of steel prices rising which, in itself, has knock-on effects in passing those rises onto customers who, in many cases, are tied into set prices with their customers over long-term contracts. Steel prices can make the difference between staying in business or not – when the price of oil went up, it seemed to trigger an enormous rise in commodity prices, including steel. We purchase about 5000 tonnes of metal a year so this issue affects us greatly.
