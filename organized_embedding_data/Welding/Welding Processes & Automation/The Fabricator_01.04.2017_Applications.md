Welding and fabrication shop attains continuous improvement with software

SITUATION

St. Louis Park, Minn.-based Weld-Kraft is a contract manufacturing shop specializing in custom-fabricated products. Founded in 1979 and purchased by owner Don Mohs in 1996, this welding and fabrication business serves the military, commercial, food, and medical industries.

The company designs and manufactures products from processing equipment to pneumatic and air systems, and its military products include small, nondestructive parts, such as fuel brackets and casings for electrical components. While most parts are made of stainless steel and aluminum, the shop also works with a variety of exotic metals.

It also offers support for new product development, cost reduction analysis, design for manufacturing, and manufacturing process improvement services. "Our value added is the quality of our welding," Mohs said. "What we do differently from other job shops is that we do design, support, and fabrication."

The shop’s equipment includes a Murata Motorum 2044T turret punch press and Accurpress press brake. While the turret punch had a small postprocessor for directing the X and Y axes, it was slow and outdated.

"…Continuous improvement is something that we want to have, but we also want to keep things standardized," Mohs said. "We were trying to find uniformity, consistency, and knowledge."

RESOLUTION

Weld-Kraft acquired two CAM software programs from Vero Software in 2013. Today the company uses Radan to program the turret punch press and Radan Radbend to program the press brake.

Mohs had been familiar with Radan before purchasing the software but realized that he could further increase efficiency by also adopting Radbend. With this software, Mohs and his team are able to generate accurate 3-D model simulations of the bending process. The software also offers automatic bend sequence calculation, automatic tool selection, and automatic finger-stop placement.

To begin a job in Radbend, the operator typically imports a solid model into the software. The program can import data in 2-D and 3-D formats, offering plugin interfaces for popular 3-D CAD systems, including the SOLIDWORKS

®

system Weld-Kraft uses. With one click of an icon within the SOLIDWORKS interface, the operator can transfer drawings and associated files into Radbend.

By programming and verifying bending operations offline, the company maximizes machine time and improves first-off part quality, which equates to a reduction in manufacturing costs.

Once the solid model is imported into Radbend, the operator selects his tools and material and exports the data onto a flash drive. He gives the flash drive to the press brake fabricator, who plugs the flash drive into the press brake, and the manufacturing begins.

"You can plug almost anyone into the brake press and they can go in there and start forming, to a degree," Mohs said, adding that skilled employees always retain their value. He explained that the software gives the operator four or five steps with less work and less waste of material. With the flash drive, the first part off the press brake is very close, and the software cuts setup time in half compared to the old manual process.

While dies still must be loaded manually, the software delivers enough flexibility to allow for changes while removing much of the guesswork and error from the process.

Before the software, the operator would take the press brake through all the bends manually, Mohs noted. "For a four-sided part, you would have to manually select punch and die. [The software] makes it much quicker and faster, and you can tweak it to make it do what you want."

In addition, the program’s collision-detection capabilities help solve problems before the job is sent to the press brake.

Prize turns profit on day 1 for IMTS waterjet winner

SITUATION

How surprised would you be if you won a waterjet? That’s what happened to John Mullet, owner of Laureate Machine & Automation, the winner from the "industry pro" side of the IMTS waterjet build-off competition. The installation of Mullet’s prize—a WARDJet E-1530 waterjet—at the modest-sized contract machine shop in Leipsic, Ohio, was the final chapter in the IMTS Build It! Challenge.

Laureate designs and builds automated finishing systems in addition to handling other types of contract work. The shop was equipped to do CNC machining, wire EDM, welding, and fabrication, but Mullet had also previously considered a waterjet purchase knowing the versatility of waterjet technology would complement his capabilities.

When it came time to accept the original prize—an E-1515 machine with a 5-by 5-ft. cutting area—Mullet realized that the waterjet might not serve his needs as well as the E-1530, which has a 5- by 10-ft. cutting area. Because of how standard sheet sizes run, WARDJet didn’t consider this to be an odd request, and the waterjet manufacturer offered Mullet a trade-up option, crediting the value of the E-1515 and allowing Laureate to purchase the larger model for the difference in price.

RESOLUTION

The machine was delivered in late November 2016. The installers did the ball bar calibration, and "it’s cutting within a few thousandths of circularity on a 24-in.-dia. circle. Pretty amazing for this large of a machine," Mullet said.

The Laureate crew started cutting parts immediately, and the shop made money on the first day of official operation. "We’ve already experienced a cost savings by cutting [in-house] some of the parts we were previously contracting outside," including some large tool and jig blanks, Mullet reported. "I am really impressed with the rigidity of this machine and the quality of cut compared to what we were seeing on parts cut on the cantilevered design machine our subcontractor was using."
