Inspect, detect, repair—done Automated pipeline inspection evolves

By Tim Heston, Senior Editor

EWI’s automated pipeline repair system can laser-scan an area to detect corrosion, then subsequently perform a repair weld, either by filling the corroded area with weld metal or by welding on a repair sleeve.

The oil and gas landscape has done a 180 over the past few years. North America now is a major world player, and with that comes the demand for pipeline fabrication. This includes new pipelines, of course, but also pipeline maintenance and repair.

In recent years Connie Reichert LaMorte has been busy on both fronts. The principal engineer at EWI in Columbus, Ohio, has led the organization’s effort to develop automated alternatives to a historically manual process: the repair of in-service pipelines. The task typically has involved an entire crew of skilled technicians including a welder wielding a stick electrode, a helper at the pipeline itself, a person monitoring the weld power source several hundred feet away, and several others to monitor the process to prevent burn-through and ensure welder safety.

Pipelines of higher-strength material require precise weld bead placement for proper tempering of previous weld layers to ensure the weld metal retained the desired metallurgical properties. In the past, if this pipeline was of certain grades, such as X80 or higher-quality material, then the stick electrodes used for repair often couldn’t provide sufficient weld quality.

To find external (OD) pipe corrosion the traditional way, technicians clean the pipe surface and then map it by creating a kind of inspection grid, with each square representing a measurement point. They then use an ultrasonic probe to take individual measurements at every point on the grid. If the surface measurement reveals that the pipe has reached a certain wall thickness threshold, technicians flag it for repair. This method can be expensive, challenging, and time-consuming to find corroded pipe sections and to repair them. “It’s an old method,” LaMorte said, “and it’s still fairly common.”

Since the 1990s, EWI has been developing and perfecting methods to automate pipe OD inspection. They have advanced a noncontact scanning laser system, usually a visible red laser having a similar wavelength to those used in laser pointers and grocery checkout kiosks. This system has cameras that detect the angle at which the laser light bounces back and use basic geometry to calculate the distance. Surface conditions cause the light to bounce back at a different angle, which the system senses and flags immediately. This information is sent back to a computer that crunches the numbers and produces a detailed topographical map of the pipe surface.

The laser doesn’t penetrate below the pipe surface. It’s looking only at the pipe’s surface condition. So to detect the wall thickness, the laser must scan a good portion of the pipe surface, which gives the system information about the pipe curvature. “We use special algorithms that reconstruct what the original wall thickness was,” LaMorte said. “We do this by looking at the [pipe] curvature and using math to calculate the original wall thickness.”

Still, because this method doesn’t take any physical measurements of the inside diameter, it really only infers the actual wall thickness. That’s why this technology often is used in conjunction with interior ultrasonic systems (called ID pigs) that measure the pipe’s wall thickness. Depending on the application and pipeline flow, the pig can be inserted inside as the pipeline material is flowing. Or, technicians may shut down the section of pipeline temporarily for the pig to do its work. In a typical application, a crew sends the ID pig through a section of pipeline. If the pig detects a section that could be corroded, that section of pipe (if undernground) is dug up for OD inspection with a laser-based system.

After detecting a corroded area, the system performs a repair weld.

LaMorte added that this kind of technology has been around since the late 1990s—and more automated OD inspection systems, some involving ultrasonics, have since been developed. Some even use hand-held laser scanners to analyze the external pipeline surface.

For a client project in 2005, however, LaMorte went further, combining corrosion detection with automated repair. She and her team at EWI integrated a bug-type system that rides on a track around the pipe circumference. This system has both a laser scanner, for detecting the corrosion, and a flux-cored arc welding gun.

The machine acts as a cladding system for filling in a corroded surface (that is, a corrosion patch repair), and as a welding system when installing a repair sleeve. To perform a patch repair, the laser scans a known section and detects the exact depth and shape of the corroded area. It sends this information to the controller, which generates the weld program, determining how many beads are required, the size and shape of those beads, and the number of passes necessary to fill in the corroded area. The system operator reviews the program and then instructs the machine to start depositing layers of weld metal, taking into account tempering and precise bead placement.

For a repair sleeve installation, 0.5-in.-thick or thicker half-pipe sections fit neatly over the pipe circumference and are clamped into place under the welding system, which then performs the horizontal V-groove welds (where the two beveled sleeve edges meet) and two circumferential fillet welds on either side.

Which method to use depends on the size and depth of the corroded area. When this system was developed several years ago, LaMorte explained that most repairs end up requiring repair sleeves, but the system did demonstrate it could perform cladding repair, which is much less costly. It’s ability to perform automated cladding has drawn much attention recently. Some companies in the nuclear industry, for instance, have looked into the inspection and cladding technology for use inside power plants, where most in-service repairs can’t be performed with a human present.

During the past five years, LaMorte has been heavily involved with the welding of new pipelines—no surprise, considering the energy boom. Specifically, she has worked on laser-based inspection systems for the ID weld bead on riser pipes during their fabrication on land or offshore in J- or S-lay operations. The system attaches to the internal line-up clamps (ILUCs). The welding is performed, followed by an automated ultrasonic inspection. Finally, the laser-based system performs the final inspection, looking for any signs of undercut, incomplete penetration, or other discontinuities.

So why have both an ultrasonic and a laser-based inspection system? “It’s about saving money,” LaMorte said. “A weld may give a false-positive [after ultrasonic inspection]. To remove that one weld may cost a company a quarter of a million dollars in time, materials, and labor. If you can prevent just one or two of those, you’ve already paid for that additional laser-based inspection system.”
