# The next level of welding helmet design

[TARİH: 01.12.2013 The Fabricator]

Technology Spotlight

Buoyed with an invention award, designer looks to roll out his take on a welding helmet

By Dan Davis, Editor-in-Chief

A metal fabricator’s talent is in finding a better way to build a metal part. Michael Buckman just took it a step farther and found a safer way to do it as well.

About 15 years ago Buckman was a young welder working in an aluminum plant, and only two hours into the job, he was overwhelmed with noxious fumes. For the next three days he battled bronchitis. It also got him to think that there had to be a better way of protecting himself and other welders from those welding fumes.

“That’s what sparked me into making this,” he said.

“This” is the WindMaker, a welding helmet design that won a 2013 Invention Award from

Popular Science

. A welder will recognize it as a welding helmet if he looks at it straight on (see Figure 1). It can be made to incorporate a polymer shell to encapsulate the welder’s head and a self-charging, auto-darkening lens. LED lights bordering the faceplates and a shroud that drapes over the welder’s shoulders, not typical welding helmet features, provide an extra layer of protection by illuminating the work area during welding and providing full-body coverage.

Figure 1

The LED lighting and shroud that protects the welder from sparks are features that aren’t normally a part of a welding helmet, but it’s the ventilation system that helps the Wind-Maker stand out from other helmet designs.

The ventilation system, however, is what sets the WindMaker apart (see Figure 2) from other filtration-type units on the market. The design (patented in 2007) uses a lightweight, self-charging battery to power one fan that draws in fresh air from the helmet’s rear, which is then circulated within the inner chamber, and another fan that ventilates the air out in a downward fashion in the front of the helmet. The outward discharge of air also helps to clear smoke from the welder’s visual field.

Figure 2

Because the helmet pulls in air from the rear, the welder breathes clean air while remaining cool as the air is circulated in the helmet. Air is discharged through the front of the helmet.

The helmet is designed to be easily maintained. If a new air filter is desired, the old one pops out of the rear and a new one can be put in (see Figure 3). If a new lens, such as a tinted lens for working outdoors under the sun or a clear lens for grinding metal, is needed, it slides out and simply can be replaced with a new one.

Figure 3

Various contaminant-specific filters can be used to clean the incoming air.

A second patent, awarded in 2009, covers wireless communications that can be built into the helmet. This could be as simple as a Bluetooth® connection for verbal communication between the welder and another person or as sophisticated as a camera that allows an inspector to inspect and document welding beads, via a monitor, as the welder is laying down the weld.

Buckman said telemetry also can be incorporated into the helmet, particularly in very challenging welding applications. For example, in shipbuilding, a welder often works in hard-to-access areas with little room to accommodate other workers. Advanced electronics could be used to monitor the vital signs of the welder and feed that information to a central monitoring station. A global positioning system could be incorporated into the helmet design as well.

The ventilation system is what sets the WindMaker apart from other filtration-type units on the market.

“Right now we have to find a manufacturing source,” Buckman said. “This would be very popular just in regular shop work.”

Future designs won’t look exactly like the current version of the WindMaker (see Figure 4), but they will contain the same functionality. Buckman is confident that there is a market for his invention.

Figure 4

Future helmets will have a more contemporary design. They also will incorporate solar panels to recharge batteries.

“A lot of people want it to monitor work being done,” he said. “For example, in welding schools, the instructor could click on any student and tell him what he’s doing wrong.”
