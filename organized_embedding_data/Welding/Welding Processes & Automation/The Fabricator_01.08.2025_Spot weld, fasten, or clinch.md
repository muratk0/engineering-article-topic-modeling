linching is a cold metal joining method that’s becoming increasingly popular in fabrication shops. The mechanical sheet metal joining process permanently interlocks two or more layers of sheet metal without any bolts, screws, or rivets. Unlike welding, it doesn’t use heat or filler material. Instead, the metal itself is deformed to form a "button" that holds the sheets together (see

Figure 1

).

Here’s how it works. In a clinching machine, a punch on one side and a die on the other work together to form the joint (see

Figure 2

). The sheets are overlapped and inserted between the punch and die. In a fraction of a second, the punch presses the top sheet into the bottom sheet and into the die cavity, clamping the sheets and then squeezing them so the material flows and locks together into a button shape. The punch then retracts, leaving behind a round dimple or button that is the clinch joint. Modern clinching machines—whether pneumatic, hydraulic, or servo-driven—can deliver between 60 and 80 clinches per minute in automated cycles.

Clinching technology has been around for decades. It was first patented in the late 19th century and gained popularity in the 1970s and 1980s for automotive assembly. Today, with industry emphasizing energy savings and green production, clinching is seeing renewed interest and rapid adoption as an alternative to welding.

Material and Design Considerations

Not every sheet metal assembly is a good candidate for clinching. The process has specific requirements for the materials and joint design to form a reliable interlock. If these conditions are satisfied, clinching can produce strong joints; if not, the joint may fail, or clinching may not be feasible at all.

Material Ductility.

Clinching works best with malleable, ductile metals that can cold-form without cracking. Metals like mild steel, aluminum (especially 3000, 5000, and 6000 series), many stainless steels, copper, and even titanium can be clinched successfully.

Brittle or hard materials are generally not suitable. For example, cast iron will crack under a clinching load, and high-carbon or hardened steels are too stiff to form a proper button. As a rule of thumb, if a strip of material can be folded in half without significant cracking at the fold, it will be a good candidate.

Sheet Thickness.

Clinching typically is used for thin sheet assemblies. Each sheet can range from 0.010 to 0.185 in. thick, and total stack thickness (if joining more than two layers) is usually between 0.019 and 0.370 in. Clinching is feasible in thicker materials too. Studies have proven successes in materials up to 0.393 in. thick, though press tonnage and tooling design are large factors.

The thickness ratio between the sheets matters. If one sheet is much thinner than the other, the thin sheet may not form a strong interlock. A good guideline is to avoid having the top sheet (punch side) thinner than about 50% of the bottom sheet’s thickness.

Flange Width and Overlap Area.

Clinching requires a lap joint—that is, the parts must have an overlap (flange or tab) where the punch and die can form the joint. Designs need a sufficient flange width to place the clinch. Clinching requires open flanges with good access to both sides for the tooling, and the flange must be wide enough to accommodate the interlocking button. At least 0.393 in. of overlap is recommended for a standard round clinch point, though smaller buttons (like those with diameters between 0.157 and 0.197 in.) can fit on narrower flanges.

Tool Accessibility.

Clinching needs access to both sides of the assembly: one side for the punch, the other for the die. So, the joint location must be reachable within the C-frame or similar throat opening of the clinch press. Parts that have deep recesses, boxed-in sections, or obstructions might not allow the tooling to reach the spot that needs joining. Accessibility to both sides of the part is crucial; if one side is completely closed off, conventional clinching cannot be applied.

Surface Coatings.

Clinching can join prepainted, powder-coated, or galvanized sheets without destroying the finish. There’s no high heat to burn off coatings. That said, very thick or uneven coatings (like a heavy plastic dip) could interfere with forming a tight interlock.

It’s recommended to keep coatings relatively thin and consistent in the clinch area. If the coating is too thick or soft (such as a thick rubber layer), the joint may be less secure, and testing is advised.

If these design and material conditions are met—ductile material, within thickness limits, with sufficient overlapping flange and tool access—then clinching can be a great choice. Engineers should factor in these requirements early in the design phase. Minor adjustments like increasing a flange width by a few millimeters or tweaking sheet thicknesses can make the difference between a robust clinched joint and an unworkable one.

Pros and Cons

How does clinching compare to traditional resistance spot welding or using mechanical fasteners, such as self-clinching nuts or rivets? Each method has its pros and cons.

Overall, clinching offers several compelling advantages. It preserves protective coatings and paint. It’s quick and repeatable, and it doesn’t require consumable items during assembly. You can verify, visually or by gauge, the button size to ensure the joint formed correctly; go/nogo gauges or simple calipers can check clinch button diameters. You also can use a gauge to check the clinch button’s thickness. For round dies, it should be roughly one-third to one-fifth of the total material thickness of the two sheets being clinched; for rectangular dies, the clinch button thickness should be half the total thickness (see

When choosing the right joining process, consider the application requirements, including strength. Clinching typically has 80% of the strength of a comparable spot weld. Multiple clinch points can be used to meet strength needs, but this approach might not be ideal for heavy structural loads or very thick stacks. Meanwhile, each spot weld, when done properly, can equal or exceed the base metal strength in thin gauges.

Fastener joint strength depends on the type of fastener being used. The joining method generally provides a secure attachment. In fact, a strong rivet or bolt can exceed welding strength in tension.

Regarding cosmetics, clinching produces a visible round button on one side and a slight ring imprint on the other. It produces no burn marks or discoloration, and coatings remain intact. Joints are consistent in appearance but, because of the protruding button, are not flush. That button might be acceptable or even hidden in many designs, but it’s not as "invisible" as a spot weld can be.

Spot welds can be very discreet; the weld nuggets can be nearly flush with the surface. However, welding often leaves a heat tint or burn mark, especially on coated or stainless materials. Cosmetic surfaces might require grinding or touch-ups.

Hardware insertion, of course, leaves a visible fastener head or flange. Pop rivets and screws have heads, and self-clinching nuts leave a slight ring or head on one side. Appearance can be an issue if a smooth surface is required. Some flush-mounted hardware can mitigate this.

Regarding speed and efficiency, clinching offers fast cycle times, about one second per joint, as well as minimal setup between joints. It gives high throughput in production, especially since there are no consumables to reload each cycle. Tooling lasts for thousands of cycles and requires only occasional adjustment. Also, operators can be quickly trained. In industries like HVAC duct fabrication, clinching has proven to greatly increase throughput.

Spot welding occurs in a fraction of a second, but each weld does require electrode alignment. Cycle time may slow because of electrode maintenance (dressing, cooling periods), and skilled operators or careful setups are needed for quality. In high-volume settings, tip changes and cooling can cause downtime.

Hardware insertion is slower, as it involves handling of separate pieces. Insertion presses typically take a couple of seconds per fastener (including feeding the nut/rivet). Holes needing prepunching add processing time upstream. Automated fastener feeders can speed up the process, but each part adds to cycle complexity. Changeover for different fastener types also can affect throughput.

Spot welding machines have moderate capital cost. Ongoing costs include electrode tips (which wear and must be replaced), electricity usage, and maintenance of water chillers or transformers. If welding galvanized or coated steel, electrodes degrade faster (requiring frequent dressing). Labor for skilled welders can be significant. For large batches, welding can be cost-effective, but frequent downtime for maintenance can reduce efficiency.

Meanwhile, fasteners have a direct per-piece cost. Insertion equipment is relatively inexpensive, though, and one press can insert many types of hardware. However, inventory management of fasteners, feeding systems, and the additional weight of hardware on the product are factors to consider.

For small-lot production, hardware insertion is simple and requires a low capital investment. In high volume, the fastener cost can outweigh other methods. On the flip side, hardware can add value (threads, for example) that other methods can’t, potentially reducing costs in other areas of the design.

In practice, fabrication shops use a combination of joining methods: clinching, spot welding, and hardware insertion. The key is understanding the tradeoffs and playing to each method’s strengths.

Where Clinching Excels

Clinch joining is best suited for sheet metal assemblies that are ductile, thin, and produced in high volumes where speed and consistency pay off. It particularly shines when traditional welding or fastening would be problematic or inefficient. In some environments, welding simply isn’t feasible—for instance, if the parts are coated in an oil or zinc that would produce toxic fumes when welded, or if the assembly area is in a location where bringing welding equipment is difficult.

When dissimilar metals need to be joined (such as aluminum to steel or copper to aluminum), metallurgical incompatibilities can make welding difficult or impossible.

Clinching is a mechanical bond and, hence, well suited for dissimilar-metal joining. Aluminum sheet can be clinched to copper bus bars in electrical applications, or stainless steel can be clinched to aluminum for enclosures—combinations that you cannot spot weld.

Clinching isn’t limited to just two sheets, either. It can join multiple layers in one go, which is helpful for certain composite stackups. Some designs clinch a layer of insulation or polymer film sandwiched between metal sheets. For example, clinching can capture a thin layer of insulation—such as a 3/16-in.-thick urethane sheet—between two metal panels. This makes clinching useful in assembling laminar components, like certain automotive brackets or electrical motor cores, where several steel laminations can be clinched at once.

Of course, clinching is not a silver bullet for every situation. Engineers must evaluate each joint’s needs. If a joint requires ultimate strength or must be invisible, or if the geometry defies a C-frame tool, then spot welding, adhesive bonding, or mechanical fasteners might be better options. Often, the best solution is a hybrid approach, using clinching where it makes sense and using other methods where they fit better.

For engineers and shop owners, the key takeaway is this: Consider clinching early in your design process. If your assembly meets the criteria for clinching, it can be a game-changer for your manufacturing efficiency.

And if not, understanding why will guide you to the appropriate alternative. In the end, the best joint design is one that balances the method to the application, knowing when a clinch will clinch it and when it won’t, is what separates a good fabrication plan from a great one.
