# Worry-free wire feeding

[TARİH: 01.10.2013 The Fabricator]

Technology Spotlight

Wire guiding system reduces friction that results in smooth operation

By Dan Davis, Editor-in-Chief

You don’t have to have a subscription to a newspaper to know that the automotive industry is hopping. The automakers need all aspects of their manufacturing operations to remain up and running if they are to be able to keep up with current demand for vehicles. Downtime is not acceptable.

But it happens. Even automated welding cells shut down. What makes it especially frustrating is when the reason for the downtime is not a robot or welding power source failure, but faulty wire feeding. If the feeder can’t muster the force necessary to pull the wire through multiple curves and over a long distance, dry-roll slippage occurs. The welder gets burnback because no wire is present, and an arc outage results.

Ed Cooper, a former welding instructor and now a developer of welding productivity tools for manufacturers, said these types of outages are rarely a one-time event. If poor wire feeding is responsible for an arc outage, the manufacturer is probably dealing with this as many as 10 or more times per month. It occurs so regularly that some companies consider it to be a normal part of the welding operation, not realizing it is something that can be minimized or eliminated.

“If the manufacturer is not tracking its arc outages, each shift will continue to deal with them over and over again. This can add up to excessive and costly downtime,” Cooper said.

Cooper recognized the problem: the skid friction that the welding wire experiences as it is being pulled through the inner diameter of the conduit that feeds the welding torch. That skid friction dramatically increases with each turn or bend that is present in the conduit feeding the welding cell.

“The big thing is the conduits,” Cooper said. “You are just pulling through a liner or a long coil. In these conditions, they will work fine for a short time, but over time you will see a difference when the liner or the coil starts to degrade. At that point, the compounding effect increases. It gets tougher and tougher to pull the wire through.”

Cooper’s company, ELCo Enterprises Inc., Jack-son, Mich., has looked at several ways to solve this problem. Some of the research resulted in a welding wire with a proprietary coating that was a better fit for flexible conduit. That wire still had issues with friction over time, according to Cooper.

That led to a focus on the actual conduit and, ultimately, to the development of the company’s Wire Guide Module (see Figure 1). The module is designed for use in areas where a bend or corner is required in the conduit used to feed the welding wire. The module design has a series of rollers with bearings that allow the wire to feed easily to the welding torch.

Because the skid friction around bends and curves is minimized greatly with the use of the modules, a manufacturer can set up a central wire feeding station from which wire can be fed to welding cells 100 feet or more away.

“At this point, the skid friction goes away. You can go out 10, 20, or 30 feet in a straight line, and you’ll feel very little skid friction. Then you make a tight turn into a cell or into a robot, and you can put a module there,” Cooper said.

Each 45-dgree module can be connected to additional modules to form turns of 90, 135, or 180 degrees. Custom-designed modules also are available.

One logical placement of the Wire Guide Modules is near a welding cell.

“The only thing you have to worry about is drawing compounds, but our module is designed so that you can actually take it apart, clean it out, and put it back together. It just doesn’t wear out,” Cooper said.

Drawing compounds used to be more prevalent in wire welding several years ago, but the manufacturers have designed wire that flows much more smoothly through conduit than previous generations. Drawing compounds still are an issue, Cooper said, and they can prove to be detrimental to other wire-feeding assist devices that rely on plastic rollers and pins. Those plastic parts simply can’t stand up to the combination of drawing compound remnants and shop dirt that accumulate within the conduit.

The company has a 5-in.-radius module for light wires and a 10-in.-radius module for light and medium wires. For the larger wires in the 0.125- to 0.15625-in. range, a 16-in. radius is available.

“We actually use tool steel rollers with bearings in them for our large modules,” he said. “The polymers are fine with light wires and aluminum.”

To help overcome any skepticism on the shop floor, Cooper said that before his company installs its Wire Guide Modules, it conducts tests to determine the load put on the welding wire being pulled into the cell and keeps track of arc outages over a two- or three-week period. That way the manufacturer has a point of reference when the modules are installed to assist with the wire feeding.

Once the modules are installed, the welders recognize the difference, Cooper said. The arc is much more consistent, not pulsating, because the feeder doesn’t have to work as hard to pull or push the wire.

The arc outages are reduced, if not eliminated, and uptime in welding cells increases. That’s a recipe automakers and all manufacturers can appreciate.
