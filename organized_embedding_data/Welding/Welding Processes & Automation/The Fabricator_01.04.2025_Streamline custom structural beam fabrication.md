Vertical beam welding makes its mark

Tim Heston

W

hen structural fabricators are asked to weld custom beam sizes, they usually first ask if those custom sizes are truly necessary. They sometimes are, especially when working with some of the extremely large beams used in bridge fabrication and other infrastructure projects.

If they need a custom beam, most U.S. fabricators rely on mechanized setups designed inhouse. Some use welding carriages and similar devices, and most weld beams in the horizontal ("H") position, with the web section parallel to the floor. They develop their own tricks for creating clean fillet welds between the flange and web, all while carefully monitoring and adjusting for distortion. The task usually calls for a large, experienced team of welders, and making it happen isn’t cheap.

"That’s not how the rest of the world does it."

That was Andy Overton, vice president of sales at Ficep Corp., Baltimore, which recently entered a partnership with Italy-based Corimpex Group to introduce a vertical beam welding system to the U.S. market. The Corimpex BWMTC beam welding machine will make its official debut at this year’s NASCC: The Steel Conference. The technology aims to streamline and standardize what for U.S.-based fabricators has been a homegrown process. The idea is to make the fabrication of custom beam sizes more predictable and not so labor intensive.

Predictable Flow

The key to that predictability, Overton said, "is tight control over distortion. The machine allows you to prebend the three components [two flanges and the web] to ensure that when they come out of the machine, they’re straight or curved as needed."

He added that the Corimpex technology complements Ficep’s Enterprise gantry drilling line, capable of handling a wide range of workpiece sizes—including some of the odd or extremely large beams coming from a beam welding line. In some homegrown setups, operations drill holes manually before welding the custom beam. Whether manual drilling takes place before or after beam welding, the task involves a lot of time-consuming calculations that don’t always account for the effects of heat distortion after welding. The result: lots of reaming and other adjustments downstream in the shop and on the jobsite.

"We’re finding that automating this [drilling] process is giving us precise hole accuracy and up to 100 man-hours savings per connection," Overton said. "We’ve had one operation tell us they’ve achieved 1/16-in. hole repeatability over 150 ft." The idea, he said, is to promote smooth flow throughout the shop and to the jobsite. And for custom beams, the flow could start with vertical beam welding.

Going Vertical

Operating the BWM-TC vertical beam welding system, a technician first loads the top flange onto the machine, while arms move the web into the vertical position. The operator measures to double-check the web position, then tacks it in place. The system then commences the welding cycle, with twin subarc wires on either side of the web laying down a weld bead to create a single- or (if required) multipass fillet weld. The operator periodically checks its position during the welding cycle. Automated torch preheating, positioned about a foot before the subarc welding guns, can be integrated into the process if needed.

The first pass creates what looks like an upside-down T. After this, arms rotate the T as the operator instructs the machine to position the bottom flange. The T is lifted into place on top of the second flange, creating the familiar I shape. The operator tacks the second flange in place, and the welding cycle begins again, ultimately creating a custom I-beam.

Most significant is the machine’s prebending capabilities. "It actually pre-bows the toes of the flange," Overton said, "so that when it welds, the beam comes out flat. There’s a lot of calculation involved to make sure you get a beam that’s within tolerance."

Specifically, the machine’s hydraulically driven concentric rollers prebend material before welding, just enough so that when the welding occurs, a straight shape emerges after cooling. If the job requires a camber (curve in the horizontal axis) or sweep (curve in the vertical axis), the machine can be configured to produce those shapes with special roll shapes before and after the welding zone. The web and flange sections can require special preparations to produce a beam with a specific camber or sweep radii, but that work is well worth it if it eliminates a cambering or angle-roll operation downstream. The machine can operate in several configurations. The typical way is to feed one flange and web section through, then return and rotate the welded "T" section toward the infeed side of the machine, where it’s run through again to weld the second flange and create the I-beam. In high-volume setups, one vertical beam welding machine welds the top flange; at this point, the T rotates and is fed to a second machine to weld the bottom flange. As Overton explained, what setup makes sense depends on available plant space and throughput requirements.

Other Shapes

Most custom beam welding occurs on I or H shapes—but the machine isn’t limited to those. It can also produce custom box beams. Welders tack together the beam, then present it to the machine. At this point, the subarc welding guns are manually repositioned during the machine setup according to the required welding path. In the "T and I" configuration, the guns are close together, positioned to weld both sides of a beam’s web section. To weld a box beam, they spread apart and position themselves to weld whatever box beam joint geometry is required. This could be a fillet weld for box beams with a flange protrusion, or a corner weld for box beams with top and bottom plates almost flush with the sidewalls, creating a half-open corner joint.

The machine can even handle unconventional beam shapes, including star beams (with profiles that look like a plus sign). Welders need to manually tack the workpieces into place, but once they do, they can load the assembly onto the beam welding line, and the subarc guns are then manually repositioned to suit the required fillets. The star beam needs to be rotated for the subarc guns to access the fillet joints above and below—but that mild amount of handling is nothing compared to the manual alternative.

Accounting for Distortion

Corimpex also offers machines that weld beams in the horizontal configuration. These work especially well for beams with a significant amount of tapered flanges and other odd shapes. But for the vast majority of I-beams, Overton said, the vertical "I" orientation has proven to be much more repeatable—especially for large beams.

"When you start welding the bigger beams, like the ones required for many of the large bridges being designed today, the ideal way of welding a custom beam is the gravity way, vertically, in the T and I configuration.

"It allows you to control distortion, and it allows you to prebend the three components to ensure they come out straight or, if need be, curved."

The vertical beam welding machine accepts flanges up to 6.5 ft. and web sections up to 15 ft. That’s quite a structural beam, and it can take serious man-hours to handle and weld in a conventional way.

Automated vertical beam welding aims to reduce those hours and, hence, streamline production. When the fabrication of custom beams isn’t quite so costly, an operation can really set itself apart.
