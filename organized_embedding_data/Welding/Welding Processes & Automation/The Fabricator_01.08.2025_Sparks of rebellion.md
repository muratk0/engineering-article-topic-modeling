The role of welding in a galaxy far, far away

By

Jo Mackiewicz

FROM

THEFABRICATOR.COM

Even though welding can be seen in many "Star Wars"-related movies and TV shows, it doesn’t mean that it’s being done correctly. Keen eyes can sometimes see some problems with, say, a Chewbacca welding attempt. It’s a Wookie mistake.

If you’ve watched "Andor," the brilliant "Star Wars" series that recounts the birth of the organized rebellion against the Empire, you might have noticed the important role that skilled trade competence plays in differentiating the good guys from the bad guys and in furthering the burgeoning resistance to tyranny. "Andor" got me thinking about the role that welding and metal cutting processes play in character and plot development in the "Star Wars" movies and in the various TV series that have extended the original story.

But first, it’s important to acknowledge that although welding in a galaxy far, far away resembles welding on Earth, it’s not exactly the same. The writers and their creative teams seem to draw inspiration from oxyacetylene welding and cutting and SMAW. But the instruments they use differ from your typical stinger electrode setup. And their welding techniques—while apparently perfectly acceptable in that far-away galaxy—would get you fired in our own Milky Way.

Take, for example, Chewbacca’s work on the Millenium Falcon in "The Empire Strikes Back." Early in the movie, Chewie sits on top of the Falcon, making repairs with a long welding wand with what seems to be a stick electrode attached. Rather than carefully laying a consistent bead, Chewie repeatedly and one-handedly (paw-edly?) strikes the electrode against the metal components, generating sparks and a sizzling sound. He holds goggles in one hand up to his eyes, but he doesn’t use gloves or any other PPE.

When I asked the "Star Wars" subreddit about welding in the films and TV series, commenter CallMeKate-E wrote:

"Ok I’m a QA inspector in real life. For 20 years I’ve inspected welds for cracks. Chewbacca is a crap welder. Stick welding should not be sparking that much. All those sparks mean he’s welding cold. Which kinda makes sense cause it’s Hoth… but it means the metal is going to crack like woah. I get that it’s a rush job of a repair, but jeez, it’s a damn spaceship. It needs to be airtight."

Even though Chewie doesn’t weld according to our standards, his work sets up one of the movie’s story lines—the misadventures brought on by the Falcon’s ongoing malfunctions. So, his welding furthers the movie’s plot.

Besides advancing the story, welding signals the good guys’ technical competence. For example, in making ship repairs, Leia shows that she, too, has mechanical know-how. While the Falcon is parked in a giant asteroidal space slug, Leia uses what seems to be an oxyacetylene torch (shaped a bit like the thing your doctor uses to check your ears) to repair yet another faulty Falcon part. As a princess, she already had moxie, but at this point in the saga, she’s got practical skills that help get the gang out of a jam. In the series "Andor," mechanic Bix is another woman who has developed skilled trade competence; she wears a welding hood and uses something that seems akin to a carbon-gouging rod to remove a ship’s part.

Welding also helps convey the scrappiness of the people in the resistance and their ability to use bricolage to resist the Empire’s oppression. On Rebel bases, sparks fly as Xand Y-wings receive repairs. As commenter MovingOn1221 on the "Star Wars" subreddit put it, "Yavin 4 welders are the hardest working people in the galaxy." Top 1% commenter CrossP insightfully pointed out that the good guys must constantly make do with less: "Only the empire tends to have stuff that looks like it all came off the same assembly line (or the CIS in the prequels). Rebels are always custom building, scrounging parts, and aftermarket modifying everything." CrossP continued, "It has a sort of anti-corporate anti-waste anti-consumerism feel."

Responding to my question about welding in the "Star Wars" subreddit, folks discussed other appearances of welding in the films and TV series. They even pointed out its importance in the associated video games. In the game Jedi Fallen Order, for example, the character Cal carries a hand-held welder. And in Star Wars Outlaws, you can get a fusion cutter to slice through metal gates. In these games, you use welding and cutting like the Rebel good guys do—to persist in the quest for freedom from strongarm tyrants.

I’m not the first one (or even the hundredth) to notice the ubiquity of welding in "Star Wars." In such discussions, fans often cite this explanation from Irvin Kershner, director of "The Empire Strikes Back": "Do you know why I had Chewbacca work with a welding torch on the Falcon? That was the only thing I could find where you could see what he was doing. If he was screwing something with a tool, you wouldn’t see anything. So whenever anything needs fixing, you’ll notice the characters are welding!"

That’s true enough. But welding does much more than that. By welding—even with the alien processes and tools that pop up in the "Star Wars" galaxy—characters show that they are both practically competent and relentlessly determined. You need to be both to beat back autocracy.

Jo Mackiewicz

is a welder and a professor at Iowa State University in Ames, Iowa.
