# Artist seeks to convey change and motion through sculpture

Travis Pond uses reclaimed metal, spontaneity to craft award-winning art

By

Lincoln Brunner

To get the right feel into his pieces, Pond likes to let ideas run freely and spontaneously while he’s sculpting—a method that MIG welding facilitates easily.

This sculpture of an eagle in flight took Travis Pond four months to construct.

W

hen Travis Pond was 19, he sold the third metal sculpture he’d ever welded—a great blue heron. In a way, that piece has come to symbolize an art career that’s flown to greater heights than any he reached when he was cutting school to hit the ski slopes in Montana.

"I majored in snowboarding," Pond, now 50, joked about his first college experience. "The school didn’t ask me to come back because they didn’t offer that as a major. So, I moved back to Portland [Oregon] and took classes at a community college, one being sculptural welding.

As soon as a I found metal as a medium for making art, nothing else would do. I was so impressed with the immediacy of structure that you could achieve with welding." Despite switching majors several times within the arts, Pond landed a scholarship at the San Francisco Art Institute. While enrolled there, he worked as the studio technician in the sculpture department. He also found an apprenticeship with Bay area artist Carl Dern, making high-end designer furniture and assisting Dern with sculptures.

"I learned how to finish metal on a whole new level—working with acids for patinas as well as what finishes to apply in which conditions," Pond said of the experience. He credited his time at SFAI and with Dern for getting him where he is today.

That would be Steel Pond Studios, south of Portland, where the avid outdoorsman and amateur arborist has lived and worked for more than a decade now. His work has won numerous awards, including, most recently, the 2025 Percival Plinth Project People’s Choice Award from the city of Olympia, Wash.

Pond creates his pieces with inspiration from myth, legend, and nature—buffaloes, dragons, bears, cranes, foxes, eagles, even musical instruments. He also fabricates functional work such as furniture, gates, and architectural elements. He fashions his pieces almost exclusively from reclaimed and repurposed metals that he harvests from a huge array of agricultural, industrial, and urban sources: microwaves, motorcycles, silverware, old army gear, and surplus tractors included. It’s all cut up with plasma cutters and band saws and meticulously organized in his studio.

"In the beginning, it was what I could afford, what was around," Pond said when asked what first sparked him to use scrap metal. "It was what I connected to. The audience can relate to all these materials because they’re all from parts and pieces of your life. It was recycling before it was cool. And I think the stories and histories of the materials have a bigger voice sometimes than the sculptures themselves.

"I try to have as many random pieces as possible—more than 50% more than what I think I’m going to need for any given project." He said spontaneity and quickness are how he infuses the subtle gestures and suggestions of movement in his pieces.

Early on, Pond took to MIG welding, so much so that despite not being allowed to use any electric-arc welding in his first classes, he bought his own MIG welding machine to use in his parents’ garage because it lent itself so readily to his fast-paced style.

"It’s just a heavy-object hot glue gun," he quipped. "So that helps with the spontaneity, as well, being that it’s so immediate with that tool."

One larger movement-inspired project that Pond is particularly proud of is a giant eagle in flight—13 ft. tall with a wingspan to match —that he spent four months constructing for a veterans memorial near Portland.

Comprising the sculpture are a World War I helmet, artillery shells, and parts of tanks and Humvees, all collected from donations from veterans and a collaborative effort with from Joint Base Lewis-McChord near Tacoma, Wash. The larger-than-life piece even includes Pond’s grandfather’s trenching tool from World War II.

"It literally has a ton and a half of cool military stuff in it," Pond said. "That was probably the most memorable aspect because the veterans who see it can recognize parts and pieces that make up the whole. It’s about the veterans and their journey. It’s very personal and quite an honor to have had the opportunity to create the sculpture.

"We found a pile of machine guns going to scrap when we were on the base, but I wasn’t allowed to have them—rules or something—but the next day two officers drove down with a smaller pile of them all de-milled and cut up so I could incorporate them into the sculpture."

Sometimes the material comes to Pond ready to go, but some other jobs, like his current commission from the American Association of Nurserymen, require slightly more work on the front end. That sculpture depicts a line of leaves blowing down a 30-ft. wall. Made entirely from repurposed steel, the line starts with a leaf that’s more than 5 ft. high and ends with one just 8 in. tall. To find the right material, Pond has been sorting and arranging piles of metal—cutting them up, bending them, and softening the sharp edges in a deburring machine—for months now.

In the middle of that, he’s been working on a stainless steel seated fox and some other smaller sculptures just to keep the creative flow going in his mind. And while, ideally, Pond would love to devote more time to the pursuits that intrigue him most—mythology and classical concepts—he’s been at this long enough to know what butters his bread.

"I have several theories that I’ve been working on for decades now, and you know, I would love to be able to follow those interests instead of commissions, but new challenges are good and that is how the system works. We gotta do what we gotta do to follow our hearts."
