Sasquatch statue surprises drivers in British Columbia

A

ldergrove, British Columbia-based welder/fabricator Dan Owens knew when he installed a 10-ft.-tall steel sasquatch on his property it would draw some attention. It was just a matter of determining how long it would take for word to get out.

"I got the call from the local paper about a month after I erected it on Dec. 10 last year," he said. Since then, he’s had a number of calls about the statue from local and national media.

"I’ve done a lot of different, very intricate jobs, but all you have to do is build some big, stupid thing and all of a sudden everybody wants to talk to you," Owens joked.

The draw of the sasquatch is undeniable, though. It stands among some trees just far enough from the road that, as Owens described, "You pass by and think, ‘Did I just see that?’"

Design Meets Skill

Owens found the design for the sasquatch online, along with many other designs for horses, gorillas, and more. Each looks a little like an origami approximation of the animal in question.

"Most of the designs are a 1-to-1 scale," said Owens. "I started by making a gorilla and moved on to making a foal and a number of horse heads. They are all well designed in that the way the metal needs to be bent means that, in final construction, there aren’t a lot of inside corner welds. They’re harder to prep."

Anyone can order the sasquatch design online, Owens is just one of the first to have built it. There were around 130 metal parts to the final construction, and each required two to four bends. Although he has a plasma table in his shop, he opted to subcontract to a laser cutting shop so that he could reduce his prep time. Owens himself did all the press brake work required.

"It’s essentially a 3D puzzle once all the pieces are ready," said Owens. "The skill at my end is getting those panels to line up perfectly so that you have a nice, even, consistent weld that you can finish properly. If you’ve got five corners all coming together in one spot and one of the angles is off or higher than it should be, they won’t come to a perfect point. The average person wouldn’t notice that sort of error, but it would bother me."

Tool Support

Owens put 80 hours into the statue. He noted that working on about a half-dozen smaller animals first helped him get a hang of how the form of the designs work.

His shop tools also helped.

"The biggest thing is that I have a hoist in my shop that I put a block and tackle so I could hang the statue and spin him around however I needed to get the best angle for welding," he explained. "If I were just doing it on the shop floor and a table, it would have been much more difficult. You can’t climb around him that well."

The other concern Owens had was how to anchor the statue.

"One of the few other shops that have built it, a guy in Kentucky, used cables coming out of the ribs to anchor it to the ground. I felt like that wrecked the whole image of it."

Owens instead anchored it with a post that goes up 4 ft. into one leg and then 6 ft. down through a plate secured on the ground.

"You can actually spin him around on the plate with one finger," said Owens. "It’s kind of trippy for some people because they won’t drive by that often and all of a sudden, he’s moved."

Fine Finish

The 12-ga. cold-rolled steel sasquatch weighs in at 800 lbs. Even though Owens built it in two halves that bolt together so that he could have it powder coated, it was still too big. He instead placed it on the property as-is to see how it rusts in place over the course of a year. If, after a couple seasons, he likes how it looks, he’ll clear-coat it to maintain that look.

"I hit ‘em with a 36-grit top-cut abrasive, and you would never know it was that coarse. By the time I blended it all, he was just shining," said Owens. "The only benefit to the powder coat is I’ll never have to touch him again in my lifetime."

Enjoying the Job

Owens started welding at the age of 5, when his father started teaching him how to stick weld. He’s been working as an independent contractor for 10 years now.

"I got tired of working for other people," he said. "I know how to do my work, and I have the patience to do the fine-detail work that I think younger welders struggle with. I work on everything from heavy industrial jobs to handrails for large homes in North Vancouver. I did one house right on the water where we were making stainless steel rails for $500 a running foot. Everything had to be handled with kid gloves to get the finish they wanted. There aren’t a lot of guys that are patient enough for that work."

Mainly, though, Owens likes being able to choose the jobs he does. He loves the work, but it’s also his entertainment. All of the sculptures started as projects for him.

"If someone wants to buy them, that’s great, but it all started as challenges for me," he said.

Most of the designs Owens has built from plans found online are a 1-to-1 scale. He started by making a gorilla and moved on to a foal and a number of horse heads, a couple of which he has turned into chess pieces for the end of his driveway.
