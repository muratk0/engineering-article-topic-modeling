# Affirming weld quality before the inspection

[TARİH: 01.08.2025 The Fabricator]

Weld Monitoring

Using artificial intelligence to prevent mistakes as they happen might be the ultimate weapon in optimizing welding performance

By

Dan Davis

In-process monitoring of the weld pool coupled with the power of artificial intelligence can help to ensure consistent, high-quality welds that make life easier for quality personnel.

M

any metal fabricators subscribe to the same quality philosophy: The best way to prevent poor-quality parts from leaving the shipping dock is to prevent it in the first place. In the coming years, artificial intelligence used as part of the welding process is going to play an increased role in making this philosophy a reality for companies of all sizes.

The foundation for this evolution is being laid today. Optical and even audio tracking increasingly are being used to monitor automated welding processes, and AI is being pulled into the scenario to help with even more real-time processing.

And, if there ever was an application where AI could be helpful, it’s welding. This isn’t just one activity, which would make life a lot simpler for those involved in developing quality tools to improve welding outcomes. Welding is made up of several processes, which entail different technologies being applied to different materials and in various applications. That’s a lot of variables, even in an automated setting.

To learn more about the promise of AI when weld monitoring, The Fabricator spoke with Xavier Jauregui, chief operating officer at Xiris Automation, a developer of weld monitoring cameras and devices that recently worked with a well-known blue chip company to develop an AI-based system for optimizing the weld inspection process in real time.

The Fabricator:

How would you describe to someone not well versed in the world of automated welding how weld monitoring and AI can work together?

Xavier Jauregui:

Xiris Automation has been a machine vision company in the welding industry more than 15 years. It comes from a machine vision background, where it developed technology for the inspection of CDs and DVDs.

How that relates to AI is that we’re able to take images from the welding process and teach the AI engine what a good weld looks like and what a poor weld looks like. Our powerful machine vision tools are very helpful in this process. These tools allow us to segment and define specific areas of the weld pool to clearly illustrate to the AI what is part of a quality weld.

With traditional image processing for AI, especially with convolutional neural networks, you need a substantial quantity of these images. In our case, because we have the first step—analyzing the images with machine vision—which is one step lower than AI, we can segment the data, and with smaller quantities of data, we can teach a traditional AI engine what good and bad welds look like. So the number of images that we collect in order to be able to come up with a conclusion is smaller.

This all happens as the welding happens. This is very important because a lot of the inspection and feedback devices today happen postwelding. As an example of the many things we can monitor, we can keep tabs on the width of the weld pool, ensuring that it corelates to a certain amperage, voltage, and speed, continuously controlling the size of that weld pool so that it corresponds to a set of welding parameters.

The Fabricator:

Can you explain how weld monitoring is not just limited to visual imagery?

Jauregui:

It is known in the industry that good welders will be able to tell a good or a bad weld from listening to it. Some welders will be 50 ft. away from a weld that somebody else is doing and then say, "Hey, that’s not working. That was a bad arc start," or something else.

For a few years now, we have been working with collecting audio. It’s easier to collect data, and the sample data that you get from audio is easier to manipulate.

Fundamentally we’re a data company. We want to understand what the data around the welding looks like. You have the image from the camera. You have the audio from the mics. That complements other data like motion and the welding parameters. Everything comes together to give you a full picture of what the weld looks like.

Today, we look at this individually. We’ll look at audio as one vertical. We’ll look at vision as another vertical. We’ll look at the welding parameters as another vertical. In the future, especially with the processing power that we now get with modern processing tools like graphic processing units, we’ll be able to process all of this data and find insights into what a good weld and a bad weld will look like.

The Fabricator:

What are the ramifications of this type of AI-powered weld monitoring?

Jauregui:

Quality control is a huge issue in this industry. The amount of waste related to bad welds and additional time workpieces spend in postprocessing is big.

We can be that filter right before the field inspection systems. We can start to segment what bad welds look like so that the user doesn’t end up producing those types of welds. It also can help to minimize the time and money spent on postweld inspection.

The Fabricator:

With AI involved in the weld monitoring process, is a welder necessary for these automated welding scenarios?

Jauregui:

In any type of process, it helps to have somebody who is a subject matter expert. It will accelerate the process and make it more efficient. That’s why it is a requirement that somebody understands welding.

These technologies are not here to replace the welder. The welder still needs to be in the loop. We need somebody who knows if something goes haywire or if the system has detected an anomaly. We need that to be verified, and we need to find the root cause, which needs to be investigated, because otherwise you’re going to repeat the same error over and over.

The best scenario, of course, is having people who know how to program a robot or automation device and also know how to weld. The tricky part is to find somebody who does both. We think a future will come where these types of tools will encourage people to develop both of these skills.

The Fabricator:

How does one teach the AI about a welding application?

Jauregui:

You need to look at this in two main ways. One, you need to detect anomalies, and two, you assist in segmentation of objects. That’s where software can help.

This is not just about having a camera in the right place. The operator uses a human-machine interface embedded in the software and relies on the teach mode. That allows the software to collect this data, detect the anomalies, and assist in the segmentation of the images, which are all used to teach the AI.

The Fabricator:

Can AI help to process all aspects of welding, or will it take more time before we get to that point?

Jauregui:

As an industry, we’re at an early stage in this process. More advanced industries are trying to put it all together, but it’s an ongoing process.

In welding, the companies that I see being more successful in implementing machine vision or AI strategies are the ones that pick one process and one type of weld. They then start learning all about that specific application. For example, you might choose to focus on the width of the melt pool during a gas metal arc welding application. You could look at the coloring, the temperature, or even the heat pattern, but you choose to focus on the width of the melt pool. So the system can be set up so that the feedback of the width of the melt pool is the parameter that is taken into consideration for possibly adjusting the current, voltage, or speed of the system.

Could you refine it and add more data to that system? Absolutely. Then you have multiple inputs that correlate to the system’s current, voltage, or speed and make the system even smarter. Now, that requires a lot more programming and a lot more interaction between your programmer and your welder.

The Fabricator:

What type of impact does AI-assisted weld monitoring hold for manufacturing in the future?

Jauregui:

Being able to provide this level of expertise to small and medium-sized companies is going to be a huge game-changer. This is really the way of the future.

As a first step in fully automated welding, we need to be able to collect the information, the vision, the sound, and everything associated with the process. All of these parameters need to be understood.

The welding industry has been slow at adopting technology, but I think that has started to change in the last two years. In the post-COVID world, the adoption of robotics and sensors has skyrocketed. The industry finally understands the importance of embracing this technology.

The ones who don’t get onboard today are going to regret it in a few years. It’s a learning curve, but it’s important for management and the technical team, even with small incremental steps, to start to adopt technologies like this. It’s time to get on the automation wagon, develop an AI strategy, or fall behind.
