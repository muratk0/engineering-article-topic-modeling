# What happens when band saw best practices are not followed?

and saws are probably among the most abused pieces of equipment in fabricating and metalworking cutting operations. Complicating things further is the tendency for manufacturing staff to skip over proper band saw machine and blade maintenance and usage protocols.

Regular consultation of manuals offering application-specific guidelines or communication with band saw blade providers or the saw machine manufacturers could help, but that doesn’t always happen. That’s why a quick reminder to reinforce the importance of band saw best practices is helpful.

Premature Blade Wear

Using the blade in a non-optimal manner can lead to increased wear and tear. It can shorten the blade’s lifespan, increase the cost per cut, and necessitate more frequent replacements. Premature blade wear is a significant concern, especially when sawing precision is critical.

For example, when a manufacturer experienced a significant drop in throughput and an increase in bad parts when cutting steel and aluminum bars, the problem was determined to be excessive band saw blade wear. As the blade wore down, the teeth became dull and chipped, resulting in rough, uneven cuts with increased burrs. Also, the dull blade started deflecting during cuts, producing parts outside of tolerance. Parts required additional deburring and postprocessing, slowing down operations. In addition, blade breakages occurred more frequently due to the excess stress placed on worn blades. And the worn blades resulted in increased resistance, causing the band saw machine to work harder.

Note that worn blades also can increase the risk of operator injury. Excessive pressure on a blade can lead to breakage, potentially causing injury to the operator, and frequent blade changes increase the risk of lacerations.

Heat Buildup

Heat buildup during cutting operations is a critical issue that can significantly affect the performance and longevity of blades. Improper feed rates or incorrect blade tension can cause excessive friction, leading to overheating. This can weaken the blade material and cause warping or brittleness, resulting in inaccurate cuts or, even worse, making the blade more likely to chip or break under stress.

Tooth Damage

Tooth damage in blades can significantly impact cutting efficiency and quality. Also, dull blades may require more force to operate.

The teeth of the blade may become dull or chipped due to inappropriate cutting speeds or feed rates, as well as using unsuitable blade types for the material, reducing cutting efficiency. For example, using speeds that are too high or too low can lead to overheating or insufficient cutting forces respectively, causing the teeth to dull or chip. Also, using an unsuitable blade type such as a wood-cutting blade on metal can cause premature damage.

Cutting harder materials than the blade is designed to handle can lead to chipping and rapid dulling. In addition, a feed rate that is too aggressive can increase the load on the teeth, leading to damage.

For instance, a fabricator was experiencing frequent tooth chipping and stripping, especially when cutting through bundled thin-walled square tubing and hardened weld seams for commercial building frame parts. Undesirable results included poor cut quality with rough surfaces and inconsistent lengths, a 60% reduction in blade life, frequent blade changes, and increased scrap. An investigation into the poor performance revealed several issues causing the problems, one of which was an incorrect tooth pitch, which led to too few teeth in the cut, resulting in tooth overload and chipping. An excessive feed rate also was being used, which caused teeth strippage, particularly when cutting the harder material sections. Also, improper fixturing of the bundles increased vibration, damaging the blade teeth.

Switching to a variable-pitch blade, properly setting the feeds and speeds, and improving the clamping systems helped to ensure smoother cutting and increased blade life twofold. The fabricator also applied blade break-in procedures to improve blade life.

Blade Breakage

In extreme cases, improper handling or excessive strain can cause the blade to break, posing a serious safety hazard. A broken or excessively worn blade can create flying debris, which is a danger to the operator and nearby workers. The risk of kickback increases if the blade binds (gets stuck in the kerf) or does not cut smoothly.

If the blade is not set correctly, it can make warped, uneven, inaccurate cuts, affecting the dimensions and tolerances of the structural components, which can compromise structural integrity, making it difficult to fit components together accurately during assembly. Non-optimized cutting may result in rough edges and inconsistent surface finishes, necessitating additional machining processes. Poor cutting conditions also slow down the cutting process.

Specific shapes also have notable challenges. Solid rounds and squares have a large, continuous cross section that generates heat when sawing. Tubular workpieces (pipe or hollow sections) typically have a relatively thin wall with a hard outer surface. If a coarse blade is used, the teeth may snag or hook on the tube edges, potentially breaking the blade, especially at the welds or blade gullets.

Saw operators cutting bundles of materials need to have the load locked down firmly to avoid vibration during the sawing process. Excessive vibrating can damage the blade’s teeth.

QUICK TIPS FOR SAWING SUCCESS

Always use blades according to manufacturer guidelines.

To minimize wear, this includes applying the right speeds, feeds, pressure, and angle of application.

Ensure that the blade is suited for the material being cut.

Using the wrong blade can lead to excessive strain and early degradation. Carbon steels, aluminum, stainless steels, titanium, and specialty nickel alloys all call for different sawing approaches.

Regularly maintain blades to prolong their lifespan.

Clean the blades after use to remove debris and prevent corrosion.

Store blades in a dry, safe place to avoid damage.

Proper storage can help maintain their sharpness and overall condition.

Frequently check blades for signs of wear, damage, or fatigue.

Replace any blades that show significant signs of deterioration. Early detection can prevent further issues and costly replacements.

Monitor and adjust feed rates and blade tension and use adequate coolant.

Regular machine maintenance and checks also can help in identifying and addressing potential problems before they escalate.

Ensure that all personnel are properly trained.

This includes how to recognize potential hazards and the importance of following safety protocols.

Invest in high-quality blades.

Make sure they are designed for the specific applications at hand.

Bundles or stacked materials present multiple contact points with gaps or irregular supports, which can cause tooth impact shock as the blade inconsistently engages with the material. These challenges can cause blade deflection, chipping, or even breakage if the tension is not controlled.

Unusual Sounds

Although band sawing is not a quiet process, operators should generally be accustomed to the sounds of their sawing operations. An exceptionally loud or unusual sound may indicate mechanical machine issues, improper setup, or blade challenges. These sounds can be squealing, grinding, rattling, or thumping.

The unusual sounds are likely linked to something that needs to be addressed, such as blade vibration that could be a result of incorrect blade tension, a dull blade, or using the wrong blade for the material. The wrong feed rate, which feeds the material either too fast or too slow, can cause screeching or stuttering sounds. If a component (guide bearing, wheel, or bolt) in the band saw setup becomes loose, it can cause rattling or knocking. A worn blade or wrong blade choice can cause squealing or chattering.

It also is important to examine the clamping required for structural materials as inadequate support can produce vibrations and drumming noises. Paying attention to the sawing sounds helps a fabricator determine where the adjustments or fixes are needed.

Sawing Interrupted

A dull or damaged blade might cause friction and overheating, stalling or stopping the band saw machine. That’s why any saw maintenance program should include regular inspection of blades to ensure that they are in good working condition.

Varying material densities can cause blades to resist cutting and jump. In those instances, it might help to use a slower feed rate.

Forcing the blade rather than letting the blade do the cutting results in misalignments and reduced blade life. In extreme circumstances, it can cause the blade to bind with the material, bringing any cutting to a halt.

Of course, fabricators shouldn’t neglect the band saw machine. Regular maintenance keeps sawing operations running smoothly by making certain that drive belts and other mechanics are in good working order. They also provide a consistent schedule for cleaning the saw and lubricating it where needed.
