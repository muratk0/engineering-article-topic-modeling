# Sureway Tool & Engineering Co.

[TARİH: 01.12.2018 The Fabricator]

Being a shop that does a lot of point-of-purchase work, Sureway Tool & Engineering Co. processes a fair amount of prototyping, and it is structured to handle it. A director of project management manages six project managers that shepherd various orders from design through production.

At more than 280 employees, Sureway is growing fast and, in a sense, is two companies in one: a custom fabrication operation alongside an assembly and fulfillment center dedicated to point-of-purchase displays, marrying sheet metal parts with wood, plastic, and other purchased components. The fulfillment center was launched four years ago to help simplify the supply chain. The fabricator used to send parts to fulfillment centers for assembly. But POP is a quick-turn business, and any move that helps shorten response times is a move in the right direction.

In this regard, a year ago Sureway Tool had a problem. Back in 2011 and 2013 it had invested in two solid-state lasers, a 3-kW and 5-kW system, both with load and unload automation—and by last year they were overwhelming the forming department, sending far more sheet metal, ranging from 24 to 10 gauge, than anyone in forming could handle. Now that bottleneck is virtually gone. How? The company got rid of some of its old forming machines and replaced them with 10 new electric press brakes. Combined with the company’s hydraulic machines, Sureway has a total of 15 press brakes.

But as managers found, the forming bottleneck wasn’t just because of the lasers’ massive throughput capability. It was also how jobs were scheduled on the lasers. Programmers would not only group various jobs together on a sheet, but also send those nests in batches of hundreds of sheets that took operators several days to work through. The result: Press brake operators and others downstream spent time waiting for parts to come off the laser so they could complete a job and send it on to the next operation. On top of this, operators dealt with planer tooling and old press brakes that took a long time to set up.

For standard jobs, it takes four or five minutes for a received order to reach laser programming and nesting.

80 percent of orders have 3-D models; 20 percent flat, DWG or DXF.

Company uses a product data management (PDM) system.

Company has seven engineers and one dedicated programmer, with backups if needed, producing about 20 programs a day, with five to nine multisheet nests being completed.

Generating nests takes from 10 to 45 minutes, depending on nest complexity.

The company orders special sheet sizes to minimize scrap; 20 percent or less is the goal, 10 percent or less is typical.

Scrap cuts are programmed to make skeleton disposal easier.

60 percent of parts are less than 12 in. wide or high; 30 percent are between 12 and 48 in.; 10 percent are more than 48 in.

When a row of nozzles reaches the yellow, it’s time to order more.

Today, the programmer places parts for only one job per sheet, though the shop does use custom sheet sizes if needed for better material utilization. A job may have five different gauges, but no matter—that job is scheduled and sequenced so that all the parts for the job are de-nested in the order that downstream operations need them.

It’s an unusual approach but one that works well for Sureway—and it goes back to the customer mix and types of jobs the company is cutting. Most jobs tend to fill a 5 by 10 sheet, and rectangular parts dominate the mix, so most skeletons have thin web sections remaining. Operators manage and use remnants when it makes sense, but again, thanks to the company’s product mix and sheet selection, most jobs tend to fill a sheet tightly.

This isn’t to say the programmer doesn’t try to improve material utilization when practical by occasionally pulling parts into the nest from other jobs, particularly if it’s a small job that can neatly fill the rest of the sheet. But when that happens, the programmer makes sure that those parts come from a job that’s in queue, ideally right behind the current job being cut. Regardless, those parts never come from jobs weeks out into the schedule. From Sureway’s perspective, the material savings would be wiped out by the cost of tracking and storing those cut blanks.

Nesting is largely static, mostly with one job to a sheet.

A current order snapshot: During one day in September, 25 orders were in process with 50 associated programs.

The job is available for nesting less than a minute after the order receipt.
