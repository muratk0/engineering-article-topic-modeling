# What a bank can do for a metal fabricator

[TARİH: 01.10.2019 The Fabricator]

Management » Chief Concerns

The relationship should be about more than just lending money

By Dan Davis

M

etal fabricators may have posted hours of business, but their days really are defined by what customers need. If an urgent shipment of parts needs to be ready for delivery the next morning, quitting time becomes the time when those parts are sitting on a pallet, waiting to be loaded onto a truck.

Metal fabricating companies have those types of schedules each day. Try as they might to schedule a full shift of production, shops still have to contend with expedited orders, engineering changes, and rework daily. The real surprise is when production goes according to the schedule.

In this type of environment, small and medium-sized fabricators can find it difficult to focus on other areas of the business. That’s why it’s important for them to rely on their partners, such as their banks. They can offer the assistance and guidance that allow fab shops to stay focused on keeping their customers happy.

The FABRICATOR

spoke with CIBC U.S.’s Tom Doherty, managing director, head of business banking, and Paul Liles, SBA banking manager, to learn just how fabricators can work more efficiently with their banks and how these institutions can make it so that shops can focus on metal fabricating instead of the ancillary activities required to keep a business functioning.

The FABRICATOR:

One of the main roles of a bank is business lending. How do you see the current climate for lending to manufacturers?

Tom Doherty:

It’s very strong. You’ve got a very low interest rate environment. Combine that with, for example, very low unemployment, and you have evidence of a robust manufacturing economy.

In my view, banks are lending money to manufacturers, and the underwriting criteria and the standards that we use really have stayed consistent over the years, which is what you want with a lender. I don’t think they’ve gotten any tighter or looser. If you are with the right partner, they remain consistent.

We are seeing an increase since 2010 in acquisitions financing. Owners of companies that were holding on through the recession now have built earnings back up and are either looking to sell, if they are nearing retirement age, or expanding the business or acquiring another company Overall, the economic environment is good.

FAB:

So we just completed our "What Keeps You up at Night?" survey, which was published in the September 2019 issue, and once again finding manufacturing talent is the No. 1 concern of metal fabricators. Another top concern is access to capital. What are some of the things these businesses can do to help make themselves more attractive in the eyes of the lender?

Doherty:

That’s a good question because sometimes there can be frustration in the process. Many times the perception is that it’s difficult.

We see it quite a bit with business owners who think they know their financial position, but don’t have the luxury of having a team of people around them that are supporting them and advising them. For example, a small company is not likely to have a CFO on staff. In these instances, having a team of partners, which might include the accountant, the attorney, and the bank, can help the business stay on a positive track.

But as far as the lending side, the quality of the financial information, not just financial statements and tax returns, is going to help this processing. We are talking about the ability to generate current and accurate information related to things like accounts receivable, inventory reports, accounts payable, equipment listings, debt schedules—anything that is needed to process a loan request. It has a lot to do with the quality of output and the turn time of making this information available.

I know manufacturers get frustrated sometimes with bank timelines, but the qualitative information is important to decision-making. The bank is always going to ask questions once it gathers the financial package. It wants to look at the key numbers on the balance sheet and the income statement, the swings in revenues, gross margins, and the main operating expenses on a profit and loss statement. Having these discussions early on with the manufacturer really speeds up the process.

FAB:

What kinds of loans are available for these business owners? Why is a bank a better source for this type of financial support than some other lending sources?

Doherty:

The right bank has a full suite of products and services that aren’t specific to one need. In other words, the banks should be able to look at your whole picture and determine the right financing for the right needs. It’s the opposite of just being an equipment finance company or just being an SBA lender.

As a bank that has all the capabilities, we can avoid the mismatching of financing to the need. In other words, you want to finance the type of asset with the right type of debt that goes with it. For example, the short-term asset like accounts receivable or inventory should be matched with a short-term bank loan, like a line of credit. You want to match the repayment of the note with the source of the funds that are going to repay it. So when a company collects the receivables, they pay back the line. On the other hand, you wouldn’t want to finance working capital with a long-term note for five years.

So a bank is going to have access to equipment financing, but the bank also is going to have the ability to offer lines of credit for account receivable and inventory financing and weathering seasonal swings in a business.

Real estate financing, whether it’s refinancing or purchasing, also can be done with a bank. With the rate environment where it is, a lot of manufacturers are looking at purchasing their current property or finding and purchasing another facility.

Of course, having options outside of the common lineup of loans is important. For example, we have our MIRF [the Midwest Industrial Redevelopment Fund] program. It’s a redevelopment program for certain lower- to moderate-income geographies in certain markets, particularly in the Midwest. We are one of the only, if not the only banks in our market that has this program where the interest rate is literally half the going market rate because it’s a sponsored program that offers the discounted interest rates.

In another example, we have a specialized team built around SBA [Small Business Administration] loans. People often think of the SBA for acquisition financing, which is very useful if there’s a collateral shortfall, but it could be used for more than that. For instance, whereas conventional financing on a piece of equipment might go five years with an equipment finance company or a traditional lender, we can do up to 10 years or the life of the equipment with SBA.

Paul Liles:

To add to that, I would say that SBA loans are a good alternative when traditional or conventional financing doesn’t make a lot of sense or it’s kind of outside the normal underwriting guidelines with the SBA for anything from acquisitions, financing equipment, financing or refinancing existing debt, or working capital.

Where we typically see a lot of our clients, especially in the manufacturing space, looking to take advantage of the program is in acquisition financing. A borrower can come in and put down typically 10 percent of the total project costs. So the SBA loan allows them to put down a small amount of equity for the purchase, but we also examine the cash flow forecasts for the business to determine the proper amount of working capital required to grow the business. Depending on whether it’s an asset or stock sale, the seller may be taking cash and accounts receivable, and we want to ensure the buyer has an adequate line of credit to support A/R for a period of time.

It really allows for an alternative to traditional financing, which might require private equity or all-seller financing. This allows individuals to buy new companies or to grow their existing companies with putting down only 10 percent.

A lot of times we look at the SBA from a collateral shortfall standpoint. So the big thing with the SBA and why people use this program for client acquisition financing is typically you’re buying a lot of goodwill in the manufacturing space. There’s quite a bit of equipment, but once you take the depreciation out of that and find the true value, there’s going to be a lot of goodwill or intangible assets. SBA then allows us to do undercollateralized loans without saying to the client that he or she doesn’t hit our normal guidelines for financing.

It’s truly a program that’s out there to help a small business to grow while putting less equity down or not having collateral for collateralized lines of credit. It also can even be used to extend the term or amortization Typically you can go out to 10 years with most transactions. In real estate, you can go out to 25 years.

So it’s truly a good tool when it comes to acquisition of equipment. Obviously, the purchase of a CNC machine or some other equipment can be a pretty large capital expenditure. With the SBA loan, that 10 percent down payment again allows you to put less down and keep cash in the business. You can stretch the amortization to maybe five or seven years if you’re working with a dealer, but with the SBA, you can go up to 10 years. If you can prove the useful life of that equipment beyond 10 years, we’re looking at 15 years for some equipment purchases.

FAB:

Besides the products that we have talked about already, are metal fabricators overlooking anything else that their banks might be able to offer them that they could use to their advantage?

Doherty:

Refinancing of equipment, debt related to real estate or equipment, or a line of credit are areas where a bank can help with examining their current situation. Sometimes companies may have obtained credit several years ago, and they’re not really looking at the terms and conditions on those deals. The bank or lender in place may not be coming back to the table offering different solutions. Somebody may have acquired their property many years ago in a higher-rate environment, and you get used to making your payments on a monthly basis. You don’t take the time to review the terms, rates, and conditions. Sometimes there can be significant savings in refinancing some of that to a more competitive rate that’s going on today. Whether that’s equipment, real estate, lines of credit—it’s always good to take another look at what you’ve got on the financing side and see if there are opportunities to lower some costs there.

FAB:

Is that type of interaction more indicative of a banking partner that is looking to work with its client rather than just offer loans?

Doherty:

Yes. We talk about lending, which is obviously very critical to metal fabricators, but there’s a whole other side of the balance sheet where the banking relationship should come into play. For example, a bank can help with managing cash flow for the business. It’s amazing because sometimes a company might have been in a certain deposit product for many years and paying fees on those when there are better alternatives out there. Having a specialist at the bank who is a dedicated treasury management specialist and having a banking team to support these small to midsized companies makes a big difference.

There are checking products. There are lockbox services for collection of payments. There are merchant services. There are credit cards. Everything should be reviewed to see if there are cost savings.

Then on the business owner side of the relationship, the bank can help with private banking or wealth management services. For example, as opposed to just working with a branch banker, who they might or might not know, the business owner could have a dedicated private banker working with them and access to specialists should the owner need something unique on a residential mortgage program, for example. These business owners deserve special attention on the personal side too.

FAB:

What can a metal fabricator do to make its bank a more valuable partner in the business?

Doherty:

They have to evaluate the quality of the relationship manager that they’re working with. Having done this for 35-plus years, it still surprises me that sometimes banks move the more experienced relationship bankers into the upper middle-market side of the bank, which covers companies with the $50 million to $100 million in revenues. Of course, what companies need the most support and help? It’s the small to midsize manufacturers. They may or may not have a CFO, or they may need the support of a team of a banker, attorney, and accountant.

I think a company should make sure the banker and team behind them understands the support that is needed. That’s what manufacturers should be looking for.

Also, they need to look for a bank that can be viewed as a trusted adviser. If it’s all about price, then that’s not a partnership built on trust.

How is that trust built? It’s built on doing what we can to support business growth.

We do seminars with our clients on topics like lean manufacturing and employee retention. These events have nothing to do with the bank or with our products or services. The bank is just trying to add value. That’s what a true partnership should look like.

FAB:

Can metal fabricators truly expect a banking relationship to be a partnership in the face of a competitive labor market where banks generally have to deal with employee turnover on a regular basis?

Doherty:

That does frustrate people. They lose their banker, and they lose that contact with the bank.

Our approach is to surround them with a team of three people. So they’ve got the relationship manager who’s the quarterback on all the needs. They’ve got a portfolio manager with 10-plus years of credit experience that gets to know the business owner and can help with financial analysis questions. Then they have a client specialist who’s the day-to-day contact for needs like line of credit advances or checking account questions.

We’re all going to have turnover. It happens. To combat that, a manufacturer should have more than one contact at the bank who knows the business. That’s important for them to look at: Who else knows you and your business other than the one person you typically interact with?
