Show Preview

Navigating through an industry in transition

By

Tim Heston

T

his year’s FABTECH will show metal fabrication at its best: ultrahigh-powered fiber lasers, the latest in blanking automation, press brakes with automatic tool changers, process simulation to eliminate those shop floor surprises, cobots with simplified programming, production control and IIoT software showing true machine uptimes and utilization.

Early August saw economic jitters on Wall Street and less-than-stellar jobs reports. Some shops were reporting noticeable slowdowns; others were seeing increased activity. The industry is many things, but a monolith it is not. One thing disparate operations can agree on, of course, is the need to reinvest in the business.

The annual show comes as the industry enters a period of transition—economically, operationally, and generationally. Each is intertwined, and how a fabricator navigates through all three will help blaze a trail forward.

The Election Year Economy

There’s little doubt that the economic cycle is in deceleration mode as the presidential election nears. Chris Kuehl, economic analyst for the Fabricators and Manufacturers Association and managing director of Armada Corporate Intelligence, had predicted an annual growth rate of a little more than 2% for fabricated metal products, according to Armada’s The Watch publication released in August. That forecast is down from previous predictions—yet an upswing is on the horizon. "The outlook … has improved and is now showing some sharp growth for the second half of 2024 and into 2025." In other words, business might be a little soft now, but for many, the rebound might be right around the corner.

As of late summer, the Federal Reserve had yet to cut interest rates. Inflation had started to subside, however, signaling change ahead. This sequence of events, Kuehl said, is actually par for the course. "The Fed raises rates until they break something," he said in a recent FMA newsletter. "Then they lower rates to fix what they broke." The Fed needs to control inflation and promote full employment. A recession isn’t fun, but unlike inflation, it doesn’t affect everyone. When the Fed "breaks something," they’d rather see a recession (or, even better, a soft landing) than runaway inflation.

As of mid-summer, a soft landing seemed to be a real possibility. According to the Forming and Fabricating Job Shop Consumption Report from the Fabricators and Manufacturers Association (FMA), more fabricators reported increased steel and aluminum prices as well as rising logistics costs. Operating capacity was down, yet new order activity was slightly up. Additionally, more fabricators reported that their capital equipment plans were on track. In fact, that "on track" number increased slightly from 2023, from 47% of respondents to 51%.

The FMA report measures specific technology needs, and despite changes to the overall economy, the needs haven’t changed in significant ways. The No. 1 need on the report remains laser cutting. In fact, the survey shows that need might be slightly more acute than it was last year. Nearly 20% of respondents said lasers were at or near the top of their list last year; this year, that number is nearing 25%. Meanwhile, demand for bending machines—including press brakes, panel benders, folders, and plate rollers—has remained steady. Altogether, nearly 20% reported bending equipment as a significant need.

Operational Transformation

Wherever the need, the holistic look at throughput is guiding the way. Consider the punch press. Not every shop utilizes it, but those that do are seeing an increased need. For sure, punching is a smaller market in the blanking arena. Among the FMA job shop consumption report survey sample, only 5% said punching was their greatest need—but that’s up from just 2.5% last year. Of the shops that utilize punching, they’re needing much more of it.

Not too long ago, punching was looked upon as a fading technology. Some technical schools didn’t cover it, simply because most local fab shops had transitioned to the "soft tool" of the laser. But here’s the thing: Lasers can’t form, and they can’t create threaded holes. Punch presses, as well as their punch/laser combo cousins, utilize form tools and on-machine tapping that have been increasingly embraced. The machines also don’t have slats that need to be cleaned, maintained, and replaced. And sure, the cutting isn’t as quick as it is with a flying-optic laser, but parts that emerge from the work envelope can skip secondary forming and tapping.

Whether punching or combo machines add value or not depends on the customer mix, which really defines the fabricator and the processes it has in-house. But wherever a fabricator sits on the metal manufacturing spectrum—precision sheet metal, structural and heavy fab, tube and pipe, welding—more shops are thinking holistically about throughput and, specifically, where they need excess capacity. It’s no longer just how many inches or strokes a particular machine can finish in a minute’s time.

Butler Photography

Similar thinking goes with laser cutting thick plate. Ultrahigh-powered fiber lasers won’t be lacking at this year’s show. Their incredible cutting speeds in thicker plate is a big selling point, but it’s perhaps not the biggest. The fact these machines can cut a thick plate that can then

skip secondary processing

can be a game-changer. Cut plates that once required processing on a vertical mill now can go directly to the next major manufacturing step. The job routing gets shorter and simpler, opening the door to even greater throughput.

This could explain the industry’s continued focus on laser cutting and blanking in general. The greater and (thanks to clean edges with no secondary processing)

more effective

blanking capacity a fabricator has, the faster it can react to changing demand, and the less chance it has of starving downstream processes—even if schedulers wait to release a job until they can ensure smooth part flow, without building excess work-in-process (WIP).

Butler Photography

That flow demands forming capacity, hence the high demand for bending machines. Still, it’s all a balancing act, juggling different jobs around ever-shifting manufacturing constraints—some of which are governed by the physical nature of the process. The industry enjoys eye-popping speeds in cutting, but a part’s time in the baking oven after powder coating is what it is.

Shops still need capacity upstream to ensure constraint processes never lack work (and in custom, high-product-mix fabrication, that constraint can shift based on the product mix on the floor). At the same time, they also don’t want to flood the floor with too much WIP—bleeding cash, increasing material handling costs as well as the potential for rework due to last-minute changes from the customer. Such a balancing act can at times feel impossibly complex—and here is where data and software come into play.

A Generation Embracing Software

Imagine a brake operator clocks in on the workstation laptop, performs the job, and clocks out. The closer the overall process time is to the estimated time, the higher the efficiency, right?

Some fabricators are finding that it’s not that simple, especially after adopting production monitoring or IIoT platforms, be they from machine OEMs, third parties, or a combination of both.

In fact, many are shocked just how little time a machine spends producing parts. Why? Often, this has less to do with the technology and more to do with a thousand little events—a few minutes searching for tools here, a problem workpiece or minor miscommunication there—that add up over time.

FABTECH attendees walk the floor to see machines, but in recent years especially, they also see screens full of charts signifying real-time measurement, machine performance data, and other displays that help personnel "connect the dots" among myriad elements that make a successful operation with the capacity to grow.

Connecting the dots helps break down those metaphorical walls between sales, engineering, and various departments in manufacturing. The focus moves from local efficiency (

how many parts did you weld today?)

to global efficiency (

how many jobs did we ship today?

).

Connecting the dots reveals the true cost of cheap material (distortion in cutting, issues in forming and welding) and how every step in the value chain affects the outcome. Such visibility also helps reduce finger-pointing and the never-ending blame game, creating those toxic environments that send the most talented people heading for the exits.

Such intelligence will complement increasingly automated workflow, with autonomous mobile robots (AMRs) carrying work from one work center to the next. Workers can access exact job locations with a few clicks—no more aimless searching, no more recutting and bending lost parts.

The use and appreciation of software signifies a generational shift. Younger workers grew up with the iPhone and find it utterly natural to navigate a touchscreen interface, viewing and manipulating 3D models of the work at hand. That’s driving the look and feel of modern machine controls. Process simulation seems natural as well, and they might even be fascinated with the concept of digital twins.

For Gen Zers entering fabrication, a robot "looking" at a workpiece and comparing it to information from a 3D CAD file, then commencing an operation (be it welding, powder coating, or anything else) seems like a logical approach. After all, they saw their first 3D printer in middle school, and they’ve had a computer for as long as they can remember. Creating something in the digital realm and then "hitting print" just makes sense. When they see a robot or any other automated system get to work with just the click of a button, they see it as something natural, not a technological marvel.

Generation Alpha—the next generation to start entering the workforce—will take artificial intelligence (AI) for granted. AI is already speeding the software development process. That, combined with low-code applications, will empower fabricators to develop their own software to suit unique situations.

Talk of AI’s impact permeated the FMA Annual Meeting earlier this year, and discussions about it will surely continue at FABTECH in Orlando. Looking at software and machines together, attendees could glean ideas for what could be some truly transformational productivity improvements. Ultimately, that’s what FABTECH is all about.
