The FAB 40

Custom and contract metal fabricators stand ready for anything

s the world navigates trade negotiations and all the uncertainties that go with that, a relatively unknown sector of the economy stands ready to adapt, tackle a reshoring opportunity that could evolve into a steady and sustainable business, and adjust should market demand change in a significant way.

Kirk Putt, CEO of R-V Industries in Honey Brook, Pa., gave custom and contract metal fabrication an apt name: "a diversified mutual fund of manufacturing." Diversification usually makes a contract metal fabricator truly sustainable. High revenue concentration does the opposite. One or two customers pull the plug, and a fab shop can be in a world of hurt.

This isn’t true for every operation, of course. Some are embedded so deeply into one or a handful of sectors, and they serve them so well, they make it extremely inconvenient for customers to stop sending work their way. You could call them the "adept specialist."

Combine these types of shops with agile generalists—those that diversify as much as practical—and you build a platform for scaling up a metal fabrication business. In other words, you get the FAB 40, The Fabricator magazine’s annual list of some of the largest custom and contract metal fabricators across the United States.

So Many Sectors

Companies on the FAB 40 represent the "large few" in an industry dominated by small businesses. Why have we yet to see even further consolidation?

The extraordinary mix of work is one reason. When contract fabricators get together at FABTECH, the Fabricators and Manufacturers Association’s Annual Meeting, or anywhere else, you might notice a sense of camaraderie that you don’t find in other industries. Fabricators share technical challenges in blanking, bending, welding, and assembly, but they don’t share the same mix of jobs on the floor—at least not exactly.

Customer mix really defines the metal fabricator, and for most operations, that mix continually evolves. That’s partly why metal fabrication remains so resilient. Metal is everywhere—in construction; in all kinds of heavy and industrial equipment; in our cars, trucks, and vehicles used for recreation. It’s spread throughout the very infrastructure of modern society. A handful of companies simply can’t service it all efficiently.

Precision sheet metal stands apart from plate fabrication. Tube and pipe fabrication is a separate animal, as is structural fabrication. FAB 40 participants manage a mix of them all, to varying degrees. You’ll see a few head-to-head competitors, but each still approaches the business in its own way, with its own mix of metal fabrication technologies and unique ways of serving customers.

Good Surfers

A prohibitively high tariff might send shockwaves through the economy, but metal fabricators are adept at surfing over the waves. The trade war presents plenty of obstacles and uncertainty. As of this writing in early May, the U.S. and China have turned down the heat a bit, replacing a drastic trade war with a more moderate one. Other trade deals are in progress, and the uncertainty continues unabated. Material price fluctuations add further wrinkles of complexity.

Fabricators have lived through such challenges before. They’ve lived through the effects of globalization, the dot-com bust, the Great Recession, the AI and data center boom. The trade war is just another wave to navigate.

How a Fabricator Scales Up

Thanks in part to the trade war, many on the FAB 40 are receiving unprecedented numbers of inquiries. Which could develop into thriving partnerships lasting years or even decades? Which could go back overseas the moment trade barriers change? What supply chains are imbedded overseas? Which can be ramped up cost-effectively stateside over the short, medium, and long term? Today’s purchasing managers are playing a complex game of chess.

How does a fabricator scale up in such an environment? The unsustainable way would be to latch onto "hot" industries—like data centers and anything else associated with the IT and AI transformation—then assume the party will never stop.

Since 2021, the top 10 FAB 40 players have collectively increased their revenue by 70%, to $3.1 billion. That’s nearly triple the revenue reported just a decade ago.

The sustainable way would take a deliberate and carefully diversified approach, especially as customers look to reshore.

Which work is here for the long term? What unique set of fabrication capabilities do they require? And how do they complement a fabricator’s existing portfolio of work?

A diverse revenue portfolio—with different volumes, demand trends (heavy orders at different times of the year), and metal fabrication requirements—can help a fabricator build a solid foundation for growth.

All this gives the fabricator either a solid foundation to acquire other companies or to be acquired. Certain contract fabricators can grow significantly in an organic way. New opportunities with reshoring might spur organic growth in a more significant way in the coming years.

For most of the FAB 40’s history, though, acquisition and consolidation have spurred some of the most dramatic jumps in annual revenue. Consolidation also can come about via private equity or other outside investors. A prime example is Cadrex Manufacturing Solutions, which CORE Industrial Partners put together in less than two years, starting in 2021. Some previous FAB 40 operations now operate as part of the Cadrex organization.

New FAB 40 entrant Keel formed early last year as investors acquired several fabricators that, on the surface, look very different, but when you dig a little deeper, you see a common thread: complex, project-based fabrication. The organization markets itself as a comprehensive defense manufacturing provider—a combination of Metal Trades and Pegasus Steel in South Carolina, both focused on heavy fabrication for the U.S. Navy.; and MERRILL Technologies Group, a diverse contract metal fabricator in Michigan serving defense, aerospace, and related sectors.

Another new FAB 40 entrant, Georgia-based ReNEW Manufacturing Solutions, was launched in 2023 by a Georgia Tech grad who knew that plenty of baby boomers who owned fabrication and machine shops were looking for an exit strategy. (You’ll read more about ReNEW in future editions of The Fabricator.)

By the Numbers

Years ago, no one thought that consistent naming conventions—"SS 304" versus "stainless 304," for instance—would really matter. It certainly matters now. Bringing a host of small organizations under one ERP umbrella, with commonly formatted data, is easier said than done. Data has been one hurdle to consolidation, though that might be changing as advances in software and AI have made data cleanup less arduous than it was.

The next challenge is finding a group of businesses with synergies: similar customers, similar types of fabrication (piece-part production vs. subassemblies vs. project-based fabrication; thin sheet vs. heavy plate); similar or complementary customers or industries; and complementary fabrication processes. Acquisition can be a risky business. Not every deal works out well. Even so, considering the political climate and potential supply chain shifts, the metal fabrication stage has been set for growth—be it organic, through M&A, or (most common) a combination of both.
