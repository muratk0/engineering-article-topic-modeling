A lot has to take place to transform a piece of sheet metal into a part for a customer. In a high-mix, low-volume manufacturing environment, production flow is always hectic but manageable, if quality is maintained and rework is avoided. Collaboration between all aspects of the enterprise can make that happen.

ne of the main reasons why Ludlow Manufacturing Inc. (LMI), Waukegan, Ill., won The Fabricator’s 2024 Industry Award was its cultural transformation, going from an organization slowly building a successful metal fabricating business to one that saw dramatic growth fueled by everyone working toward similar quality and safety goals.

The introduction of an advanced product quality planning (APQP) process in 2019 has been a key to bringing all aspects of the company together. The process basically documents all stages of a manufacturing job and keeps quality at the forefront during the entire fabrication cycle (e.g., planning, product design and development, process design and development, product and process validation, and feedback and continuous improvement).

APQP meetings allow everyone to have their voice heard and keep everyone informed. This increased collaboration has been a boon to the company—helping to increase on-time delivery rates, improve overall product quality, and streamline customer relationships.

Putting a Contract Fabricator Spin on APQP

For contract fabricators, the APQP process is quite different today when compared to how it started in the automotive industry. The APQP process that the Big Three—Chrysler, Ford, and GM—established in the late 1980s was put together by merely documenting their existing processes and for parts they had designed. By requiring the APQP process of their suppliers, the automakers had a valuable tool to ensure that their suppliers would be using the same uniformed processes to ensure receipt of quality product.

The challenge for metal fabricators is that APQP is normally done on new parts that they have won the right to manufacture, but that they did not design. (Most contract manufacturers are not going to hold APQP meetings on everything that they quote, and usually not even for all work that they are awarded. For the APQP team members, the APQP meeting is the first time that they are seeing the parts.) The process can seem like a series of unenviable tasks to verify processes, controls, and checks for work that you’ve already promised to a customer. But if the process is used as a sanity check for your quoting process and embraced for the efficiency it provides, the benefits of APQP far outweigh the drawbacks.

LMI tracks on-time delivery and customer approvals on initial submissions of PPAPs (aka production part approval process, documentation that verifies a supplier’s ability to fabricate a part), as KPIs. When LMI excels in these areas, it feels it leads to more new business from existing partners and makes a very good first impression with new partners.

Getting the APQP Team Together

LMI has a standard weekly meeting to review the prints of all new parts coming into the shop. From those meetings, APQP meetings are held for parts that are determined to warrant individual reviews.

Because each part is different, the APQP meetings include at least one representative from each process that is relevant to the part or parts being reviewed. Todd Ludlow, LMI’s founder and CEO, calls this a "cross-function team," and it usually involves at least one team member from quality, engineering, manufacturing, customer service, purchasing, and quoting. Department managers are invited to the APQP meetings, and invitations can be extended to any LMI employee.

Everyone, at all job levels, are candidates to attend. This includes managers, supervisors/leads, and operators.

This wide-open invitation can be a challenge for any job shop, and it was at LMI as well. Like most things in business, time and money are valuable. Taking people away from their general work assignments can be difficult. But it can be done, as was proven when LMI committed to this process in the midst of the pandemic.

Risk Assessment

LMI’s APQP process, which shares a similar foundation with the ISO 9001:2015 standard, takes into account the risk associated with all aspects of part fabrication. While this can be done by several different methods, LMI performs this assessment through a risk register. Based on defined criteria, the cross-functional team assigns a number ranking to seven categories. These risks are associated with:

1. Processes (i.e., the interconnected activities or steps undertaken to achieve a particular goal) assigned to fabricate the part.

2. Actions or "outputs" that arise from the APQP meeting. Examples of these outputs include designing and fabricating weld fixtures, purchasing inspection tools, or assigning work to specific machinery within a department.

3. Materials needed to fabricate the part.

4. Methods (i.e., specific techniques used to accomplish a task or solve a problem) assigned to fabricate the part.

5. Labor required to fabricate the part.

6. Measurements and controls needed to fabricate the part.

7. Delivery and packaging.

There isn’t always a consensus for scoring, but the conversation about the score is always informative. The goal is to close the gap between different scores. When the scores are within one point of each other, LMI’s policy is to assign the higher score to the part. It’s better to be safe than sorry.

These discussions also produce other important intelligence, such as ideas for continuous improvement activities and lessons learned that can later be shared with others.

LMI’s number ranking system for risk is designed to mesh with the four Ts of risk management: tolerate, treat, transfer, and terminate.

Tolerate.

Sometimes it’s OK to do nothing. The likelihood and impact of the risk is low. You might decide to simply retain the risk because it is acceptable without further actions.

Even if the risk is low, it should always be logged and monitored because retaining a risk should always be an informed decision. You should not find that your organization has retained a risk by default.

A score of 1 or 2 is tolerated.

Treat.

In this instance, issues have been defined and actions assigned. The goal is to guarantee that these recognized risks are managed by effective controls, such as the use of a weld fixture or boosting the frequency of in-process inspection.

A score of 2 or 3 is treated.

Transfer.

In this case, issues have been defined and are eliminated or reduced to a tolerable level by transferring the issue to a third party. This type of insurance policy incurs a cost to the organization, and senior management must consider the cost and benefit of transferred risk. An example of this level of risk management would be contracting a certified welding inspector (CWI) to take on approvals of certain welds because the company doesn’t have a CWI on staff.

A score of 3 or 4 is transferred.

Terminate.

Issues have been defined and deemed to create more risks than benefits. What might lead to such an action? Perhaps the materials needed for the job are on a restricted list, or there is an unavoidable safety risk for the methods required for fabricating.

A score of 5 is terminated.

Once the rankings have been entered, the scores of the seven categories are averaged and overall scores are provided for the APQP processes and actions. These scores let LMI know if more discussions or actions are needed (to ensure parts are fabricated correctly and due dates can be met) or if the project can be released to the shop.

Scores at or above 3 are unacceptable. These risks are conveyed to senior management for review.

Once complete, this risk assessment information is useful in ISO efforts. Historically, organizational knowledge has been one of the hardest ISO requirements to capture. LMI found that this type of review and risk assessment is a great way of showing how it meets this standard requirement.

The outputs from the APQP meetings also become the inputs for other ISO documentation and provide evidence of how LMI meets customers’ expectations.

Controls

Once risk is established and parts are released to the floor, methods and processes are needed to ensure repeatability and conformity. Some of the tools used to ensure that parts meet agreed-upon expectations include process failure mode and effects analysis, a structured method used to identify and assess potential failures in a process; a plan that clearly defines fabricating steps; and an optimal process flow that is clear and efficient.

Validation

A process is only as good as its results. Collected data verifies that APQP actions and controls have been successful. Tools used to collect this information include statistical process control, measurement system analysis, and PPAPs.

Impact at Ludlow

After implementing the APQP process, LMI showed an immediate increase in its on-time delivery and in PPAP approvals after initial submissions. Both rates historically were around 82%, but over the last six years those rates have averaged 96%.

Employees also feel more empowered. After just a few of the initial APQP meetings, team members understood the meeting flow, the topics being discussed, and their roles in the meetings. Minimal training was needed.

In fact, it was just a matter of asking them to participate. Each team member is asked to provide input based on their knowledge of the methods or processes under their control, how it applies to the part being discussed, and to provide a realistic time for assigned actions to be accomplished.

This success can be replicated at other metal fabricating companies. They just need to find what works for them.

The formula is simple: Define the process, set goals, and track performance, all while including the experience and knowledge of the entire workforce.

Also, a metal fabricator doesn’t have to have an APQP team review on all parts. Companies that commit to APQP meetings should review their actions from prior meetings. If they find that a small number of actions have been issued, they might be meeting about parts that don’t require APQP team reviews.
