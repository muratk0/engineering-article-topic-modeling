Metal fabricators reveal mixed emotions about tariffs

Even though President Donald Trump showed some hesitancy pushing forward with his reciprocal tariffs plan, announcing a 90-day pause on April 9, a significant portion of the metal fabricating community indicates a willingness to support the effort over the long term, according to a recent survey sent to almost 50,000 subscribers to The Fabricator and members of the Fabricators and Manufacturers Association.

When asked if the reciprocal tariffs unveiled on April 2 would affect their metal fabricating-related business in 2025, 55% said their operations would be affected "negatively." However, 18% replied with "no effect," and 27% said the moves would end up "positively" affecting their business.

In fact, 53% reported that their capital expenditure plans for 2025 remain unchanged, even with the tariffs in place. Another 9% said they actually plan to increase their spending. Thirty-eight percent said they will decrease their investment plans.

The motivation for the support of tariffs appears to be a belief in their ability to reshore manufacturing activity and reestablish the U.S. as a self-sufficient maker of goods, much as it was in the years after World War II. When asked if they saw the tariffs as being good for bolstering U.S. manufacturing in the long term, 45% said "yes," and 16% said "maybe." Thirty-nine percent reported "no."

When asked directly how they saw tariffs affecting their business, the responses were mixed:

• Tariffs and how they are being handled are driving uncertainty and higher prices. This is causing customers to hold on issuing new purchase orders.

• The main issue created is with our foreign suppliers. We are now discussing a purchase or joint venture located in the U.S.

• We expect increases in purchasing costs, which we will need to pass on to our customers.

• America has had tariffs placed on all we do for a long time. I do believe that implementing the reciprocal (equal back and forth) taxes will hurt initially but will get us into a better spot in the end.

• It will make customers more dependent on manufacturers here in the states than going to China. I think my business will increase.

Innovated Machine & Tool Co. plans expansion

Innovated Machine & Tool Co. Inc., a sheet metal fabricator based in Newport News, Va., has announced plans for a 30,000-sq.-ft. expansion to its 60,000-sq.-ft. facility. The project, which will include a building addition and new equipment, is expected to create 25 jobs.

The expanded space will enhance the company’s ability to support the growing demand for data center power distribution enclosures. The additional area will be dedicated to mechanical assembly, as well as the storage of raw materials and finished products. The company also will add an AMADA combination laser-punch machine with automated loading systems to increase its metal cutting capabilities.

Sarah Richards, visionary and CEO of Jones Metal in Mankato, Minn., is like many others who have found a home in the metal fabricating industry. She’s had to catch up with peers who have been at it for decades.

In 2004, Marcia Jones Richards’ husband died, and she was stuck with a metal fabricating business that her parents, Mildred and Cecil Jones, had started in 1942. One option was to sell, which would have been a shame given the company’s manufacturing ingenuity and many inventions. The other was to reach out to the third generation to see if it interested them.

Sarah Richards, visionary and CEO of Jones Metal Inc., Mankato, Minn., and chairwoman of FMA’s board of directors, believes in fighting for a well-prepared manufacturing workforce.

In 2008, Sarah Richards answered the call. She had spent her years after graduating from the University of St. Thomas working as a PGA golf professional in the Twin Cities and Florida. She was ready to tackle a new course.

In the ensuing years, she’s embraced the family’s legacy of entrepreneurship and helped to grow Jones Metal. During that time, Richards said, the Fabricators and Manufacturers Association has played an important role in her growth as a leader, offering opportunities to learn about the industry and connect with her peers and technology experts.

For 2025, Richards is serving as the chairwoman of the FMA board of directors. The Fabricator spoke with her about her time on the board and what it’s been like as its leader. An edited version of the conversation follows.

The Fabricator:

What made you get involved with FMA, and what led you to be interested in pursuing a position on FMA’s board of directors?

Sarah Richards:

I got a taste of the FMA and how it could help me in our company after attending a few FABTECH events. Then I had started attending the FMA Annual Meetings and FMA Leadership Summits. And, of course, I learned a lot about the industry, machine tool options, leadership, and different things that helped me in my position at those events.

The Fabricators and Manufacturers Association is a supporter of the worker lifecycle—attracting individuals to a manufacturing career, developing their talents and knowledge through publications and training, and engaging them as they continue to enhance their skills in the metal fabricating industry.

What really struck me was how much I learned from the other attendees. We were all focused on similar challenges with the economy, the workforce, promoting manufacturing, capital investments, and continuous improvement activities. So many of these people had more experience than I will ever have just because I was a latecomer to the industry. I just decided I wanted to be around them as much as I possibly could.

So I took in more events that the FMA would put on, like The Fabricator Technology Tours, and just recognized even more how much the people and other FMA members could really help me. And they were willing.

Of course, some of the most entrepreneurial people in manufacturing are often found giving back. They’re really talented, and they are volunteering their time. I also recognized right away that some of the people that I gravitated toward most were FMA board members.

After hanging around some of them, I think they thought maybe I had something to offer, and I definitely was interested. So when I was asked about it, I jumped at the chance because the FMA is the best organization bar none in our industry. I wanted to be a part of the organization.

The Fabricator:

When did you first join FMA?

Richards:

When I first became president of Jones Metal, which was in 2012, I became a personal member. We joined as a premier company member around 2015.

The Fabricator:

What’s it like being chairwoman of the FMA board of directors? What have been some of the highlights?

Richards:

First of all, it’s been really nice. Everybody has been very gracious and welcoming when I first joined the board and as I moved through the different positions on the board. Now I’m super honored to hold the chair position this year, and I really appreciate the support of all the other directors and the staff. Being able to see the organization more closely and see how FMA works together, drives the mission, and works to support all of us in the industry, it’s really been a pleasure. A lot of times with boards you don’t get to interact with different staff members that much.

I’ve also had a chance to get to know many people in general over the years who have been FMA board members and SparkForce board members. Their mentorship and friendship is invaluable.

There’s also the coolness factor that comes with the chairwoman title. That isn’t too bad either.

The Fabricator:

In 2024, FMA rebranded and placed a new focus on supporting what it’s calling the worker lifecycle. Do you think the rebranding and support for the worker lifecycle messaging is resonating with metal fabricators?

Richards:

I do. When I first started with Jones Metal in like 2008, I was tasked with workforce development issues—attracting, engaging, developing, and training people. How do we get people into these programs at the trade schools? How do we build up the workforce? So it really resonates with me.

Over the years, the people I’ve talked with or gravitated to, they are leaders in our industry. They’re involved and always thinking ahead. But when it comes down to it, it’s all about people, right? No matter how much automation comes into our industry, it’s still all about people.

And we have to compete to attract, engage, and develop the best of the best out there.

If you’ve been a forward-thinking entrepreneurial type of metal fabricator, you’ve been working on this for a long time. That’s where FMA can help. It really captures that spirit of connecting with the next generation. That’s what SparkForce is all about. It really connects with the forward-thinking, entrepreneurial fabricators who’ve been working on this for a while.

FMA also helps to develop the talent that it attracts to the industry. FMA has so many different educational programs and events, all of which are opportunities to help people and engage people. It really helps to get manufacturing under their skin.

It’s been fantastic. The rebranding really captures what the organization has been working on and doing for the industry for more than 50 years.

The Fabricator:

What have you learned interacting with other board members during your time serving on the FMA board?

Richards:

Good governance. I appreciate organizations like FMA or other boards that I’ve been a part of and how they handle the governance side. It’s tricky.

When I talk about governance, I’m not talking about Robert’s Rules of Order so much. I’m talking about making sure that you have a good board, that the board evaluates its own performance, and that board makeup is representative of who the organization is serving.

The board makeup is especially important. Who’s on your board and what areas do they represent? How do you balance that, and what should the balance be?

The strategy part of being on the board is fun too. To be a part of strategic planning and see how other organizations do it is interesting.

Over time, what I’ve learned is that even though I don’t have 40 years of experience in the industry, I have skills or knowledge of different things that can be useful. So learning how to lean in and be part of the conversation has been a really great experience for me.

The Fabricator:

What would you tell another fabricator who might be considering joining FMA? What stands out as reasons for them to do that?

Richards:

I would tell them that no other organization offers complete support in the metal fabricating industry like FMA. You get up-to-date news and information about the economy. You get information about standout people and companies in the industry. You get updates on all the different technology and automation that you can consider employing in our businesses. You get all the educational tools for current staff members—and even future ones.

If I were starting a brand new fabrication company right now, I would look to the FMA as a place to provide me all the cornerstone pieces that I really need. A lot of fabricators, when they start out small, they get busy. They end up wearing many different hats. We don’t realize that there’s an organization like the FMA that can make your life a lot easier.

The value is incredible. Even if you take advantage of just one event or education program, it’s really a worthwhile investment.

The Fabricator:

What do you see happening in the metal fabricating industry for the rest of the year?

Richards:

It seems like it’s changing daily right now. There’s no doubt that there’s some trepidation and instability in terms of not knowing what’s happening, because so much of it is beyond what we can really control.

In the past, for example, if the agricultural sector was starting to sag a little bit, you could see it coming and have some time to prepare. Right now, we’re living in this instantaneous society where decisions are being made on a daily basis, and we can’t simply flip a switch and react.

Having said that, I am cautiously optimistic. There are sectors that are growing and will grow no matter what. Things like power generation and data centers—they aren’t going away any time soon.

I guess my advice would be that people need to be as balanced as possible with their portfolio. It’s been a challenging 12 months already with our business, but I still am optimistic about 2025.

The Fabricator:

Any advice for the chair that will succeed you or anyone else joining the FMA board of directors?

Richards:

For people who are coming into the board of directors and then eventually the executive committee, I would like to see them be fierce about the workforce lifecycle. We want to attract people to the metal fabricating industry, develop their talents, and engage them along their journey.

With FMA, I want to make sure that we’re taking care of the team and that we’re developing future leaders. I know that FMA President/CEO Ed Youdell is passionate about the FMA team and the organization’s activities, but I want to make sure that the board continues to see how important it is that we are just as fierce about the workforce lifecycle internally as we are externally.
