# Metal fabrication meets Big Tech

[TARİH: 01.12.2024 The Fabricator]

Fabtech 2024 Recap

How metal fabrication can replicate the best of the tech industry

By

Tim Heston

Images: Butler Photography

R

honda Dibachi has spent time in both Big Tech and the precision sheet metal fabrication world. She ran a fabricator in Southern California that made custom industrial lighting and knows her way around a punch press. She also has a long history in tech. Back in the 1980s, she was hired as the first development manager for Oracle’s manufacturing applications.

At FABTECH 2024, she gave a poignant presentation during the FABx Tech Talks that opened the mid-October show. Her message: The tech industry has some fundamental advantages that allow it to make more money and attract the best people. Manufacturing hasn’t shared those advantages—but it doesn’t have to be that way.

"Digitalization is a big deal," she said. "If you do something right, digitize it, and do it over and over again." She added that this creates the backbone of what’s supported the modern tech industry and, more broadly, some of the most successful companies in the North American economy:

reliably recurring revenue.

The insight might sound mundane, but it reflected a goal behind a lot of the concepts and technology on display at the industry’s largest event in North America. Despite coming on the heels of a hurricane, this year’s FABTECH drew 30,000 people and 1,500 exhibitors. Many touted equipment and (especially) software that, at the end of the day, could increase throughput and shorten the time between the initial order and final shipment.

The demand for quick, reliable response has brought to the fore the importance of software, data analytics, and the potential of artificial intelligence (AI). Like nearly every other modern enterprise, metal fabricators now find themselves in the data business. Good data unleashes the power of software, empowers collaboration, and breaks down walls between departmental fiefdoms.

All this sets the stage for a ballet of metal fabrication efficiency on the floor, with machines and people moving at just the right time, all working to produce as much value as they can with the least amount of resources.

This unleashes the capacity of quick response, brings customer loyalty, which in turn brings about reliably recurring revenue, Dibachi’s favorite aspect of business.

About COGS

"I’m talking about the fear, uncertainty, and doubt that limit manufacturing. It’s about how you can take the wonderful things that the tech industry does and apply them to manufacturing, so that you can make more money."

That’s how Dibachi began her talk on FABTECH’s opening day. Today, she’s founder and CEO of HeyScottie (

heyscottie.com

), a marketplace for metal finishing that offers instant quoting. The aim is to break down the barriers of acquiring new customers, especially those that could lead to recurring revenue.

"Running a manufacturing company was a lot harder than taking a tech company public," Dibachi said. "It was hard to hire people. It was harder to get a bank loan. It was harder to get a product to market. It was harder to collaborate. In fact, every single step in creating a manufacturing company was harder than the technology company that I had cofounded earlier. That made me angry."

She added, however, that her industrial lighting company was extremely successful. Few could compete with the firm’s ability to respond quickly with a customized solution. "We could ship a $100,000 order and turn it around—including the engineer and quality assurance—in less than a week. It really was a great company."

Speaker Rhonda Dibachi, CEO and founder of HeyScottie, has experience both in Big Tech and the manufacturing world. She spoke about what manufacturing could learn from the best-in-class players in tech, and how those lessons could set the foundation for growth.

Even so, tech companies have a lot going for them. As Dibachi explained, "Tech companies don’t have COGS [cost of goods sold]. How wonderful it would be if you could make money without having to deal with COGS. And how about capex? Tech companies don’t have to buy punch press machines or press brakes. They often just buy furniture, chairs, and desks. That’s what a tech company calls ‘capex.’"

Despite Hurricane Milton’s unwelcome visit the week before, FABTECH Orlando still drew 30,000 people.

Operational expenses are lower too. Manufacturing has hourly wages that create paperwork and overhead. Moreover, manufacturing companies must meet complex requirements to bring a product to market, like certifications. "With software, you usually just put it out there," she said. "You don’t have to have UL listings for software. It’s like playing tennis without a net."

Beyond all this, tech companies have preferential revenue recognition laws. "You can recognize revenue almost as soon as you think up a product. In manufacturing, you recognize the revenue only after you’ve incurred all the cost.

"All this causes the biggest problems in manufacturing companies. There’s not as much money in it. So, when you have lower valuations, you have a hard time attracting quality people, and you can’t buy the equipment you want."

So, what can a manufacturer do to overcome all these problems? After all, a company can’t make things without COGS and capex. The idea, she said, is to establish that predictably recurring revenue. Tech companies famously do this through subscriptions. Sell the subscription once, and the revenue keeps rolling in. "The biggest reasons tech companies receive high valuations is that they have that recurring revenue. That means you don’t have to be there to make money. You make money automatically."

Traditionally, fabricators have done this with what Dibachi called the "whale accounts," those that send predictable blanket orders that keep the machines humming. "How do you trap these whales? I suggest three things: rapid response, competitive pricing, and suitable lead time."

Problem is, responding rapidly to quotes isn’t easy, especially when an estimator is trying to get the cost of a product just right. Dibachi described a different approach: top-down pricing combined with job categorization and some guardrails.

"Top-down pricing takes patterns of jobs, groups them together, and treats them all the same from a pricing perspective. Don’t drill down into the actual details. Try to keep it at the highest level. Category A has a shop rate of $345, but category B has a shop rate of $456." Combining this with job categorization—grouping different jobs together around similar cost structures—can help refine the pricing model.

"Finally, I have guardrails," she said. "I don’t tackle jobs that are too big, jobs that are not formed correctly, or don’t meet other parameters. I don’t bet my shop on it."

Predictability Upstream

To be sure, metal fabrication isn’t a monolith. Whale accounts help facilitate growth, but too few of them can lead to high revenue concentration and great risk. Still, a constraint in sales and quoting can choke an entire operation, as can late deliveries and rework caused by operational chaos, be it in order processing (including customer communication) or on the shop floor.

The general idea is to make metal fabrication a bit more like Big Tech, with rapid, predictably recurring revenue. The vast collection of technology at FABTECH could help fabricators reach that goal.

An old machine that breaks continually adds unpredictability; the shop is less likely to deliver on time, which in turn hurts customer relationships. It’s certainly not a way to achieve predictably recurring revenue.

New machines make setup more predictable. Schedule-based preventive maintenance and sensor-based predictive maintenance minimize unexpected downtime and ensure equipment is available to produce. Automated loading and parts stacking make the denesting process occur in the same way at the same speed, every time. And software helps look at the entire part flow, weigh the demands—customer due dates along with material yield requirements—to help planners come up with the best solution.

"Ultimately, it comes down to connectivity and flexibility," said Doug Wood, director of North American sales, sheet metal solutions, at Hexagon (

hexagon.com

). "Are you able to make decisions on the fly? If you need to run, say, three jobs at the same time, do you need to increase that to four? What does that do to my material utilization? Do I have more or fewer remnants, and what kind of machines are running these nests? Can those machines handle remnants, or would remnants throw a wrench into things? Today, shops need to make so many decisions, and it’s important to be able to build tools that can help make the process as automatic as possible while still maintaining the ability to override these decisions that impact downstream applications."

Predictability Downstream

The more predictable upstream processes become, the greater potential for automation downstream. This includes welding automation that incorporates simplified and even automated programming. As just one example, Path Robotics (

path-robotics.com

) showcased a cell that not only automates welding but also the entire fitting process. David Mayell, account executive, pointed to the robot in action. "This robot does everything from unstructured bin picking to reorienting the part itself, to then fitting it up and welding it out. This cell can do various components. It’s doing everything, and the operator isn’t involved at all."

After uncertainty during the days leading up to the show, the weather cleared, and 1,500 exhibitors participated in this year’s FABTECH. FABTECH organizers also established a relief fund to support communities in Florida and the surrounding states affected by the fall hurricanes.

FABTECH exhibitors also showed cobot automation advancing as well. For instance, Universal Robots (

universal-robots.com

) has released a software capability to its OEM partners that allows a cobot to track a part that’s moving—like one attached to a rotating headstock or tailstock.

Companies also expanded the use of cobot welding beyond the gas metal arc process. For instance, THG Automation (

thgautomation.com

) offered cobots wielding plasma cutting torches, gas tungsten arc welding torches, as well as a laser welding gun.

In another example showing just how far the cobot has come, during a ceremony at the show, Modine Manufacturing Co.’s plant in Jefferson City, Mo., won the 2024 Welding Excellence Award from the American Welding Society’s Welding Equipment Manufacturing Committee (WEMCO). One reason the company won was its nontraditional adoption of cobots.

Traditionally, cobots have been seen as a stepping-stone for low- and medium-volume production, before adopting traditional robotics for high-volume production. Modine, working together with fixture provider Flextur (

flextur.com

), has adopted a quick-change system that ensures consistent setup for production runs.

"These quick-change cobot tables have fixtures that pneumatically clamp into position," said Doug Scheetz, vice president of marketing and product development at Flextur. "So, you can take it off, then put it back on in the afternoon, or run it a year from now, and it will zero-out the system the same way."

Flextur’s Steven Wengerd, director of sales, said he sees a new perspective among manufacturers looking at cobots. "They no longer are looking at it as stepping-stone automaton. They have welders that can program the cobots. They don’t need welding engineers. They don’t need to know the exact torch tip location. They have no desire to go to traditional robotics. That represents a shift in thinking."

FABTECH still had plenty of traditional robots on display, of course, a technology that won’t be replaced anytime soon. In fact, their adoption overall will likely rise as technology becomes more flexible and upstream processing becomes more predictable. Adoption is even spreading to grinding and polishing. Companies like Titan Robotics (

titanrobots.com

) and Gray-Matter Robotics (

graymatter-robotics.com

) offered self-programming systems that could change the reliance on manual labor for those tedious steps at the final stages of production.

Automation applies not just to how machines handle and process parts but also how parts are tracked in between processes. More fabricators are looking into the use of autonomous mobile robots, but beyond this, they’re looking to track where work-in-process (WIP) is and how quickly it’s flowing, from raw stock to the shipping dock.

For instance, WISER Systems (

wisersystems.com

) showcased its real-time location tracking systems using ultrawide-band technology. During one FABTECH presentation, Logan Maxwell, director of technical sales, pointed to a PowerPoint slide with a shop floor map, populated with hundreds of dots, each showing WIP locations on racks and on carts being transported down the value stream. The WISER system collects WIP location data that can in turn be fed to other software systems. At present, the system is most often being used as a location tracker for stored items (no more hunting for lost items), but the technology could be applied for other uses—including the monitoring of part-flow velocity.

The show took up 750,000 sq. ft. of exhibit space at the Orange County Convention Center in Orlando, Fla.

Think of it as an advanced form of the "paper trick," where fabricators place a piece of paper on raw stock or cut parts and record a timestamp. The paper stays with the work all the way through the value stream until the order ships—revealing the overall manufacturing time for a specific job.

Real-time location systems (RTLS) could allow shops to track every part and every job as they flow through the plant. WIP on the move shows that everything is flowing smoothly and perhaps even better than expected. A cluster of dots could reveal a growing queue of work in front of a work center, indicating a constraint, perhaps a problem with a machine or a scheduling mishap.

Software Steps Up

Today, quoting technology—on display at FABTECH by software providers like Paperless Parts (

paperlessparts.com

) and being developed in-house by several industry players—might help fabricators become more like their tech company counterparts. So could software designed to perfect setups and sequencing on the shop floor, so that workflow velocity increases and WIP never sits idle for long. Predictive maintenance technology can help prevent unexpected machine downtime, making production capacity more predictable. All this and more could help the industry’s productivity challenges in recent years.

"Labor productivity in manufacturing has been flat or negative for the past 14 years. You know what else happened in that time? We had 4G, then 5G, high-quality software, a shift to the cloud. We’ve had huge technological steps forward, yet we’re still losing productivity. Why? We lost the context. We’re good at providing single-point solutions that help people optimize the way they create a widget. There’s a compartmentalization that takes place in manufacturing when we try to make everything incrementally more efficient."

That was Aaron Lober, vice president of marketing at CADDi (

us.caddi.com

), a manufacturing intelligence platform designed to "connect your entire parts record—every piece of data with the manufacture of a custom part—back to a drawing." He said that the aim is to "get people across the supply chain looking and thinking about the same thing in an apples-to-apple way, a shared context … Often, the salesperson, the procurement person, shop floor operation, and engineering don’t have equal context on what’s happing in production on any given part. That really is the first thing we need to solve."

Imagine an extraordinarily powerful laser cutting blanks that then sit on a rack for months, or perhaps an amazing robot that welds the wrong part. Why? Lober attributed this to compartmentalization. Machines offer eye-popping productivity, but miscommunication in the supply chain still holds overall productivity back.

CADDi was just one of many software providers at the show. Indeed, a person who last attended a FABTECH two decades ago would be shocked to see the number of screens on display and the kind of solutions being discussed.

Machines continue to become more powerful. Intelligence in programming—in bending, welding, even automated powder coating—will help spread automation up and down the value stream on the floor. Even so, more fabricators are looking for holistic improvement—fabrication empowered by information, so people and machines work on exactly what’s needed at the right time.

Imagine products designed with manufacturability in mind, where supplier and fabricator collaborate closely, where everyone has an eye on real capacity, and where quick response has shortened the order-to-cash cycle. As reflected by Dibachi’s FABx presentation that opened the show, new ways of thinking could help fabricators become more like their best-in-class Big Tech counterparts—generating predictably recurring revenue and reliably growing month after month, year after year.
