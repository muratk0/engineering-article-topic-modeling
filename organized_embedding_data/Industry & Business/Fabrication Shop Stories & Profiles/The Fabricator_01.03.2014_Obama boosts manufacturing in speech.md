President Obama continued to buff his manufacturing policy credentials—at least rhetorically—in his State of the Union address.

His Jan. 28 speech to Congress boasted about “a manufacturing sector that’s adding jobs for the first time since the 1990s.” The president also announced the administration would add another six manufacturing research “hubs” to the two it is already funding.

The two existing hubs, called Institutes, are located in Youngstown, Ohio, and at North Carolina State University in Raleigh, N.C. The latter is just getting underway and will be devoted to next-generation power electronics. The first institute was created in 2012 and called the National Additive Manufacturing Innovation Institute, but now refers to itself as America Makes. The president said these institutes, plus the six new ones, give the U.S. the chance “to beat other countries in the race for the next wave of high-tech manufacturing jobs.”

The institutes conduct research related to manufacturing technologies, which may or may not morph into either new processes or products, which may or may not lead to the creation of new manufacturing jobs. America Makes, which just announced its second round of funding, does not create jobs, nor do any of the research companies receiving America Makes grants. The same will be true for the new North Carolina institute and any that come after them.

“As we advance manufacturing, we are advancing jobs, but it is difficult to take a thermometer and measure the job creation,” said Scott Deutsch, the spokesman for America Makes.

Scott Paul, president, Alliance for American Manufacturing, welcomed the president’s strong rhetorical focus on manufacturing. But he added, “Progress, despite the rosy picture painted by the president, has been painfully slow. And in some cases, such as the trade deficit with China, we’ve seen backsliding.”

Paul highlighted a number of things the president could do to boost the economy, but that he failed to mention in his State of the Union address. Those include designating China a currency manipulator, tightening “buy America” compliance among federal agencies, ensuring the U.S. isn’t exporting energy products to nonmarket economies in a way that cuts into domestic supplies for U.S. companies, and launching an executive effort to cut in half the trade deficit with China.

Obama did throw in a pitch for ostensibly improving the U.S. trade deficit with Europe and the Pacific Rim.

“We need to work together on tools like bipartisan Trade Promotion Authority to protect our workers, protect our environment, and open new markets to new goods stamped ‘Made in the USA,’” Obama said.

Trade Promotion Authority (TPA) has been available to every president in the past 30 years for some time during his tenure. It lapsed in 2009.

TPA allows a president to submit a trade pact to Congress without either the House of Representatives or the Senate being able to offer amendments. It is also called “fast track” authority.

The administration started negotiating a trade pact with the European Union last year and one with 11 Pacific Rim countries during the administration of George W. Bush. Those deals would have a much better chance of being finalized if TPA were granted by Congress. The Bipartisan Congressional Trade Priorities Act, which does that, was introduced in Congress in January. But despite significant Democratic and Republican support, the bill suffers from the opposition of Senate Majority Leader Harry Reid, D-Nev.
