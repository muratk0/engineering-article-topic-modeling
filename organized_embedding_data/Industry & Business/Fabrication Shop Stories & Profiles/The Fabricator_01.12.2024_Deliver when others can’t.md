# Deliver when others can’t

[TARİH: 01.12.2024 The Fabricator]

Biz Talk

Quick response sets a metal fabricator apart

Tim Heston

I

t’s the end of the year, a good time for reflection and to think about what’s to come. I write this just a week after the election, and I’m hearing and reading a steady stream of disparate views about what the 2025 economy will look like. One trend, though, likely won’t change: Shops will need flexibility, including the ability to quote and (ideally) win work quickly, then deliver faster and better than anyone else.

To make this happen might require new ways of thinking about a host of operational assumptions, especially in the custom and contract metal fabrication arena.

Will taking three or more days to respond to a quote cut it anymore? How about lead times of four to six weeks?

Questioning common perceptions can be a healthy exercise, and two areas in particular might be worth some additional scrutiny:

machine uptime and inventory

.

Thinking Anew About Machine Uptime

The gold standard has been overall equipment effectiveness, or OEE, which incorporates utilization (production hours versus available hours), performance (standard versus actual cycle times), and quality (number of good parts compared to the total produced).

"The behaviors and outcomes generated by using this metric are suboptimal because the measure is related to equipment, not the production system." That was Joyce Warnacut in her book, "The Monetary Value of Time: Why Traditional Accounting Systems Make Customers Wait."

Her point with OEE and with traditional utilization metrics overall is that they focus on, and therefore incentivize, the productivity of individual machines and work centers. That laser focus on

local

efficiency ignores, and sometimes hinders,

overall

throughput: that is, how quickly work can flow from order release to the customer’s receiving dock.

Specifically, Warnacut writes, OEE might incentivize people to "keep machines running whether the schedule is driven by demand or the production is going into stock; run large batches to minimize changeover time and maximize utilization; run as many cycles as possible, as quickly as possible; [and] limit the number of rejects, regardless of cost."

This perspective challenges the assumption that a machine that’s running is making money. The opposite is true, in fact. A running machine costs money, consuming energy and material. A

shipped order

makes money.

Uptime might be overrated, but

immediate equipment availability

remains critical. The concept can require a nuanced view. A machine that’s not producing, yet is immediately available to start, can be extremely valuable. Planned machine downtime for preventive maintenance (PM) is like a good insurance policy. PM might mean the machine isn’t available, but it can help ensure equipment remains ready and available when needed. An unexpectedly down machine—be it a mechanical or labor issue—obviously isn’t immediately available and, hence, not a good thing.

Similarly, an idle machine that’s tied up in an excessively long or challenging setup is unavailable and, hence, detrimental. The challenge here is defining what "excessively long" really means. Small batch sizes get a shop closer to single-piece flow. It reduces work-in-process (WIP) and overall lead times. A shop can use common methods from the lean toolbox, like 5S, having tools near the point of use, proving out setups offline, and documenting setup procedures. But at what point do numerous setups become detrimental, forcing overall throughput to decline and lead time to grow?

This calls for a little thought experiment. Imagine a fabricator that tracks

productivity

(how many parts per hour a machine produces for a certain job, for instance) as well as

machine availability

. To track workflow velocity, they put a location tag on all the WIP flowing through the shop.

On a screen, they see a simulated traffic pattern showing all the job routings. They watch for red flags and monitor production—similar to a traffic reporter watching a highway. Stalled WIP means queue times are getting longer.

The traffic’s getting heavier. Why? Is there a setup problem? Is the material unavailable? Did an operator call in sick? How can we prevent this from happening again?

Meanwhile, shop leaders look to the shipping dock to measure success. They’re looking for more orders consistently shipping on time over a certain period. Overall productivity rises, each employee creates more value for the customer, and the potential for revenue growth increases.

Is Inventory an Asset?

That depends. Traditional cost accounting says it is, but various improvement methodologies, including lean manufacturing and quick response manufacturing (QRM), put forth a different view. Lean purists would argue that any inventory that’s not moving—raw, WIP, or finished goods—is a waste and should be avoided. Raw material should be "drip fed" to the shop, sent immediately into the value stream, then shipped out the door.

That sounds great, but it doesn’t account for real-world problems, like supply chain issues that became abundantly clear since the pandemic. Customers sometimes order more or less than expected. Purchased components might be delivered late. Raw material might not be available immediately. What if a prospect calls and needs something in a rush? Inventory has costs, but the opportunity costs stemming from unavailable material could be even greater.

During a FABTECH panel discussion in October, Eric Miller, president of Miller Fabrication Solutions, a fabricator in western Pennsylvania, described his overall approach to inventory. "I want raw stock and I want finished goods, but nothing in between. I don’t want WIP. I don’t want work that’s semi-finished."

The operation does have some strategic inventory buffers to absorb variability, of course, but as Miller explained, the shop continues to strive for that "zero WIP" ideal. Having the right finished goods (those with little to no chance of becoming obsolete) as well as maintaining sufficient raw stock can help the fabricator deliver when others can’t.

Strategies might change depending on a fabricator’s mix of customers and suppliers, including its relationship with service centers, component suppliers, and outside service providers (powder coating, plating, heat treating, and so forth). Regardless, the ability to produce on demand, better and faster than anyone else, will continue to set most metal fabricators apart.

Is inventory good or bad? Inventory has costs, but the opportunity costs stemming from unavailable material could be even greater.
