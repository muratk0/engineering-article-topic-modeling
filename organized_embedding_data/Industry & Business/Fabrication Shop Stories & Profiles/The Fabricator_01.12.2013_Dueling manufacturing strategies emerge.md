# Dueling manufacturing strategies emerge

[TARİH: 01.12.2013 The Fabricator]

Around Washington

One focuses on investment, the other on reducing regulations

sbarlas@verizon.net

.

Two manufacturing policy initiatives have sprung up, one on Capitol Hill and one off. But they seem to be going in opposite directions.

Twenty-two Senate Democrats are behind one of the initiatives. They have wrapped 40 ideas, some of them already existing bills and a few with some GOP support, into one package called the Manufacturing Jobs for America initiative. (For more details, visit

www.coons.senate.gov/manufacturing/policy#madeinamericamanufacturinga ct.

) Sen. Chris Coons, D-Del., a former member of the National Association of Manufacturers (NAM), is the lead sponsor of the package.

Meanwhile, NAM itself announced its own Manufacturing Competitiveness Initiative on Oct. 29, within one day of the Coons announcement. NAM’s own effort involves deploying two former members of the Senate—Blanche Lincoln, D-Ark., and George Allen, R-Va.—in some unspecified effort to “highlight the competitiveness challenges facing manufacturers.”

Obviously, there is a lack of coordination between the debuts of the two initiatives. NAM President Jay Timmons did issue a statement saying the 22 Democrats had “the potential to provide a critical path towards bipartisan agreement on issues facing manufacturers and their employees.” Lincoln and Allen initially will focus on “reducing the impact of unnecessarily burdensome regulations on manufacturers.” The Senate Democrats’ list of 40 bills does not include a single, focused deregulatory proposal.

Republican support in the House exists for initiatives in one of the four areas touched on by the Senate Democrats’ Manufacturing Jobs for America: access to capital.

Rather, the Democrats’ bills, most not yet introduced, are grouped into four categories: investment capital, job training, research and development, and exports. In the skills training area, for example, Coons has a proposal called “Race to the Shop,” which aims to create an annual $150 million competition to promote reforms in workforce education and skill training for advanced manufacturing in states and metropolitan areas. No presumptive Republican co-sponsor is listed. The Made in America Manufacturing Act and Rebuild American Manufacturing Act, for example, have been introduced, and there are no Republican co-sponsors for those either.

When asked why no Republicans are listed with the 22 Democrats, Ian Koski, Coons’ press secretary, said, “Senator Coons and others have been talking with a number of Republicans, and our hope is that they will join this effort and work with us to build support for manufacturing jobs.” The Democrats have not talked with the Republican Senate leadership about the initiative, however.

As to the path forward, Koski explained, “The senators who are leading those bills are going to renew their effort for co-sponsors and see how much support they can get. Several committee and subcommittee chairs are exploring hearings on these bills and ideas. The bills that earn the most bipartisan co-sponsors will receive the most attention. The bottom line is, no one wants to bring a bill to the floor if it’s going to fail. No one wants another partisan exercise.”

Republican support in the Senate for any manufacturing initiative is critical because the House is controlled by the Republicans and a bill will not pass in the House without some Republican backing. Republican support in the House exists for initiatives in one of the four areas touched on by the Senate Democrats’ Manufacturing Jobs for America: access to capital.

The House Financial Services Committee held a hearing Oct. 23 on bills related to business development companies (BDCs), which have become more prominent as sources of capital for small manufacturers over the past few years because of the lending hesitancy of regional commercial banks. Two House bills, H.R. 31 and 1800, would ease the leverage limits for BDCs, allowing such firms to maintain a greater ratio of debt to asset valuation on their balance sheets. That would allow them to finance more companies.
