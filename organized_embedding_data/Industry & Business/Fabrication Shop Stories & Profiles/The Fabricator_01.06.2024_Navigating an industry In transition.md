With interest rates as they are, people are thinking twice before making big purchases, like recreational vehicles. Others—particularly those related to infrastructure, power generation, and semiconductors—are still going full steam ahead. Many in the FAB 40 reported mildly sluggish times earlier this year, but the story might change dramatically as this election year draws to a close.

Supply chain problems have ebbed, but they haven’t disappeared. Most fabricators aren’t experiencing acute supply issues, but other players in the supply chain are, which can create lulls in business—hence the need for flexibility. Navigating swings in demand isn’t new. A custom or contract metal fabricator that isn’t flexible usually doesn’t last long in the custom or contract metal manufacturing business. Even so, the FAB 40 list this year looks different from the one 10 years ago. Revenue numbers are much higher, of course, but other changes are in the works too.

OEMs are reshaping their supply chains, at least to some degree. Near- and reshoring are real, but we’re not living in a postglobalization world either. Fabricators need to deliver on promises to earn those long-term relationships that have helped sustain so many shops over years and decades. On the surface, most companies are producing parts and assemblies just as they have for years. But underneath, some significant changes are afoot.

The Generational Shift

Age escapes no one, and owners of small and medium-sized businesses are weighing their options. Private equity and other investors are pumping money into this industry and consolidating what used to be small (and some not-so-small), privately held, often family-run businesses.

Some look upon private equity and other outside investors with caution. Rollups don’t always go well, especially if investors swoop in to buy the assets of a company that isn’t doing well in the first place. The companies in the FAB 40 tell a different story. Investors are seeking already successful companies and looking to help them grow.

Servant Leadership

Investors looking to buy never ignore a shop’s financials and revenue mix, of course. A fabricator’s revenue diversity, drawing from across the economy, is partly what makes this business so attractive. When some sectors are down, others are up.

The business model isn’t a mystery, either. Unlike some Big Tech acquisitions and other plays that get media attention, how profit is created in metal fab isn’t complicated. The plants can be operationally complex, granular data from the shop floor might be lacking, and processes might not be controlled well at all times, but the business model is clear: Raw stock comes in, value is added, products are shipped, and the fabricator is paid. Know your costs, maximize the value, minimize the waste, and you’ve got profits to reinvest and grow.

Still, people matter. A poor cultural match can turn a merger into a nightmare. Talking to larger fab shops with acquisition plans, I hear many refer to the value of servant leadership and the detrimental aspects of a command-and-control environment. Company founders with their hands in every aspect of the operation might work for a startup, but not necessarily for a $40 million-plus organization.

New Technology Mixes

Industry consolidation is taking myriad forms. Sometimes, new owners put companies under a loosely assembled corporate umbrella, though the individual brands stay intact. Other times, it entails a new regional or even continent-wide brand.

Consolidation also involves evolving a technology mix to serve what customers need. What used to be a "precision sheet metal shop" now has machining, plate fab, and a plethora of other component-creation technologies in its wheelhouse. Then comes value-add services like assembly, design for manufacturability, and project management, overseeing and coordinating how fabricated metal is installed, used, and maintained in the field.

In the past, company leaders might look at an opportunity and think, "That’s outside our sandbox." Today, fewer are thinking about sandboxes. Instead, they’re thinking about what their best customers need.

There are limits. No company can be everything to everyone. Regardless, more than ever, the needs of the customer mix are shaping the contract metal fabricator. Some of the largest players in the industry can be difficult to pigeonhole.

Automate for a Better Workday

No one wants to spend a career hammering out blanks from a cut plate—or, at least, that’s not the kind of labor customers are paying for. They’re also not paying to move parts or to manually key in production data into an ERP. Instead, customers are paying for better ideas, quality components, and attention to detail.

Fabricators want to align what people do during the day with what customers pay for. This spurs the need for automation, both in machinery and (especially) software—streamlining the flow of information. The more time people spend sorting parts and manually reporting data, the less time they have for everything else.

More than that, automated data-gathering reveals hidden inefficiencies—perhaps a setup that takes longer than it should, frequent changeovers and low uptime in laser cutting, or rework that points to less-than-ideal material. Inefficiencies can stay hidden for years in the high-product-mix world of metal fabrication. Software is bringing these inefficiencies to the footlights.

About Collaboration

Many complain about the lack of skilled operators and welders, but in truth, the entire metal fabrication process chain is suffering from the lack of some basic knowledge about how a sheet metal part is created: how a press brake tool air-forms a radius, how distortion occurs in welding, why metal can bow after cutting, and why some cut edges have more dross than others. Get the design right from the get-go, and many manufacturing challenges go away.

This is just the beginning. Design might affect how a product is installed or used, which in turn can lead to a metal fabricator expanding its role in the supply chain. No longer just providing parts or subassemblies, it’s communicating with myriad stakeholders to ensure all goes smoothly. That customer relationship can stand in stark contrast to sourcing overseas.

The country’s largest custom and contract fabricators are broadening their reach up and down the supply chain, becoming the keystone that holds vastly complex projects together. For an industry, that’s not a bad place to be.
