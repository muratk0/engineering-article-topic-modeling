# ACE Metal Crafts keeps the flow

[TARİH: 01.12.2024 The Fabricator]

Cover Story

Dynamic scheduling in a lean, high-product-mix environment

By

Tim Heston

Employees at Ace Metal Crafts observe the shop’s dynamic schedule, posted on screens throughout the shop.

Images: Ace Metal Crafts

W

alk the shop floor at Ace Metal Crafts, a 138-employee custom stainless steel fabricator in Bensenville, Ill., and you’ll find scheduling boards where everyone can see, at a glance, where jobs stand—if they’re ahead of, on, or behind schedule—and, most important, what needs to be worked on next.

Workers might see the job sequence change or perhaps a new job appear. Customers might make a new request, or the loads in certain areas might be higher or lower than expected, and so the schedule adjusts to keep everything flowing.

Angela Pitzo, vice president of manufacturing, pointed to a cart where workers perform gas metal arc, gas tungsten arc, and even hand laser welding. The part kits comprised cut and formed pieces from upstream processes, milled parts from the company’s machining operation, as well as various hardware and purchased components.

The cart was staged before welding, the constraint process that’s the heartbeat of the entire high-product-mix operation. Constraints might move depending on the mix of jobs on the floor, but for Ace Metal, it’s almost always in welding. That’s why everyone in the shop works to keep those constraints continually fed, ideally with all parts arriving before welding at nearly the same time. The constraint process affects how parts are scheduled and flowed through the shop’s primary cutting processes, including band sawing, tube laser cutting, and flat sheet laser cutting.

"Just one job might have 60 or 70 subassemblies," Pitzo explained. "Those will get kitted together for welding. Individual kits might be sequenced together with dozens of other kits and different jobs, all of which flow toward final kitting and assembly."

Ray Baade, vice president of information systems and layout engineering, chimed in: "So far this year, we’ve run nearly 6,000 different jobs through our laser cell alone. That’s typical for us. There’s just a huge variety in what we do here."

You won’t, however, see piles of work-in-process (WIP)—telltale signs of traditional batch processing and long lead times. You’ll instead see signs of lean manufacturing in action: organized work areas, part kits on carts moving from one area to the next. WIP exists, but most of it is staged in specific areas—namely, after cutting and just before welding.

The reason behind WIP placement and overall part flow has to do with the product mix Ace Metal now fabricates. The company’s history proves why metal fabricators are so difficult to pigeonhole. What works in one shop can utterly fail in another. Why? When the product mix changes, so does everything else.

Then and Now

I last visited the fabricator in 2008, back when the shop had several CO

2

lasers, a shiny new tube laser with tapping capability, and a very different planning and part-flow methodology.

The operation at the time focused mainly on piece-part manufacturing. The final step usually involved some welding and minor subassembly, but after that, the work shipped off to food processing and other equipment makers for final assembly. This mix of work suited traditional sequential manufacturing extremely well. Instead of grouping like jobs together and overproducing (looking far ahead in the schedule) to reduce or save on setups, everyone’s focus turned toward quick changeover and sheet remnant management at the lasers. A brake setup might take a little longer, since one job might be very different from the next, but no matter; the approach kept jobs flowing, and within just days, parts would ship out the door and arrive at the customer’s loading dock.

Ace Metal’s place in the supply chain changed significantly during the ensuing years. Shop managers and sales personnel gradually landed more welding, assembly, and supply chain management work. Today, the shop produces not just parts or weldments or subassemblies, but pieces of equipment involving an extensive list of components. Put another way, instead of parts, Ace Metal now is fabricating entire systems.

This has changed how the shop manages part flow. Lead times are longer than they were in the piece-part manufacturing days, simply because of the nature of the work. The overall flow time, from initial cutting to final shipment, used to be about eight days; now it can be several weeks—but the metric needs to be put in context. Systems fabrication requires significant coordination involving dozens of subassemblies coming together with purchased components in final assembly.

The change in product mix increased fabrication complexity in a big way, but as sources pointed out, that’s a small price to pay, considering all the value employees are providing to customers. Generally speaking, piece-part fabrication has a more transactional customer-supplier relationship. It’s not so hard for the customer to move its business elsewhere. But when the fabricator continues to add more value—including assembly and even supply chain management—that customer-supplier relationship and overall competitive position becomes stronger. That’s not a bad business model, as long as shop floor operations can handle the increased complexity.

A welder works on a subassembly. Ace Metal’s welders can perform gas metal arc, gas tungsten arc, and laser welding.

This describes Ace Metal’s evolution. It remains a job shop; much of what’s on the floor won’t be seen again. It still produces piece-parts and weldments, too, but the majority of its product mix now involves comprehensive, complex assemblies. In this arena, the playing field isn’t so crowded.

Very Mixed-Mode Manufacturing

Ace Metal’s market position puts its scheduling methodology in context. Work has become incredibly various, which has created a staff of talented welders and fabricators. That said, people don’t learn the craft overnight, and that directly affects the speed at which products flow through welding.

As Pitzo explained, "Say we have a number of very tight-tolerance jobs in our current batch of work flowing through the shop, but we happened to have a few new welders, and I need some easier work to give them." In this case, she adjusts the schedule to ensure a few easier jobs get pulled into the sequence.

She clarified that new welders are given challenging work, but they often work at a slower pace. It isn’t their fault; challenging stainless steel fabrication work simply takes experience. By receiving a mix of easier and challenging jobs, newer welders can maintain throughput while gaining valuable experience. But if the mix happens to involve a whole series of challenging jobs, welding can become an even greater constraint than it already is—hence the need to tweak the job sequence.

A vertical rack from Eurostorage, designed to prevent scratching, stores sheet remnants near the laser.

Years ago, Ace Metal had several CO

2

lasers. Today, it has just one automated fiber laser.

Upstream, new Bystronic press brakes—programmed offline—ease changeover. And today, just one automated Mitsubishi fiber laser can do the work of several CO

2

lasers. In recent years, the fabricator has invested in several IPG Photonics hand laser welding systems, which help welders create those long stringer beads with minimal heat input. But laser welding can’t replace gas tungsten arc welding (GTAW, or TIG) entirely, and in most cases, the speed of that process governs the pace of work through the entire shop.

When any change happens in the schedule, an employee sees it immediately on monitors showing color-coded scheduling boards. The change sometimes happens because of a mismatch between available resources and the work flowing through the shop. Often, however, scheduling changes occur due to requests from the customer.

These remain a common conundrum in custom metal fabrication. A major customer calls, and the shop bends over backward to squeeze the job in. These "fast pass" expedited jobs can wreak havoc on flow and send other jobs behind. Just saying "no" to a major customer might be a nice idea, but realities are more complex. After all, many custom fabricators sell themselves on their ability to change direction on a dime. When a customer calls with a change, they do everything within reason to accommodate.

Here, good data points at Ace Metal help planners make strategic decisions. They don’t throw an expedited order blindly to the floor. First, they analyze the ripple effects. The shop has years’ worth of clock-in and clock-out data, part of the scheduling module within ECI Solutions’ M1 enterprise resource planning (ERP) software.

"We have another layer in our scheduling system that tells us how badly an unexpected move [such as a change-order] will affect our current production load," Baade explained. "The system tells us exactly where we stand, and when a new order comes in, it will recalculate the loads."

He added that the system identifies optimal release times for new orders that will best fit the production schedule and, most important, send as few ripples as possible into the overall flow.

"Once the change is made, the new sequence appears immediately on the scheduling boards," Pitzo explained, adding that quick communication helps people on the floor react and, ultimately, prevent those small ripples from becoming tidal waves.

Sequencing at the Laser

Employees call the laser cutting department "the pacemaker." The moniker doesn’t necessarily refer to how quickly the laser head cuts or finishes a particular nest. It instead refers to how quickly and effectively the laser can meet downstream demand. The last thing planners want is for the machine to spend hours cutting jobs that aren’t needed immediately, just to improve material yield and laser cutting uptime. At the same time, they can’t completely ignore the efficiencies gained by combining a multitude of jobs on a single nest on a specific gauge.

Angela Pitzo, vice president of manufacturing,

and Ray Baade, vice president of information systems and layout engineering, work to ensure WIP stays on the move, from the first cut to the final shipment, with few stops in between.

Jobs might require a half dozen different material thicknesses, and looking just hours or a few days out into the schedule would likely result in a lot of sheet remnants. A big reason shops processing cosmetically critical work choose not to manage remnants is the increased chance of scratching. Storing a small filler part on a rack for several weeks seems like a better alternative than a stainless steel remnant becoming scratched during handling, sending it all to the scrap bin.

Even so, Ace Metal handles a significant number of remnants, made evident by the Eurostorage remnant storage system adjacent to the laser. Organized by gauge, sheets are stored vertically on supports that protect them from scratches. A single operator can pull the vertically loaded sheets out to start the loading process.

Today, the software looks out one to two weeks into the schedule, pulling 100 to 200 jobs. "That’s not everything we have," Baade said. "It’s still looking at a specific timeframe. But from that, it will group various components from different jobs by gauge, so we can optimize material yield."

The custom shop cannot, however, rely on "filler parts" to fill a nest. The variety of work is just too great. This is where remnant management plays an important role.

Baade pointed to an area adjacent to the laser cutting sorting tables. Besides the staging area before welding, this is the only major WIP location in the shop. "We’ve got multiple sheets and multiple gauges per job, and we’ve got about 25 to 30 jobs being kitted on parts at the same time. [WIP] usually sits between three and five days. Our average stagnation time [queue time] here is 3.1 days."

This WIP area was chosen after careful scrutiny. Managers had thought to move the WIP area after graining and deburring. After all, since every job requires the process, why not have part flow directly from the laser-cut sorting table to the graining and deburring machine?

The issue is in graining; most parts require a specific finish, and each nest can incorporate pieces from various jobs. If sorting and kitting occurred after graining and deburring, maintaining those job-specific finishes could be a challenge. Hence the reason why WIP is where it is—just after laser cutting, where it can be sorted into job-specific kits, after which each stainless steel blank receives the surface finish required.

Managing the WIP

That 3.1-day WIP stagnation time after laser cutting isn’t set in stone; it varies depending on how many shifts the shop is running. This year, Ace Metal has run just one shift in laser cutting, which has meant it now takes a little longer to respond to unexpected demand surges. Next year, the shop plans to run two laser cutting shifts, which would shorten the queue time to just a day or two.

From this WIP location, part sorters stack blanks and kit parts in a way that best makes sense for optimal flow. Standard lean practice from here would state that the shop should strive for single-piece flow—that is,

all

the blanks that go into a job should come together in a single kit that flows down the value stream to bending, welding, and assembly.

Ace Metal’s high-product-mix environment isn’t quite that simple. Considering all the parts that go into a single product, that "single-piece-flow" kit would be truly massive. Some jobs have upwards of 70 subsassemblies and literally hundreds of different components. The bills of material (BOMs) are neither brief nor simple.

Subassembly kits do involve components from the same job—to ensure consistency in surface finish and other job-specific characteristics. But multiple subassembly kits from one job might not flow together or sequentially. Instead, the sequence of those subassembly kits promotes optimal flow through forming and welding.

A single welding station could weld a sequence of subassemblies from multiple jobs during a shift. The idea is to have all the jobs flow to final assembly in the most expedient way possible. Having welders work on

all

subassemblies for one job, one after the other, usually isn’t the fastest way to get the job done.

The WIP kitting area before welding represents a kind of "starting line" for the final sprint, a place where the results of significant planning and myriad components

all

come together: flat cut and formed parts from the laser and brakes, cut tubes from the band saw and Mazak tube fiber laser (a recent upgrade, with integrated tapping), machined parts from Ace Metal’s nearby machine shop (which it purchased several years ago), and various purchased components.

Ace Metal’s purchasing department orders from outside suppliers—hardware, motors, and myriad other components that go into the stainless steel assemblies—almost immediately after the order is received, well before laser cutting starts. Once these components arrive, they’re married with cut and formed parts right before welding.

Pitzo added that outside of a few special cases, only when all components for a job are available do those subassembly kits move forward to welding. Even if missing components don’t require welding, the kit doesn’t move. That’s because welding is a constraint resource, and the shop doesn’t want to use it until planners know everything is available.

Put another way:

Why use a constraint resource if the shop won’t be able to ship the job anyway?

Once everything is available and kitted, it’s off to the races. Within a few days, subassemblies are welded and assembled, after which everything moves on to final assembly. Even at these final stages, however, work is distributed to ensure optimal loading, with subassemblies from multiple jobs spread across multiple welding stations. From there, they head to final kitting and assembly.

Truth on the Shop Floor

Ace Metal might have digital scheduling boards and a host of job data accessible with just a few clicks. But as onetime industry consultant Dick Kallage said during his many seminars hosted by the Fabricators and Manufacturers Association,

truth resides on the shop floor.

Pitzo pointed to evidence of that truth—a queue of kitted parts, ready for welding and assembly. "This gives us a visual signal," she said. "If the kitting area starts loading up with jobs, you immediately know there’s a problem."

Ace Metal might not make use of the lean manufacturing toolbox in a traditional way—this isn’t Toyota—but many fundamentals still apply, including fast changeovers and the reduction of WIP.

"We do not want jobs to enter our shop floor, build up as WIP, and just sit," Baade said. "When we release jobs to manufacturing, we want to process it straight through in as short of a window as possible."

Jobs do take a pitstop after laser cutting and just before welding. Sequences change to balance the load, and the dynamic schedule adjusts continually to accommodate a forever-changing production reality. All this ensures that, even in an extraordinarily high-product-mix environment, the work keeps flowing forward.
