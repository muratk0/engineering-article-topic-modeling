# The future of the automated finishing cell in job shops

[TARİH: 01.11.2024 The Fabricator]

Finishing

Can the cobot do for surface finishing what it has done for welding?

By

Dan Davis

Cobot-focused surface finishing cells are being used in a lot of high-volume manufacturing settings, such as those in the aerospace and automotive industries, but will the world of high-mix manufacturing be as open to the use of cobots for grinding and polishing?

Phuchit/iStock/Getty Images

I

n 2023, the collaborative robot market revenue surpassed $1 billion for the first time, reaching $1.07 billion. Anyone involved in metal fabricating doesn’t find that hard to believe.

Cobots are a common sight at FABTECH. They can be found in shops that previously didn’t dabble with robotics because of the expense and complexity involved with programming industrial robots. The lower price point for cobots, the simplicity in setting up a cobot cell, and the cobot’s flexibility—being able to be rolled from one application to the next in a matter of minutes—have opened the door to automation for not just small metal fabricating companies, but also larger organizations looking to optimize their production efforts.

An overwhelming amount of this growth in the metal fabricating industry has been tied to welding applications. Automation integrators, well aware of the difficulty that companies have in finding welders willing to work for the wages being offered, focused on developing cobots that could be deployed to the welding departments. Now, a large number of integrators, robot manufacturers, and even machine tool manufacturers offer a cobot-focused table that can be rolled out and put to use in a matter of minutes.

With the main focus on welding, it’s understandable that other metal fabricating processes could be overlooked by the cobot community. But one has to wonder if cobots can have the same impact on something like surface finishing as they have made on welding. Will there come a day when fab shops have a cobot-focused finishing cell next to their cobot welding operations?

Ready for Cobot-Focused Surface Finishing?

Dynabrade, a manufacturer of pneumatic hand tools and surface treatment products, launched a new subsidiary over the summer focused on automation of surface conditioning applications and optimizing finishing processes. It chose to debut Autobrade at the International Woodworking Fair for a very specific reason.

"The woodworking industry has often been seen as ahead of the metal fabricating industry in terms of automating surface finishing applications because of industry demand and application, technological maturity, customization and variability, and market readiness," said Kevin Walgate, Autobrade’s sales manager.

"However, the metal fabricating industry is poised for rapid adoption of automation technologies, driven by increasing demand for precision, efficiency, and handling complex processes."

David Pietrocola, the co-founder and CEO of Cohesive Robotics, also feels like metal fabricators are ready to embrace the possibilities of cobot- related surface finishing. Although lagging welding applications, he said he sees it following a similar path.

"It’s pretty exciting to be in the space where we’re at right now," Pietrocola said. "I think as education picks up and there are more solutions and options out there for fabricators and manufacturers, we’re going to be seeing more of it."

What’s Been the Holdup?

Despite the potential, metal fabrication as an industry has been somewhat slow to investigate the use of cobots to take on surface finishing. One of the reasons for that might be the variability associated with the activity.

In automated welding, wire can be used in the gas metal arc welding process, for instance, to overcome inconsistencies in the gap to be welded. In automated surface finishing, a cobot has to mimic a lot more manual movement than welding, which is usually just following a simple path.

The discussion about automating surface finishing actually begins with a different question than it would with welding: Is it

part-to-process

or

process-to-part

? When it comes to part-to-process, think of a cobot with a gripping device that picks up a metal part and presents it to a grinding wheel. When referencing process-to-part, a cobot’s end effector with some sort of finishing tool, like an angle grinder or a buffer, approaches the workpiece to be finished.

So that defines the basic process. The movements are much more challenging to replicate. What are the correct movements that human operators would make? How do they approach the part, or how should the part be presented? How much pressure should be applied during each step of the process?

Then comes the conversation about the proper hardware. What are the best tools for the job? What are the best consumables? Does the cobot have the prerequisite payload and reach to do the job? How will the part be gripped if it’s part-to-process?

Next comes the trial and error of trying to create the correct programming for the cobot. Software can get you close, but it still takes a little bit of work, according to Michel Beauchemin, product manager, Robotiq, a maker of products for collaborative robots, including grippers, sensors, vision systems, and software.

"Let’s say that I want to apply an orbital sander, and I want to apply 5 N to achieve the finish," Beauchemin said. "The cobot is capable of doing it, but it’s a matter of doing it how the human does it and achieving the same result."

That’s where the trial and error comes in. Given the variables involved and the time needed to map a process, it takes some time.

With the use of scanning technologies and advanced programming controls, setting up a cobot surface finishing cell is getting easier for those shops worried about the learning curve normally associated with automated surface finishing applications.

Help Is Available

But it doesn’t take a Herculean effort. Software is available to assist.

Beauchemin said that force sensors in modern cobots allow programmers to register the exact force being applied during a finishing activity. An operator maneuvers the cobot similar to the way a human would, and the sensor captures the force during the entire exercise. It then can be replicated. It’s up to the operator to fine-tune the programming to achieve the desired finish.

Pietrocola said Cohesive Robotics has taken steps to create templates and starting points for metal fabricators that at least give them a head start. The reason for this is because the entirety of the metal fabricating universe really doesn’t have a general standard for surface finishes. One customer’s No. 4 finish (typically defined as a brushed or satin finish for stainless steel, with a smooth appearance and short, parallel lines) might not be the same as someone else’s.

"When you’re talking about something like surface roughness, that’s certainly a quantifiable metric that you can try to standardize around," Pietrocola said. "But when you get into aesthetics like a No. 4 finish, which has a surface roughness range, but it’s actually about the looks, that becomes most important."

He added that’s why the software allows for operators to make tweaks if necessary. They are the experts, and they also know how the fabricator defines the surface finish look and what the customer expects, in most instances anyway.

Cohesive Robotics has worked with the National Ornamental and Miscellaneous Metals Association over the years, and the organization does offer a finish guide. While industry as a whole hasn’t coalesced around this or any other type of standard, it does provide a starting point for those shops that might be looking to standardize the definitions of certain finishes.

What’s Next?

Metal fabricators should keep in mind these few considerations before chatting with automation experts about their surface finishing needs:

It’s about more than just deburring cut edges, weld seam removal, and surface polishing.

"In addition to the commonly recognized applications, metal fabricators might overlook several other valuable uses for a surface finishing cell, such as surface preparation for coating, edge radiusing, texture creation, oxide and scale removal, surface roughness control, polishing and buffing complex shapes, high-volume batch processing, cleaning and debris removal, and customized finishing for high-value components," Walgate said.

Don’t feel obligated to automate the entire process.

Sometimes trying to automate 80% of a finishing operation still might deliver significant return on investment for a project. "The last 20% of the application is where you will spend a lot of money trying to automate," Beauchemin said. That final 20% might include expert surface effects that only a veteran power tool user can deliver or basic deburring activities that are just out of reach for a cobot. Sometimes less is more, and it might make the difference in an automation project being approved or not.

Guarding might be necessary.

The idea of formal protective zones that keep humans outside the operating area of a working piece of automation is not something that people associate with collaborative robots, but that might be necessary with a surface finishing cell. While a cobot can be programmed to move slowly and shut down should some sort of unexpected contact occur, a cobot armed with an abrasive is a whole other story. "You still have to consider the fact that you have an abrasive that’s moving around at a high RPM, and you need to protect your co-workers from that," Pietrocola said.

Keep your surface finishing experts in the loop.

This applies not only to the in-house surface finishers that have done the job, but also automation integrators with experience in this field.

It’s Happening

While not widespread, cobots are being used for various surface finishing tasks in metal fabricating settings. The adoption rate compared to welding cobots is a bit slower, but the need grows with each passing day.

Manually grinding down welding seams or applying specialty finishes over a large surface can be very physically taxing over an entire shift. Replicating those movements every day pushes the human body to its limits. Asking older workers to do those same demanding tasks is unfair.

Of course, that is only applicable if fab shops can find people to do that work. It’s an entry-level job in most instances, and young people are likely to say "No thanks" if an angle grinder is shoved into their hands on their first day on the job.

If they have the payload and the reach, cobots can be programmed to do those jobs.
