# Make safety your foundation

[TARİH: 01.10.2024 The Fabricator]

Safety

Continuous improvement, trust, and maximum company value begin with the basics

O

SHA compliance doesn’t sound fun, let alone like something to rally behind.

Compliance programs may initially seem like they are forced on companies by government fiat, like a tax, but I would assert that OSHA compliance creates and adds value, not only for employees but for employers, shareholders, and the community.

Beyond the legal requirements of OSHA compliance, the safety journey that manufacturers undertake to become compliant gives them the opportunity to understand various perspectives and recognize strategic advantages that may not be apparent on the surface.

When considering safety and compliance, it’s important to understand the cultural impact on employees, the foundation provided by the safety program to propel continuous improvement, the accountability and rigor required to support the program, the economic and commercial advantages, and the value added to a company for exit strategies.

Cultural Perspective

In his Hierarchy of Needs pyramid model that ranks motivation for human behavior, Abraham Maslow placed safety near the bottom, which means that it is foundational. People must feel safe. It’s a basic need that leaders must provide at a minimum for co-workers to flourish.

Expanding on Maslow, Frederick Hertzberg developed the Motivator-Hygiene Theory, essentially splitting Maslow’s pyramid model into two parts: hygiene issues (like safety), without which employees feel dissatisfied; and motivators, which build job satisfaction. To improve employee engagement—an important and primary topic for leaders—employers must turn first to the fundamentals, one of which is safety. When safety is absent or deficient, employees feel dissatisfied, and companies can experience low morale and high turnover.

From a cultural perspective, companies will have a difficult time hiring and retaining top talent if they don’t meet employees’ basic needs. In manufacturing, that means a safe and compliant workplace—a culture of safety. When employees feel like the company has their safety in mind, they tend to view that favorably. But when they are asked or expected to set safety aside for the sake of deadlines or profitability, trust erodes. If that persists, it can permanently damage morale, and that’s when turnover kicks in. Companies lose good people who can see right through their smokescreens.

The battle for skilled workers—be it fabricators, welders, or robot programmers—is all too real. Fabricating companies need to use every tool in their tool belt to attract and retain the best talent.

When people tour my facilities, my policy is to be open and honest: "We’re not going to hide people in the corner. Feel free to ask them whatever you want." If we just put on a show for observers, that reinforces a lack of trust from the people forced to put on that show.

I have personally worked in more than 100 manufacturing locations as a consultant and as an executive. I can say unequivocally that those that allow and ignore unsafe acts and conditions have lower morale and higher turnover. They also fail to attract and retain top talent.

When leading manufacturing companies, I look for workplace stability. If I see a hot spot of turnover or performance issues, I look to see if the company is meeting the basic hygiene needs of employees—things like a safe work environment, competitive pay, fair company policies, and quality supervision. If so, then we focus on motivators such as career advancement, development opportunities, recognition, and responsibility. To achieve a strong and positive company culture, we have to get the fundamentals right first, and safety is the starting point.

Continuous Improvement

Having a mature safety program and being compliant with OSHA regulations also can benefit continuous improvement efforts.

When helping companies and clients improve performance, I ask them first to tell me about their safety program, and then show to me. I want to see if the company has proven it can not only create but maintain a program that requires documented best practices, teamwork, problem-solving, and accountability. If it can, then I know we have a great foundation on which to build a quality and continuous improvement culture.

Safety and quality are very closely linked in the compliance mindset. It’s so much easier to build a quality system on the foundation of a solid safety program because the structure is already present. It’s not new—it’s an accelerator. We can get there from here a lot quicker if we don’t have to start from scratch. Here are rules; follow them and we will be fine. Let’s look at these as a team and improve them on a regular cadence. Collect data and work on the things that are most beneficial. It all makes sense and works very well together. That being said, if we only have safety on paper and not in practice, then we have a different issue to deal with.

Accountability and Discipline

Having a mature safety compliance program introduces the concepts of accountability and organizational discipline and rigor. This isn’t discipline in terms of punishment, but having a culture that follows established norms in a persistent and consistent manner.

A strong, compliant safety program gives all employees a foundational understanding and cultural baseline for what is considered normal and acceptable. In these shops, workers who follow the rules and act safely are recognized and rewarded. Conversely, those who do not comply receive coaching and retraining to get realigned. And individuals who continue to buck safety standards face potential termination.

Having these established norms with a well-documented and -understood set of rules allows people to be safe, but it also provides a base on which to build other foundational programs and processes that continue to mature the company and workforce.

Commercial Perspective

Being noncompliant with OSHA and having a poor safety record can affect a company’s commercial opportunities.

Large companies that contract to other businesses, especially manufacturers, require them to report on their safety performance and compliance programs. If a manufacturer cannot meet the requirements or has a bad safety record, it may be denied the opportunity to be a vendor. Also, if the manufacturer acts in an unsafe way or creates unsafe conditions while on-site with the customer, it can be banned entirely. This is real.

If a manufacturer starts from the position that it is going to do things by the book and not only comply but excel in safety, then good things follow. If it wants to play with the big dogs but is not prepared, then it’s already playing catch-up. The better move is to understand what it takes to be successful and work towards that goal. "Begin with the end in mind," as author Stephen R. Covey once said.

Valuation

During my time in industry, I have conducted due diligence analysis on prospective acquisition targets. This includes understanding the risk involved in the company’s operations and how that might affect future cash flows, litigation, workers’ compensation claims, and the general maturity of the organization from an operational perspective. If a company is inherently unsafe, it is not always reflected in the purchase price, but it should be. It is like when you are looking to buy a home and you hire a home inspector to tell you if it’s a safe purchase. If the inspector says, "The roof is worn out and this appliance is on its last leg," you can either get a home warranty or ask the seller to discount the price. That is due diligence.

My work tends to focus on operations in the industrial and manufacturing space, and I am very interested in the safety program and performance of these companies as a basic understanding of maturity and risk. Safety is a sound starting place for understanding how well an operation is run; how engaged the employees are; the leadership priorities; how performance data is gathered, prioritized, and acted upon; and even why turnover is high. All these things are foundational to understanding the health and maturity of a company, in my opinion.

Summing It Up

Beyond legal requirements, OSHA compliance offers strategic advantages by enhancing employee satisfaction and retention through a culture of safety. This also supports continuous improvement efforts, introducing accountability and organizational discipline, improving commercial opportunities by maintaining a good safety record, and positively impacting company valuations during acquisitions by demonstrating operational maturity and risk management.

Compliance programs, though often seen as government mandates, create value for employees, employers, shareholders, and the community. Safety is foundational to employee engagement and trust, and companies that prioritize safety can attract and retain top talent.

A mature safety program also facilitates quality improvement and organizational discipline, setting norms for acceptable behavior and providing a base for other foundational programs. Noncompliance can negatively affect commercial opportunities and vendor access, while a strong safety record can enhance business prospects. During acquisitions, safety performance is a key indicator of operational maturity and risk, influencing company valuations. Overall, OSHA compliance is not just a legal requirement but a strategic advantage that supports long-term business success.
