# Fabricator recreates the past for the Ohio Governor’s Residence

Rose Iron Works answers the call for period-appropriate lighting

DAN DAVIS

T

he Ohio Governor’s Residence in Bexley, Ohio, was completed in 1925, so when a new fixture or finish is required, caretakers just can’t run down to the local hardware store. That’s when it pays to know someone.

Luckily, the staff knew who to call when they needed new lighting in the home’s great room, a spacious room dedicated to hosting large gatherings. It reached out to Rose Iron Works, Cleveland, a firm that helped with a chandelier project for the living room more than a decade ago.

Modern Company, Classic Skills

Rose Iron Works, which can be found in the historic St. Clair-Superior neighborhood, was founded in 1904 and has run a blacksmith shop in the neighborhood for more than 100 years. Today, it also is involved in more commercial endeavors, through its Rose Metal Industries name, but it’s still doing craftsman-type work that allows it to create fabrications similar to those from the early 20th century.

In 2024, Emily Clarey, a member of Gov. Mike DeWine’s and first lady Frances DeWine’s staff, reached out to Rose Iron Works with a request to design two chandeliers for the great room. The challenge was to complement the design of the sconces that were installed on the walls many years prior.

"It was important to the first lady for them to feel as though they were original," said Bob Rose, the third-generation company owner and president.

The Rose Ironworks craftsmen created the classic shapes using common fabricating techniques such as shearing, rolling, and welding, but then added their special touch with surface textures produced by heating the metal components and hand hammering with specially created tools.

) were painted to match the exact finish of the existing sconces, Rose said his team frequently goes the extra mile by using darkening agents combined with hot wax application and hand burnishing to create special lusters for the appearance of age.

Not the First Chandelier Job

In 2013, Governor’s Residence staff reached out to Rose Iron Works to create a special period-specific chandelier for the living room. The governor at the time, John Kasich, and his wife, first lady Karen Kasich, were delighted to find someone with the expertise for the work, according to Rose.

Staff provided Rose Iron Works with a picture of the chandelier from Cincinnati Artistic Wrought Iron Works Co., which had fabricated fixtures for houses similar to the Governor’s Residence in the past. Rose knew it had a familiar feeling. In fact, it looked similar to a drawing created in 1930 by Paul Fehér, a Rose Iron Works designer. It was the inspiration for chandeliers that were made for The Union Club in Cleveland.

Instead of replicating what was in the picture, Rose took elements of the room and his own interpretation of Jacobean Revival, a popular architectural and decorative style from the late 19th and early 20th centuries, to create a new design. Quatrefoils found in the room’s plaster ceiling and the fleur-de-lis found in the sconces were incorporated into the metalworking design. Rose also elected to simplify the look of the chandelier, emphasizing basic shapes within the arms, oval, and rectangle.

"Back at our studio, evenings and weekends were spent with Paul Fehér, my grandfather [Martin Rose], and my father [Melvin Rose] looking over my shoulder as I sat at the drawing board each of them had used," Rose wrote in a summary he did of the job.

After fabricating parts by hand, forging different elements like the arms, welding the pieces together, sandblasting the workpiece, finishing it, and wiring it up, the chandelier (see

Figure 2

) was ready for delivery.

Even though it practices metalworking skills that were commonplace many decades ago, Rose said the company is still interested in using the latest fabricating technology when it makes sense. He added that’s the same attitude his grandfather had when he founded the shop.

"While many blacksmiths shy away from and criticize the use of technology such as laser cutting, the Rose team believes it’s important to utilize the efficiency of such equipment, as long as the quality and integrity of the work is not compromised," he said. For example, Rose Iron Works even uses 3D printing to make samples for design review prior to forging them in metal.

Not all metal fabricating companies have the ability to recreate the past. Luckily, the skills haven’t been lost to time. As a result, we all can appreciate history more today.
