# "My way or the highway" Is on its way out

[TARİH: 01.01.2025 The Fabricator]

Editor’s Corner

Relationships matter to younger workers, but do shops recognize that?

EDITOR-IN-CHIEF

DAN DAVIS

W

ho would have thought that noted philosopher and stoner Jeff Spicoli from the 1982 film "Fast Times at Ridgemont High" might have been an excellent manager of Generation Z workers? Think about this exchange with his foe, and ultimate friend, Mr. Hand during a history class:

Mr. Hand: Am I hallucinating here [having witnessed a pizza being delivered to his class]? Just what in the hell do you think you’re doing?

Jeff Spicoli: Learning about Cuba and having some food.

Mr. Hand: Mr. Spicoli, you’re on dangerous ground here. You’re causing a major disturbance on my time.

Jeff Spicoli: I’ve been thinking about this, Mr. Hand. If I’m here and you’re here, doesn’t that make it our time? Certainly, there’s

nothing wrong with a little feast on our time.

Now Spicoli does end up having the pizza taken away, with Mr. Hand deciding Spicoli was correct and then sharing the pizza with the class. And our surf-loving slacker probably spends another year with Mr. Hand. But he does understand that Mr. Hand’s traditional "It’s my way or the highway" approach to learning is not working for him.

Generation Z is saying the same thing. An Ernst & Young study of these young workers revealed that they weren’t necessarily interested in helping an employer build their company; they had more of an interest in their own growth and enjoying their co-workers. It’s a continuation of the backlash that millennials (born roughly between 1981-1996) demonstrated in their refusal to accept the same work expectations that governed Baby Boomers (1946-1964) and Generation X (1965-1980). They wanted to have more of a work-life balance that apparently escaped the two generations before them.

But this is where it gets interesting. Generation Z actually doesn’t mind working, even if the motivations differ from their predecessors. In its study, Ernst & Young reveals that Generation Z workers trust their supervisor 71% of the time, which for the most part are millennials. Eighty-one percent of them also admit to liking their co-workers.

What’s even more interesting is to see this type of study actually playing out in the real world. The more young managers I speak with, the less time we spend on lamenting the state of young people’s willingness to work. Now, these young people do admit that they have turned people away, but when they find someone who is the right fit for the team, both parties recognize how the relationship can be beneficial.

Toward the end of an interview that was the source for the "Changing of the guard at Goodhart Sons" feature in this issue, Jody Leakey, a former superintendent for the Lancaster, Pa.-based metal fabricator, admitted that he wouldn’t want to be a manager of younger people today. He looked at the role as being not just a manager of people, but also a master motivator, getting the young workers engaged to do the job properly.

Wyatt Imhoff, one of Goodhart Sons’ new superintendents, along with Jody’s son Cameron Leakey, responded by saying that the latest new hires at the shop are neither lazy nor entitled. In fact, they are meeting—and exceeding—expectations.

"Some of the young guys that we’ve gotten over the past couple years are outstanding. You can push them. You can give them challenges. They’re doing really well," Imhoff said.

"And they want to work, too. They really, really want the hours."

Imhoff admitted that they had met some "ding dongs" along the way, but they didn’t become a part of the team. He and Leakey weren’t going to sacrifice a welcoming and effective working environment to accommodate someone that might disrupt it.

In early December, The Fabricator Podcast dropped an episode with Blake Hamar, founder of Cali Raised LED and Apex Fabworks, in Fort Worth, Texas. The 26-year-old employs about 40 people, and most of them are younger than him. It sounds like an unusual environment for a metal fabricating company, but Hamar said he wouldn’t want it any other way. He looks for people who are hungry and a good fit, even if they are inexperienced. Skills can be taught. It’s much more difficult to teach people not to be a pain in the butt.

"Some of the young guys that we’ve gotten over the past couple years are outstanding. You can push them. You can give them challenges. They’re doing really well. And they want to work, too. They really, really want the hours."

— Wyatt Imhoff, Goodhart Sons

Like a good coach, Hamar supports his employees, even if they choose to leave. He’s witnessed a few move on to become managers at other fab shops. Hamar said he’s thrilled that they are able to get that type of experience.

"We’re kind of mentoring these kids," Hamar said. "They’re making really good money. They’re in a great environment, and in turn they’re really happy. We have like zero turnover.

"But I tell our guys, if you get an opportunity to upgrade because you guys are learning so much and you’re super skilled at what you do, go do it. Go run another shop."

Being a part of a team is nothing new, but it’s really become part of the modern workplace. Large companies have encouraged it, and educational institutions have been trying to make it a part of the learning experience. It’s no wonder that Generation Z is looking for that type of work environment.

Additionally, Spicoli would remind younger workers that metal fab shops have excellent sets of tools. They can be used to fix and make anything.
