# Advances in exoskeleton technology improve productivity, safety

Today’s exoskeletons are lighter, cooler, more comfortable

Exoskeletons designed for humans include upper-body, mid-body, lower-body, and full-body to assist with many industrial tasks, such as welding, inspecting, and assembly.

M

aking workers safer and more productive do not have to be competing objectives. Worker safety is important because without safe workers, there is no productivity. When productivity demands are high and workers are expected to work an abundance of overtime, they get fatigued and the potential for injury increases. Add to this an aging population and the risk for injury goes up even more. So, what can be done about it?

Workers can wear an external skeleton, or exoskeleton. An exoskeleton is a reinforced covering that supports and protects the bodies of some types of animals. The literal meaning of the word exoskeleton is "outside skeleton." Many invertebrates—animals without backbones—have exoskeletons.

Types of Exoskeletons

Exoskeletons that are designed for humans come in four basic categories. In general, these include upper-body, mid-body, lower-body, and full-body exoskeletons (see Exoskeleton Categories sidebar).

These four categories of exoskeletons come in many different forms, each with their own level of complexity, cost, and weight. According to Exoskeleton Report, a website dedicated to news and information about exoskeletons, there are seven consumer, 50 industrial, 62 medical, and four military exoskeleton types.

Some are powered and use batteries or electric cable connections to run sensors and actuators. Others are passive, using only springs and cables or stretch materials to support or restrict movement.

Great resources and catalogs of the different types and configurations of exoskeletons on the market are available via an internet search.

Design Evolution

It was not until 2015 that commercialized exoskeleton technology and markets advanced rapidly. The speed at which exoskeletons are being introduced into the market has slowed over the last year. Potential reasons for this slowdown may be that more time is being spent on the technology advancements to make them more flexible, comfortable, and reliable.

Some companies are focusing on more complex designs, and many others are chasing specific design innovations for specific applications. This is viewed by some as a good sign that the potential users are starting to ask the right questions before purchasing an exoskeleton and taking time to select the right one to meet a specific need.

When exoskeletons first hit the market, they came with safety, weight, and comfort concerns. User feedback prompted redesign of the exoskeleton. It is clear the market is responding to what users like or do not like, because more of today’s exoskeletons are lighter, cooler, and more comfortable.

Moving from rigid designs to flexible designs has been a recent evolution for most exoskeletons. Rigid designs are applicable when posture correction is needed; otherwise, a flexible design is more natural and comfortable.

Designs using powered actuators, electric clutches, and microcontrollers to replace springs and cables are starting to make their way into the market.

Exoskeleton Categories

Upper-body exoskeletons

are selected by people performing work in elevated arm postures to support the arms, elbows, shoulders, and neck.

Mid-body exoskeletons

are designed for people who perform repetitive lifting to help alleviate the compressive forces on the back, thereby reducing the potential for injury.

Lower-body exoskeletons

help people who encounter repetitive squatting, ankle flexion, standing, and walking. Full-body exoskeletons assist people, such as military personnel, who transport packs and supplies from point to point in extreme and various conditions.

Smart sensors that help control gait or provide static support are becoming a reality.

Robotic gloves for improved hand strength also are gaining acceptance.

Another advancement allows for attachments. For example, one optional attachment provides head support engineered to make the head weightless when leaning forward or backward during assembly or welding. Wider strap configurations over body contact points are becoming a norm.

A recent development is a smart exoskeleton that uses sensors to monitor posture and movement over the course of the day to provide useful data. For example, the data can be used to quantify use time, different postures encountered during work, or repeatablity of work cycle time and travel distances.

Worker Acceptance, Rejection

After fitting and interviewing thousands of workers who were asked to try an exoskeleton on their job, it became clear that any exoskeleton design has to be comfortable and provide full range of motion. Heat buildup and weight are a killer for employee acceptance.

Any interference with reaching, walking, crawling, and working in tight places is an impediment to worker acceptance. Lack of flexibility with bending or twisting motions can be good or bad, depending on the job. Providing built-in comfort features like breathable pads for backs and hips, and cooling fans, gell pads, or padded armrests and a lumbar support all helped with employee acceptance. Comfortable and breathable adjustment straps also were important.

Making the wearing of exoskeletons mandatory or voluntary have their own sets of employee acceptance challenges. Convenient line-side storage and regular maintenance also help with acceptance. Listening to employees and providing a range of support options to fit the job will make all the difference. If exoskeletons are "easy in and out," with quick adjustments when needed, safety and acceptance are likely to occur.

Exoskeleton Limitations

Exoskeletons do not remove all risk for workers, so understanding their limitations is important.

Limitations are determined from performance testing in the lab and in the field, with real workers performing real jobs. For example, depending on the design type, an exoskeleton may provide maximum benefit when the user’s arms are elevated between 90 to 135 degrees, or during trunk bending at 45 degrees.

One exoskeleton may be designed for standing, but not sitting, and another may be designed for both. Some may be ideal for handling weights less than 12 lbs., while others can handle more than that. Some combination of mid-body and upper-body units may be great for changing tires on a lift with arms extended at 90 degrees, but not while kneeling on the ground. It is important to remember that exoskeletons are designed with a specific use in mind. Using it outside its intended purpose can come with its own set of challenges, safety risks, and employee acceptance concerns.

Testing Shows Impact on Productivity and Safety

Extensive field and lab research on how the body and the worker respond to wearing and working with and without an exoskeleton has shown the positive impact the technology has had on worker safety and productivity.

Because some of the innovative developments in exoskeleton design can impact worker safety, productivity, and acceptance, numerous review boards have been formed. The intent is to make sure that the designs and their impact on the human body are safe for deployment and daily use. For example, ASTM Intl., a standards organization, formed Committee F48 on Exoskeletons and Exosuits in 2017, which comprises numerous subcommittees working on voluntary consensus standards for what is being manufactured today and in the future.

Safety and productivity go hand in hand. A technology that can benefit both is a winner.

Toyota, one of the earliest adopters of exoskeletons, started using them in 2017. Records of their use showed a reduction in worker compensation claim costs. Shoulder injuries dropped to zero on one assembly line after exoskeletons were adopted and provided as personal protection equipment.

Not every company will experience the same level of success, but any risk reduction is a chance to avoid an injury.

Considering both the injury reduction and productivity gains, exoskeleton technology makes sense. Exoskeletons are not going away, and with innovation and advancements, the technology will continue to help reduce injuries and employee suffering, improve quality of life, permit employees to work longer, and boost productivity.
