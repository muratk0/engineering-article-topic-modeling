ost workers in private industry don’t measure the success of a day by if they are killed or not, but those in manufacturing might consider it. It’s a profession ripe for disaster.

The Bureau of Labor Statistics provides the shocking evidence. In 2013, 312 individuals were killed while on a manufacturing job. Thirty of those were killed working around material handling machinery or cranes.

It’s not that hard to believe. A recent story shared at a Fabricators & Manufacturers Association safety conference reinforced that belief.

This job shop, which at the time thought it had an adequate safety record, was in the process of installing a large piece of equipment in the early 2000s. The technicians who were charged with installing the new machine tool wanted to use the overhead crane to assist in moving the equipment into its intended spot. Meanwhile, an electrical contractor, using a lift, was in the shop installing power conduit and running wire through it. When the equipment installers started the crane, which wasn’t locked out, it knocked the lift over. The electrical contractor grabbed onto the crane rail, hung on, and was able to escape injury. The lift, however, collided with two small machining centers on the shop floor and knocked them over. Amazingly, nobody was injured, but it did provide a major wake-up call for the fabricator, which today boasts an industry-leading safety record.

Safety in the workplace has improved greatly over the years. There is no doubt. The Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA) reports that since 1970 workplace fatality rates have been cut by 66 percent, and occupational injury and illness rates have declined by 67 percent. During this same time, the employment ranks within the U.S. economy have almost doubled.

That is a noteworthy decline, but is it enough? Should any death or debilitating injury be tolerated in a manufacturing environment?

Manufacturing companies can’t afford to believe that any major injury—much less death—is acceptable. They have too much at stake if you truly believe that the industry is struggling to find young people interested in pursuing a career in manufacturing.

Indeed, many parents incorrectly associate manufacturing with a dark and dangerous environment. However, the demand for a safe workplace might actually come from the very same young people that industry is chasing to fill entry-level positions in their organizations.

Millennials, loosely defined as those born between 1980 and 2000, were raised to be aware of the dangers of smoking, alcohol, sugary foods, and strangers. They sure aren’t going to be enthusiastic about working in a setting with shoddy guarding around machine tools and a dearth of documented safety processes and personal protective equipment. They might define risk as just entering a manufacturing facility.

Tom Weiss, the manufacturing manager for The Dupps Co., a manufacturer of heavy-duty rendering equipment for the food processing industry in Germantown, Ohio, has seen this attitude with the new hires that the company has made over the past several years. Unlike old-timers, who might focus on completing the task in what they believe to be the most sensible way possible, this new generation of fabricator isn’t going to take shortcuts.

Weiss offered up the example of a manufacturing veteran charged with moving a midsized fabrication that looks like it can be moved without the aid of any other human or mechanical assistance.

"They might think, ‘Well, I can pick that up,’" Weiss said. "Well, you really can’t do that. You should do it the appropriate way."

The younger worker, on the other hand, is going to ask how much that object weighs and then devise the safest way to move it. These millennials can work independently, but as many generational researchers will suggest, they also like guidance and clearly defined processes. If a manufacturer doesn’t have that type of safety-first environment as part of the company culture, these younger workers may push them toward that direction.

Frankly, that’s a good thing. It keeps OSHA from becoming a major headache. It keeps employees healthy, which boosts morale. In the end, productivity is enhanced because downtime related to injuries is minimized—and, hopefully, eliminated.

The Dupps Co. has achieved this type of environment. Employees have worked 2 million hours without a lost-time accident occurring. You can read more about the company in "Safety: For the people, by the people" on page 82.

Millennials can frustrate manufacturing managers with their know-it-all attitudes, but keep in mind that they actually may know something. Their belief that safety comes first is a lesson the gray-haired set still needs to learn.
