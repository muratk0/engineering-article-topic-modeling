From the Editor-in-Chief

Success is based on what you know, not what the degree supposedly says you know

J

effrey Clark, president, Waukesha

®

Metal Products, Sussex, Wis., isn’t surprised his company has experienced tremendous growth over the past several years, which includes the recent purchase of a facility in Mexico. Yes, it was part of a strategic plan, but it really was about survival. If the company had not tried to evolve from being strictly a stamper of heavy-duty brackets back in the early 2000s, everyone was going to be out of a job sooner or later.

"The nature of the product we stamp and process now has changed and morphed so much over the years. They are getting so much more detailed," Clark said. "The requirements for tolerances, cleanliness, and cosmetics have changed completely from what we were doing 10 years ago."

Waukesha has done quite well as it has grown into being a valuable member of several key supply chains. The metal fabricator and former doesn’t shy away from challenging projects and actively engages customers in discussions about cost reduction and design for manufacturability. The winner of

The FABRICATOR

’s Industry Award for 2015—which you can read more about on p. 60—is trying to become a supplier of expertise, as much as a provider of metal parts and assemblies.

On the front lines of this battle to capture the customers’ ears and win their trust are people that Clark describes as "technically proficient solution providers." These are knowledgeable individuals who are the main points of contact within the Waukesha walls. They typically can answer difficult questions about material selection or manufacturability, and if they can’t, they know just whoM to ask or where to find the answer. They are in the position to assist in problem solving, not just take a purchase order.

Now the Technically Proficient Solution Provider title might actually exist on business cards in Silicon Valley, but in the Rust Belt, it’s meant to be a general description. In all honesty, titles don’t accurately summarize the collective knowledge and skills of these roughly 10 Waukesha employees. Some have mechanical engineering degrees, and others have completed studies at the graduate school level. Others are tool- and diemakers. But they all know how to fabricate and form metal in the most costeffective and expedient way possible.

Rob Bauer is Waukesha’s engineering manager who doesn’t have an engineering degree. He is a graduate of the ACME Institute of Technology, where he studied tool and die design. When he attended in the 1980s, employers were lined up around the corner to offer jobs to the school’s graduates. The ensuing years obviously have been marked by a manufacturing decline and then a comeback. In between, ACME closed its doors, and Bauer embarked on a journey to collect new manufacturing experiences.

"I kind of worked backwards. I became a designer, and then I got experience in the shop. I’m not a journeyman," Bauer said. "Everything I’ve done has been on-the-job experience for 25 years."

He describes the rest of his technically proficient co-workers the same way.

"They’re going to be more knowledgeable because they’ve seen more. They’ve seen all the situations you don’t see when you’re just building a tool, for example," Bauer added.

In a day when human resource executives in the corporate world toss out résumés simply because an applicant doesn’t own a degree from a four-year educational institution, knowledge still trumps all at small and medium-sized metal fabricating and forming shops. The lack of the right degree is not an anchor that prevents someone from continuing down a career path.

Bauer said he tries to share that message with students he meets in industrial technology programs in community colleges. The key is getting them to focus on the long-term, not short-term, rewards, which can be challenging, considering nearby manufacturers are offering these students $15 an hour to stand in front of a machine, load parts, push a button, and then unload parts.

"We just want them to stay the course [in their studies]," Bauer said. "You’ll start out at a lower wage, but eight to 10 years from now, you’re going to be at $25 to $35 an hour as a tool- and diemaker or a journeyman. You can even get into management or engineering."

People talk of "disruptive" technologies, such as 3-D printing, shaking the traditional manufacturing landscape. Could a manufacturing education be looked upon as a disruptive force in the field of education? Students bypass the traditional fouryear model. They receive hands-on training, not just lecture-based lessons. In many instances, they are paid to learn instead of relying on borrowed money to finance an education that may not translate into a vocation.

Waukesha Metal Products—and metal fabricating companies in general—is finding success in developing manufacturing talent by letting experience determine career paths and interacting with local educational institutions. They saw a problem, and they are looking to solve it. That’s what they do.
