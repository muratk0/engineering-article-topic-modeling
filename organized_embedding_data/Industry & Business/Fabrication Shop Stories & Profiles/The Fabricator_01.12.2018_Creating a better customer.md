# Creating a better customer

[TARİH: 01.12.2018 The Fabricator]

From The Editor-In-Chief

Unrealistic design expectations pose an obstacle to efficient fabrication operations

Read more from Dan Davis at

www.thefabricator.com/author/dan-davis

I

n recent editorials, I’ve written about the need to reach out to the next generation to entice them into considering a rewarding career in manufacturing and the difficulties metal fabricators often face when working with OEM engineers that really don’t understand the real world of metal fabricating. Now I have the opportunity to combine the two topics. Metal fabricators need to do a better job of reaching out to the current generation of engineers who lack metal fabricating knowledge and often rely on 3-D CAD tools to guide their decisions.

Any reader of Gerald Davis’ monthly column, Precision Matters, knows that modern CAD software gives the user a lot of designing power. A CAD jockey can create works of art in minutes instead of having to slave away in front of a draft table just to create 2-D plans. That power creates a false sense of knowledge, however, as the designer assumes the software’s programming is gospel. That’s when fabricators have to step in and play the role of heretic: "What the CAD software suggests is not really what you need!"

A recent note from a metal fabricator drove this point home. He was looking for a reference that documented industry tolerance standards for precision sheet metal work and gas tungsten arc welded parts. The metal fabricator hoped to share that reference with an OEM customer that wanted fabricated light-gauge sheet metal parts to maintain a tolerance of ±0.005 inch even after welding, which can be quite challenging. The design engineers use ±0.005 as a default tolerance on all dimensions on their drawings.

Of course, such a resource would be an invaluable tool, but the job shop world is built to take on work that has a tremendous amount of variation—from an order-size, material-type, and delivery-date perspective, just to name a few. If it were easy to do, OEMs probably wouldn’t source it out.

Take the "Reasonable tolerancing for press brake bending" article that ran in the September 2018 issue of

The FABRICATOR

. Senior Editor Tim Heston does a great job of sharing the thoughts of press brake guru Steve Benson, the author of this publication’s Bending Basics column, and Larry Boden, press brake tooling product training specialist at Anoka, Minn.-based Mate Precision Tooling. While the accuracy and repeatability of modern press brakes and precision-ground tooling have helped make bending a lot faster and more predictable than they used to be, variation in metal can be the obstacle that gets in the way of simply mass-producing tight-tolerance parts without much thought. Material makeup, yield strength, and tensile strength can differ depending on which coil the material came from. Even what might be considered to be visible variation—such as material thickness—is not easy to ascertain because the difference could be as little as a couple thousandths of an inch.

"This really causes the most problems when working with tolerance callouts," Benson said in the article.

It gets more frustrating. As Heston explained, when fabricators think about 10-gauge mild steel, they probably think it’s about 60 KSI and 0.135 in. thick. But these aren’t exact specifications; they’re averages. For instance, typical 10-ga. A36 material has a gauge-zone tolerance of ±0.006 in. This means that 10-ga. can be anywhere from 0.129 to 0.141 in.

Do you think most design engineers who sit at a computer know the challenges that material inconsistencies pose? I bet most press brake operators do.

So what’s a fabricator to do if they can’t send a customer "Realistic Tolerances for Dummies" for Christmas? An ugly sweater might be in order because that goofy gift can be the prelude to some serious discussions.

These type of frank discussions are often easier when personal relationships exist between the fabricator and customer. If those don’t exist, perhaps they can be addressed during a regularly scheduled meeting between the parties.

Some fabricators have taken steps to formalize their educational efforts. For example, Superior Tube Products Inc., Davenport, Iowa, started to hold classes and invite its customers after realizing that because customers didn’t know the basics of tube fabrication, Superior couldn’t be as efficient in its operations as it wanted to be. By attending classes that teach the finer points of tube benders, tooling, material behavior, dimensions, and tolerances, design engineers and purchasers gain a better understanding of the real world of metal fabricating. A design guide that they leave with is the constant reminder of what was discussed. (This story was covered in "Classes for the masses,"

The Tube & Pipe Journal

, November 2014, p. 26.)

If creating class content, even for a half-day event, sounds like too much of a commitment, consider creating a video for your company website. That can be shared easily with customers, and it’s likely to be found by others looking for information through a search engine.

Engaging in conversations like this with customers can be a challenge, but the possible results make it worth it. Simply put, a better-educated customer is a better customer.
