# Supply Chain Resiliency Growing in Importance

[TARİH: 01.11.2015 The Fabricator]

By Mike McDonald

flexibility to respond to unexpected demand

Manufacturers hold their suppliers accountable in many ways. We recently completed the Prime Advantage annual "Purchasing and Manufacturing Survey" ("PM Survey") of our member companies and found some fascinating results about how today’s manufacturers are measuring their suppliers. The survey participants agreed that you need to maintain the standard three metrics of quality, delivery, and cost. But companies are also placing growing importance on flexibility to respond to unexpected demand, as well as responsiveness to change requests.

Managing unpredictability is a constant in most businesses, especially coming out of a downturn. No company can accurately predict every customer demand. Managing your suppliers is the key to meeting unexpected customer orders. The quality of your supplier relationships can make or break you when the customer calls.

No one can afford to keep large amounts of inventory on their shelves. It’s money just sitting there that could have been used in other parts of the operation. Reliable historical data is a first step, but having the ability to analyze and forecast from that data is just as essential. Our recent "PM Survey" reports that over 50 percent of our member companies have spend analytics and reporting tools in place in their organizations. Another 15 percent are working to implement this technology over the next 12 to 24 months. This critical data can help procurement teams drive toward lower inventory, while still meeting their customers’ needs. Not surprisingly, 75 percent of respondents to the survey consider analytical skills as the most important talent to creating strength in their procurement team.

Even the best technology and most talented professionals cannot generate a 100 percent accurate forecast. Having a great relationship with both your customers and suppliers will allow you to nimbly react and retain business, even in the face of planning failures that leave you temporarily out of stock. The members who participated in the survey look for flexibility from their suppliers, especially during times of unexpected demand. For manufacturers this outranks even technical support and other business services that many suppliers offer as the most desired quality.

Tight and in sync supply chain partnerships are essential to making businesses run to full potential. And open communication is the key for both sides of any successful relationship. Although we put massive amounts of energy into it, no one will be perfectly accurate when projecting their business. Organized teamwork throughout your supply chain will quickly solve problems as they arise while creating the level of partnership required for success, no matter how your company measures it.

Vision and Its Role in "Noticing"

In my prior article, "Visioning for Development," we looked at visioning as a tool for self-development. Here we’ll be looking at an amazing central nervous system function that can help us even further. This function arises from a structure in the brain called the reticular activating system (RAS), housed largely in the reticular formation in the midbrain and brainstem.

The function of the RAS primarily involves mediating the waking and sleeping transitions. A noteworthy secondary function is to influence "noticing." All of us have experienced buying a new car — or a car new to us — and been surprised that for the next three weeks everywhere we go we see our car, our color around. We never saw them before, but suddenly we see them everywhere. This is due to the RAS.

It’s thought that the RAS functions as a filter or gateway, blocking our perception of things it deems irrelevant, and allowing in perceptions it deems relevant. If we didn’t have this filter, we would be drowning in sense data. We need it. It can present a problem, though. There are very many accounts of automobile drivers who "didn’t see the motorcycle coming." Motorcyclists understand that they are "invisible." They tell us that drivers can be seen looking right at them and then pull right out in front of them. This is the RAS at work. Nonbikers are looking for something horizontal, not vertical. The RAS filters out the vertical. It’s not that the drivers weren’t looking, it’s that the RAS blocked out the motorcycle.

How does this help us in self-development? The RAS can be trained. It becomes sensitized to anything we repeatedly pay attention to. Visioning seems to train it especially quickly. If you spend time visioning something, the RAS will open up its aperture and cause you to notice things relating to what you’ve been visioning. This is a huge advantage.

"subconscious" or "unconscious"

When you actively vision, you are programming your "background processing" (a term I prefer to "subconscious" or "unconscious"). Visioning accomplishes the internal work. Then, as the RAS become sensitized, you begin noticing things related to what was envisioned. It’s like things just jump out of the woodwork at you.

I spend a lot of time thinking and writing about leadership. Anymore I can’t watch a movie, a TV show, the news, or even commercials without spotting the intentional or inadvertent leadership messages in them. Examples include "The Edge" with Anthony Hopkins, "Scent of a Woman" with Al Pacino, "Battle: Los Angeles," and more recently "Ender’s Game." It’s like living in a gigantic leadership library. Whether it’s movies, books, or whatever, your RAS will bring to your attention things you can use to accomplish the external work. Blend the external and internal work and you’re on your way.
