S

en. Gary Peters, D-Mich., probably has a bumpy road in front of him if he wants to see his idea of a National Institute of Manufacturing (NIM) enacted into law. Peters, who is up for re-election in November, introduced his proposal in June, but there is no bill yet.

Peters is waiting for input from interest groups and other senators before actually writing a bill. Maybe he could be criticized for not doing that important spadework first, but that is a quibble. However, it is noteworthy that the Alliance for American Manufacturing (AAM) called Peters’ proposal "a very ambitious idea … and it’s not a bad one." Peters’ NIM would be led by a chief manufacturing officer who would report directly to the president.

The AAM went on to say that it agrees with the "central thrust" of the proposal. But the AAM, which has labor union and manufacturing company members, added that among the things that such an organization should encompass is a "clear role for labor in NIM decision-making; Buy America procurement rules in our manufacturing strategy; and plenty of attention paid to capital-intensive, trade-sensitive manufacturing sectors."

There is, of course, already a major federal effort directed at advanced manufacturing: that would be Manufacturing USA, run by the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST), composed of 14 manufacturing institutes around the country, all of which are involved in high-tech manufacturing. They are Advanced Functional Fabrics of America, Cambridge, Mass.; American Institute for Manufacturing Integrated Photonics, Rochester, N.Y.; America Makes (Additive Manufacturing), Youngstown, Ohio; Advanced Robotics for Manufacturing, Pittsburgh; BioFabUSA, Manchester, N.H.; Clean Energy Smart Manufacturing Innovation Institute, Los Angeles; Institute for Advanced Composites Manufacturing Innovation, Knoxville, Tenn.; Lightweight Innovations for Tomorrow, Detroit; Manufacturing times Digital, Chicago; NextFlex, San Jose, Calif.; National Institute for Innovation in Manufacturing Biopharmaceuticals, Newark, Del.; Power America, Raleigh, N.C.; Rapid Advancement in Process Intensification Deployment Institute, New York; and REMADE (Reducing Embodied-energy and Decreasing Emissions), Rochester, N.Y. The NIST also funds other federal manufacturing programs such as the state-based Hollings Centers, also known as the Manufacturing Extension Partnership.

There are also many lesser U.S.-administered manufacturing-related programs, 58 scattered among 11 federal agencies, according to the Government Accountability Office. Ostensibly, a NIM would impose order on that unwieldy mass of programs, eliminate duplication, and improve effectiveness.

OSHA Considers Changes to Lockout/Tagout Standard

The Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA) announced in mid-May via a request for information (RFI) that it is considering revising its lockout/tagout (LO/TO) standard to take recent technological advances into account. The standard comes into play for users of metal cutting and other machinery.

The agency’s move comes three years after it issued a permanent variance from the standard to a manufacturer of steel wire rod and coiled rebar for grinding rolls on a roll mill. The OSHA variance allowed Nucor Steel Connecticut Inc. to use a trapped key concept and monitor safety-rated power relays in combination with administrative procedures.

The LO/TO standard, established in 1989, currently requires that all hazardous energy from power sources and energy stored in the machine itself be controlled using energy isolating devices (EIDs) when an employee is performing servicing or maintenance of a machine or equipment. OSHA’s definition of an EID excludes push buttons, selector switches, and other control circuit-type devices. But technological advances since the standard was issued in 1989 suggest that, at least in some circumstances, control circuit-type devices may be at least as safe as EIDs.

The original LO/TO standard was influenced heavily by a consensus standard issued by ANSI in 1982. ANSI reaffirmed that standard in 1988 and 1992. But ANSI issued a new consensus standard in 2016 that requires that LO/TO "be used unless the user can demonstrate an alternative method will provide effective protection for persons." Before using an alternative method, the employer is required to complete a practicability/justification analysis, a risk assessment, and other applicable evaluations.

The trapped key system was designed to replace a locked-out EID and to function similarly to a lockout device, in that only the employee in possession of the key could restart the machine undergoing maintenance. The single key was controlled through administrative group lockout procedures.
