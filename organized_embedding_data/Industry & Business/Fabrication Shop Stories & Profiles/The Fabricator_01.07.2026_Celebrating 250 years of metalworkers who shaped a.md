# Celebrating 250 years of metalworkers who shaped a nation

Playing With Fire

American metalworkers have transformed raw materials into opportunity, prosperity, and progress

By

Josh Welton

Metalworkers and welders helped to build the U.S., and they’ll play an important role in building the future as well.

Darla Welton

A

merica’s history is often told through the names of presidents, generals, documents, battles, historical moments, and monuments. Yet behind every chapter stands another group of people.

They forged horseshoes and railroad spikes. They cast cannons and locomotive wheels. They raised bridges, welded ships, built automobiles, crafted art, and fabricated rockets.

For 250 years, American metalworkers have transformed raw materials into opportunity, prosperity, and progress. Most were blue-collar workers whose names never appeared in history books, despite helping build the nation we know today.

Here’s to those who helped forge America.

INDIGENOUS METALWORKERS

Every metalworker knows the feeling. You pick up a piece of metal and see something—a tool, a sculpture, or a machine. That instinct is much older than America itself.

Thousands of years before the first colonists arrived, Anishinaabe people around Lake Superior were mining copper. With stones and fire, they transformed raw metal into tools, fishhooks, knives, weapons, and trade goods. Their work connected communities across vast distances and proved that metalworking has deep roots in this land.

The principle was the same then as it is now: look at a piece of raw material and imagine what it could become. Long before the first colonial blacksmith lit a forge, native metalworkers were proving a truth every welder, fabricator, and maker understands: Innovation doesn’t start with technology; it starts with human creativity.

THE BLACKSMITH

One of America’s first metalworking legends was the village blacksmith.

America was built by thousands of anonymous smiths who forged the hinges, tools, nails, horseshoes, cookware, wagon hardware, and farm equipment that held early life together. When English settlers established Jamestown in 1607, they brought a blacksmith with them. On any given day, a colonial smith might shoe horses in the morning, fix a farmer’s plow after lunch, and finish a set of door hinges before supper.

They were the machinists, welders, fabricators, toolmakers, mechanics, and entrepreneurs of their day rolled into one. The blacksmith’s shop was as essential to early American communities as the church, the mill, or the general store.

Some owned their own shops. Some apprenticed under master craftsmen. A few were women who carried on operations after a father or husband died. Others were enslaved people whose skills kept farms, plantations, ironworks, and businesses operating.

Not every piece they made was purely functional. Some were simply beautiful—and meant to be. American metal art didn’t begin in galleries. It began at the anvil—and it never left.

Paul Revere is a well-known example of how far a skilled metalworker’s influence could reach. Most Americans remember him for his midnight ride. But before that, he was an accomplished metalworker and silversmith, trusted for his problem-solving skills and civic leadership. His story reminds us that in early America, the same hands that forged everyday objects also helped forge a new nation.

As tensions between the colonies and Great Britain grew, metalworkers found themselves at the center of events. Blacksmiths, founders, and gunsmiths forged tools, repaired equipment, cast cannons, produced shot, and supplied the hardware needed to support the revolutionary cause.

When America fought for its independence, it did so with the help of skilled craftspeople who knew how to turn raw materials into the tools and defenses of "We the people."

Winning independence created new opportunities—and enormous demand. Generations of founders, ironworkers, and machinists would be needed to reshape both the landscape and the economy.

IRONMASTERS AND MACHINISTS

Throughout the late 18th and 19th centuries, furnaces and forge fires became a familiar sight across the country. Somewhere in every growing town, an ironworker was casting stove plates and wagon parts. A machinist in a river-town shop was repairing a farmer’s equipment in the morning and building something that had never existed by afternoon.

Their work traveled wherever Americans traveled—across rivers, over mountains, and into new territories as the nation expanded westward. As the nation grew, so did the complexity of its metalworking trades. Blacksmiths remained essential, but they were increasingly joined by machinists, patternmakers, foundry workers, and mechanics. By the time of the Civil War, small machine shops and foundries were the centers of innovation and manufacturing, producing everything from locomotives and steam engines to industrial machinery and military equipment.

Soon, they would help build something even more visible.

IRONWORKERS IN THE SKY

As the 19th century gave way to the 20th, steel began replacing iron as the backbone of American infrastructure. Stronger and more versatile than what came before, steel made it possible to build longer bridges, taller buildings, and larger projects than previous generations could have imagined. Riveters and ironworkers assembled the skeletons of a modern nation, often hundreds of feet above the ground.

Among the most celebrated were the Mohawk ironworkers, whose skill at working high above city streets earned them the nickname "Skywalkers." For generations, they helped construct bridges and skyscrapers across North America, becoming legendary for their balance, courage, and craftsmanship.

Yet even as riveters and ironworkers transformed the skyline, another technology was quietly changing the future of metalworking. By the end of the 19th century, inventors were experimenting with electric arc welding—a process that would eventually revolutionize manufacturing, shipbuilding, construction, and warfare. But it also created a new opportunity for skilled workers and laid the groundwork for the welding profession as we know it today.

THE WARTIME WOMEN: ROSIE AND WENDY

When most Americans think about women in wartime manufacturing, they think of Rosie the Riveter. The iconic image symbolized the millions of women who entered factories, shipyards, and production facilities during World War II. They built aircraft, assembled vehicles, operated machinery, and helped power what President Franklin Roosevelt called the Arsenal of Democracy.

Less well-known is Wendy the Welder. While Rosie represented the broader industrial workforce, Wendy represented the skilled tradeswomen who learned to weld ships, tanks, and critical infrastructure. Across the country, women struck arcs, ran torches, and mastered skills that many had assumed belonged only to men.

The work was demanding, dirty, and dangerous. They met the moment anyway. In shipyards, factories, and fabrication shops, they helped produce the equipment that supported Allied forces worldwide.

Their contributions extended far beyond the war itself. Rosies and Wendys challenged long-held assumptions about who belonged in the skilled trades and opened doors for future generations of women welders, machinists, ironworkers, fabricators, and metal artists.

Like every generation of metalworkers before them, they answered a national challenge with skill, determination, and craftsmanship.

POSTWAR METALWORKERS

World War II demonstrated what American manufacturing could accomplish under pressure. In the decades that followed, the country didn’t slow down.

Welders, machinists, toolmakers, diemakers, foundry workers, and fabricators went to work building the products that defined modern American life—automobiles, appliances, industrial equipment, farm machinery, and the infrastructure that supported a rapidly growing nation. Many worked in massive factories. Others worked in small machine shops and family-owned businesses. Aircraft builders, aerospace fabricators, welders, machinists, and technicians developed the skills and technologies that pushed aviation forward. Together, they helped create one of the most productive manufacturing economies in history.

And then they aimed higher.

THE METALWORKERS WHO HELPED REACH THE MOON

When Americans think about the Space Race, they usually picture astronauts. Metalworkers tend to picture the welds.

Long before a rocket left the launch pad, skilled workers had to build it. Welders joined critical components. Machinists shaped precision parts. Fabricators assembled structures. Toolmakers created the specialized equipment used to manufacture spacecraft components. A bad weld or a poorly machined part could jeopardize an entire mission.

American metalworkers rose to the occasion.

When Neil Armstrong stepped onto the lunar surface in 1969, he represented the efforts of hundreds of thousands of Americans—among them welders, machinists, fabricators, and technicians whose names rarely appeared in headlines but whose craftsmanship helped carry humanity to the Moon.

One giant leap for mankind was also a remarkable achievement for the skilled trades.

TRADES ENDURE

The decades that followed the Space Age were not easy ones for American manufacturing. Steel mills closed. Factories went quiet. Entire communities built around metalworking were reshaped by economic forces beyond any one worker’s control. But the trades endured—carried forward by men and women who stayed out of stubbornness, pride, and an unshakeable belief that making things with your hands still mattered.

The tools evolved. CNC machines, plasma cutters, laser tables, and robotic welding changed what a shop looks like and what a metalworker needs to know. But the core never changed. Someone still has to understand the metal, read the blueprint, catch the mistake, and know when the machine is wrong.

Through layoffs, recessions, and reinvention, American metalworkers adapted—the same way they always have.

Today’s American metalworkers are descendants of every blacksmith, silversmith, ironworker, founder, forgemaster, riveter, boilermaker, welder, tool- and diemaker, machinist, millwright, pipefitter, fabricator, CNC operator, metal sculptor, and instructor who came before them. They repair what breaks, create what doesn’t yet exist, and help turn ideas into reality.

The tools and technology have changed. The mindset hasn’t: See the possibility in raw material. Shape it with skill. Build something useful, beautiful, and enduring. Pass on the knowledge.

Metalworking is not merely manufacturing. It’s craftsmanship. It’s skill. It’s creativity. It’s design. It’s restoration. It’s art. It’s culture. It’s people.

As America celebrates its 250th birthday, let’s raise a torch to the countless metalworkers who came before us—and to those who continue to forge its future.
