The story of the Waukesha parade memorial

RAFAEL GUERRERO

T

he city of Waukesha, Wis., was forever changed in November 2021, when a man drove an SUV right through the city’s annual Christmas parade, killing six and wounding 62. The incident deeply affected the residents of Waukesha, Greater Milwaukee, and other southern Wisconsin communities.

Clay Knapp wasn’t there, but he remembers it well because it was the same day his daughter was born.

"It really put into perspective the fragility of life," Knapp said.

Years later, Knapp and his employer, Jackson, Wis.-based COST of Wisconsin, played an integral part in the construction of a memorial at Waukesha’s Grede Park to honor the victims. Organizers formally dedicated the memorial in November 2024, three years after the incident.

It’s a project that not only highlighted the company’s creativity, but it also tied it to the area in a way that its theme park, museum, zoo, and tourist attraction work might not.

COST of Wisconsin is primarily known for its work in theme parks, zoos, museums, aquariums, casinos, and other tourist attractions.

Heart in the Right Place

Six ground-level ribbons—one for each of the six individuals who lost their lives—meet at the center of the memorial and rise into the sky to form one large heart. The 20-ft.-tall ribbon/heart sculpture ultimately resembles six heart pieces. The ribbons trail away to form the paths to six benches that reside on the perimeter of the memorial.

"The heart of this project is the community itself. From the thousands of donors who gave what they could to the contractors who built each piece with care," reads the description on the memorial webpage. "Today, the memorial stands as an open public space where anyone can pause, reflect, and find solace."

COST of Wisconsin was one of several local companies contracted to make the parade memorial a reality. Others include Waukesha-based Thrive Architects and Pewaukee, Wis.-based VJS Construction.

The ribbons and benches are made from a special white mix cement concrete with No. 3 rebar reinforcement. Knapp, an assistant project manager with COST of Wisconsin, said the company’s steel shop made the rebar cages that form the interior of the concrete ribbons and benches.

When it came to the large heart pieces, the COST team scrapped plans for rebar-reinforced concrete pieces in favor of steel structures clad with composite material. The team argued this option was better overall for the sculpture’s longevity and future maintenance.

"That was a big change that happened late in the game," Knapp said.

The change now called for the heart piece’s interiors to be rolled I-beams.

"However, there was no ability to have a company roll a premade I-beam. So, we had to fabricate an I-beam," said Knapp. "Max Weiss [Company] was a big part in helping us roll all of the heavy steel plates."

Max Weiss Company, a Milwaukee fabricator that is a short drive away from COST of Wisconsin, has nearly eight decades of experience.

"We’ve used them before for similar things. They have large machines that can roll pretty heavy-duty steel," Knapp said. "It was a no-brainer for us to reach out to them to have them do this for us."

Al Sanders, the president of the Max Weiss Company, said the company was honored to donate its bending services and to help get the pieces to the required specifications for this memorial project.

"Given the circumstances, we were willing to participate and get involved in the community," said Sanders.

The large heart pieces at the center of the memorial are made of steel and are clad with composite material.

Organizers formally opened the Waukesha parade memorial in November 2024, three years after the tragic incident.

On the project, Knapp said: "It was an ongoing structural design process. The look was there, and everyone wanted that look to be achieved. It was up to us to figure out in real construction work how to make it happen; that took probably three-quarters of a year to figure it out."

A Special Project

The Waukesha parade memorial is about 27 miles south of COST of Wisconsin’s Jackson headquarters. Geographically speaking, this project stands closer to home than much of the theme park, zoo, museum, aquarium, casino, and other tourist attraction fabrication projects the company is known for. For example, it was involved in work for the new Universal Epic Universe theme park in Orlando, Fla., that is scheduled to open this spring.

COST of Wisconsin has come a long way since its first major project in 1957, back when it built habitats for the Milwaukee County Zoo. In addition to its Jackson headquarters, the company also has locations in Orlando; Berryville, Ark.; and Winnipeg, Manitoba in Canada. It can work in shotcrete, GFRC, FRP, epoxies, urethanes, acrylic, metals, and other materials and has multiple steel shops, automated bar bending equipment, waterjets, CNC machines, and more.

The parade memorial, though, is a special project for the COST of Wisconsin team, said Knapp. Some of the employees are from Waukesha or have loved ones there. Some were at the 2021 parade.

Knapp said people feel the memorial is a "very beautiful representation" of those who were died or were hurt, and those still grieving. Much like Sanders at Max Weiss, Knapp was honored to be a part of this memorial.

"It’s definitely a special project that a lot of people will think about and remember for a long time," Knapp said.
