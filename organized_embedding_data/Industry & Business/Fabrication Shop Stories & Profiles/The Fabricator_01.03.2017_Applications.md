Fiber laser, panel bender spark product redesign savings

SITUATION

Richards-Wilcox Inc., Aurora, Ill., is one of the longest continuously operated factories in the Chicago area. Established in 1880 as a manufacturer of hinges and flat-track sliding door hardware systems for stables, barns, and garages, the privately held firm produces Aurora Storage Products and Richards-Wilcox brand overhead conveyors, door hardware products, and office filing and storage systems at its 300,000-sq.-ft. facility for distribution throughout North America and Europe.

Several years ago the company purchased a museum-grade storage cabinet line. The requirements and tolerances of the new line were more extensive than the company’s previous shelving units.

"Our challenge was to upgrade and replace our older punching and bending machine with new technology that would allow us to compete in this new market," explained Tom Glennon, engineering manager. "Another need was laser cutting capacity. We were outsourcing nearly $300,000 for laser blanks each year."

RESOLUTION

After a thorough search of fabrication equipment from various builders, management decided to purchase the Prima Power 4-kW Platino

®

fiber laser and the Fast Bend panel bender.

"We were able to purchase the new Prima Power equipment based on the new museum product line," continued Glennon. "We were at a point where we needed to upgrade the facility, and the equipment that Prima Power was offering opened up a lot of new opportunities for us to make parts using less steel, less time, and with better construction."

The fiber laser cutting machine offers energy efficiency and maintenance savings; a variety of automation modules; and ease of installation, use, and maintenance. The machine can be used to cut a variety of materials, including highly reflective metals such as aluminum, copper, and brass. It cuts mild steel up to 0.78 in. thick with efficiency and quality, the company reports. Productivity increases particularly with thin- and medium-gauge sheet metal.

The addition of the fiber laser allowed the company to save the $300,000 it was spending on outsourced laser parts.

"Realistically we went from two to four weeks’ lead times to a one- or two-day turnaround," said Glennon. "Within 30 minutes we could have emergency parts off the … fiber laser instead of waiting three days. We have actually offloaded some of our other machines because of the capabilities of the laser. This has allowed the older machines to increase their reliability and productivity. The … laser runs two shifts per day. We’ve been getting 12 to 14 hours a day out of the laser for the past two months.

"When you shear or punch a blank, you have to make sure that the tooling stays sharp, or the subsequent operations are more difficult," continued Glennon. "By having the clean edges and the consistency of the laser, we can run quicker, with less setup time [and fewer] part-to-part deviations, while making the processes downstream better and quicker."

Prototype creation has gone from weeks to a couple of days, allowing the company to begin to work as a product development group.

"One of our company’s strengths is that if our customer needs a large order of standard product and a few custom parts to make it all fit together, we are very accommodating," explained Glennon. "Now that we have the fiber laser, we are able to test all those custom parts, get them assembled, tested, and proven before we ship them to the customer."

The servo-electric panel bender helps reduce the labor-intensive tool setup, programming, and part handling that can occur when forming metal in a traditional press brake. According to the company, the bender reduces or eliminates non-value-added costs associated with traditional press brake operation.

The panel bender can form different angles, hems, multiple corner radii, Z offsets, and closed tubular profiles with the standard set of tooling. The automatic tool change (ATC) option, automatic feed table, and automatic inversion of positive and negative bending blades allow for more bends per side in an automatic sequence without manual intervention. The operator is only required to load, rotate, and unload the part.

Part setups as fast as 10 seconds can be achieved when using ATC in conjunction with the optional bar code reader and operator instruction display screen. The operator can initiate a part program by scanning the bar code on the part. The instruction screen notifies the operator when the tool setup is complete and displays graphically how the part blank is to be loaded.

Soon after the bender was installed, the engineering department took on the task of designing the museum cabinet. The goal was to achieve a snap-on design that would virtually eliminate welding. According to Glennon, the main target of the redesign was to reduce the cost. "We knew the importance of the new museum product line, but we needed to redesign in order to compete in the marketplace."

"The flexibility of the additional short blades (ASP) tooling was a key feature," explained Dan Santoro, industrial engineer. "We were able to redesign features around that … and of course the repeatability of the machine. Before the [panel bender], we really weren’t able to do any interesting or unique features because they wouldn’t necessarily come out the same way twice."

"One of our company’s strengths is that if our customer needs a large order of standard product and a few custom parts to make it all fit together, we are very accommodating. Now that we have the fiber laser, we are able to test all those custom parts, get them assembled, tested, and proven before we ship them to the customer."

—Tom Glennon, Richards-Wilcox Inc.

The panel bender’s repeatability allowed the company to design the box pieces to snap together, eliminating welding and setup time costs.

The addition of the fiber laser and panel bender—which gave the company the ability to laser-cut its own blanks, eliminate welding, and reduce secondary operations—cut the cost of the cabinet by 60 percent.

"We have been able to use these two machines as a flexible manufacturing cell," Glennon explained. "There is next to no setup time and infinite possibilities for lighter-gauge material. The [machines] have allowed us to adequately supply a high-mix, lowvolume product to our various markets in a timely and cost-effective manner."

Heavy-duty vacuum cleans up safety concerns, unscheduled downtime in abrasive blasting operation

SITUATION

Cianbro operates in more than 40 states across a diverse range of markets, self-performing civil and structural projects from building bridges, to operating fabrication and coating facilities, to working in power plants and paper mills. The employee-owned firm has a total commitment to safety and a long list of safety awards to show for it, including national recognition as the Healthiest and Safest Company in America by the American College of Occupational and Environmental Medicine.

When the firm wanted to eliminate an ergonomic hazard in which workers manually removed steel shot from an auger system connected to a cabinetstyle blast machine at its 40,000-sq.-ft. fabrication and coating facility in Georgetown, Mass., Safety, Health and Environmental Supervisor Kris Chipman researched methods to eliminate manual handling of the heavy material.

The Georgetown facility fabricates structural steel beams for bridge and building projects. Many of these projects begin with beams moving via a conveyor system through the cabinet-style blast machine, where steel beads called shot shoot at the beam to remove rust and mill scale in preparation for welding or painting. Once the steel shot hits the beam, it drops into an auger system that reclaims and feeds the material back into the blast machine for continual reuse.

The sandblasting process is taxing on machinery, and when breakdowns occurred, about once a month, workers had to remove all blast media from the system to perform maintenance. On those occasions, two workers spent eight hours sweeping and shoveling the steel shot into drums and, using a drum dolly, manually moved them from the production floor.

Focused on safety, the company’s goal was to alleviate the ergonomic issues of shoveling heavy media in a tight space and manually moving heavy drums. In researching solutions, Chipman had seen some big shop-type vacuums and thought something like that might work.

After speaking with the VAC-U-MAX rep, who had worked on another Cianbro project to vacuum sludge for a bridge application, Chipman quickly realized that the commercial, shop-type vacs were insufficient to handle the volume of heavy blast media at Cianbro. "He did understand exactly what we were looking for, and after more discussion, we realized this could actually save money on the machine, downtime, production, and more."

RESOLUTION

To handle the heavy blast media, the vacuum needed to be designed for high-volume recovery in an industrial setting, and the VAC-U-MAX 15-HP 1020MFS continuous-duty vacuum was the best choice to eliminate the need for workers to go down into a 3-ft.-deep trench with limited space and awkward footing to shovel and scoop heavy blast media.

Even though the unit can recover up to 5 tons per hour and is designed to pick up heavy materials, including steel shot, foundry sand, metal powders, and sludges, Chipman wanted final assurance that the vacuum could pick up Cianbro’s particular blast media.

"This is heavy stuff," she said. "Each 55-gallon drum weighs about 2,000 pounds, and from the beginning we weren’t sure if what we wanted to do was even possible. So we sent VAC-U-MAX a sample of the shot media, they did some testing, and guaranteed it would work for us."

Although the 1020 unit is portable with 14-in. rubber tires and a dual swivel caster wheel for maneuverability by a single operator, Cianbro mounted its unit to a skid.

"Push-pull is a hazard in our book, and we do all that we can to avoid it," said Chipman. "So we mounted it to a skid and we move it with equipment. It is very portable for us because we have the equipment on-site—fork trucks and loaders—and we can move it around anywhere in our facility that we need it.

"The great thing about this vacuum system is that the hoses are long enough that we can leave the collection drums where they can be mechanically moved, instead of moving drum dollies manually," she continued.

Since acquiring the vacuum, the facility has cut the labor required to empty the machine by more than 50 percent. The task of emptying the pit, which used to take eight hours using two workers, now takes one worker three hours.

"Now we can shut it down in the morning, vacuum it out, and have it repaired all in the same day, using one person," Chipman said. "The reduced labor on maintenance, which is something that can’t be billed, is now redistributed to work hours on a project that is billable."

Shawn Ramsay, operations manager at the Georgetown facility, said that in addition to the vacuum "reducing the risk of injuries and allowing us to quickly and safely clear the steel shot out from the machine pit to perform maintenance, it also proved to be very helpful in the daily maintenance and cleanliness of the areas around the machine."

After implementing the vacuum, "we’ve only had to clean the entire pit out twice in the last year for unscheduled maintenance, which is an 80 percent reduction in unscheduled downtime and increased production time," said Chipman.

"This product is an investment," she continued, "but when you look at the potential for injury and on top of that potential show hard numbers that we know this will limit downtime—which has a cost—and also reduce replacement parts and wear and tear while increasing the longevity of the equipment, it makes it easy to put the case together to make the investment."
