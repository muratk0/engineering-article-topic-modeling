# Tackle information waste

[TARİH: 01.07.2025 The Fabricator]

Biz Talk

And shave days off the quote-to-cash cycle

Tim Heston

"M

anufacturing generates more data than any other industry, but there’s an important distinction to make. Data is very different from information. Having connectivity across all the organizational silos gives us the ability to integrate data in a way that turns it into information, which helps you make better decisions."

That was Jason Ray, CEO and co-founder of the quoting platform Paperless Parts, during its user conference, POWER 2025, in downtown Boston earlier this year. At that event I met a wide range of people, from estimators who had churned out quotes for decades, to shop leaders half my age with dreams of reshaping the way job shops run.

A common thread flowed through all the discussions I had: The greatest waste—and potential for improvement—relates to how companies use and share information. The late Dick Kallage, longtime metal fabrication industry consultant (and former columnist for this magazine), used to call it "information waste." With all the uncertainties of late, including the forever changing tariff landscape, minimizing that information waste is more important than ever.

Consider the machine tool technology fabricators work with and the thousands of different jobs they process. Do customers

really

know what they need, and can custom fabricators

really

provide that service, down to the smallest detail—material availability and quality, cut edge requirements, brake tooling, order sequencing, hardware, masking requirements before powder coating? The minutiae seem endless. Anything missed—including the ensuing rework, unanswered emails, and other communication roadblocks—represents information waste.

In the future, office workers in metal fabrication might monitor automated information systems, ensuring the digital manufacturing thread stays intact and the supply chain remains aligned. That’s not a bad way to spend a workday.

What about machine or automated system breakdowns? Does a job push a machine to its limits, or is it well within the machine’s capability? In either case, the root causes usually touch on training or maintenance issues, each of which has to do with communication (or lack thereof). Technical support for that machine might be lacking too. That’s another information issue, in this case with the machine or tooling vendor, operator, or another stakeholder. Information waste raises its ugly head again.

This is where what I like to call

continuous information improvement

comes into play. Judging by the fabricators I’ve visited and lean manufacturing initiatives I’ve covered, such improvement involves three steps. The first is dissecting the current state.

As Ray put it during the user conference, "I believe the key to success happens through process mapping, starting from that initial request for quote to when you receive cash for the job. You cannot improve something you don’t measure."

Second, identify process steps that could be streamlined, better communicated, or outright eliminated. Sometimes a little org chart restructuring might be in order, especially if people in different departmental silos are doing duplicative work. I’ve seen shops employ visual management techniques, including schedule monitors mounted on the shop floor or even in the office, showing the state of things. No issue stays hidden or unanswered in someone’s email inbox.

Third, look into what I like to call

information automation

, a broad category that can involve software, robotic process automation (RPA), and, yes, artificial intelligence that can automate or eliminate the need for tasks that the customer doesn’t really pay for—like interpreting drawings or building out the bills of materials, or keying in data from part inspections, perhaps documenting calibration data for inspection tools.

Such automation was touted at Paperless Parts’ user event earlier this year. One session involved an interview with 1factory, a company that effectively automates quality data management and aims to help build that "digital thread" through the supply chain.

Such improvements shave not hours

but

days off the quote-to-cash cycle. Combined with AI and other forms of information automation, these innovations might totally change a fabricator’s front office. Years from now, robots will not have taken over the shop floor. Material handlers might still be driving fork trucks or using pallet jacks. Humans will still need to program, monitor, and maintain the factory automation.

Office jobs, however, might look entirely different. Someone who starts in estimating today will in 30 years retire from a job that might be entirely different. They might be monitoring the automated information systems, ensuring the digital manufacturing thread stays intact and the supply chain remains aligned. That’s not a bad way to spend a workday, and it sure beats manually keying in data to build a ridiculously tedious, multilevel bill of materials.
