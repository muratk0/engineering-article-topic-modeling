# What does cybersecurity have to do with safety?

[TARİH: 01.07.2025 The Fabricator]

Editor’s Corner

In a digitally connected world, unauthorized access to a manufacturer’s internal information systems puts employees at risk

DAN DAVIS

T

he FMA Safety Conference, which you can read more about in this issue’s Industry News section, had a segment dedicated to cybersecurity this year. While it sounds like a strange addition to a conference where the Occupational Safety and Health Administration, machine guarding, and indoor air quality are common topics, it makes a lot of sense in today’s internet-connected world.

If you like spy novels, you already know the connection. For example, the villain works his way past stringent security to access the information systems of a nuclear reactor and threatens to cut off water flow, which could lead to the reactor core overheating and a very nasty situation. Luckily, in this scenario, the hero has infiltrated the villain’s island lair and disrupted his dastardly plans—saving the nearby community and the nuclear reactor’s staff.

While not having grandiose intentions like movie villains, today’s cybercriminals are very much interested in breaching manufacturing companies’ firewalls. In the cases where they gain access to information systems and lock out users, manufacturing workers face a true risk of injury. Once hackers have access, they can control machinery software and tamper with safety systems.

Those hackers also pose a threat to the supply chains that are connected to breached information systems. All kinds of data, some of which might be highly valuable, is at risk of being stolen. Obviously, this isn’t about physical harm to individuals, but the damage that hackers can do to a manufacturing company and its supply chain partners is great.

This cybersecurity threat to manufacturers is real and growing. IBM’s 2025 X-Force Threat Intelligence Index names manufacturing as the No. 1-ranked targeted industry by cybercriminals, who are mostly interested in extortion and data theft, more specifically accessing financial assets and stealing intellectual property.

Even in the face of declining use of malware to gain information, such as log-in credentials, manufacturing still stood out as the main target for ransomware cases, outpacing other industry sectors in 2024. The report stated that manufacturers are most vulnerable because of "outdated legacy technology."

Where does this leave companies in metal fabricating? Louis DeWeaver, PhD, cybersecurity consultant at Marsh McLennan Agency and a speaker at the most recent FMA Safety Conference, said that companies with any sort of internet-facing access need to lock that entry point down.

"All transactions are untrusted. It’s zero trust," he said. "That means no one’s trusted. No traffic. No user. Everyone has to authenticate. And once they’re in the system, they’re going to have to authenticate again to get to data or get access to anything else in the plant."

The requirement to have people authenticate their identities multiple times as they navigate around an organization’s network sounds like a pain, but that’s the way it’s meant to be. Hackers hate it.

"You can frustrate these guys. They’ll go away," DeWeaver said. "I talk to these guys all the time, and I know that they’re not going to spend their time trying to access these systems. They don’t care how much money you have because they can go to someone else who has a lack of controls."

DeWeaver also believes that training staff to avoid phishing emails is wasting everyone’s time. This is definitely a hot take given that the IBM report states the number of "infostealers"—malware that looks to collect log-in information, credentials, and other personnel information—delivered via phishing emails per week has increased by 84% since the 2024 survey. Cybercriminals are seeing success, so they continue with what is working.

That’s why trying to train employees to be information technology security experts is futile, DeWeaver said. Even the best professionals are getting fooled by phishing emails. It’s only going to get worse as the bad actors use artificial intelligence tools to construct professional websites and incorporate deepfake imagery into their phishing attacks.

That’s where multiple authentication helps to prevent deep access into systems by intruders. Getting access, with the ample amount of logins and passwords available on the dark web, is the easy part. Working to maintain the access is the challenge that cybercriminals aren’t interested in, according to DeWeaver.

Here is some more advice from DeWeaver to keep your systems safe:

• Look for software that tracks internal movements within a network, not just the endpoints where people can gain access. Software that calls out something like an attempt to encrypt information is the equivalent of walking in while a burglary is in progress. It’s a solid safeguard.

• If you have Microsoft Suite, you need to shut off access to the administration portal. It’s not done automatically. This keeps everyday users where they need to be.

• If you go into settings, add a Russian keyboard to your choice of an American keyboard. Russians apparently don’t hack other Russians. Maybe there is honor among some thieves.
