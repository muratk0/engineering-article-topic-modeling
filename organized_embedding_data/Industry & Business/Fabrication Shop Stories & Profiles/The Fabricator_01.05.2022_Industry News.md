HGG dedicates Houston service and support center

During a formal dedication of HGG Profiling Equipment’s new service and support center in Houston, technicians demonstrated one of the company’s pipe profiling machines in action. Pipe traveled down an in-feed conveyor into the work envelope where the machine plasma-cut a complex contour.

"The challenge has gone beyond solving problems related to the lack of skilled labor. [Fabricators] are automating, and now they want to push that automation harder. The flow of material in and out of machine is now becoming increasingly important. Their machine isn’t making money unless it’s cutting. That’s why we’re demonstrating this machine, which we developed for the North American market. The U.S. market’s demand for producing at lower cost is similar to elsewhere in the world. But the volume of demand here is so much higher."

So said Gerrit Teunissen, HGG’s general manager for the Americas, as he and his team demonstrated the company’s ProCutter 900RB, a machine designed to handle and cut complex profiles from tube and pipe up to 36 in. in diameter and up to 42 ft. long. The system—capable of plasma cutting, oxyfuel cutting, and marking—integrates an automated material handling system with an infeed conveyor, threejaw chuck cutting head, and two-point fume extraction system.

A Dutch company, HGG has had a stateside presence for the past dozen years. It now has more than 150 machines installed in North America. The 6,500-sq.-ft. facility in Houston has been opened "to better service and support the company’s growing list of customers and profile-cutting machine installations in North and South America," the company said in a statement. "Along with maintaining an onsite staff with service and support capabilities, HGG is using the new facility to warehouse spare machine parts and plasma consumables for quick customer turnaround." The statement added that the center eventually will be used for some light machine assembly.

"Over 12 years, automation in industry has proliferated," Teunissen said during the event, "and one area that continues to be developed heavily is automated welding, because of the skilled labor shortage and all the other challenges manufacturers face. But you can’t automate welding unless you achieve a good, quality cut that can be reproduced." He added that the proliferation of automated welding and the demand for automated 3D profiling equipment "has almost occurred in parallel. We’re growing together."

Teunissen pointed out that increased material prices also have pushed more fabricators to automate. "If someone cuts a hole in the wrong spot, that’s an expensive mistake. Shops might need to buy the material again."

He added that this is where soware continues to play a critical role. "We live in a 3D world," he said, describing the company’s involvement with Building Information Modeling (BIM) partners and other soware players. "With 3D modeling so ware, if you can dream it, and it’s within the realm of mechanical possibilities, then you can model it and cut it on the machine. This opens the door for [fabricators] to consider alternative cutting strategies that might reduce welding volumes or save costs elsewhere."

This, combined with the skilled labor shortage, rising material prices, and unabating demand for greater throughput, has put industry at a kind of inflection point. "It’s really an amazing time," Teunissen said. "Material costs are high, and the skilled labor shortage isn’t going away. The companies that are automating are leaving those that aren’t behind. They just can’t compete any longer."

MODEX 2022 focuses on resiliency, automation

Two years ago at MODEX 2020 in Atlanta, attendees and exhibitors at the material handling and supply chain tradeshow talked of shutdowns in Asia and Italy. Supply chain resiliency entered the conversation, but the pandemic hadn’t gone global yet, and few if any predicted the tumultuous times that would follow.

"We’ve moved from an evolutionary stage of the supply chain to a revolutionary stage—revolutionary innovation, revolutionary adoption of the technology, and revolutionary investment in the supply chain."

So said John Paxton, CEO of show sponsor MHI, Charlotte, N.C., during his keynote presentation at MODEX 2022, held in late March in Atlanta. He added that although the pandemic had exposed challenges in the global supply chain, "it also has kicked off a wave of innovation and investment. We’ve seen double-digit increases in sales volumes. We’ve seen investments in ports, warehousing, distribution, and automation. All this has kicked off the revolution we’re seeing in the supply chain."

More than 37,000 attended the first in-person MODEX in two years. Exhibitors and educational programs discussed several trends that those in the metal fabrication arena know all too well: the labor shortage, the use of data, and the continued push for more automation. All interrelated, these issues existed prior to the pandemic. Events during these past two years have amplified them and accelerated the pace of innovation toward improvement and resiliency.

"Resiliency is really about two things," said New York-based David Shillingford, chief strategy officer at Everstream Analytics, during a panel discussion about how COVID-19 reshaped supply chains. "One is redundancy, but of course redundancy costs money. And because it costs money, it matters where you have that redundancy in the supply chain. The other is agility, which is about having a plan, having optionality. It doesn’t necessarily require a huge investment in Plan B, but companies do at least need to have a Plan B, and [they need to know] as soon as possible that they need to move to that Plan B, or a hybrid version of Plan A and B. In these cases, the value of data and analytics, on a global basis, hasn’t changed. The pandemic didn’t’ change that reality. What has changed is that more people now fully realize its value."

"The visibility we have to our products throughout the supply chain is fascinating," said fellow panelist Rick Blasgen, a Chicago-based supply chain strategist. "Some of us recall the days when you didn’t know it was in a container until it showed up at your distribution center. Now we know, before it’s built, what the raw materials and the packaging will be, and we can see it all along its way. That eliminates so much variability. And we take that for granted now."

Such visibility also reveals the levels of inventory throughout the supply chain, a data point that might become even more critical in the coming years. Companies want enough inventory to respond but not so much as to be a cash drain on the business.

"Interest rates haven’t always been near zero, and now they’re going up again," Blasgen said. "We’re going to hear companies start talking a lot more about carrying costs, because it’s going to cost more money to carry inventory. CFOs will start thinking about the amount of cash that is consumed by the amount of inventory their companies carry."

Inventory challenges and the everfickle consumer have driven the supply chain industry to realize the need for new distribution channels, including so-called "micro-fulfillment" centers that place distribution points closer to the final customer. It’s a trend now seen by the explosion of e-commerce, which, together with the worker shortage, has pushed demand for automation—especially when it comes to picking.

"Right now, when you visit a warehouse, it’s the picking [of products] that’s still manual. We’ve figured out mobile technologies, we’ve figured out conveyor systems, we’ve figured out replenishment," said Ross Diankov, CEO of Mujin Corp., Atlanta "But it’s the picking that’s left over. People still perform this manually."

During his presentation, Diankov demonstrated a fully automated micro-fulfillment center that’s triggered into action immediately after someone places an online order. Within a space small enough to be situated in an urban setting, less than a mile from the customer’s home, an automated storage and retrieval system retrieves the bin with the product. The bin is positioned in front of a picking robot, which places it in a returnable tote, which is staged and presented for customer pickup.

Beyond opportunities presented by the building of these micro-fulfillment centers—which, of course, call for plenty of metal fabrication services—the intelligent automation within them could be applied on the future shop floor. Think automated assembly and, especially, packaging.

"The pace of adoption of materials handling automation keeps accelerating," said Chuck Harris, vice president of distribution and fulfillment at automation provider MHS, Mount Washington, Ky., during an automation roundtable. "Through the pandemic, the rise in e-commerce drove a huge volume of small-quantity orders through facilities that, coupled with the supply chain issues and the labor shortage, created huge issues for the industry. That’s why many are saying [automation technology] is critical for their supply chain. They can no longer have those weak links."

Harris added that this kind of interaction would work in concert with broader flow-detection sensing throughout a facility. "When I think about vision and AI, I think about operator safety and energy consumption, much in the same way we use motion detection to turn off and on lighting when needed. We’ll soon start to see the use of flow detection, which can regulate the speed of automation throughout a facility."

Automation will become more robust as people interact more with technology. "Human-robot collaboration will be critical," said fellow panelist Dan Poppa, PhD, director of the Louisville Automation and Robotics Research Institute at the University of Louisville, Louisville, Ky. "By this I mean the human interacting with technology using a variety of senses. It could be tactile interaction, using guidance motion. Another is the handling of cognitive overload, making the human a part of some processes in the automation chain."

He explained that machine learning and adaptive control technologies will push automation into new arenas, but to be adopted widely, the automation needs to be aware it can’t handle a problem and ask for help. This echoes the refrain of Industry 4.0, where autonomous technology adapts while people look for patterns and handle the exceptions. It’s a goal everyone’s working toward, within metal fabrication and throughout the supply chain.

Connecticut bridge wins top NSBA prize

The I-91 Interchange 29 Exit Ramp Flyover Bridge in Hartford, Conn., has been named the 2022 Bridge of the Year.

The National Steel Bridge Alliance (NSBA) and participants of the World Steel Bridge Symposium in Denver selected the 880-ft . bridge for the top honor in NSBA’s Prize Bridge Awards program. This is the first year NSBA has presented the Bridge of the Year Award.

The interchange now features a two-lane, high-speed ramp that showcases a novel approach: a triple I-girder straddle bent that provides load path redundancy, eliminating fracture-critical designations and the associated long-term inspection costs.

"The triple I-girder straddle bent cap is a highly innovative and very eff ective solution," said Prize Bridge Award Judge Domenic Coletti, PE, principal bridge engineer at HDR. "It took advantage of steel’s benefits all around."

An additional benefit became clear during fabrication: The triple I-girder configuration is economical to fabricate, costing substantially less than a comparable box girder with reduced shipping costs as well. The team found even more economy by choosing uncoated weathering steel, in which a patina protects the structural steel from corrosion.

The American Institute of Steel Construction and National Steel Bridge Alliance have recognized outstanding bridge design since 1928. Eight bridges won Prize Bridge Awards in the 2022 competition.

AP&T receives order from Mercedes-Benz for partial press hardening system

AP&T in Germany has received an order for its TemperBox partial press hardening system from Mercedes-Benz in Sindelfingen, Germany. The order also includes AP&T’s multilayer furnace, automation equipment, control system, and integration.

"TemperBox makes it possible to combine hard and soft zones in one and the same structural part, thereby creating new possibilities for innovative design, making the next generation of vehicles lighter and safer," said Achim Krauß, area sales manager at AP&T in Germany.

The system will be used for test manufacturing a number of different parts. The blank to be formed is transported from a loading station to one of the usual furnace layers with the help of a 4-axis SpeedFeeder. After being heated, the blank is transferred to the TemperBox module for partial cooling. It then moves on to be fed and formed in the press tool.
