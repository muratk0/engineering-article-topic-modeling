Those Who Live by the Commodity Will Die by the Commodity

Right now there is deep concern focused on Brazil — the country of the future and always will be. It seems like only yesterday that Brazil, the "B" in the BRIC designation, was being hailed as the leader of the emerging market states. It announced its arrival in the world by bidding to host the World Cup in soccer and the 2016 Olympic Games. Perhaps the drubbing Brazil took from Germany in that soccer championship was an omen, as now many are calling for the Games to be postponed or even moved because of the arrival of the Zika virus, political chaos, and the woeful state of readiness for the competition. Brazil has fallen far and fast. The question is why.

Some assert this is the same old challenge Brazil is always presented with: corrupt politics and a working culture that can be extremely lackadaisical. There are certainly elements of this, but these challenges are not new and the country has been able to withstand the impact before. The problem now is far more serious and essentially out of the hands of the government. The exports that Brazil depends on have lost their market.

China had become an insatiable buyer of what Brazil produced in the way of iron ore, soybeans, manufactured goods, and the like. The slowdown in China robbed the Brazilians of that crucial market, and no other country is willing and able to fill that void. Europe and the U.S. were once key outlets for Brazil’s food, raw material, and manufacturing, but they have had their own issues and buy far less than they once did.

Brazil started to turn its attention to developing its own consumer and its own domestic demand, but it has come too late and has not been able to supplant the export side of the economy. Brazil has created many of its own issues, but it probably could have coped with them if not for the loss of these markets and the inability to shift direction.
