# “What exactly do you do?”

[TARİH: 01.05.2014 The Fabricator]

FROM THE EDITOR-IN-CHIEF

Just how does a metal fabricator respond to that question?

Dan Davis, editor-in-chief of

The FABRICATOR

, can be reached at dand@thefabricator.com.

Some occupations are easily summed up by the job title, like firefighter or teacher. Others require a modifier, like car salesperson or marketing consultant. And some require several sentences and the questioner’s undivided attention.

I imagine metal fabricators fall into the last category. Unless they make a standard range of products, such as architectural and structural metals or boilers, they fall into that very descriptive category of “fabricated metal products,” per the colorful categories of the North American Industrial Classification System.

Believe me when I say that “fabricated” just mentally throws some people off. Try explaining to other people in the publication industry that you work for a magazine called

The FABRICATOR

. They think you sit around writing stories about recent sightings of Elvis or the latest ice cream-only diet.

Even just the phrase “metal products” leaves open a lot of room for explanation: What kind of products? This is where the inevitable examples are shared to bring the answer to life. It’s one thing to say that a business makes parts for Caterpillar, but it’s another thing to describe the huge arm for an earthmover and describe the time and effort needed to create it to exact specifications.

Honestly, my favorite part of the job is to walk a shop floor and have someone simply explain where the parts are headed and for what applications. (Unfortunately, many of these stories aren’t for public consumption as the customers’ identities or the metal part designs themselves are confidential.) Engaging a metal fabricator in a conversation like this is a real eye-opener for anyone without a deep knowledge of just who provides the demand for metal components in the U.S. You instantly learn that some industry segments are much larger than you ever believed (there were more than 92,000 snowmobiles sold in the U.S. and Canada in 2013!), and others exist when you had no idea (grooming tables for dogs qualifies as a market segment?).

So if I’m still surprised even after visiting almost 100 fabricating operations over the past decade, you have to believe that an average person walking down the street would be as well. That is, if they only knew what really happened behind those large bay doors or chain-link fence.

Look at Greiner Industries Inc., a metal fabricator that will be profiled in an upcoming issue. This Mount Joy, Pa., business looks like many other industrial companies, but you only begin to put the pieces together after talking with company management. The company fabricates everything from 80-ft.-long railroad girders that have to be delivered on schedule at the risk of thousands of dollars in fines to decorative staircases that have to be installed before a wedding day at the risk of a furious bride. And the building labeled “Blasting and Coating” handles the application of protective coatings on these giant fabrications. And those cranes over there belong to the company—or to other companies that want to rent them—for installing those huge components. And those trucks in the parking lot are for moving these “overdimensional” loads to the erection site.

But don’t call this company a structural fabricator. It charges by the hour, not by the pound. It is a metal fabricating job shop.

That’s just one story, but it’s an example of the unique companies that make up the metal fabricating community. It’s hard to provide a general description of this sector, much less a single entity within it.

Even educated folks that prepare marketing research reports for the investment community don’t have a really good feel for the makeup of this market.

The FABRICATOR

editors routinely get calls from these market watchers asking them to place a monetary figure on the market. It’s hard for us to do when readers of our publications might range from an iron fence maker to a shipbuilder.

If you look at the conveniently tabbed NAICS code 332—Fabricated Metal Product Manufacturing—you see an industrial segment that contributed $137.8 billion to the manufacturing GDP and accounted for 1.4 million jobs in 2012. But within that segment are forgers and stampers (NAICS 3321) and machine shops (NAICS 3327), companies that may not fit the traditional definition of sheet and plate fabricators. Not included in NAICS 332 are areas such as agriculture, construction, and mining machinery (NAICS 3331) and motor vehicle body and trailer manufacturing (NAICS 3362), pretty significant areas of fabricating activity. Size is in the eyes of the consultant, I guess.

With the uphill battle associated with informing people just what metal fabricating is and its impact on the U.S. economy, how should a metal fabricator reply when asked for details on the profession? Well, I’ve put together this response: “Metal fabricators bend, cut, form, and join metal to create parts that go into the end products that people from all walks of life depend on every day.” That opener works, but only a metal fabricator can make it come alive with illustrated examples that are sure to follow.
