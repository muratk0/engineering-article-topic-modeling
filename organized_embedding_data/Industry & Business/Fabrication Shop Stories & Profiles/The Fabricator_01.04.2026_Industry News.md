Consolidations continue with Morton’s purchase of Superior Tube Products

Consolidation in the U.S. metal fabrication sector continues to remap the industry with Morton Industries’ recent acquisition of Superior Tube Products, Davenport, Iowa.

Completed at the end of last year, the purchase is expected to add about 5% to Morton’s revenue, listed at a projected $135 million for 2025, which places it 15th on The Fabricator’s 2025 FAB 40 list of the nation’s leading fabrication companies. Morton—headquartered in Morton, Ill., near Peoria—guaranteed employment to all 47 Superior employees following the acquisition. Team members were offered role-based flexibility, including comprehensive relocation packages to any Morton facility or remote work options, and the company sponsored visits for employees and their families to tour facilities and explore options.

"Most of them [required] relocation, and many of them did do that. We were surprised at the number that actually took it, so we’re very happy with that," Morton President/CEO Chris Ober said. "It’s part of our commitment to making sure that the people that we do find are going to keep jobs."

Morton now has about 750 employees working at six locations. Ober said Morton purchased Superior, in part, to bridge a gap in its own product offerings—for example, equipment and tooling to bend 7-in.-OD tube. The company relocated Superior’s 7-in. bending equipment and tooling to its headquarters in Illinois, along with equipment that Superior was using to bend arced rollover tubes for off-road vehicles.

Other excess equipment once owned by Superior is being auctioned off, Ober said.

"Every competitor has their own unique strengths," Ober said. "In Superior’s case, they really were specialists in some of the larger-diameter bending that we like to do. It was very attractive to acquire not only the equipment but a customer list to support the use of that equipment and tooling, and then the trained workforce to come run it. It couldn’t have been any better suited for what we’re looking at."

In addition, Morton decided to move equipment that supplied another Superior niche—higher-production handles for consumer lawn and snow-removal equipment—to its facility in Dunlap, Tenn. Morton recently signed a lease for an additional 60,000 sq. ft. of production space in Dunlap, and the Superior acquisition came at the right time to make good use of it.

"Tennessee has been a great location for us," Ober said. "We just knew that with the growing market, we were going to need some more space."

Faith Technologies to open new facility in Alabama

Faith Technologies Incorporated, a provider of engineered electrical assemblies and modular systems, has selected Opelika, Ala., as the location for its newest manufacturing facility. The $79 million project is expected to create 200 jobs.

FTI currently operates facilities in Appleton and Little Chute, Wis., and Olathe, Kan., and it’s developing additional capacity in El Paso, Texas. The company builds electrical components in controlled environments for installation in the field.

DeWys Manufacturing receives $115,525 award to expand workforce training

DeWys Manufacturing, a Marne, Mich., provider of custom steel fabrication and metal manufacturing, has received a $115,525 Going PRO Talent Fund Award from the state of Michigan to expand workforce training and career development.

The fund is a Michigan workforce development program that provides employer training grants to help companies train, develop, and retain employees. DeWys will use the funding to support manufacturing training and skilled trades education for both existing team members and new hires across key production and leadership roles.

The award will help the company expand training aligned with core skills and certifications essential to modern metal fabrication and OEM manufacturing, including:

Welding and fabrication training.

Advanced manufacturing and production best practices.

Quality systems and technical certifications.

Lean manufacturing and continuous improvement.

Supervisor and leadership development.

Safety and operational training for new hires and current team members.

2026 Great Designs in Steel coming to Michigan May 20

The American Iron and Steel Institute will host the 2026 Great Designs in Steel symposium on May 20 at the Vibe Credit Union Showplace in Novi, Mich.

For more than 20 years, Great Designs in Steel has showcased the latest steel trends and material applications in new cars and trucks. The event will offer a full day of insights from industry professionals on topics such as advanced highstrength steels, future mobility, automotive advancements, and manufacturing technologies. Attendees will discover the innovations that are shaping the future of automotive design and engineering while learning about the lightweighting, durability, and sustainability benefits of steel.

This year’s agenda includes presentations by keynote speakers John Cardwell of ArcelorMittal North America and Elizabeth Krear from the Center for Automotive Research. In addition, representatives of four automakers—GM, Honda, Hyundai-Kia, and Rivian—will cover such topics as formability of AHSS, virtual welding quality assessment, steel side sill tubular design for battery protection, and design of a front crush structure.

Power Solutions Intl. acquires MTL Manufacturing & Equipment Inc.

Power Solutions Intl., a Wood Dale, Ill., manufacturer of alternative fuel and conventional power systems, has acquired MTL Manufacturing & Equipment Inc., a metal and steel manufacturing company based in Beloit, Wis.

MTL engineers, manufactures, and supplies fabricated products including switchgear subbases, electrical enclosure assemblies, and fuel tanks used in large power generation products for applications such as data centers. With capabilities including welding, automated laser cutting, bending and forming, and painting, the company is certified to UL142, ULC S601, and UL2085. Its two Beloit locations total more than 185,000 sq. ft. of manufacturing space.

Maximum Fasteners acquires Weld Engineering & Fabrication

Maximum Fasteners Inc., a Santa Fe Springs, Calif., provider of fasteners to the construction, automotive, agriculture, and furniture markets, has announced its acquisition of Weld Engineering & Fabrication Inc.

Weld Engineering & Fabrication Inc., also of Santa Fe Springs, is a multigenerational, family-owned business serving industrial and commercial customers. Canopy Brands completes acquisition of Dixie Metal Products

Canopy Brands has announced the acquisition of Dixie Metal Products, a manufacturer of engineered metal products serving the utility, infrastructure, and industrial markets. The workforce development lifecycle personified

Jay Phillips, FMA’s new board chairman and longtime metal fabricator, wants to help prepare the next generation of skilled workers

The Fabricators and Manufacturers Association positions itself as the guardian of the workforce development lifecycle: attracting young people to the industry through its SparkForce foundation and its scholarships and summer camps, developing their talents with the help of certificate programs and technical content as they find their way in the metal fabricating field, and engaging them along the way through networking events and trade shows. FMA’s new board chairman, Jay Phillips of Valley Iron Inc., a metal service center with three locations in California, has gone through his own workforce development lifecycle, connecting with mentors who paved his way into metal fabricating, growing his skills over time, and now finding ways to connect with industry peers and mentor the next generation of fabricators and manufacturers.

Jay Phillips of Valley Iron Inc. addresses attendees at the Fabricators and Manufacturers Association’s Annual Meeting in Clearwater Beach, Fla., on Feb. 25. Phillips is currently serving as the chairman of the FMA board of directors.

Phillips, who took over the chair in October 2025, has spent most of his life in the metal fabricating industry. Early influences set him on his career path, and a connection with a machine tool vendor introduced him to the FMA community. He’s reached the point where he wants to give back to the industry that has done so much for him, and he views his time with FMA as the perfect way to do just that.

The Fabricator spoke with Phillips to learn more about his journey and what it has meant for him to be a part of FMA and its board of directors. The Fabricator: How did you get involved in the metals industry?

Jay Phillips:

It started when I was 11 years old. It was the first time I saw someone arc welding. I watched for a while and then was given the chance to try it. I’ll never forget it. That night when I went home I couldn’t sleep. My eyes burned liked crazy from watching the arc welding, and I ended up crying myself to sleep. I wanted to be a welder ever since that day.

Four years later as a sophomore at Paso Robles High School, I enrolled in our regional occupational program [ROP] for welding. I have not forgotten my first welding teacher, Bill Stroud, who is still alive today at 95 years old. now. At the end of my junior year, I applied for a job working for Keith Kirkland at Paso Robles Welding.

Then during my senior year, 1989, I used that job as my occupational work experience. I worked there for a total of seven years, from high school through junior college.

I loved working there. All of the ex-Navy guys, hull maintenance technicians, or HTs, I worked with were older than me and offered their advice. They said, "You need to go to school, kid." I took the advice, went to the local junior college, Cuesta College, and earned an associate degree. I then transferred to Fresno State, where I began working for the Briscoe family at Valley Iron Inc. in 1993.

I worked at Valley Iron Inc. during my time at Fresno State, and I graduated with a bachelor’s degree in geology. But I never really used it. I realized the trade skills that I had learned in the ROP welding program and that experience at a young age paid for all of my education. I was a high-wage earner at the time, and I graduated debt free. So, I kept working for Valley Iron Inc. for a couple more years while I worked on my teaching credential.

The Briscoe Family bought a 20-acre property about a mile south of the original Valley Iron Inc. location, and they planned to open a brand new facility. They asked if I would help and be a part of the new growth. I said "yes." That was in 1999. As of this year, I’ve been with Valley Iron Inc. for 33 years.

When we opened that new facility in 2005, we added a warehouse only a year later and started our value-added processing department with a Peddinghaus plate processor. That was our first piece of equipment. It was a lot of money, but that machine paid for itself and the next machine, an ALLtra burn table. The Briscoes let me spearhead that project.

I had the chance to help design the building, place the machines, interview the personnel, and select the software. It was a great experience.

We were heavy into structural at the time, but then we bought a waterjet table because we sell sold stainless steel and aluminum as well. From there the business just grew and grew. I believe it was God’s blessing to work for great people who believed in me and mentored me to be successful in this career.

When did FMA get on your radar?

That goes back to the Peddinghaus machine. I would attend the Peddinghaus FABTECH dinner, and Lyle Menke, who was also chairman of FMA and now retired from Peddinghaus, asked if I knew anything about the FMA. I didn’t know anything about it, so he said he would put me in touch with a person who could tell me everything I needed to know. That was Ed Youdell, the FMA president and CEO.

I sat next to Ed at the 2015 FABTECH dinner, and he told me all about FMA. He invited me to the annual meeting in 2016 in San Diego, and my company quickly became members of the FMA. I then submitted my board application, and it was approved.

What made you want to get involved?

You get to a point in your life where you’ve learned a skill set, but you’re not perfect. You don’t know everything, but you want to learn more and help others succeed. You want to do something bigger, like attract, develop, and engage the next generation of metal fabricators and manufacturers. When you go outside of your everyday work schedule and join an association like the FMA, which spans three countries, you learn how they are driving the industry forward. It is bigger than I imagined, and to be a part of that is phenomenal.

I’ve personally been through that cycle. I was attracted at a young age to the industry. I learned everything I could and developed the skills to be successful. What are some of the things that you’ve learned working with your fellow board members?

One thing I did learn is there’s life after working at one place for so many years. I’ve met quite a few people who have left one job and gone to another after 20 or so years, and they’re doing well. Everything’s fine for them.

Another thing I’ve learned is we all have something to offer the association, and our input is important in driving the industry forward. I have transferable skills I learned over the years, not just welding and fab, but also plumbing, electrical, engine repair, hydraulics, programming, etc. You don’t want to be limited in life; you want to learn as much as you can because it opens opportunities for the rest of your life. Being a member of FMA does that, too. We open doors for opportunities to network and to learn the current trends and technology in the industry. We also come together to support one another.

What would you say to people who aren’t members of FMA to get them to consider joining the organization?

If you own, work in, or supply a metal fabrication and manufacturing shop, you need to join the FMA! As Ed Youdell says, "We exist for the industry. The industry doesn’t exist for us." The more members we have, the better the association becomes.

For any organization, it is only as strong as its members, so it’s the members that drive the FMA. And FMA is driving the industry forward. There are an estimated 18,000 job shops in America, and we all are in this together. We share a niche culture. Our mindset, work ethic, and love for the industry are the same. We all face the same day-to-day industry problems, and FMA is trying to solve those industry problems with input from its members. That’s why we offer training and education for the industry.

I hear the same issues from everyone I talk to: "We need to build up our workforce" or "I need skilled labor." I say to them, "Come to the annual meeting, visit FABTECH, join a user group or an advisory council, or donate to the FMA Foundation, SparkForce. The foundation provides scholarships for students earning metal manufacturing degrees and grants for manufacturing camps that are getting kids interested in metal fabrication. We’re making our industry attractive to these kids. They are going to jump on board.

Then they’re going to ride it, grow up, and work in a shop. This is the next wave of potential employees coming. A skilled workforce is what we’re ultimately looking for. I lived it, and I know it can be done!

What do you think the future holds for the metal fabricating industry over the next five years?

That is a big question. But my answer would have to be energy and tech.

When I first started in metal fabricating, those big satellite dishes represented the big change in technology. Remember the 6-ft.-diameter satellite dishes for TV reception? At Paso Robles Welding, we’d cut hundreds of 3-in. Sch. 40 black pipe to 7 ft. long and torch-cut some tabs on them before they were cemented into the ground. Then the satellite dish was mounted to it. Technology has completely changed since then; now you can stream a movie on your phone.

The current tech trend is AI, and it is having a huge impact on the industry. There are a lot of shops across the nation dedicated to setting up some of the data center infrastructure for AI, and with that, plenty of energy is going to be needed for AI to sustain itself. But no matter what tech trend comes along, I do know this: There will always be a demand for a skilled metal trades workforce to fabricate and manufacture the equipment for that new tech trend.
