In some cases, the answer is yes, and to make it happen, those fab shops have drawn from various improvement methods: quick-response manufacturing, lean manufacturing, the theory of constraints, among others. A common thread that made each implementation a success: a holistic approach, focusing on

everything

Two Manufacturing Models

Offering more services is the classic way custom fab shops grow with their largest customers. The relationship might start with piece-part manufacturing, but as trust builds over time, customers ask for more: forming, welding, powder coating, assembly. Traditionally, a shop might add an assembly area near the shipping department. This follows the classic, process-based shop layout: Assembly is another "process" and, thereby, needs another department.

The assembly process itself, though, reveals the reality of a typical fabricator product mix. The company really has two (or even more) "businesses" under one umbrella: (1) repeat, higher-quantity contract work and (2) nonrepeat or low-quantity job shop work (and even a spectrum of work in between).

A shop often specializes in one or the other, but the mix of work is still there—especially if the operation is trying to build its contract business. New orders start small. Recent KPI surveys from the Fabricators and Manufacturers Association reveal that new customer dollars usually make up just between 3% and 5% of overall revenue. The sales department attempts to grow small accounts into bigger ones, but in the meantime, those on the floor need to manage workflow for the entire product mix. That’s not easy.

This becomes painfully obvious when trying to add or expand an assembly operation. One area might be full of pallets holding kits of low-quantity jobs, while another area might have assemblers working in cells or lanes positioned next to controlled inventory locations. It’s like you have two manufacturing models in one department.

Thing is, look upstream and you find that this mix of work is wreaking havoc on flow and, hence, consuming capacity that can’t be sold to others. A laser is tied up for hours cutting a large batch of identical parts. Those large batches build work-in-process (WIP) inventory and extend lead times. Meanwhile, the laser next to it is cutting a dynamic nest full of different parts for several or even dozens of different low-quantity jobs. Organizing it all takes time, which adds more variability. Large batches keep machines running, but this creates huge piles of WIP staged before final assembly. All that WIP makes life difficult for assemblers trying to find the parts they need.

Finding Rhythm

Gary Conner, a retired lean manufacturing consultant, knows the precision sheet metal business. He got his start at a sheet metal shop in the 1980s in the Pacific Northwest. At the time, he worked on a team that, even with the punch, laser, and press brake technology of the era, effectively separated some of the job shop’s "high-runner" work into separate value streams, where products flowed from punching to bending to hardware and final assembly—all in a small corner of the shop (see

Figures 2

and

3

).

Conner said his shop did this out of pure necessity. "We continued to punch, form, and insert hardware for 600 units in a batch," he said, adding that it took the operation about a month to flow the entire job through the shop. "Soon, however, we were told we needed to fabricate and assemble 150 units a week and deliver 30 a day."

This would require a multitude of setups at the punch and brake. "Meanwhile, we had nearly 100 other customers to satisfy."

As Conner recalled the story, the customer demand spurred an entirely new way of thinking—one that focused less on machine uptime and more on part flow velocity. An idle, well-maintained machine that’s available to produce isn’t necessarily a concern. WIP sitting days or weeks on a rack, though, should raise the alarm. As Conner put it, "To quote Taiichi Ohno’s favorite sayings, ‘Sleeping parts make no money.’"

This thinking pushed Conner’s team to reorganize the shop floor and dedicate a cell to that high-volume product—from punching all the way through assembly. He added, however, that designing that cell took more than just arbitrarily moving machines to a specific area. To uncover the necessary resources required time studies that revealed how long it took for cross-trained operators to cut and bend a part, insert hardware, and perform final assembly.

They standardized the punch tools used in the cell, then set up a series of brakes—including staged tooling across the brake bed and quick-changeover practices—so they could handle all components within every kit in the product family. They measured the cycle times, then divided the work to establish the required resources (machines and people) and balanced the flow to meet the required demand of 150 units a week. After running the calculations, the team found that they needed to produce one unit every 14.4 minutes:

This

Moreover, cross-trained workers could move as needed to maintain flow and the component inventory assemblers needed to complete their work. As long as they completed a unit every 14.4 minutes, all was well.

Adapting to High Product Mixes

Cellular manufacturing isn’t a foreign concept to contract fabricators. Many have organized certain high-quantity work into multiprocess cells. Others have dedicated "quick-response" cells for low-quantity prototype work. That said, some job shops find it difficult adapting cellular manufacturing and value stream mapping in general. The variety of work just seems too great.

Here, Conner recommended a hybrid approach, grouping similar jobs into one value stream and using weighted-average cycle times based on common, shared elements among those similar parts. Products might share certain geometric characteristics (all enclosures, for example), and material characteristics (specific thicknesses or grades). Most important, one element can serve as a "common denominator" to calculate a manufacturing rhythm, or

takt

, for a particular group of products.

The trick here, Conner said, is to avoid analysis paralysis. The idea is to couple available data with real-time observations and conversations with shop floor leads. "Experts on the floor can help," he said. "They can help you establish ballpark numbers that allow you to jumpstart your production planning. Don’t worry about absolute accuracy. You can perfect the numbers over time."

Conner suggested looking for patterns. These could emerge within the entire product mix or within a segment of the product mix that could be grouped into distinct product families—or as Conner describes them, "part types."

For instance, say you have a mix of jobs that require aluminum, mild steel, and stainless steel parts that go into repeat orders for 17 different products that need to ship daily, as shown in

Figure 4.

These repeat orders make up a significant part of the overall product mix. Still, the sheer variety of work seems overwhelming. Each part has different dimensions and a multitude of manufacturing requirements. How can you possibly group these into value streams?

Here’s where finding that common denominator comes into play. Yes, the parts all have different dimensions and processing requirements, but they’re all sheet metal, 0.25 in. thick and less, and they all fit into the higher-quantity portion of the product mix.

You see that the easiest parts to process happen to be aluminum. Everything else takes longer. This gives you the common denominator. Processing time for every part is some multiple of a typical aluminum part. Again, these are rough averages. In reality, every piece has a unique cycle time at every work center, but establishing a common denominator at least gives you a rough starting point.

After talking with shop floor leads and reviewing basic clock-in and clockout data from your enterprise resource planning (ERP) system, you find that it would make sense to group certain aluminum and mild steel components into a single family that you call "Part Type XYZ," as shown on Figure 4.

Components with the XYZ part type have similar attributes, but there’s also significant variation. Stainless steel parts within the mix take roughly five times longer to process than the aluminum parts. Again, variation abounds here: geometry, part size, tolerances. But for these groups of parts, material type happens to be the best way to differentiate the processing times.

Again, these four "part-type families" come together to create 17 products (or "sales units," as Conner described them in Figure 4) that must needed breaks). Each part-type group has an average cycle time that varies primarily by material type. Part Type XYZ consists of two aluminum parts and two stainless steel parts. Each aluminum part takes one

work unit equivalent

—the baseline or common denominator. Again, Conner doesn’t use exact cycle times here. The common denominator just gives a ballpark to get things started.

Each stainless part takes five times longer (or, as Conner puts it in the figure, is five times as complex). So, two aluminum parts (2 parts × 1 complexity level) and two stainless steel parts (2 parts × 5 complexity level) give you 12 work unit equivalents.

Do the same calculation for the other three part types, and you get 43 work unit equivalents, which workers need to "produce" every eight hours (408 minutes, which accounts for breaks). You divide 408 minutes by 43 work unit equivalents, and you get a 9.5-minute

takt

time. This means that every 9.5 minutes, the equivalent of one "work unit" must be complete to meet demand.

Like any high-mix operation, of course, not every product runs through every process. As shown in Figure 4, all of Part Type XYZ is cut, but only 50% of Part Type ABC is cut. Multiply the observed operator cycle time (OCT) by the percent the process is used, then add up the totals for all four part types, and you get an 11-minute "weighted average" for the cutting cycle time. Divide 11 by the 9.5

takt

time, and you get 1.2 operators— or at least two operators to "keep the flow" at the cutting workstation. (Editor’s Note: For a deeper dive into this, visit

thefabricator.com

and type "Value stream mapping for job shops" in the search bar.)

The concept can be expanded to the assembly operation itself, with different groups of operations designated at each assembly workstation. Picture Figure 4 again, but with a multitude of columns after "weld" showing each assembly step measured by "weighted-average" work units. All this could help a shop separate those "high-runner" jobs into a separate value stream—from cutting to assembly.

Conner added that direct flow from fabrication to assembly might not be possible for every product family. Still, maintaining that takt—the manufacturing rhythm—can make flow much more predictable and, hence, reduce the need for a massive kitting area or racks of WIP before assembly.

Again, the idea is to start with ballpark estimates, based on initial measurements and conversations with experts on the floor. With this, the shop initiates the value stream and then perfects the flow over time. This could be from some combination of simple observation; clock-in, clock-out data in an ERP or a manufacturing execution system (MES); and even actual machine cycles and other data from an IIoT platform.

Supplier Management for Lean Assembly

Predictable throughput and small batches help minimize or even eliminate WIP before assembly—a big deal especially today, when external disruptions (like tariffs or a trade war) can create drastic changes in demand. One project could be put on hold indefinitely. That’s not good, but since part flow is so rapid, the punches and lasers haven’t cut blanks yet, so there’s no WIP to worry about—just raw material. In another case, a customer might want to reshore and ramp up quickly. A shop with a predictable manufacturing rhythm can make that happen.

But what about purchased components that go into that final assembly? A fab shop might be forced to use certain suppliers, and some of them might have long lead times—especially if they’re overseas. A large OEM might consider domestic suppliers, but with all the uncertainty around tariffs, they’re hesitant to make big supply chain shifts. Long lead times mean a fab shop might need to order large batches to ensure they have adequate safety stock.

Here, an idea presented by Rajan Suri back in 2010 could help. The professor emeritus at the University of Wisconsin-Madison developed an improvement method called quick-response manufacturing (QRM), a central component of which is manufacturing critical-path time, or MCT. This isn’t just the lead time promised to a customer. Suri defines MCT as the time it takes to make a product "from scratch." This includes those purchased components. A supplier that requires a long lead time prolongs the MCT.

Of course, sometimes overseas suppliers—despite having a long MCT—simply have an edge. The price might be significantly lower, even with tariffs, and using local suppliers might be seen as cost-prohibitive. To mitigate risk, Suri described a concept he calls "time-based dual sourcing."

In his book, "MCT Quick Reference Guide," Suri writes: "You can mitigate the drawbacks of the long-MCT by finding a second supplier with a short MCT, such as a local supplier, even if the unit cost is somewhat higher. Here, you source a portion of demand from the long-MCT supplier, based on long-term forecasts, and order the remainder much later from the short-MCT supplier as demand patterns become clearer."

From Supplier to Partner

Many contract fabricators grow by asking a simple question:

What else do customers need?

This is where additional services like assembly enter the picture, and supply chain management follows close behind.

The goal is to shift from a "supplier," one that might be dropped as soon as business goes south, to a "partner" that’s integrated deeply into a customer’s operations. Subassemblies arrive on time, when needed, presented in just the right way to make final assembly easier.

As Conner explained, the key is for fabricators to know their manufacturing rhythm, continuously improve to uncover untapped capacity, and always be ready to respond. That way, a few more of those small accounts—the "long tail" on the product mix—could make their way up the revenue curve.
