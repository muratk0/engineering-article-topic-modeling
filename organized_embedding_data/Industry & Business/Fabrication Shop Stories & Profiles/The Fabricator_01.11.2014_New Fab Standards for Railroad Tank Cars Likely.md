R

ailroad tank cars that carry oil and ethanol on their way to refineries are going to have to undergo major fabrication redesigns over the next few years once two federal agencies finalize a new safety rule.

The Pipeline and Hazardous Materials Safety Administration (PHMSA) and the Federal Railroad Administration (FRA) are very likely to require, at a minimum, about 50,000 Department of Transportation (DOT) 111 standard tank car manufacturers to upgrade their steel components to prevent punctures in the case of derailments. Moreover, come 2015 new tank cars coming into service and carrying the most hazardous chemicals will have to meet a brand-new construction standard called DOT 117.

The PHMSA/FRA proposed rule was moved to the front burner after a July 6, 2013, catastrophic railroad accident that occurred in Lac-Mégantic, Quebec, Canada, when an unsecured and unattended freight train transporting crude oil rolled down a descending grade and subsequently derailed, resulting in the unintentional release of lading from multiple tank cars. The subsequent fires and explosions, along with other effects of the accident, resulted in the deaths of 47 individuals.

The upgraded standards would, for example, require DOT 111 cars to use TC-128 steel because of its superior strength and toughness. Further, the head and shells of DOT 111 cars, now required to use 0.4375-inch-thick steel, would be a minimum of 0.5625 in. thick.

The proposed rule calls for all crude oil and hazardous material to be transported in the upgraded cars by October 2017. The railroad industry appears to have a bigger problem with retrofitting those existing tank cars than with building safer tank cars to DOT 117 by 2015, although the industry appears to prefer using some of its own upgraded standards rather than the proposed DOT 117 standard.

The Railway Supply Institute’s Committee on Tank Cars (RSICTC) said it is technically infeasible to retrofit DOT 111 cars with so-called metal jackets because of structural design and railroad clearance requirements.

“The process of modifying and welding an existing tank car, as would be required to add a metal jacket, can negatively impact the long-term integrity of the car in ways that are unknowable today,” the trade group, which represents railroad car manufacturers, said in its comments to the PHMSA. “Many repair facilities do not currently have the capabilities needed to undertake such a complex modification requirement. The availability of skilled labor that could be properly trained and certified for the work tasks required will likewise be a major undertaking with uncertain results.”

OSHA Finalizes New Reporting Requirements

New accident and injury reporting requirements that the Occupational Safety and Health Administration has been considering were finalized in September.

They become effective Jan. 1, 2015.

Under the final rule, employers must report the following events:

• Each fatality resulting from a work-related incident, within eight hours of the death. This requirement applies to all fatalities occurring within 30 days of a work-related incident. This is the same as the current regulation and the proposed rule.

• Each in-patient hospitalization resulting from a work-related incident, within 24 hours of the hospitalization. Under the current regulation, employers are required to report, within eight hours, in-patient hospitalizations of three or more employees, for hospitalizations occurring within 30 days of a workrelated incident.

• Each amputation resulting from a work-related incident, within 24 hours of the amputation. Under the current regulation, employers are not required to report amputations.

• Each loss of an eye resulting from a work-related incident, within 24 hours of the loss of an eye. This is a brand-new requirement.
