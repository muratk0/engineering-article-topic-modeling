# The economics of speed

[TARİH: 01.09.2024 The Fabricator]

Tech Talk

The evolution and economics of on-demand fabrication

Caleb Chamberlain

W

hen we launched our on-demand fabrication service six years ago, our default lead time was two weeks. By the end of 2019, we had dropped our standard lead time to five business days.

We also realized that with our order processing automation, we could serve our customers even more quickly, albeit at a higher price. In early 2020, we enabled same-day production. Customers could upload a part, get an instant price, submit an order, and have a prototype sitting on their desks the next day. For the next four years, we left our lead time and pricing model mostly unchanged.

Rush economics worked out well for us. In fact, rush pricing has accounted for roughly half our net income. Higher margins have made it easier to hire and retain great workers, buy new equipment, and improve our services. That’s a win for our customers, our employees, and our company.

It’s all very compelling, but rush fees don’t feel great to everyone. Most customers seem to get it, but occasionally people don’t respond well. One customer commented, "I’m not willing to pay to jump in line." High-mix shops that compete on a quick-response model might go to market saying they treat every job as a rush job. After all, someone jumping the line automatically makes others wait longer, right?

Rush or Not to Rush?

The reality is more complicated. Cutting in line is socially unacceptable because it gives one person a benefit while hurting everyone behind them. It’s selfish. But in our factory, a rush order doesn’t delay other orders. We maintain enough excess capacity to inject rush orders while still shipping normal orders on time. In effect, we reserve capacity at real cost so that customers who need it can get parts fast. Customers who don’t need ultrarapid speeds get to pay less. Meanwhile, if other companies treat every job like a rush job, what that really means is that every job is a little slower than it could be. If every job is a rush job, no job is.

There’s a big caveat here, which I’ll discuss later. What would it take to ship every small job same day? Simply put, you’d need enough production capacity to handle peak demand at all times, with zero forewarning. Longer lead times allow peak days to be smoothed backwards, filling in slow moments with work due in the future. With that capability, you only need capacity slightly in excess of average demand. But to guarantee that every small on-demand job will ship same day, you’d need enough equipment and people at all times to meet peak demand. That might be more than double the average. If you double equipment and labor costs to do the same work faster, then every customer has to pay more, whether they need the speed or not.

Hence the need to charge more for rush orders: If you gate faster lead times behind higher costs, then fewer customers choose it, and less excess capacity is required. And the cost of excess capacity is shifted to customers who benefit from it.

WIP Factors

There’s a wrinkle in this analysis. While we theoretically benefit from leveling peak days backward, we don’t actually do it that often, because it creates excess work-in-process (WIP) that sits around. It clutters the shop and causes confusion. Yes, if the lasers have a huge peak-demand day, we’ll pull the cutting ahead to make sure we can get it done on time. But as a general rule, we prefer to cut parts just in time. The result? A five-day default lead hasn’t been providing all that much production leveling benefit. In fact, we don’t start most small jobs until a day or two before they are due.

We realized this a few months ago and had to ask, if we don’t start small jobs until just before they are due, why is the default lead time five days? Sure, it gives us some buffer and planning capability, but it’s barely leveraged right now, and we tend to ship on time anyway.

The real answer is that four years ago we settled on a quick, standard lead time, then added a profit center in the form of rush adders. We didn’t even think about accelerating the default lead time for small jobs, because we developed an economic model that disincentivized it: Half our profit comes from rush adders. What happens when we shorten the default lead time enough that customers stop paying rush fees?

A New Delivery Model

I’ll be honest, that was an uncomfortable realization. We are operationally capable of fulfilling all orders faster by default, and yet our cost structure and economics were built on the premise that we could collect more revenue for that faster service. The optics on that aren’t great. Meanwhile, we had competitors that were fulfilling orders faster than us without charging extra. They couldn’t guarantee lead times, but they were by default cheaper and faster all the same. We concluded that if we can better serve our customers, we should. If that also makes us more competitive, all the better.

Changing our lead-time model was a scary proposition, because a rush fee goes directly to bottom-line profit. Well, that’s mostly true. We’ve incurred real costs to support faster production, with millions of dollars in custom software development and excess production capacity to fulfill on-demand orders. But those costs are now all baked in, contributing to regular depreciation and amortization that affects our balance sheet and our income statement. Beyond those significant sunk costs, a rush adder is pure profit, injected directly to the bottom line. So, changing our model in a way that reduces rush-order income is a truly scary proposition. What if, by reducing lead times, we cut our net income in half? That’s a pretty big deal, particularly for a bootstrapped operation like us.

Even so, I was convinced that this was a move we needed to make. So, we rolled out an experiment, gradually applying an accelerated leadtime model to a percentage of our customers: Our default lead time for small jobs dropped from five days to three.

We applied the new model gradually so that we didn’t create a spike of demand that would overwhelm the shop, and so we could get a feel for how the new model affected rush orders. Will fewer customers pay to expedite? Will more customers convert? How will this affect revenue and profit? Comparing the old and new models back to back would tell us a lot about what to expect as we transitioned to a faster service for all customers.

After two months, the numbers were clear: Reducing our base lead time from five days to three did decrease rush revenue. By a lot. The number of orders with rush adders fell by 10 percentage points, contributing to a significant annual hit to net income. We were prepared to stomach a loss if it meant improving our service long term, but it was pretty rough to stare at a guaranteed cash loss of hundreds of thousands of dollars.

But as we watched, we saw another trend start to emerge: New customers were nearly 20% more likely to convert when on the accelerated model. Revenue growth from new customers alone not only replaced lost rush revenue; it resulted in higher net income.

Let me emphasize how incredible that is. As mentioned, rush adders don’t directly affect the cost of goods sold or fixed costs. So, a rush adder flows directly to net income. Regardless of the increased conversion rate, we expected to lose money on the change, because the impact of increased revenue is mitigated by gross margin. If your gross margin is 30%, then replacing $100,000 of profit requires $333,000 of added revenue! But we saw a lift in conversion rates high enough to replace hundreds of thousands of dollars of lost rush-adder profit. Clearly, customers like getting parts fast.

The Power of Scale

So what’s the endgame here? Free same-day shipping? Perhaps, but it’s not so easy. What I said earlier still applies: Fulfilling orders faster means you need enough capacity to meet peak demand at all times. Even with a three-day default lead time, we have way more capacity than we’d need if our lead time was, say, two weeks. That means shops with longer lead times might tend to have better prices than us simply because their capacity is better matched to their average demand.

The answer to that problem is scale. This is the big caveat I alluded to earlier. For example, suppose I own an on-demand shop with one laser. Suppose that single laser is 80% utilized. That’s great: It gives a little extra capacity to avoid pileups if something goes wrong, and if lead times are long enough, I can look forward and level production backward to tackle peak demand days. Now suppose I want to fulfill incoming orders same day. To deal with peak days, I immediately have to buy a second laser and hire operators to run it. I’ve just doubled my labor and equipment costs to do the same amount of work, faster. Clearly, I have to charge more.

Now suppose that I have 10 lasers, and they are also 80% utilized. Compared to the single-laser shop, I’m doing 10 times the work with 10 times the number of lasers. If I want to fulfill orders same day, how many more lasers do I need? Possibly one, maybe zero.

The magic here is that scaling average demand by 10 times does not scale volatility by 10 times. There’s a bunch of math to support this, and we have data showing that demand variance doesn’t scale 1-1 with the mean. The simple result is that while peak days for a single-laser shop would require double the equipment and labor, peak days for a 10-laser shop might not require anything extra at all. With 10 lasers at 80% capacity, there are actually two lasers worth of unutilized capacity in existing equipment. As shops scale, the incremental cost of same-day shipping approaches zero asymptotically.

If you want to offer same-day shipping, you either have to charge more or you have to be really big. Scale isn’t enough on its own, of course. Manufacturing has to be optimized to handle high-mix production, supporting perhaps thousands of unique part numbers per day. Software automation is key. We’ve also ignored all kinds of logistical complexity in this discussion. For example, shipping same day means we rarely get to combine jobs to improve nesting efficiency. I’m certain that scaling same-day shipping is going to strain operations in other unexpected ways.

With that backdrop, I definitely see an end state where we ship small jobs same day without charging extra. We started with a two-week lead, and over the last six years, we’ve managed to whittle that back to three days. We aren’t all that far from zero. I think it’s a worthy goal, but we’ll have to take it a step at a time.
