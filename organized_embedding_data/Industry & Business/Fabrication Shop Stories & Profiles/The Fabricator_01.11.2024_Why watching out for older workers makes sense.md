# Why watching out for older workers makes sense

[TARİH: 01.11.2024 The Fabricator]

Editor’s Corner

If a metal fabricating company is desperate for new hires, it had better take care of its top contributors who already are on the shop floor

Dan Davis

I

f 50 is the new 30, I pity anyone who has to live their 30s like I’m living my 50s.

I’ve seen more doctors over the last five years than I’ve seen the 20 years before that. I’ve had appointments with podiatrists, internists, orthopedic specialists, and sleep specialists. I should probably see an allergist, but I’m afraid I’ll be told that I’m allergic to certain hops used in beer-making.

Living with aches and pains isn’t unexpected. It’s just bothersome, especially when your mind is still sharp enough to remember how you could once bounce back with only a few hours of sleep after being out most of the night.

Your body changes whether you want it to or not. How you deal with those changes plays a big part in how you are able to enjoy an active life after 50.

That’s an important lesson for leaders of manufacturing companies as well. If they want to keep their experienced employees contributing as they have always done, they need to ensure that they are physically fit to do so. That means staying away from injuries.

The Bureau of Labor Statistics, using data from 2022, shows that the median age of manufacturing workers in the U.S. is 44.3 years. While industry has done a good job of recruiting younger workers to offset the departure of thousands of baby boomers who have retired over the past several years, 51% of manufacturing jobs are still held by people 45 years or older, according to 2023 Lightcast labor market data. More than half of the individuals that make up the domestic manufacturing workforce are about five years away from when they should start looking into a colonoscopy.

Being realistic, companies like young workers for reasons that extend beyond their entry-level wages and their willingness to be trained. These youngsters are likely quicker and stronger than their older colleagues. These workers don’t wear medical alert bracelets because they can actually get back up when they fall.

But with the labor market as tight as it is, manufacturing companies have to keep their older workers safe. They are very much needed, and in most instances, their extended absences would be noticeable.

Travelers released its 2024 Injury Impact Report in late summer, and it provides a good idea of just how long this type of injury impact might be. After examining more than 1.2 million workers’ compensation claims from 2017 to 2021, the company learned that the average number of days manufacturing employees missed because of workplace injuries was 67.

That’s two months of being without that particular worker. It’s also the added administrative duty of keeping track of how the employee is healing and trying to decide on how they can be re-integrated back into the reality of the shop floor without the risk of re-injury. It’s a scenario that a lot of fab shops undoubtedly would want to avoid if they could.

Slips, trips, and falls are actually an easy target for continuous improvement activities because they are visible. Employees don’t need a lot of convincing to understand why it’s important to clear work areas of power cords and other obstacles.

The same research report revealed that the most frequent causes of injuries in manufacturing were overexertion (34%) and slips, trips, and falls (16%). Being struck by an object (10%), repetitive motion (8%), and being caught in or between hazards (7%) rounded out the top five.

Slips, trips, and falls are actually an easy target for continuous improvement activities because they are visible. Employees don’t need a lot of convincing to understand why it’s important to clear work areas of power cords and other obstacles. Remember that a 50-year-old doesn’t have the same balance as his co-worker half of his age. A trip and a fall could wind up being more tragic than comedic.

Overexertion is trickier to address because this might involve purchasing support devices, whether a hydraulic assist at a press brake or a height-adjustable table in a welding cell, to name a few examples. Because of this, don’t expect for shop floor workers to step forth with requests. They likely don’t know what’s available to help them.

This is where management might have to be a little more aggressive in protecting workers from themselves. Some shops have included a safety angle in their launch meetings for new parts headed to the floor. This is a prime opportunity for a manager to put employee safety front and center before any fabrication activities even take place.
