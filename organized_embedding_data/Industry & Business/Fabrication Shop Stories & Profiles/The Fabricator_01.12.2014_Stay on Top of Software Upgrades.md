XP is probably still the operating system of choice for many PC owners. They are familiar with it, and it provides the functionality they seek. Plus, they don’t have to worry about the totally different look of Windows 8 and its embrace of the tablet approach to computing.

Windows XP is perfectly fine, if everything operates as it should. If problems arise, however, the PC owner is out of luck. Microsoft Corp. ended mainstream support of the operating system back in 2009. No security patches or performance improvements have been designed over that same amount of time. There is no chance of resuscitation for XP if it goes under. It’s time for the PC owner to upgrade, if he or she decides to stay with the same platform.

Imagine this same scenario, but involving a metal fabricator operating a multimillion-dollar piece of capital equipment with a PC that has programming software that hasn’t been upgraded in several years. The machine tool company has issued several new versions of the software over the years, but the fabricator hasn’t stayed on top of the updates. The version in place works just fine, and the one license is all that’s needed.

All is fine until it isn’t. One morning the fabricator comes in and starts up the laser cutting machine, only to find out that he can’t program any new jobs. The PC that has been used to program the laser for the last 10 years has died. Hopefully, his data has been backed up, but he can’t program any new jobs until he gets a new PC. The problem is made worse because the only new PC available runs Windows 7, and his programming software version is not compatible.

A call to the machine tool developer reveals that it hasn’t supported that software version for a couple of years, as several upgrades have been issued since then. The fabricator has no recourse other than to upgrade the software, which can prove tricky and costly if he is using a much older version. That’s not to mention the additional training that may be necessary to optimize all of the new software features.

In the meantime, the laser cutting machine is down. Software!

If a software problem prevents a PC user from accessing his Mel Torme’ medley or photos from his last model railroaders convention, it’s not the end of the world. Life goes on. On the other hand, if the metal fabricator can’t cut metal parts, that’s the end of profitability for that machine on that day. The business can’t move forward unless cutting capacity can be found somewhere else.

This scenario is not that far-fetched. The stories are heard much too frequently. Most metal fabricators know that their job is to produce metal parts and really have no desire to be involved in the information technology (IT). They don’t realize that the two almost go hand-in-hand in today’s business world.

Robust software and hardware help metal fabricators make the right decision at the right time. If a customer sends an engineering change order, an enterprise resource planning software system can immediately pass along the change to the planning module, which makes the appropriate revisions to the laser cutting machine’s control software. Scrap is avoided because unwanted parts are not cut.

A fabrication shop with a strong IT architecture also squeezes time out of the customer-order-to-parts-delivery cycle. Software automates front-office and part design functions, delivering work instructions to the floor much more quickly than a process reliant on manual activity. Such automation helps to eliminate possible mistakes related to inputting incorrect information as well.

In lean metal fabricating environments where the operations director or the owner’s son in marketing also doubles as the on-staff IT guru, you can understand why software and hardware issues aren’t at the top of mind. That has to change, however, as these tools are inextricably linked to modern equipment and the potential for high-performing results. IT can’t be ignored.

The emergence of cloud computing may be a great fit for some fabricators looking to improve shop management.

These enterprise software packages are hosted at a secure site, likely far away from the company’s facility, and users access the software through the web. Upgrades are automatic, and the subscribing business doesn’t have to worry about maintaining a closet full of servers, which typically would be needed to host the software.

Fabricators that don’t upgrade their machine programming software really don’t have any good alternatives. Machinery needs to run. Software needs to be updated. That’s the reality of modern metal fabricating.
