# The era of vertical integration

[TARİH: 01.10.2025 The Fabricator]

Tech Talk

Rebooting the industrial supply chain

CALEB CHAMBERLAIN

M

uch has been said about how difficult it is to manufacture things in the United States. Yes, our industry can provide comprehensive services when it comes to sheet metal and tube, but what about the myriad components that go into a final assembly? Zoom out and you’ll find that, from concept to product, very little can be made domestically in the U.S. Even among products that are manufactured here, we remain deeply dependent on overseas suppliers for raw materials and component parts. This is true in nearly every industry and product category.

I wrote recently about one viral social media post wherein a buyer complained about how impossible it was to buy a custom kiosk enclosure in the U.S. The experience in China was easy. Here, it was time-consuming, extremely difficult, and prohibitively expensive. The same story plays out repeatedly as business owners, deeply affected by tariffs, try desperately to reshore, only to find that the United States is deficient. It’s simply impossible to have many things made here.

In one high-profile example, Destin Sandlin of the YouTube channel Smarter Every Day set out to develop and manufacture a custom, made-in-America grill scrubber. He contracted with a domestic stamping company to make the handle. But his innovative design required custom chain mail, a product which—unsurprisingly—is very hard to buy anywhere but China. The single domestic vendor simply didn’t have the capacity. Out of options but unwilling to source from China, Destin found a vendor in India who could provide the material. When it arrived, Destin discovered that it had been white-labeled. It came from China after all. In the end, Destin’s made-in-America scrubber had to launch with a critical component coming from overseas. The full video can be viewed on his YouTube channel.

This story and others like it make me nervous. It would be less alarming if we were friendly with all our overseas neighbors, but we aren’t. Geopolitical tensions are high. It’s hard to feel comfortable with a global supply chain that is critically dependent on one of our biggest adversaries. If we are unwilling to source parts from China, and we can’t get parts made domestically, what is the solution?

Fine, I’ll Do It Myself

Ordinarily in circumstances like this, companies solve problems by integrating vertically. When a marketplace is young and still developing, there are few companies meeting needs. Businesses can’t just go out and buy a specialized part for a product that doesn’t exist yet. So, they make it themselves. This was famously true of the steel industry in the late 1800s and early 1900s. Carnegie built an integrated empire capturing iron ore mines, coal fields, railroads, and mills, creating efficiencies and driving down costs in a relatively young marketplace. The automotive industry was similar. Ford’s River Rouge complex ingested iron ore on one end and rolled working automobiles out the other.

Techa Tungateja/iStock/Getty Images Plus

Tesla provides a more modern example in the automotive space: batteries, electric motors, and even software didn’t exist suitably for mass production of electric vehicles. So, Tesla brought it in-house. Even in tech, vertical integration often leads the way in young markets. In the mid-1900s, for example, IBM owned the entire computing stack, from hardware and operating systems to applications, sales, and service.

Of course, as markets mature, they trend away from vertical integration and toward disintegration. New companies occupy more narrow slices of the value chain, often performing better than large businesses that have their hands in too many processes. Companies offload heavy capex and associated manufacturing technology risk to create far more attractive returns while still benefitting from efficiencies created in the mature marketplace.

The path from integration to disintegration recurs cyclically. Industries start young, with many unmet needs, which leads to vertical integration. As markets mature and become commoditized, companies disintegrate to compete more effectively in focused slices of the value chain. But over time, disintegration also contributes to calcification, opening up opportunities for disruption by new technologies or business models that challenge the status quo. Industries progress from integration, to disintegration, and back to integration.

Where Do U.S. Markets Fit?

It’s interesting to view the U.S. manufacturing ecosystem from this lens. Almost universally, the market is dominated by large incumbents who have pushed disintegration to its logical extreme. Imagine an aerospace company, for example, that knows how to engineer financial statements but has spread manufacturing across so many thousands of suppliers that it forgot how to build airplanes.

Aerospace manufacturing has at least stayed here, for the most part. But other industries disintegrated by offshoring. Name a brand. It’s almost guaranteed that it isn’t making its own products at all. Apple doesn’t make its computer chips; TSMC does. Apple doesn’t manufacture iPhones; Foxconn does. Nike doesn’t make shoes; Pou Chen does. I could go on, but I think we all get it.

Manufacturing markets are "mature" in the sense that services have been commoditized, margins compressed, and services fragmented across thousands of manufacturers. On the other hand, U.S. manufacturing in particular bears all the hallmarks of a young, undeveloped industry: a massive swath of manufacturing capability just doesn’t exist here, so companies have to vertically integrate for as long as it takes to build capability.

I can imagine Destin saying, "I couldn’t get chain mail anywhere, so I started a company to make it." I can imagine a frustrated kiosk designer saying, "Nobody would make our enclosures, so I bought a laser, a brake, and a welding power source and started a company so we could do it ourselves." That’s the essence of vertical integration.

I acknowledge that it’s complicated, and that every industry is different. Theoretical ideas about the integration-disintegration cycle won’t serve companies well if they bring manufacturing in-house and still lose the price war to China. And it can be said that manufacturing is globally mature, if not regionally. That was the entire point of globalization, after all. People can’t buy some manufactured goods in the United States, but they can get them from overseas. Demand shifted to the most efficient producer, and that wasn’t us.

The next wave of vertical integration in manufacturing won’t just look like a bunch of legacy companies buying machines and bringing manufacturing in-house. There will be some of that, and I applaud it. But at the macro level, vertical integration and scale can be highly disruptive and industry-shaping. Think about Carnegie and the early steel supply chain. He created an empire on the back of new capabilities that simply hadn’t existed before. It changed the world.

Could something similarly disruptive be brewing in the United States? I’m plugged into a variety of different manufacturing communities, including the FMA; hundreds of young manufacturing business owners; and thousands of my own customers spanning hundreds of industries. If you don’t know it already, let me tell you: People are absolutely clamoring for better manufacturing experiences, and they don’t want it overseas. They want it here. Something has to change.

Envisioning a New Future

There is nothing sacred about where the metal supply chain has landed, at the macro level. We have mills, service centers, contract manufacturers, job shops, and OEMs all adding value—and margin—before metal becomes a product. It’s not margin efficient.

I imagine a different world where metal component manufacturing is bolted directly to mills, River Rouge-style—thousands of lasers, hundreds of thousands of CNC machines of all kinds, taking raw materials and cranking component parts out by the millions. I imagine a new type of business, enabled by easy, fast, cost-effective access to custom parts, to be assembled like Legos to create a world of exciting new products and product companies, right here in the United States. A scaled, vertically integrated giant could revolutionize domestic manufacturing as we know it.

Even in theory, this kind of ecosystem would be impossible without modern technology. The internet is required to allow demand aggregation at a massive scale. Sophisticated software is required to manage incomprehensibly massive high-mix manufacturing systems. Modern CNC equipment, with offline programming, is critical to simplify manufacturing processes. Robotics is required to manage part flow. A mature, API-enabled parcel and freight delivery system is required to bring finished goods to downstream vendors and customers. All of these puzzle pieces exist today, but very few have been combined under one roof in a way that could enable the ecosystem I’m envisioning.

The United States may or may not get here. I’m perfectly confident that, once we’ve arrived, it will work extremely well. But getting there is the challenge. It will take extremely high capex, deep software and operational expertise, regulatory navigation, and plain old-fashioned patient execution over decades. We certainly won’t get there by adding a thin layer of coordination over the existing supply chain.

As grandiose as the vision sounds, it’s really all about enabling a kind of manufacturing that people just can’t get in the United States today. There exists a future where anyone can buy custom manufactured component parts in the U.S., at any quantity, at globally competitive prices, with absurdly fast lead times. Tens of thousands of new businesses will be enabled by that kind of efficient, easy, capital-light access to advanced manufacturing technology. I’d love to live in that world.
