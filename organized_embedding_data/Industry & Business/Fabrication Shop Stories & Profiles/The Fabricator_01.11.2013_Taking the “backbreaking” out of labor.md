# Taking the “backbreaking” out of labor

[TARİH: 01.11.2013 The Fabricator]

Technology Spotlight

3-axis positioner designed to streamline assembly, welding

By Tim Heston, Senior Editor

Working in his garage about 20 years ago, Earl Hagman’s back was killing him, so he did something about it.

The Swedish engineer moved to the U.S. while working for ABB. He then worked at Curtis Instruments, a family-owned business that makes controls for electric vehicles, including power wheelchairs and rehab mobility equipment. It was during his time at Curtis, after a backbreaking weekend of tinkering in his garage, that he developed a work-holding device that could grasp and maneuver workpieces. This was the beginning of Ergotech, a Danbury, Conn., firm that designs workpiece positioning systems for assembly and welding.

Earl Hagman kept hurting his back while tinkering in his garage on the weekends. He built a work positioner to relieve the pain—and ultimately launched a new company.

“One area I always thought was neglected was the factory side [of worker ergonomics],” Hagman said.” “Even today, when you visit big companies and see how things are made, you’re amazed at the problems.”

Tackling the heavy fabrication market, the company knew such shops have unique challenges. “If you are working on a heavy object, and you need to work on the bottom of the workpiece, you need to get a crane, hoist, or other lifting device over to the workstation,” Hagman said. “You use slings, and several people help flip the work over, which is itself not only dangerous, but also very time-consuming.”

This market already has plenty of options for single-axis positioners and similar devices. At the other end of the flexibility and price spectrum is the fully robotic positioner designed as part of a comprehensive automated system. What was missing, Hagman said, was a happy medium between these two extremes.

Hence the company’s Ergo Control®, a 3-axis positioner designed for large workpieces up to 13,200 pounds. Instead of a small pedestal, this product has a large pillar from which protrudes a lateral support. The lift shaft (X axis) moves up and down. The support arm can turn 360 degrees (Y axis), as can the turntable on the arm (Z axis). When the worker completes the assembly or welding job, he can lower the workpiece onto a pallet for transport to downstream operations.

Designed to adapt to a high-product-mix situation, the system’s positioning steps can be programmed during the initial setup using a teach-in method (the user directs the positioner to a certain point for the system to record it, then moves it to the next point, and so on). The control can hold up to 100 programs in memory, though more memory modules can be added for additional storage.

On the positioning support is a baseplate with holes for mounting a range of fixtures, though the company has designed custom baseplates for different situations. Regardless of the fixture used, the unit must be in the “load” or “home” position for the operator to clamp or unclamp the fixture holding the workpiece.

The unit also can be integrated with mats, light curtains, and laser scanners for safe operation. “Still, the positioner’s movement isn’t like a piece of robotics,” Hagman said. “It’s not moving quickly. The system often is moving a multithousand-pound workpiece, so it moves at a very smooth, slow speed.”

The 3-axis positioner can manipulate workpieces that weigh more than 13,000 pounds.

In recent years manufacturers have found that what’s good for ergonomics usually is good for productivity. But it’s not just about making things convenient and accessible. As Hag-man explained, flexible positioning presents more options for the design engineer, because the worker has easier access to more sides of the assembly.

Quality considerations also enter the design equation. For instance, a technician may weld all the joints in one area, retrieve a crane to reposition the work, weld all the joints in another area, and so on. In this arrangement, the welder is laying beads in a concentrated area, which can cause weld shrinkage and distortion. So engineers may make certain sections thicker or alter other workpiece characteristics to mitigate that distortion, or deal with additional manufacturing steps for rework.

Rather than all this, though, the weld sequence may also mitigate that distortion. By welding on one side of a large workpiece, then on an opposite side, then back to the original side, and so on, a welder effectively lowers heat input. From a productivity standpoint, this usually just isn’t practical, because moving a workpiece with a crane repeatedly takes too long. This may not even be physically possible, depending on the workpiece design. But according to Hag-man, with a positioner a technician can perform this weld sequence—welding a little bit on one side, then the other, then the other—without hindering overall productivity.

He added that some companies have eliminated or streamlined certain procedures. Say a large assembly requires tack welding, so that it can withstand the stresses of being lifted by a hoist multiple times during welding and assembly. Knowing that the part will be clamped once in a positioner, designers may choose to instead cut tabs and slots in certain plates, making it a self-locking assembly. This allows technicians to load the assembly onto the positioner’s fixture and weld it in one setup.

“It’s about shortening the [workpiece] manipulation time,” Hagman said. “By eliminating that, you convert that lost time into productive time.”
