I

t’s hard to overstate the impact that collaborative robots (cobots) have had in the North American metal fabricating community. For many shops, working with cobots is their first real experience with flexible automation.

In the future, it will be hard to walk into a fab shop without noticing cobots. Grand View Research believed the market for cobots to be just over $2 billion in 2024, while anticipating a 31.6% compound annual growth between 2025 and 2030. That’s a lot of cobots.

So it stands to reason that more metal fabricators will be joining this trend. These manufacturers continue to struggle to find the right people for their welding operations. Meanwhile, the cobots are becoming easier to program.

Pik Rite, a Lewisburg, Pa.-based maker of agricultural equipment and heavy-duty work trucks, joined the cobot fraternity in 2024 with its first cobot installation. For this being its first foray into automation, the company has found out that even a small investment can produce big dividends.

Making the Most of Welding Talent

Pik Rite got its start in 1986 after Elvin Stoltzfus and Joe Yoder commercialized a machine for harvesting tomatoes. With backgrounds in farming and welding, the duo were perfect candidates to make such an endeavor a success. Those three original Pik Rite machines were the foundation that led to the development of other harvesting equipment for cucumbers, peppers, gourds, zucchini, squash, pumpkins, and carrots.

Toward the late 1990s, Pik Rite wanted to diversify its product offerings. As a result, it began to manufacture commercial waste-handling vacuum tanks and water vacuum tankers. By the 2000s, the company had introduced manure-spreading equipment, leaf collection and compacting units, and dump truck bodies.

Today, Pik Rite sells products all over the world. The small company of about 100 employees has a global reputation for manufacturing quality.

To be able to produce such customized equipment for demanding buyers, Pik Rite relies on a skilled staff of fabricators and welders. Given the nature of the work, welding various material types in many different sizes, the welders in particular need to know what they are doing to avoid unnecessary rework and untimely production delays.

The growth of the company’s fabricating activities and the dearth of welders in the area led Pik Rite to explore the possibility of adding some sort of welding automation to its shop floor. At FABTECH 2023 in Chicago, the team found THG Automation and its cobot welding system.

"We were looking for a solution to increase our efficiency and our throughput," said Caleb Thomas, Pik Rite’s director of technology development, in a video interview. "We want to keep our skilled welders on the things that truly require their skills."

Pik Rite, Lewisburg, Pa., manufactures vegetable harvesting equipment, manure spreaders, vacuum tanks, and heavy-duty truck bodies.

Cobots and Welding

Matt Hendey cut his teeth on industrial robots in the early 2000s. His first experience was with a welding robot. He received a few days of training, and after that, he was on his own.

The ensuing years saw Hendey get more comfortable not only with robotics, but also other areas of the manufacturing business. He spent time in product design, process engineering, production scheduling, and operations management.

Around 2009, he struck out on his own. The Hendey Group (THG) was founded as a product design firm, but one with the expertise to think about design for manufacturability, even taking into account the possible use of automation to streamline production efforts.

During this time, Hendey was still taking on the occasional robotics project. But in 2020, one project altered the course of his business.

"We quoted the project, and we decided to use this new cobot technology," Hendey said. "I’m like, ‘People are welding with these cobots, and this company doesn’t need anything super complicated. They needed something simple, with a small footprint, easy to deploy, and easy to program."

A few months later, he received a phone call while on location for another customer, and he learned that the company was ready to issue a purchase order for the cobots. Up until that point, Hendey had never even touched one.

"With my background in robotics, I wasn’t too worried about it," he said. Pairing a Universal Robots robot with a Fronius welding power source proved to be a combination that worked well, especially on aluminum.

THG received that initial order in June 2020. Realizing that it made more sense to have a separate company focus on these types of cobot installations, Hendey launched THG Automation. By September 2020, the company was incorporated, and the website and social media were up and running.

When Pik Rite realized that it wanted its welding automation to tackle longer parts, it added a linear rail to the cobot welding table.

Enter the Cobot at Pik Rite

Being new to automation, Pik Rite had some reservations. Would employees see the cobot welding system as something that would take jobs? Would it be user-friendly? Would welders warm up to the technology enough to make the investment worthwhile?

"They were concerned about the training," Hendey said. "So it took them a little time to get through that, learn to get comfortable, and make this work for them."

Like most cobot systems, the THG Automation system has a free drive button that allows the operator to move the cobot manually to the start of the weld. The rest of the programming for the system offers a bit more than other cobot systems, according to Hendey.

Some cobots can perform only linear welds, and while that might be fine for some applications, it really limits their ability to tackle the variety of jobs with which welders are often tasked. Using the THG Automation system’s URCap software, the operator can program actions in between the beginning and the start of a weld.

"What we’ve done makes it so much easier for our customers to do more advanced stuff if they choose to," said Hendey, referencing the cobot’s ability to work with welding positioners and rotational devices, should a company like Pik Rite choose to add that type of equipment in the future.

The cobot welding system did a good job of taking on tasks at Pik Rite that were repetitive and not too complex. But after working with it for several weeks, the company realized that it needed something to accommodate larger parts with similar characteristics.

Instead of having to purchase a new larger setup, THG Automation added a linear rail to the existing installation. Now the cobot can travel along a linear axis to weld joints longer than a few inches.

"There’s still a big need for skilled welders, and there’s also a need for automation," Thomas said. "They work hand in hand beautifully."

With the addition of the rail, Hendey said that training was slightly different for the Pik Rite team. Obviously, they had to get familiar with the new capabilities of the cobot welding system, but they also had to think about how the system could be used. It wasn’t just about adding longer parts to the list of jobs for the automated system. Perhaps a job could be staged so that the robot could do different welds at three stations, with an operator handling the movement of the workpiece from one station to the next.

"We just need to think about our whole process and improve it around a great piece of technology that allows us to be more efficient," Thomas added.

Words of Advice

For other metal fabricators who haven’t headed out on their own automation journey, Hendey recommends that they start small, like Pik Rite. They just need to start welding with a cobot and get used to programming and running it. Once they understand the cobot welding system’s capabilities, they can then think about possibly moving on from just a table-based unit or perhaps adding more cobots.

"If someone doesn’t have any experience with robotic automation, the worst thing you should do is start overcomplicating it," Hendey said.

The worry is that management might want to include more capabilities for an initial welding automation investment to help justify the purchase. Hendy said that this adds more complexity to an equipment installation that already has enough technical and cultural barriers to overcome.

Pik Rite started small and quickly learned that it was ready for more. Even small steps start you down your most important journeys—something more small and medium-sized shops will realize as they start to look at adding welding automation to their shop floors.
