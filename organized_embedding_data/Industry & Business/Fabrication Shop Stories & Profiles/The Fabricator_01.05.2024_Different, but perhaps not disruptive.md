# Different, but perhaps not disruptive

[TARİH: 01.05.2024 The Fabricator]

Tech Talk

What software automation means for custom fabrication

By

Caleb Chamberlain

L

ast February I boarded an airplane and left Utah in the middle of a snowstorm. Four hours later, we touched down in clear, warm weather in Tampa, Fla. The Fabricators and Manufacturers Association (FMA) held its annual meeting in Clearwater, at a hotel right on the white sand beach.

Regrettably, I didn’t set foot on the beaches. Instead, I met a lot of great people and had many interesting conversations throughout the week and into the evenings. One conversation in particular galvanized a lot of thought. Just before the conference ended, I sat down with Tim Heston and Dan Davis for an interview with The Fabricator Podcast. Among other things, they asked whether what we are doing at OSH Cut (my fabrication company) is "disruptive." I told them no, but I’ve thought a lot about it since then. Could our software technology disrupt sheet metal manufacturing?

No Sales Staff, Just Software

If you haven’t been following my monthly column, here’s some context. My brother and I started OSH Cut in 2018. We are an on-demand, online sheet metal manufacturing company. We use custom software to automate quoting, design for manufacturability, and online ordering, allowing us to analyze and quote a little over 12,000 parts per week as of 2024. When customers upload 3D models of their parts, our system unfolds them to create the flat patterns, then simulates the bending process right in the web browser for the customer to see. If there are problems, our software tells the customer right away by showing how the parts will form (or can’t be formed) on our tools. By the time customers submit their orders, we already know we can make them.

People upload parts and tinker all the time, so while we quote a lot of parts, in the end we make around 2,000 unique parts of varying quantities each week. Our service is narrow—we offer blanking and bending on a press brake and (as of this year) tube cutting—but for our customers, the service is extremely fast, economical, and easy to use. We don’t do complex custom work at all, and so far our service is more transactional than relationship-based. In fact, we have zero marketing, sales, quoting, or engineering staff. Instead, we have a software team.

About Disruption

OSH Cut is a different kind of business, but is it

disruptive?

That’s a loaded term. It conjures imagery of arrogant technophiles sauntering into an industry, breaking and remaking it, then exiting five or 10 years later with gold-lined pockets. Manufacturing has always been about providing real, sustained physical value. Manufacturers’ "long-haul thinking" is inimical to the scale-and-exit strategy sometimes trumpeted by the world’s self-proclaimed disrupters. So, when people wonder if our business model is disruptive, it makes me wary.

Even so, it’s a good question. History is littered with highly successful and admired companies that faded as the world changed around them. Within my lifetime, I’ve seen the rise and fall of countless companies both inside and outside of tech: AOL, Yahoo, Circuit City, Blockbuster, Polaroid, Compaq, Palm, iomeg—the list is endless. Technology’s relentless pace created all-stars and then put them out of their misery barely years later. Businesses come and go faster now than ever before.

Manufacturers are in some ways shielded from the chaos. There are only so many ways to make parts out of metal, and new technologies—like metal 3D printing—aren’t even remotely cost-competitive compared to more traditional processes. If that ever changes, fabricators are a single machine purchase away from taking advantage. Widespread use of fiber lasers, cobots, modern brakes, and other automation indicates that manufacturers are plugged in and evolving with technology.

Khanchit Khirisutchalual/ iStock / Getty Images Plus

To me, software automation isn’t so much a disruptive tech as it is the next logical step for manufacturing. Fabricators understand waste and how to fix it. Software really just adds new, powerful tools to the toolbox.

That said, in our chaotic, tech-fueled environment, survival might depend on understanding technological trends and getting onboard quickly. The problem with disruptive tech is how irrelevant it might look at first. Disruptive technologies democratize access, offering products or services that are more convenient, affordable, or efficient. They often start by targeting niche markets or overlooked segments, gradually gaining momentum until they become mainstream. This gradual ascent can catch established players off guard, as they dismiss the new technology until it’s too late to catch up.

In a way, that might describe what OSH Cut and a few other companies are doing right now. We use our software expertise to streamline work that many shops don’t want, like one-offs that probably won’t convert to production. It’s an underserved niche. Our limited scope means we probably haven’t yet made a dent in traditional fabricators’ bottom lines. But I can say without guile that we’ll be a very different kind of company in 10 years, serving a much broader market.

About Scope

In one sense, metal fabrication is a collection of manufacturing technologies, not an industry. The equipment and expertise required to make customized, safe pressure vessels is very different from what it takes to make server racks, which is itself extremely different from making medical devices or I-beams or generator enclosures or whatever.

A fabricator that broadens its scope too much risks becoming mediocre at everything instead of great at a few things. So whatever OSH Cut does in the future, we can’t magically use tech to absorb the specialized knowledge fabricators have earned over decades of hard work, across diverse industries. We’d die of indigestion.

Intersection of Skills

So again, is OSH Cut disruptive? My answer is still mostly no. Our business model isn’t about to transform sheet metal manufacturing on its own. OSH Cut and others might challenge businesses that live in our space and haven’t streamlined quoting, ordering, and production in the same way. But even for our direct competitors, that problem might be resolved by a single third-party software license. Outside the on-demand space, I’m fairly confident we aren’t going to build bridges, medical devices, and pressure vessels.

That isn’t to say fabricators can rest easy. If customized tech can improve your customers’ experience, and you aren’t offering it, then someone else might come along and do it in your place. It might be a company that doesn’t even exist yet.

To me, software automation isn’t so much a disruptive tech as it is the next logical step for manufacturing. Fabricators understand waste and how to fix it. Software really just adds new, powerful tools to the toolbox. OSH Cut isn’t unique because we are better at making metal than other shops. We’ve only been around for five years—how could we be? We also aren’t the best software company in the world. But it’s the intersection of those skill sets that has allowed us to optimize for our market niche: We can make great software, designed specifically to solve our particular problems.

I mentioned earlier that we don’t have quoting staff, but we have a software team. I wonder if that’s a shift that will become more common, especially as younger generations take over. Perhaps it’ll become normal for fabricators to have in-house software expertise.

As I think about disruptive technologies, I keep going back to the FMA conference at Clearwater Beach. This was my first time attending, and I was blown away by the depth of experience represented—seasoned fabricators with decades of experience, gathered together to forge connections, solve problems, and plan for the future. I was surprised to find that people were aware of what we are doing at OSH Cut, and humbled that they acted far more interested than threatened. The fabricators I met have spent their careers solving the hardest kinds of problems. None of them are blind to the opportunities and potential threats posed by technology.

In the face of that experience, it would take a special kind of hubris to saunter in and think that a few years of software development was going to somehow upheave the industry. Software might make things a lot easier and a lot more accessible, but the parts we make are still cut on a laser and formed on a press brake, just like the shop down the road.
