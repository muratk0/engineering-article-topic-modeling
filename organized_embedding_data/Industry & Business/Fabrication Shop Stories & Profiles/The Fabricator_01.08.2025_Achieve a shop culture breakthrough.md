M

etal fabrication technology marches forward with automated and even autonomous systems. Machine controls have become more user-friendly than ever, and software is starting to help even the most high-product-mix operation transform data into actionable information. At the same time, with all the tariff talk and political unrest, uncertainty pervades the global economy.

Within this complex environment, one challenge has remained, backed up by survey after survey, study after study, and countless anecdotes: At most shops, the skilled labor shortage remains the chief concern.

If we could just hire good people, delivery and quality performance would improve, and the operational ship could be righted

.

Some blame manufacturing’s image problem. Others blame worker pay gaps. Many take the "these kids today" tact, blame society, and opine for the days when people knew how to put in an honest day’s work.

Recently, The Fabricator spoke with Dan Burgos, the founder and president of Alphanova Consulting, a firm that focuses on mid-market manufacturers. Burgos has spoken at FABTECH and numerous other manufacturing conferences.

He approaches the issue with direct practicality, detailing several disciplines for strong manufacturing management. Implementing these isn’t rocket science, but success doesn’t happen with a flip of a switch either. It takes time, patience, persistence, and empathy. Eventually, though, the breakthrough will happen.

A Good Culture Doesn’t Build Itself

"If you’re not intentional about creating an environment where people want to work, then the culture is going to build itself."

Burgos made this comment in context of hiring challenges typical in metal fabrication and other manufacturing specialties. With the never-ending skilled labor shortage, shop owners and hiring managers find people who might not have experience (because so few do) but at least seem to exhibit a good work ethic.

The new hire is onboarded and training commences—but then problems arise. Conflicts and performance issues ensue, and the new hire leaves. Turns out, that person just wasn’t a "good fit" for the culture. And so, the manager pins it on what’s thought to be an immutable reality:

People with a good work ethic are just hard to find

.

As Burgos explained, this might be a good time for a fab shop to look inward. A custom fabricator might have poor operational performance, like on-time delivery of 80% or even less (which, according to KPI surveys from the Fabricators and Manufacturers Association, isn’t all that unusual).

Working in these environments certainly requires serious effort. Many might take pride in bending over backward for customers, doing whatever it takes to get a job done. "People get a rush out of it," Burgos said. "We have a name for it. It’s called the ‘hero syndrome,’ an addiction to heroic recovery. You work through the night to get an order out. The customer says, ‘Oh, you’re the best! That was amazing!’ That makes you feel good. Now, imagine you have an organization full of people who think this mindset is normal. It becomes a habit, part of day-to-day operations. They think there’s no problem. That’s where we need to start. We need to establish that this is a problem. People can burn out."

This can lead to high turnover, and it can also severely limit a company’s growth potential. "Shop owners have told me, ‘I don’t want to grow anymore. It’s too painful.’ Well, it’s too painful because of the way you’re trying to grow. It’s not the optimal way."

It’s a vicious cycle. Heroic employees help the fab shop deliver. Customers are impressed and so order more. That requires more heroic effort to "muscle" jobs through. Eventually, the shop’s heroes leave, so it’s left with apathetic employees. Some don’t care enough to learn, while others—including those who might be experienced and knowledgeable about specific processes or machines—don’t care enough to share what they know.

Five Disciplines of Strong Manufacturing Management

This, Burgos said, dovetails into some fundamentals that help break the cycle. He boiled the concepts down to five disciplines, each of which help build strong processes and standardized procedures.

1. Establish the right metrics.

"If you don’t measure, you can’t improve," he said. "And you need to measure the right things. Most important, you need your measurements to define what success means."

Specifics depend on the operation, but the focus should be on what makes a business sustainable and profitable. A shop might measure machine uptime, which is great, but if they don’t track rework, they might be putting those machine uptime metrics in the wrong context. Sure, the machine is always producing—but considering all the rework, it’s not always producing good parts.

2. Embrace problem-solving.

Consider that machine producing lots of parts that need to be reworked. Why? "Many just go by a gut feel," Burgos said. "They think something happened for a specific reason, but there’s no formal process, no root-cause analysis that breaks down the issue."

3. Be hands-on.

"Leaders tend to be reactive. They’re not involved, and they’re not hands-on. They just allow issues to happen, and they’re not on the floor, watching to understand what the real issues are."

This harkens back to an adage the late Dick Kallage, a longtime consultant who worked with the Fabricators and Manufacturers Association, used to say:

Truth resides on the shop floor

. Yes, leaders need to be working "on" the business, not "in" the business. But this doesn’t mean leaders should distance themselves from the actual operation. If leaders don’t witness "the truth"—what’s really happening in the shop—they’ll have a harder time effectively working "on" the business.

Dan Burgos is the founder and president of Alphanova Consulting. He has more than a decade of experience in operations and materials management, supply chain, and continuous improvement

.

4. Use data to make decisions.

You have the metrics. You embrace problem-solving, and you’re hands-on—that is, you know the issues. Now, what are the root causes?

At this point, many jump to conclusions too quickly. "They then think, if I can remove this obstacle, the problem goes away. And it doesn’t. Nowadays, we tend to make decisions without data, without really investigating and being inquisitive," Burgos said. "We go on opinions and speculation."

5. Embrace process management.

At many custom fabricators and other job shops, owners and managers tend to think that the product mix is just too variable to establish standard processes. As Burgos explained, "I sometimes hear, ‘We don’t make widgets. We don’t make the same thing over and over. We can’t standardize.’ I always tell people to look at law enforcement. Every case and every situation they handle is different. Do you think they don’t have standard processes? If they didn’t have standardized procedures, they wouldn’t be able to complete an investigation. That analogy translates over to high-product-mix production."

Burgos described one instance in which a shop, after analyzing its product mix, dedicated highly variable orders into a separate value stream. This move reduced chaos in the rest of the plant and allowed the operation to establish specific procedures for each value stream.

He added that whether this works or not depends on the operation. "Regardless, the key is to establish a process management mentality, where you try to develop standard procedures that span across different workers, shifts, and plants."

Process development is never a one-time event. It’s part of continuous improvement. "Be ready," Burgos said. "Mistakes will happen. You’ll have disagreements. You need to engage your best people until you reach a consensus, where most agree that a certain procedure is the best way you know how to do it. It’s a continuous cycle. You continue to refine it to the point where reexamining and improving processes just becomes second nature to everyone involved."

Align Incentives

These fundamentals provide a foundation that helps leaders build a shop culture that, ideally, should make hiring a lot easier. But for a culture change to truly be successful, however, metrics must align employee incentives with business success.

Burgos recalled a manufacturer that, beyond regular overtime pay, handed out bonuses based on the number of hours worked. The more hours people worked, the higher their bonuses were.

A shop owner might implement this structure simply because "muscling jobs through" got the fab shop off the ground, out of the garage, and into a plant with thousands of square feet of production space. The "heroes" in the early days (some of whom might have had equity in the business) did what it took to get a job done, and the company had come a long way because of it. More hours worked, the thinking went, meant more dedication.

"Think about this, though," Burgos said. "This sounds backward, doesn’t it? It implies, ‘The longer I take to do something, the more you’ll pay me.’" In this situation, changing the bonus structure helped align incentives. Ultimately, bonuses were tied to operational results, including safety, quality, delivery, and cost.

Embrace Change

Managing by process and making decisions on good data can help control the chaos, and aligning incentives helps people buy in to change. But as Burgos explained, some will continue to resist.

The feelings can go deep. A skilled machine operator might have avoided layoffs at a previous employer, simply because he knew how to run that machine better than anyone. His skill was his job security, and he wasn’t about to share it. Others simply grew comfortable with the status quo. They might have liked how things were (remember those heroes who enjoyed the rush). Whatever the reason, people resist change.

Here, Burgos recommended a technique called

self-discovery

. "Many take a top-down approach and try to direct people’s action. The effectiveness can be minimal. There’s a big difference between being told the right answer and having a journey of self-discovery, so you learn things on your own."

For example, instead of telling someone they need to break down the press brake multiple times to process the schedule and ensure on-time delivery—a directive—the conversation could start by asking questions. "‘What do you think happens at customer plants, where they’re complaining about late orders? How can we get these parts to shipping sooner, so they’re not late?’" They’ll then discover the right answer on their own."

Burgos added, "Cultural transformation takes high levels of psychological and emotional stamina. You’ll experience all sorts of resisting behavior: denial, anger, rejection. As a leader, you need to power through all that with care, empathy, and positivity."

Some might not change, and hard decisions will have to be made. "Quite often, though, people who continue to resist tend to leave the company," Burgos said. Where most see positive change, a few might see a company culture they didn’t sign up for.

"All this takes patience and persistence," Burgos said. "It’s going to take time. But eventually, the breakthrough of culture change will happen."
