# How to become an employer of choice

[TARİH: 01.12.2024 The Fabricator]

Speakers and panelists address the industry’s greatest challenge

By

Tim Heston

This year’s FABTECH panel on skilled labor included individuals from welding education, a professional association, as well as tube and precision sheet metal fabrication. Images: Butler Photography

A

drian Little runs JETechnology Solutions, central Florida fabricator of customized fall protection equipment in the aircraft maintenance arena. During the opening FABx Tech Talks at FABTECH 2024, he posed a question: "What’s the No. 1 challenge you have?" The audience responded, almost in unison: "People!" "Good," Little said. "I believe you’re in the right spot."

Little runs a high-mix, low-volume shop, the type most North American metal fabricators share—and one that can be extremely difficult to manage, especially during disruptive times. As Little told the crowd, "A few years ago, business was horrible. I found out my office manager was arrested [for conduct outside work]. I was pulling levers and pushing buttons just trying to get anything to work. I gave out bonuses, PTO, and spent money I didn’t have. The environment could have been a reality show on AMC."

His story of transformation reflected a theme that threaded itself through the tradeshow and conference: finding, training, retaining, and fostering rewarding careers for the next-generation employee. It’s a tall, yet vital, order. The best technology in the world can’t help a company with disengaged employees on the front lines and incompetence at the helm. To address this, several presentations at this year’s FABTECH brought attendees through three ingredients—culture, communication, and transparency—that, when mixed together just right, can help fabricators become an employer of choice.

Good Culture

Everyone knows a healthy shop culture starts at the top. Even so, many can become blinded by busyness and endless firefighting that they fail to realize the problem isn’t with everyone around them; it’s with the person in the mirror. Little learned this in a reality-show-like way.

"I had a leadership coach, and after looking at the company financials, he said, ‘If I were you, I’d fire myself!’ And guess what? He fired me. I was paying him, and he fired me. From that point, I knew I had to get down to basics."

Those basics included people, processes, and technology—in that order—and required some hard decisions, especially regarding people. "First, I knew I had to replace my operations manager. I whittled it down to three people, two of whom I knew I could do the job, and one person who had less technical experience but who I felt was a great leader. I chose the better leader. That in turn started it all."

How did he know that person would be a good leader? First, Little said, was his communication skills—especially when it comes to listening. A top-down, intimidating leader makes people on the front lines disengage and not care, which in turn makes even the best processes and manufacturing technology ineffective.

"I had to define our culture," Little said. "What kind of person would be able to succeed in our environment? We needed the right people on the right teams. And we needed to make sure our employees could be independent. They needed to be comfortable making decisions."

Processes were standardized and everyone was trained, but standard, documented processes couldn’t account for everything. If people on the front lines continually waited to do only what their supervisor told them to do, nothing would get out the door. Documented processes defined what decisions they could and couldn’t make, but Little knew they needed to make those decisions without fearing retribution at the same time. This fact drove his choice for a new operations manager.

"We live in a constantly changing world. New information and tools are available all the time. How do you process it all? You do it by having people work independently and interdependently." That is, people are empowered to make decisions independently, yet also collaborate when needed and recognize the interdependency of every action taken in the shop, up and down the value chain.

"Finally, we identify our superpower of thinking positively," Little said. "That’s the one thing we can control in life: our attitude, how we look at and approach life—with exuberance."

Good Communication and Understanding

Lori Tapani is co-president of Wyoming Machine, a custom metal fabricator in rural Minnesota about 30 miles northeast of the Twin Cities. In recent years, her shop has reached out in various ways to local youth and children. She recalled showing a laser-cut and formed wand to a troupe of Girl Scouts. "We talked about different kinds of metals, magnetic properties, and put sheet metal fabrication in a light that can really connect with kids, even in elementary school."

Tapani recounted the story during a FABTECH panel discussion about attracting and retaining the next-generation employee. Not surprisingly, the panel was one of the best attended of the entire show, and as Tapani described the events that help connect metal fabrication with schoolchildren—most of whom probably didn’t know metal fabrication even existed before their visit—people in the audience smiled and nodded.

"Welding is always a big hit. We laser-cut hand mirrors and put welding glass in them, so they can come right into the welding booth and observe the process. And with high schoolers, we give them a tour of our entire process, from beginning to end. They get to meet our engineers, talk about what they do in CAD, see the computer systems, and move through the entire facility.

"These tours are also a great retention tool for current employees," Tapani continued. "Team members really love having students visit and having a chance to present what they do. It adds a big dose of happiness to their work."

Fellow panelist Angela Phillips, CEO of Phillips Tube Group in Middletown, Ohio, described a similar event in which the fabricator hosted more than 100 students from the local middle schools. The company also hosts weeklong events for teachers, introducing them to the concepts of tube fabrication and the knowledge and skills that employees use.

She added that such events mitigate the scrubbing of vocational education from middle and high schools—a fact that leaves many recent grads wondering what to do next. "Statistics from our local high school show that 22% of high school graduates had a post-high school plan, be it college, going into the military, or something else," she said. "Only 22% of them have a plan. That’s terrifying. What are the remaining 78% of students going to do?"

Panelists emphasized that events that host schools should help make connections. Student groups touring passively through a shop is better than having no event at all, but engaging them is even better.

"When we do our tours for high schoolers, students typically make products they can take home with them," Tapani said. "We often make a desktop phone holder that can be hand-formed. They see it being drawn in engineering; then they see how we cut it on the laser. They see the blueprint, and they work with one of our employees, who show them how to fold them up, creating something they can take home and use."

When these students need a place to put their phone, they’ll see the holder, think of sheet metal and all the effort that went into making that simple object—the microtabs that allowed for hand forming, the cuts that created the perfect edge. Will they pursue a career in precision sheet metal? Maybe not, but at the very least, they won’t take sheet metal—and manufacturing in general—for granted.

After hearing Tapani’s story about cell phone holders, fellow panelist Michael Cole chimed in with another anecdote. He’s regional campus president at StrataTech Education Group, which owns Tulsa Welding School (TWS), one of the largest trade schools in the country. In a lobby at TWS stands a large-scale, formed, and welded imperial walker.

"I point out the AT-AT and say, ‘Isn’t that cool?’" He joked that, yes, he knows the technical name from "Star Wars," a fact that shows "what I thought was cool. But we can’t focus on the things we thought were cool. Some students today have no idea what an AT-AT is. The cell phone is what’s cool now. It’s important to spend the time to really know the folks coming to work for us."

Panel moderator Max Ceron, executive director of Canada’s CWB Group (previously known as the Canadian Welding Bureau), followed this up with a poignant question: "Is it important that employees learn your life, or you learn theirs? We in the older generation often expect them to come to us rather than us coming to them."

Tapani commented that when she started at Wyoming Machine 30 years ago, most job applicants wanted a full-time job—and the more hours the better. Many also expected a career behind a welding hood or in front of a machine, not in idea generation and communication. Soft skills weren’t front and center.

"When I started here, I had experience as a CPA, and I recall being part of multifunction teams that worked together to hash out problems. Everyone had input. Well, when I started [at Wyoming Machine], we didn’t have the same skill set. We’d sit down in a conference room to discuss a problem, and someone would say, ‘Well, John doesn’t know what he’s doing,’ and that was the end of the discussion.

"Things have changed now. There’s value in teamwork. You’re coming together, sharing your values. Even before the Millennials and Gen Zs started working for us, we were already working more collaboratively. We’ve determined working together as a team is much better than the top-down approach.

"Now, flexibility seems to be key," Tapani continued. "Maybe not everyone needs to work the same hours or as many hours."

Adrian Little of JETechnology Solutions described how his company underwent a "reset" by going back to the basics; keeping things simple; and focusing on people, processes, and technology.

Phillips concurred, explaining that youth graduating from school today think differently about careers and, more broadly, how they want to spend their time. "When we were young, it was all about that 40-hour work week," she said. "How can you buy that car and do the things you want to do? That’s no longer the drive. Many apply for a job that’s 20 hours a week, then do something else with the rest of their time … I think this represents a major cultural and mindset shift in manufacturing.

We have to be OK with part-timers coming to work for us, even though it’s not a model we’re used to. Job flexibility is going to be critical as we move into the next decade. We need to find ways to make that work."

Ceron agreed, reiterating just how different many young people today are from those just a decade ago. "When I was young welder, we started on night or weekend shifts, and you worked your way up to those day shifts, the gravy hours. Now, when I teach, I bring up the phrase ‘night shift,’ and they like that. This gets into communicating expectations, about whether the company should accommodate the employee rather than the employee accommodating the company."

Panelists agreed that there’s a way to meet in the middle. Manufacturing requires certain people to be present at certain times, so flexible work hours aren’t so easy to accommodate. But as the skilled labor issues persist, fabricators are starting to think outside the box about who does what and who needs to be on the floor when.

This becomes especially important as skilled tradespeople retire, exacerbating the vast productivity gap between veterans and young people entering the field. Skills like welding simply take time to learn.

"Both sides need to recognize the experience gap," Cole said. "We’re trying to replace people who have done a job between 20 and 40 years with someone who’s been doing it for 12 months. You need to realize that their efficiency might not be what you’re used to getting."

At the same time, the new generation is more comfortable with technology and touchscreens, which in turn smooths the adoption of ever-changing equipment and software. As panelists described, shops need veterans to mentor the young, but they also need to cast a wide net to attract the next generation’s best. It’s a balancing act that represents metal fabrication’s greatest challenge.

Full Transparency

During the past year, this magazine reported on one fabricator—Kansas-based Vortex Companies—that implemented pay transparency, with posted pay ranges correlating with every shop floor position. And this year, the idea of pay transparency made its first significant appearance at a FABTECH skilled labor panel.

"At CWB, we went through a full pay transparency project," Ceron said. "We state the pay ranges everyone is in. This is what many of today’s youth expect—pay transparency in the workplace. I didn’t grow up with that. I grew up in shops where I was told, ‘This is what you make and don’t tell anyone.’ But of course, the second you walk out of the office, you tell everyone. There’s a threat: Don’t talk to your fellow worker about pay. But that already creates a toxic environment."

Transitioning to pay transparency can be a bumpy road, Ceron said, especially as inflation and stringent competition for workers has pushed starting pay higher. "We’re going through this right now," he said. "A big part of it is defining a career pathway so everyone is clear about what their path could look like. Be open about it. At the end of the day, you want to convey that you want everyone to be more successful as the company gets more successful."

To be sure, pay transparency might not work everywhere. That said, the fact that the idea is starting to be discussed—at the industry’s largest North American event, no less—does signal some potentially significant change on the horizon when it comes to workplace transparency in general.

Cole added that transparency is a two-way street. Fundamentals like character, work ethic, training, job performance, and (especially) attendance still matter. "At Tulsa Welding School, we make sure the focus on attendance is there. We hold our students accountable to show up on time, and throughout the day, we want them working in their welding booth."

He also said that the results—what fabricators need to accomplish to grow and remain competitive—can’t change. "But I do think the way you go after the results and accomplish the organization’s mission has to change. You can’t let your results suffer, and so you adapt."
