fter spending weeks talking with participants in this year’s FAB 40—a list of some of the largest contract metal fabricators in the country—I came to an odd realization: Contract fabrication really isn’t a unified business sector full of direct competitors, all fighting it out for market share. There’s plenty of market share competition, of course, but zoom out and you see more nuance. In truth, metal fabrication is a collection of manufacturing technologies—laser cutting, punching, bending, welding—that serve a variety of customers in different businesses. The product- and customer-mix variations are infinite, as are the ways operations serve manufacturing supply chains.

To be clear, many fab shops begin in similar places. In a garage or rented facility, entrepreneurs start with a welding cell, their first press brake, perhaps a small plasma table. As they grow, they start to differentiate. They develop value streams or even "mini factories" devoted to a specific range of customers, sectors, or part geometries. The more they become "plugged in" to what customers produce, the more they grow.

Growth often stalls, perhaps around $10 million or $20 million in annual sales, depending on the kind of work a fabricator does. Sometimes it’s intentional. Owners or key stakeholders might simply like managing a smaller organization. Other times, market forces cause revenue to hit a ceiling. New opportunities require such immense scale that they exceed a fabricator’s capability to deliver without disrupting work from its existing core customer base.

Some break through that growth barrier through organic growth, others through acquisition, and many use a combination of both. Regardless of the strategy, long-term customer partnerships usually reside at the center of it. Fabricators nurture those partnerships and work to protect them.

Fabricators know they can’t be everything to everyone. This creates an industry of "coopetition" at different levels of the supply chain: among custom and contract fabricators, service centers, and OEMs. Service centers have performed cutting and other first-step fabrication processes for years. Some OEMs decide to bring more fabrication in-house. Fabricators send each other jobs to work through demand peaks and ensure customer promises are kept.

Meanwhile, "web shops" like OSH Cut, SendCutSend, Fabworks, and Proto Labs offer the ability to upload files and receive an immediate quote. Xometry, which works with a wide network of suppliers, does this as well. If a core customer requests a prototype or low-quantity job, a fabricator might outsource the work—either to these web shops that automate the front end, or to a shop they know in the area. Coopetition really makes metal fabrication tick.

Traditional competition is still fierce, of course. Plenty of fabricators play in similar or identical markets (like, of course, data centers and all things related to AI). An OEM that brings metal fabrication in-house might decide to sell excess capacity and start a contract fabrication division. In fact, some manufacturers intentionally grow with a diverse range of product lines and custom work.

One thread ties this diverse industry together: a genuine curiosity and shared passion for manufacturing, including its people and the technology they use—at least, that’s the ideal.

The manufacturing landscape is changing. Metal fabrication shop owners are retiring, and not everyone has an exit strategy. So, they look to outside investors—and there are plenty of them, especially now.

They’re reading the tea leaves. They know how AI could utterly change some sectors of the economy, especially software.

At one point, "digital platforms reshaped industries, software firms eclipsed manufacturers, and competitive advantage appeared increasingly detached from physical constraints. That worldview … is now breaking down. Software remains essential, but it is no longer scarce. Artificial intelligence has turned software into an abundant input. … What is becoming scarce again is hardware—broadly defined to include infrastructure, energy systems, materials, manufacturing capacity, transport and supply chains."

So wrote Paul Achleitner, a strategic adviser to Divergent Technologies, in The Economist back in March. His insight rings even truer today, with the Iran war and all the supply chain and input-cost uncertainties that go with it.

Investors read that and similar analyses, see the data center boom and its potentially incredible runway for growth, then turn to industries like metal fabrication. Thing is, if they’re looking for a quick, easy return on investment, manufacturing isn’t an ideal place to be. Machines run off software, but the products they produce aren’t 1s and 0s. Not every step can be automated or perfectly standardized.

Design for manufacturability can make all the difference—but not when red tape and bureaucracy make even minor design changes difficult. And again, every fabricator is unique, so rolling them up into one larger entity isn’t as common as you might think. (Cadrex Manufacturing Solutions, a combination of 11 previously independent companies, is one major exception.)

Regardless, scaling up isn’t a straightforward affair, which is what makes those on the FAB 40 list so significant.

If investors enter manufacturing with that genuine engagement, the story changes. Coming into this business with eyes wide open to the challenges, but also with a core belief in manufacturing’s importance and a deep appreciation for the people this industry employs, an investor can go far.

As Achleitner concluded, "The next era of global competition will be decided not by who writes the best code, but by who can build, power, and supply the physical systems on which the digital world ultimately depends."

Considering this, metal fabrication—with its horizontal reach and incredibly diverse business-model possibilities—is one of those sectors sitting at the heart of the global economy’s next wave of innovation. Competition is tight; challenges are immense. But for metal fabricators, the potential opportunities are incredibly exciting.
