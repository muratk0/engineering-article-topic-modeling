s Robert Kamphuis, chairman, president, and CEO, MEC Inc., addressed the challenges of running a fabrication company with more than $300 million in annual revenues, I heard echoes of prior conversations I have had with metal fabricators. Kamphuis wasn’t talking about "shifting paradigms" or "capitalizing on synergies"; he was focusing on the need to streamline processes and maintain fabricating flexibility. He was talking like the owner of a 25-person shop.

Well, that’s not the case. MEC, which rhymes with Shrek, is a manufacturing behemoth that is making a lot of green. It has 17 locations, approximately 2,000 employees, and reported 2014 revenues of $335 million. MEC has absorbed another fabricator that was almost as large as it was at the time of the acquisition three years ago and has expanded its manufacturing footprint in Virginia. MEC’s growth is a good thing because it shows that the model that makes small to medium-sized shops prized members of a community is scalable. MEC’s growth is not like that of a company run by a private equity firm, which buys into a company, expands to boost top-line growth, squeezes the business until the last bit of perceived fat is drained from it, and sells it to the highest bidder for a nice profit. MEC is expanding its business with current customers, branching out into new geographic areas, and acquiring other metal fabricating operations that have solid financials and customers that complement MEC’s current lineup. The goal is to fill capacity and look for new business that might cause the fabricator to invest in new capacity. An expanding fabricating operation keeps employees working and provides for growth opportunities.

It also should be noted that Kamphuis and his staff make these decisions with MEC employees in mind. They also happen to be the company’s shareholders. Last year was the 30th anniversary of MEC becoming an employee-owned company.

This is all great news for MEC and its shareholders, but why should other fabricators care? Large companies tend to grab the attention of those who make policy, or who at least influence the policymakers. Large companies employ large amounts of people, and those people represent a large amount of potential voters.

I hear the complaints often enough about how political leaders take small and medium-sized fab shops for granted. They make policy aimed to help manufacturing, but those actions rarely result in true benefits for metal fabricators. In fact, many see it as just another example of how the federal government finds a way to spend more money to appease a certain voting bloc.

A fabricator with a lot of employees and spread out geographically is more likely to have substantive conversation with policymakers because that organization has gravitas. It also can speak for an entire industry, which happens to be a significant contributor to the U.S.’s economic well-being.

Let’s face it. Many people, let alone politicians, don’t even know what metal fabricating is. Here’s one thing they can understand: The fabricated metal product manufacturing sector (defined as North American Industry Classification System code 332) contributed $146.2 billion in real manufacturing gross domestic product in 2014, according to the Bureau of Economic Analysis. That represents about 7 percent of all manufacturing GDP. It’s also a sector that has shown consistent growth since the end of the Great Recession, reaching levels unmatched over the last 20 years.

Using 2012 data, the Manufacturing Extension Partnership reported that the sector has about 58,000 manufacturing establishments in the sector, representing about 1.4 million workers. Those are attention-grabbing numbers.

This is all great news for MEC and its shareholders, but why should other fabricators care? Large companies tend to grab the attention of those who make policy, or who at least influence the policymakers.

MEC could easily shrink from the spotlight and go about its business without scrutiny from the outside world, but it chooses not to. It’s confident in its role as a provider of manufacturing expertise, quality product creation, and even supply chain management, and it is a proud supporter of the local community and regional educational efforts. It’s topped the FAB 40 list of successful U.S. fabricating companies for five consecutive years now and has embraced the role of being a large fish in a pool filled with very talented competitors, albeit on a smaller scale.

Finally, MEC’s success is an inspiration to all shops. A fabricator once told me that he had a moral obligation to continue expanding the business because it provided more opportunity for employees to grow and increase their compensation. It’s a noble gesture with a foundation of truth to it. Larger companies simply have more opportunity for career development for their employees.

Metal fabricating is a big business that deserves to be recognized. Big businesses increase the chance of that happening.
