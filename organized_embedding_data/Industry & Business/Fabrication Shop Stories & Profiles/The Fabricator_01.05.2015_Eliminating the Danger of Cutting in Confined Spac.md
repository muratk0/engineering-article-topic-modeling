# Eliminating the Danger of Cutting in Confined Space

[TARİH: 01.05.2015 The Fabricator]

Technology Spotlight

A new device shuts down oxyacetylene flow to keep fabricators safe when working in tight confines

By Dan Davis, Editor-in-Chief

Repair work on marine vessels, such as this tug boat, can result in fabricators working in confined spaces. These tight spaces inspired Dave Logan of Pacific Marine to design a cutting torch safety system to eliminate the possibility of explosions.

W

hen Dave Logan is not busy trying to make the world safer for fabricators who work in confined spaces, he’s a fabricator repairing marine vessels, which happens to take place in confined spaces.

And like any fabricator that believes there is a better way to do something, he set forth to do just that. The result is a new cutting torch safety system for those who work in confined spaces.

"Safety systems for all kinds of equipment have evolved, but the torches haven’t evolved. Everyone knows that a torch leaks eventually," said Logan, who runs Pacific Marine in British Columbia, Canada.

He remembers the day that marked the beginning of his foray into developing a way to prevent gases from leaking from cutting torches. He and his small team were repairing a section of a barge with a relatively new torch. Logan wanted to keep the torch tip clean, so he placed it in an empty 5-gallon bucket. When he came back to strike an arc on the torch, the bucket blew up.

"We went, ‘This is insane! There’s got to be something on the market to alleviate this problem. We Googled and searched everywhere, and there was nothing," Logan said. "We decided we had to come up with something."

Logan was all too familiar with the confined spaces that he and his co-workers found themselves in while repairing marine vessels. When working deep into the compartment sections of something like a barge, a fabricator can be striking arcs routinely, and a gas leak can lead to a deadly explosive situation. After developing the device for two years, Logan believes he has a system that can keep fabricators safe from accidental gas leaks.

How It Works

The control box (see

Figure 1

) is the heart of the safety system. Fuel gas lines are attached to the inlet side of the box, and the power line is attached to the bottom. The specialized fuel gas line that will feed the torch is attached to the side of the safety box.

Logan said it is designed to be a naturally closed two-stage system. If a fabricator opens up the supply source of the cutting gas, the gauges react only if the torch is in use.

For example, the pressure gauges on the control box do not activate until the handle activator switch on the torch is depressed. When that switch is released for longer than two seconds, the gas feed is cut off. (The two-second delay before the flame is completely extinguished allows the operator to move the torch from one hand to the other before the torch goes into automatic shutdown.) The gauges on the control box confirm that gas is no longer flowing to the torch.

Figure 1 The control box is the heart of the safety system for controlling and cutting off gas flow to a cutting torch. The gauges notify the fabricator within the confined area and the standby person when gas flow is occurring and when it has successfully shut down. The emergency stop almost instantaneously ends gas flow.

Figure 2 The prototype handles on these torch models eventually will be replaced with high-impact, heat-resistant material when the safety system goes into mass production. Handles are being designed to fit all the major torch brands.

The box also has an emergency switch that a standby person outside the confined area can shut off, thereby monitoring the fabricating being done by his or her partner in the confined area. If any evidence of trouble arises, the person on the outside can hit the emergency button and almost instantaneously shut down the gas flow to the cutting torch.

In fact, any disruption of power flow between the control box and the torch automatically shuts down the system. If the gas line is breached by a sharp object, the gas flow terminates. (To combat potential damage caused by molten slag, the gas lines supplied with the safety system are wrapped in a fire-resistant liner.)

"People look at it and go, ‘Oh my God, why didn’t somebody think of that sooner? It’s so obvious,’" Logan said. "I guess nobody thought about it. We are trying to be innovative and do things differently to be competitive."

Looking Ahead

As he looks to the future, Logan said he thinks that his safety system can do more than just eliminate the threat of explosions; he thinks it also can save fabricators money.

For example, if a torch attached to an acetylene bottle is leaking gas into the atmosphere when the torch is not being used, that fabrication shop is figuratively losing money to the wind. If somebody happens to leave that torch connected to the acetylene tank and doesn’t shut off the tank, that fabricator is going to lose more than $100 in fuel.

In the meantime, Logan said he is looking forward to mass production of the torches (see

Figure 2

) and control boxes. They still require approval from Underwriters Laboratories and the Canadian Standards Association before any manufacturing can begin.

He said the manufacturing world is in absolute need of his invention. No one should have to worry about going to work and possibly not coming home.

"I am 100 percent convinced that every one of the accidents involving a cutting torch where there was an explosion was preventable," Logan said.
