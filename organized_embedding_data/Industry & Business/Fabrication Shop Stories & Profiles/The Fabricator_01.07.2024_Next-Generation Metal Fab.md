# Next-Generation Metal Fab

[TARİH: 01.07.2024 The Fabricator]

Tech Talk

A preview of FMA’s newest podcast

Caleb Chamberlain

C

aleb Chamberlain’s regular column will return next month. This month, we’re featuring a preview of Next-Gen Metal Fab, part of The Fabricator’s Podcast Network at the Fabricators and Manufacturers Association (FMA). The podcast will cover the current state of metal fabrication but also will focus on what’s ahead—a world that, thanks to software and AI algorithms, might look very different from today.

When it comes to launching a business, be it metal fabrication or anything else, they say that everyone is in sales. Everyone, no matter their job title, should be thinking about how a product or service is (or isn’t) benefiting the customer. One day, everyone in business also will likely be "in software"—managing it, working with it, and to varying degrees, developing it as well.

Chamberlain is a prime example of this. Six years ago, he launched Utahbased OSH Cut, aiming to ease the customer experience. Today, he’s one of a handful of U.S.-based sheet metal fabricators that offer online ordering with instant quoting and design-for-manufacturability feedback in both cutting and forming. Chamberlain explained his journey during in The Fabricator’s new Next-Gen Metal Fab podcast. Excerpts from that inaugural episode follow.

Heston:

We’re calling this the Next-Gen Metal Fab podcast because we’ll be talking about the future. The industry seems to be at a kind of inflection point. We see all sorts of interesting pieces of the digitization puzzle being put to use, and there’s incredible potential. Everyone is at a different stage, and they’re starting to solve the puzzle in different ways. But Caleb, to just get us grounded, tell us your story. How did you get into the precision metal fabrication business?

Chamberlain:

I’ll get into that story, but just riffing on what you just said, this is really an exciting time to be in metal manufacturing. It’s hard to see it when you’re in the weeds, but if you look at the trajectory, of what’s changed already in our lifetime, it’s incredible. I spoke with my dad recently, and he has seen some huge changes, starting from fill-the-room computers and punch cards all the way to what we’re doing now. We now have access to some amazing computing power that’s driving massive AI engines. In the mix of that is manufacturing and how we build physical products. These technologies are finding their way into fabrication in unique ways.

Back to OSH Cut’s origins, in my past life I was an electrical engineer and built a lot of circuit boards. You design a board, you produce a set of files that define the solder masks and the copper layers, the silk screen layers, and all the other details the board needs. You upload that file online at any number of places, you get a price instantly, and you can pay extra for quick delivery. That’s the norm in the circuit board industry.

At one point, my brother and I were working on a variety of different projects that all required fabricated metal. We found the purchasing difficult, primarily because we were bringing the kind of work to shops that nobody wanted to do—high-mix or one-off parts that they would never see again.

Looking back to our experience with custom circuit boards, we wanted to provide something similar in metal fabrication. So, we decided to launch a company to streamline the process. We founded OSH Cut, and we bought a laser. Immediately, we found out customers also needed bending, so we added press brakes within a year.

This year, we moved to a new facility. We now have 53,000 sq. ft., 50 employees, three flatbed lasers, a tube laser, three press brakes, and several deburring machines. We’ve seen a lot of traction and a lot of growth, mainly because we’re meeting that middle market area, and most shops just aren’t structured to do that kind of work.

A new podcast delves into the industry’s open questions: How much consolidation can there be? What will supply chains look like? What manufacturing and software technologies will shape the industry’s future? Most important, how can metal fabricators ride the technological wave—and not get drowned in it?

As you know, we build our own software. About six months after launch, we had our first app that allowed you to upload a flat pattern like a DXF. A year later, we released the second version and added 3D model support.

The basic idea behind it all is that we wanted to remove complexity around the quoting process. Ordinarily, you might upload a part file, print PDF, or other job information to a website, or you send it via email. The shop’s quoting personnel brings it in, analyzes the print, ensures the brake tooling works, and determines whether it’s manufacturable at all. It’s very labor-intensive. That’s fine for production, but it’s not so fine for a job that you’re going to do once and never again.

So, we wanted to automate all of it. Today, when customers upload a part, our system automatically unfolds the 3D model to create a flat pattern. Customers enter a quantity and material, and the system instantly quotes a price. The system shows the customer how the piece will be nested, so they can get an idea of how their parts will utilize material, and whether there might be waste. They might see that if, say, they shave a 1/2 in. off the part size, they can save money simply because it fits better on the sheet.

It also does a manufacturability check on bending. It puts the job on our tools and renders the bend sequence. If it collides, our system shows this and tells them what the problem is, so that the customer can iterate the design and reupload the file.

Heston:

This approach reverses the traditional roles, correct? Traditionally, a fab shop might perform a manufacturability analysis on its end, make the necessary changes, send it to the customer for approval, then launch the job. In your case, the manufacturability aspect stays with the customer. You communicate the changes they need to make. They then make the changes on their end, then reupload file. You’ve switched this relationship, right?

Chamberlain:

That’s correct. Ultimately, this approach creates what we call our "production pipeline." In every step of fabrication, we follow certain standard methods. We cut on a laser, we form on the brake, and we send parts through a specific kind of deburring machine. Jobs that fit our pipeline are the jobs we can do really well. If we can’t do it—that is, if we don’t have the equipment, or the piece would require special engineering to, say, break a part into multiple pieces that then can be joined together—we reject it on the front end.

Meanwhile, the parts that do fit just flow seamlessly, because they fit our standardized processes. Everyone on the floor knows how to process these jobs. We’ve developed custom software that automates our scheduling, and we have digital consoles with work instructions. Anything that gets put into the system tends to get through it very efficiently.

As the quoting system says "no" to certain kinds of work, because we don’t have the right dies or certain equipment, it exposes areas where we could widen our sales funnel. If we do find a way to make these parts, then we build the automated processes to ensure we can do them efficiently, and now our addressable market just increased. For instance, we started with sets of gooseneck punches on our brakes, but now we have sets of swan neck punches, which are twice as deep and so can accept very deep return flanges.

There are analogs here with the printed circuit board [PCB] industry. With circuit boards, there’s only so many things you can do on the board, and you have specific constraints on, say, how narrow a copper trace can be. So, you need to design around the shop that’s going to produce your part. And we’ve taken that approach with our shop. Ideally, customers should know what tools they need to design for.

Heston:

There are a handful of companies out there now offering automated quoting, and one large operation in Europe, 247TailorSteel, which I’ve covered several times over the years. The company now has numerous flatbed lasers, tube lasers, and press brakes with automatic tool change, along with automated guided vehicles carrying pieces from one station to the next. At this point, at least, it’s still just offering cutting and bending, though—no welding, no assembly. I know you do offer powder coating with a partner company, but as far as fabrication goes, do you plan to go beyond cutting and bending?

Chamberlain:

We find we’re acquiring about 200 new customers every month at this point. A very high percentage of these companies are just trying to simplify their supply chains. That said, it’s very hard to take an automatic engine like what we have and add on welding and assembly. How do you create the weld fixture? What are the critical dimensions that need to be nailed every time, and what does the customer need on the back end? Do they need this shipped in a very specific way? That work becomes very specialized, and it’s difficult to fit within our pipeline of work, at least for now.

How far can we go? Here, metal fabrication is very different from the PCB world. The PCB industry has seen some significant consolidation, but again, boards need to be fabricated within a narrow set of constraints.

Fabricators are different. The industry has seen some consolidation, but how much consolidation potential is there? The metal fabrication business serves so many verticals with so many different demands and industry- specific procedures. It goes way beyond just cutting a blank on a laser and bending it. On our end, how many capabilities can we bring in-house to serve our customers and maintain our quality and reliability? It really is an open question.

In fact, we see a lot of open questions out there, and we hope to delve into them in this podcast. We talked about our shop in this episode, but in future podcasts, I really hope to take a deep dive into industry trends. We’ll certainly use what we’re doing as a touchstone, but there’s so much happening outside of our company, with new waves of technologies and new approaches to running a business. We’ll talk about what’s changed already and what will change in the future. Put another way, how can we ride the wave and not get drowned in it?
