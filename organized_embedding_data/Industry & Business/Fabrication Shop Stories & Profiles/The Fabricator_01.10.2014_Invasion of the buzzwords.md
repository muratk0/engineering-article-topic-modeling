Manufacturing is mostly made up of people who say what they mean and do what they say. These folks aren’t flashy and don’t regularly seek the spotlight. As a result, manufacturing isn’t often recognized as a career path for bright high school students, but those that do follow the path typically are rewarded with a job that challenges them in a different way almost on a daily basis. They make parts that make products better.

The computer software industry isn’t like that. CEOs become rock stars and appear on CNBC because their companies are darlings on Wall Street. New graduates flock to these companies in hopes of getting in on the ground floor of the next big thing or spending just enough time at those companies that are currently considered big-time to bolster a resume. They contribute to the creation of programs that hold the potential to make lives better—if they are ever fully commercialized.

Manufacturing has buzzwords like

just-in-time

,

design for manufacturability

, and

lean

manufacturing—all pretty clear and self-explanatory. The software industry has

the cloud

,

big data

, and the

Internet of Things

. What?

Well, the cloud simply describes the concept of software as a service. (Again, what?) In other words, a user logs onto the Internet to access a program that is hosted on a server at another location not owned by the user or his or her company, but likely owned by the software provider. The user’s firm doesn’t have to worry about having an information technology infrastructure or staff.

Big data is not something that has to fit inside the roll-up door at the shipping/receiving dock; it’s the term that describes the large amount of data that is not analyzed or utilized because an organization simply doesn’t have the computing power to do so. In this instance, decisions are being made without all possible data—some pertinent and some not-so—being considered.

The Internet of Things sounds the most confusing, but the concept is easy to understand. It’s the connectivity of objects, such as an appliance, a medical device, or even a machine tool, over the Internet that eventually leads to more interconnectivity between these objects. Real-time and accurate data is the result, leading to better decision-making and operational efficiency. That’s not a bad concept, even if it is described by what sounds like three randomly picked words.

At the most recent IMTS, Sept. 8-13, I heard these software industry buzzwords more than I had at any other recent industry event. Worlds are colliding, and maybe that’s not a bad thing.

The soft and fuzzy world of software development is wading into the tumultuous waters of manufacturing, where hard deadlines and actual deliverables can pull a firm under quickly. Overpromising and good intentions aren’t a lot of help in getting quality products out the door in a timely manner.

Manufacturers, both small and large, are wading into the waters of this new digital world. The immersion could be as dramatic as a fabricating shop adopting a new cloud-based enterprise resource planning software suite in which new job orders automatically trigger work center assignments and timing, or which tracks labor time and attendance and feeds that seamlessly into payroll applications. Or it could be as simple as a new pipe fabricator putting a new orbital welding machine to work and watching it shut off after a couple of uses because the control system connected to the gas metal arc welding torch head senses that too much power is being fed into the process. In either case, the automated collection of data influences streamlined decision-making.

These are the first steps on the road to …

Industry 4.0

. That’s what the German government is calling the future of manufacturing, a hugely important sector of its overall economy. This fourth industrial revolution (following the steam-, electric motor-, and digital technology-powered revolutions) will be heavily influenced by these intelligent networks that are interconnected via the Internet. The factory of tomorrow will be able to tackle large-scale, high-mix manufacturing tasks more efficiently than ever because software systems will optimize scheduling, and resources and data pulled from the shop floor will give human operators instantaneous information to keep production humming at incredibly efficient levels.

Sure, Industry 4.0 is another buzzword, but it sounds pretty cool. The end results also might live up to the hype.
