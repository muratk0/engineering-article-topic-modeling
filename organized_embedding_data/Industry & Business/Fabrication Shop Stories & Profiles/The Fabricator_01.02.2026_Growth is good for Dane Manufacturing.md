# Growth is good for Dane Manufacturing

[TARİH: 01.02.2026 The Fabricator]

Cover Story The Fabricator 2026 Industry Award Winner

An employee-first business model proves key to creating a culture that can scale

By

Dan Davis

Dane Manufacturing works to maintain an "Employees Come First" culture with a strong commitment to safety, training, team building, and employee recognition.

W

hen he was first hired about three years ago, Greg McManis, vice president of marketing, Dane Manufacturing, was digging through some handwritten minutes from the early days of the company, which was founded in 1917. In his research, he learned that during World War II, the company shut down manufacturing operations because the federal government mandated that all metal was going to be used for military-related manufacturing. Company owners had the foresight to see that coming out of the war, the economy would take off and competition for metal fabricating services would be strong, so instead of simply closing the doors for an extended time, they invested in equipment and reorganized the plant to make operations more efficient.

Over the last 25 years, history has repeated itself at the metal fabricating company. Having moved from rural Dane County, Wis., to Waunakee, Wis., in 2019, Dane Manufacturing wasn’t going to let the pandemic slow the company down. Under the leadership of Troy Berg, who purchased the business in 2001, Dane Manufacturing invested heavily in advanced fabricating technology and material handling automation, inspiring employees who were a part of the company’s rapid growth starting in the 2010s and customers who saw the potential of working with a company that was ready to grow with them. All of this occurred on a manufacturing campus with a legacy of its own, once being the home of Marshall Erdman and Associates, a pioneer in prefabricated houses and buildings. The campus has 500,000 sq. ft. of manufacturing space, enough to accommodate manufacturing activities for today and tomorrow.

And now, instead of repeating history again, Dane Manufacturing is making it. An employee-first culture and a modern manufacturing environment have fueled the company’s rise to levels that not too many other metal fabricating companies reach, particularly in a few short years. After hitting the $20 million mark in sales by 2019, Dane Manufacturing came roaring out of the pandemic. It’s witnessed revenue growth of 191% since 2020, with revenue growth of 18% expected in 2025 alone. The company reached No. 33 on The Fabricator’s FAB 40 list for 2025 with just over $51 million in annual revenue in 2024, and it expects to eclipse $60 million in 2025.

What is remarkable about most of Dane Manufacturing’s growth is that it’s tied to its existing customer base. In 2025, more than 90% of the company’s revenue growth came from its existing customer portfolio. (Seventy-five percent of current customers have been with Dane Manufacturing for at least five years.)

Not too many metal fabricators are able to scale their operations so rapidly and still maintain such positive customer relationships. The growth is notable, but the environment that allows it to happen is the main reason that The Fabricator has awarded its 2026 Industry Award to Dane Manufacturing.

WELCOME TO THE WORLD OF FABRICATING

Berg, a mechanical engineering graduate, cut his teeth in the manufacturing world with roles at large industrial, aerospace, and automotive companies. Along the way, he learned to trust people who knew what they were doing and to incorporate automation where possible, eliminating redundant and taxing processes for human workers.

A company simply can’t grow without automation, according to Berg. That hearkens back to a time when sweat, brawn, and beaten-down bodies pushed manufacturing forward.

"That’s a 1950s mentality," he said. "You cannot scale a company off the backs of people. You have to scale it off the backs of automation. That’s when the knowledge workers can do what the automation can’t do."

A technician checks coating thickness on a part.

After a stint as the owner of a product development business in the late 1990s, Berg had an itch to get back into the world of manufacturing. A broker who knew his background told him that a fab shop was for sale in Dane, Wis.

Berg was interested, but he had an investor pull out at the last minute before closing. His mother decided to take another mortgage on her house after having just paid it off, supplying Berg with the funds necessary to close the deal. He was officially in the metal fabricating business.

"My mom and my wife believed in me when others didn’t," he said. Others would come to believe in Berg as well, especially as Dane Manufacturing grew.

The growth, however, didn’t come fast at first. By 2005, Dane Manufacturing had reached nearly $6 million in annual revenues with about 35 employees. The company had a laser cutting machine, a couple of press brakes, and some welding cells. It was pretty much an average shop, but Berg’s experience was more than that.

"One of the things that I love about this industry is that it’s full of a bunch of people that just say, ‘Oh, we’ll figure it out,’" he said. "It’s the cando spirit of the manufacturing world that never left for China or Mexico. We’re the last industry standing here and still saying ‘Made in America, and we’re proud of it.’"

Getting to $20 million in annual sales would require a lot of work, but the Dane Manufacturing team intended to figure it out. It landed its first big-time contract manufacturing job in 2015, which provided a solid foundation as it sought more opportunities. Berg hired a chief operating officer in 2016, which allowed him to focus on sales instead of being bogged down by the daily details of running the shop. The company also purchased Dantherm Cooling, a manufacturer of commercial cooling units, in Spartanburg, S.C., in 2018. All of that pushed Dane Manufacturing to the precipice of its monetary goal, but it also revealed one major problem: The company was running out of room.

Over the years at its original location, Dane Manufacturing had gone from 24,000 sq. ft. to 60,000 sq. ft., but that was going to be the end of the line. It was landlocked, bound by three streets and a railroad track.

To complicate matters further, Dane Manufacturing had started operating a powder coating line in a 40,000-sq.-ft. space in Janesville, Wis. With an intent to grow even more, Berg knew he needed to find another facility.

Why was growth so important to Berg? It goes back to his personal belief about manufacturing and the people that find themselves a part of this important economic segment.

And then from that, if the employee does a good job taking care of the customers, you generate a profit. In that order. That’s my belief in my core and my center."

FINDING A NEW HOME

It’s not often that a physical plant actually plays an important part in an organization’s story, but the tale of Dane Manufacturing’s rise is not often duplicated. In this case, it’s eventual home would be the launching pad for the rocket-like growth of the company in the 2020s.

So as Berg wondered what made the most sense for the next stage in Dane Manufacturing’s existence, he kept his eyes open for potential new locations. One possible site that caught his attention was a huge manufacturing campus in Waunakee, Wis., that he passed on his way to and from work. Most days a locked fence kept the outside world from entering the parking lot, but one day, Berg noticed the gate open and a vehicle in the lot. A conversation revealed a contact, and Berg finally found himself conversing with the caretaker of the property.

After some initial skepticism from the property manager and follow-up meetings, Berg won over the property owners, the Erdman family, who were excited to see a young, energetic business owner with big plans.

In 2019, Dane Manufacturing took the first step to moving its operations to Waunakee. It moved its powder coating operations from Janesville and occupied roughly 45,000 sq. ft. of the 180,000-sq.-ft. main building. Even then, Berg envisioned what he wanted to do with the entire space. Powder coating was located at the south end of the building, which was opposite the loading docks where Berg envisioned material being delivered, flowing through fabrication processes, and working its way back to finishing, assembly, and shipping.

In 2020, Berg was approached by the property owners about buying all 500,000 sq. ft. on the campus. Berg called it "a very big bite" at that moment in the company’s history, but the Erdmans made it possible with a deal that came with favorable financing. The family was happy to see a buzz on land where its business once thrived.

Dane Manufacturing initially planned to occupy about 90,000 sq. ft. of the main building. But this wasn’t a simple move. The property had not hosted manufacturing activities for years.

"We cleaned it up and started with the improvements, making it better for everyone," said Seth Reppen, the company’s vice president of sales. "When we moved our operations, we not only brought equipment; we brought the entrepreneurial spirit of the people and our empowered culture into this space."

As the pandemic struck and the world started to shut down in the summer of 2020, Dane Manufacturing kept on working as an essential manufacturer, supplying parts and assemblies to health care, telecom, and energy customers. Some of its top customers shut down briefly but got up to speed soon thereafter. What Dane Manufacturing found, however, was that customers who laid off workers instead of furloughing them had trouble getting people to come back to work. And some of those customers absolutely needed the company’s help to get their businesses going again.

With supply chains in disruption because of the pandemic, Berg recognized the potential for domestic manufacturers who would have to step up to fill the gap in the months and years ahead. Dane Manufacturing wasn’t going to pull back during this international crisis.

"We really leaned in during 2020," Berg said. "We put in a new ERP system, and we put in orders for two new machine tools: a big punch [a TRUMPF TruPunch 5000 with a SheetMaster parts loading, unloading, and sorting system] and another laser [a TRUMPF 10-kW TruLaser 3030]. That ended up giving us capacity that our customers wanted."

Team Lead Jeremy Boreman leads an SQDC+ team huddle before a shift.

In summer 2021, Dane Manufacturing made the official move to the Waunakee facility. New metal fabricating equipment was delivered, and Berg’s vision of his own factory of the future was coming to fruition.

"Then in 2022, people started coming out of their shells, and we were able to get customers to come and see this facility, the new equipment," Reppen said. "They could feel that buzz and that energy that we have here."

They could see ample room in which Dane Manufacturing could grow. The south part of the building had plenty of storage space for those looking for inventory management or just-in-time delivery services. The north part of the building, which some customers said resembled an airplane hangar, had plenty of room to accommodate fabricating activities, including assembly and packaging.

Customers’ renewed desire for fabricating services and Dane Manufacturing’s newfound capacity proved to be a winning combination. The company topped $51 million in 2024, doubling its revenue in only a couple of years.

INVESTMENT CONTINUES

Having seen how robotics had changed the way vehicles and automotive parts were made, Berg knew that the world of metal fabrication was destined for the same type of disruption. It was just going to take a little longer.

By 2012, he recognized that automation was on its way. On a technology tour of European metal fabricators, Berg saw the way shops were using automated material handling technology, minimizing labor costs associated with a non-value-added activity.

"That’s when I started planning. What would that look like for us?" Berg said. "I never pulled the trigger on it back then, but we started to accumulate the automated machines, just not the automation to load and unload them.

"With the new facility, though, we got a redo," he added. "It was a clean sheet."

Berg worked with TRUMPF officials to design a factory layout built around a STOPA automated material handling system. This installation would act as the backbone for most of the fabricating operations in the north part of the main facility.

After nearly 25 iterations, Berg had a design that made sense for what he wanted. The 420-shelf tower system was installed in 2023.

The STOPA system initially was linked with the new punching machine and laser cutting machine, two slightly older versions of those same machines, and a TruBend Center 7030 panel bending machine with a Starmatik system that enables robotic unload and palletization of parts. In 2025, a TruBend 5230 robotic press brake cell with a ToolMaster automated tooling changer was added to the automated material handling system.

"We ended up with a big machine like this because we’re getting known for fabricating big parts," Berg said.

The equipment situated around this hub of automated fabricating is run by a handful of individuals each shift. They are there mostly to check on operations and take the steps necessary to meet deadlines and maintain quality standards.

Randy Hilgart, Dane Manufacturing’s vice president of quality and continuous improvement, said that the company’s culture has helped with the integration of more automation. Employees know that such investments are less about cutting costs and more about expanding opportunities for everyone in the company.

"They actually embrace the automation," he said. "They know that means growth and that good things are coming for them and their families."

Since adding the STOPA system, Dane Manufacturing has seen a 24% increase in machine usage and a 48-hour improvement on production start times. The metal fabricator has additional support equipment that surrounds the facility’s automated backbone. Three punching machines, an automated shearing and punching cell, and 12 press brakes are all located nearby. The latest addition is a new TruLaser Tube 5000 tube cutting machine, which reportedly cuts tube three times faster than the company’s older laser tube cutting machine.

An expansion of data center business led Dane Manufacturing to expand its welding operations. It now has 10 cells and one robotic welding cell.

A 30-ft.-wide path encircles the entire manufacturing operation, allowing visitors to take a tour in a golf cart in a few minutes. A command center at the center of the facility acts as central location for team meetings and supervisor discussions.

ADDITIONAL PROCESS IMPROVEMENTS

Dane Manufacturing has been an ISO 9001-certified manufacturer for more than 20 years, so it’s no stranger to formal quality management systems. Additionally, it holds W. Edward Demings’ Plan-Do-Study-Act model for quality management as a mantra for its shop floor activities. Wanting to take its continuous improvement efforts one step further, the company hired Hilgart in 2025.

"We’re looking to put a little more structure behind it because as a company grows and you add people, you can’t rely on tribal knowledge to keep things going," Hilgart said. "As people come on board, you have to be intentional about your culture and about making sure your processes still produce good parts."

The metal fabricator recently implemented an SQDC+ framework as a way to track key performance indicators and promote more cross-functional communication and teamwork in the organization. With visual boards scattered over the shop floor, everyone can see how performance is going related to safety, quality, delivery, and cost. (The "+" refers to 5S activities for keeping an organized and clean work environment. Sort, set in order, shine, standardize, and sustain are the five components of the methodology.)

At daily team huddles, the SQDC+ metrics are the main topic of conversation. Everyone is empowered to address any area that might emerge as a concern. Larger, more involved projects result in a continuous improvement action plan, which calls for a team to come together to find solutions to problems and ensure that the issues are solved once and for good.

"We don’t want to implement countermeasures and just walk away," Hilgart said. "Let’s go back a month later and check on this to see if things are working."

Since the formal introduction of the SQDC+ framework in August, the shop floor has seen a "handful of wins" and has about 10 other continuous improvement action plans in process, Hilgart said. "People are starting to embrace it because they’re seeing the fruits of their labor."

Dane Manufacturing also has started a strategic initiative centered on quick response manufacturing (QRM), which it hopes will lead to reduced lead times and the elimination of non-value-added efforts throughout the company— even in the front office and engineering. While the effort is still in the early stages, the executive team and key leaders have been trained on QRM principles and have been asked to apply that to strategic planning.

One of the first offshoots of this training is the establishment of a QRM cell to meet the demands from a major customer. The cell, with all blanking and bending functions occurring inside it, has been running since November 2025, and the company hopes that it will provide the responsiveness necessary to keep the customer satisfied.

In the front office, Dane Manufacturing is working to streamline its marketing and business development functions. It’s implementing a new customer relationship management suite to tighten alignment between marketing and sales activities and a Paperless Parts digital estimating tool for speeding up turnaround on project estimates.

During a time when many metal fabricating companies have been overwhelmed with data center work, Dane Manufacturing has made these moves to be more responsive to customer needs while maintaining a fairly balanced customer portfolio. Well-known brands in the data center market make up about 25% of the company’s business, but that’s offset by construction, industrial, and automotive equipment at 15%; kitchen appliances at 15%; and power generation at 5%. Company officials see further growth opportunities with new customers in the entertainment industry and food and beverage sector.

EMPLOYEES FIRST

Dane Manufacturing has undergone a great amount of change since the pandemic. New fabricating technology has been put in place, and new continuous improvement efforts are underway. Meanwhile, more jobs keep hitting the floor, keeping everyone on their toes.

The change that occurs as an organization scales can be a threat to the very culture that helped the business to grow. It seems like Dane Manufacturing is avoiding that unfortunate scenario.

The metal fabricator topped 200 employees in 2025, almost double what it had in 2021. In 2025, an average of less than 1% of its employee roster turned over each month, outperforming industry benchmarks of 2% to 3% monthly turnover.

Skills development is key to retaining existing employees while also filling the talent pipeline for new openings within a company. Dane Manufacturing recognized this and launched the Dane Academy in 2025 to provide employees with personal and professional skills training. Since classes began last summer, more than 25% of the company’s workforce has participated in a structured learning session. Some of the classes offered have been English as a second language, QRM, and general communication skills.

Some might say that it’s bold for Dane Manufacturing to claim it has an "Employees Come First" culture, but company management is making that statement with confidence. In a recent survey of its workers, 85% responded that their job makes them feel they are part of something meaningful, and 90% would highly recommend working at Dane Manufacturing.

"We have people on the shop floor that know how to make things happen. They know how to make good parts and get them out the door. They are well led and being led down the right path. We have good customers and good customer relations," Berg said. "We’re headed in the right direction."
