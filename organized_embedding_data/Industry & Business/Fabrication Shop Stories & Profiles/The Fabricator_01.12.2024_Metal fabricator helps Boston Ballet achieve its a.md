# Metal fabricator helps Boston Ballet achieve its artistic vision

A folded paper structure is transformed into a full-scale metal set piece

Dan Davis

C

hallenging projects are not unique in the metal fabricating world, but when one comes from the Boston Ballet, it’s a story worth sharing.

Mark Marenghi has been working with the Boston Ballet for years, thanks to a personal connection with the ballet company’s production manager. (Marenghi actually once took a summer off and traveled with the crew as a carpenter when the Boston Ballet toured Spain.) He’s been doing it for just over a year now as owner of Norwood, Mass.-based MJM Fabrication Design Engineering, a four-man shop offering engineering and drawing services and specialty fabrications made from metal, wood, or both. Before that, he worked for a fab shop for more than 16 years, first in the engineering department and then as a project manager. He’s literally been working in machine shops and fab shops since graduating high school in 1996.

Marenghi said the latest set piece for the ballet company was noteworthy because it needed to look like a paper structure, so it was thin in nature. Ironically, the way the set designer tried to express what the fabrication should look like was with an actual paper sculpture.

He thought about using wood as the main material because it was cost-effective and shaping it with a CNC router, but he said he just couldn’t see the wood being thin enough to make people think it could be paper. That’s when he turned to metal, but he was going to need some sheet metal bending expertise, particularly because his shop didn’t have a press brake.

"I reached out to a metal fabricator friend of mine, George Henriques at Den Mar Corp. [in North Dartmouth, Mass.], and I asked him, ‘Do you want to build this with me?’ He was like, ‘Sure, but you have to do all the drawings and models and basically figure it all out,’" Marenghi recalled.

He initially thought the set piece could be made of a bunch of different metal pieces with about one bend each, minimizing the press brake work and the headache of trying to keep track of multiple bends for just one part. Henriques and his team actually requested that he approach it from another perspective: Use 5-ft.-wide sheets and incorporate multiple bends into those sheets to minimize the amount of welding required. Design for manufacturing has relevance in the world of the arts as well!

This is how the set piece looked at Den Mar before final assembly and finishing.

With some help from the team at Den Mar Corp. in North Dartmouth, Mass., Mark Marenghi of MJM Fabrication Design Engineering, Norwood, Mass., was able to figure out the most efficient way to create this set piece for the Boston Ballet’s most recent performance.

Images: Mark Marenghi

That gave Marenghi the direction he needed. The result was three larger sections that could be moved easily and joined on stage for final assembly. Each section is made up of multiple laser-cut aluminum parts that have multiple bends. For instance, the bottom section has four separate pieces, with five bends per part.

The set piece, which is roughly 16 ft. long, 9 ft. tall, and 8 ft. deep, is held in place by fasteners that attach to brackets in the back of the structure. The pieces are lifted on top of each other, and the set technicians can put the fasteners in place.

Marenghi said that the Den Mar team needed to touch up the fabrication a bit with grinding and polishing before the textured matte white powder coating was applied. This was a result of the intricate bending patterns used in the fabrication.

"To get the bend relief that was needed, I had to cut back on the corners, so when they bent, the flanges didn’t tear into the aluminum where the bends intersected," he said. Because of this, the fabricators had to go into those bend reliefs and fill those corners with welding wire. The grinding and blending helped to deliver a smooth finish where no weld joint could be detected.

Marenghi said he never knew career opportunities like the one he now has even existed when he was in high school. Fortunately, metal shop and woodworking classes were available, which helped him stay in school and get his degree.

"I’m always looking for something new. I want to build cool stuff. I want challenging work," he said. "I’ve worked in manufacturing, and making that first part is fun, but once you make thousands of the part, you get bored of it. Now I don’t have to worry about that because I get to build some crazy stuff."

The set piece made its debut on Oct. 24 in the ballet "After," which was part of Boston Ballet’s program Fall Experience at the Citizens Bank Opera House. Marenghi was in attendance to see how his fabricated art piece fit into a grander artistic performance.
