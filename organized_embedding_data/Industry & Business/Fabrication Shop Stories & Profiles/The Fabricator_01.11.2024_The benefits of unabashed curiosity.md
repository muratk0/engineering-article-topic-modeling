# The benefits of unabashed curiosity

[TARİH: 01.11.2024 The Fabricator]

Biz Talk

Themes of technology and cultural change at FABTECH Orlando

Tim Heston

"C

hange is a powerful engine for both growth and discovery." So said Andrew Kossowski, owner of Largo, Fla.-based Veterans Metal LLC, during the opening sessions at this year’s FABTECH in Orlando, Fla. Four years ago, he and his wife purchased a "completely manual, completely analog" metal finishing company in Largo, Fla., and since then they carried the company through a digital transformation that entailed "not only changing the physical space, but also about redefining the business."

Kossowski’s comment was a prescient opening salvo to what became a common theme at this year’s show, which experienced a smooth start and robust attendance despite Hurricane Milton’s unwelcome Florida visit the week before. More than 1,500 exhibitors—machine makers, software companies, and service providers—welcomed tens of thousands to the Sunshine State Oct 15-17.

Kossowski told the story of how he acquired a company "with good bones, but outdated methods and practices, outdated equipment, and inefficient processes," a place where "you only knew something was wrong when it broke. We wanted to digitize 100%."

Kossowski’s story reflects that of so many small shops, both in metal finishing and metal fabrication. People remain stuck in old ways. New leaders take the reins, and change ensues, with the idea of creating a high-tech environment full of documented processes, a place where no one hesitates to share what they know.

You could call it

authentic transparency

—no drama or fuss, just the truth about where the business stands and how well jobs are flowing, from that initial quote to the final shipment. More than ever, exhibitors showed the importance of tracking and measuring both machine uptime and the movement of work-in-process (WIP).

Chatter around artificial intelligence (AI) continued unabated, not least because so many are imagining its potential. Imagine a shop using ultrawide-band location tracking of WIP. Marry that data with machine performance and run-time metrics, job-specific information, and the production schedule.

Now, imagine a shop floor where WIP is

always

on the move. As blanks are stacked onto pallets, they’re immediately moved to the next operation, where machine setup is completed at exactly the right time. Traffic is always flowing, despite tumultuous demand. The schedule adjusts seamlessly in the background, and everything keeps moving, all thanks to the astounding analytical power of software and the AI behind it all.

We might never get to that ideal completely, but the concept is intriguing, and the technologies FABTECH exhibitors are touting are at least planting the seeds of possibility. After all, the "hard" automation—material handling towers, part sorters, bending and welding robots, and the autonomous mobile robots that could carry work between processes—is just half the story. The other half is the "soft" automation: software, AI, and the almighty algorithm. All this might give the metal fabrication industry that technological "wow factor" to attract the next generation of workers.

Of course, technology doesn’t build a good culture. Engaged people do, especially those with unabashed curiosity. In fact, I met someone who fits that description on the second day of the show. Blake Hamar is president of Cali Raised LED, an aftermarket truck parts manufacturer (think tricked-out Toyota Tacomas and Tundras), and Apex Fabworks, a precision sheet metal job shop. His Fort Worth, Texas, operation has come a long way in a very short time. He started with a plasma table and a used press brake and welding power source. Now, he has a flourishing fab shop with the latest in fiber laser, press brake, and even robotic welding technology. He now employs several dozens of people. And, oh yeah, he’s 27 years old.

He flew from his home in Fort Worth, Texas, to his first FABTECH, arrived at the Orange County Convention Center, and (according to his telling) his eyes widened. "This opens up a whole new universe for me. I’m learning so much here. It’s amazing what I don’t know. I’m just drinking it all in."

Both his unabashed curiosity and his authenticity really struck me. He never hesitates to ask questions. He asks machine tool representatives to walk him through a process; he absorbs what they say, gives it a try, fails, tries again, and learns something. He grows as a person, and so does his business.

In fact, he encourages everyone at his shop to do the same. If someone wants to learn a new machine or skill, co-workers with that skill spread the knowledge. When people up their skill, they become more valuable, and they’re rewarded financially. Salary ranges are no secret, and neither is what people need to get ahead in their careers. The shop doesn’t give automatic raises just based on tenure. When people add more value—be that becoming a better welder or a cross-trained technician who can run multiple machines—they’re paid more.

And again, their pay isn’t a secret. Indeed, this was the first FABTECH I’ve visited where I’ve heard talk of pay transparency. A skilled labor panel described how such transparency helps attract the youngest workers. Transitioning to it isn’t always smooth. Seeing today’s higher, inflation-adjusted entry-level wages might ruffle feathers among company veterans. But in the long term, the culture can benefit.

That pay transparency goes back to what I saw as FABTECH’s underlying theme: authentic transparency throughout the enterprise. When departmental fiefdoms rule, company culture suffers, and work is thrown haphazardly over that metaphorical wall to the next area, where it’s someone else’s problem. A complete digital transformation, combined with an authentic and transparent shop culture, can help break those barriers down, make everything flow, and really take shops to the next level.
