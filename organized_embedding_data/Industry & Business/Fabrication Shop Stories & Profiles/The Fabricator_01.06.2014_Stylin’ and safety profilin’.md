# Stylin’ and safety profilin’

[TARİH: 01.06.2014 The Fabricator]

FROM THE EDITOR-IN-CHIEF

Keeping employees safe should be a priority for all metal fabricators, even if it affects personal style choices

Dan Davis, editor-in-chief of

The FABRICATOR

, can be reached at

dand@thefabricator.com

.

My worst-dressed award this year was earned during a visit to a shop that specializes in large structural fabrications. I left the tie at home because I wasn’t traveling with my boss, but my combination of black FMA-logoed golf shirt and pewter-colored trousers looked quite spiffy. It was the shoe accoutrements that threw off the whole outfit. I didn’t have steel-toed shoes, so the fabrication shop manager provided me with steel covers that slipped over my black leather shoes.

Clip-clop. Clip-Clip. Clip-clop-clip-clop-clip-clop (as I tripped over my own feet). I had to move into more of a marching motion to get from Point A to Point B in a more fluid fashion. This tour wasn’t going to be a simple stroll in the park.

But I didn’t mind. I’d rather be safe than sorry. The metal fabricator thought the same way. That should be the case for all shops, actually.

I haven’t spent a lifetime in metal fabricating shops, but I’ve spoken to enough people who have. Despite the best of intentions, even the most well-lit and nicely organized shop floor harbors dangers that can result in a serious injury or even death. Have you ever heard about a situation in which a band around a steel coil was cut, popped off wildly because it was under tremendous stress, and sliced a leg wide open? Certainly you know of a lift truck story in which the operator mistakenly left it in gear instead of turning it off, jumped out to clear something in the aisle, and watched the lift truck take off, running into another co-worker? Of course, you have learned that even the most automated workcells carry hidden dangers after hearing how someone bypassed a security gate, entered a restricted workspace, and was hit with tremendous force by a material movement device. The ways that a person can be injured on the shop floor are as endless as the types of products that can be fabricated in that same environment.

But for many shops, safety remains an assumption rather than a continuous improvement activity. This is bad for the shop and its employees. They aren’t being safe; they are just being lucky.

It’s no coincidence that you’ll find me writing about the need for an aggressive safety culture in this column at least once a year. It usually coincides with the annual FMA Safety Conference, which was held in Rock Island, Ill., in late April. You see, when it comes to safety, repetition is good. A manufacturing company simply can’t talk about safety enough.

It’s a simple concept: If employees care enough to step forward and correct co-workers … they are engaged in activities that go beyond their tasks for the day.

At this year’s Safety Conference—which you can read more about on page 22—keynote speaker Gary Kopps, John Deere’s associate director for global work and life safety, talked about his company’s commitment to a “safety-first” environment and some of the successes the company’s plants have enjoyed recently in terms of keeping employees free from injury. During his address, Kopps shared a conversation that he had with the former Alcoa chief executive and secretary of the U.S. Treasury in 2013. Paul O’Neill was talking about his tenure at Alcoa and the company’s safety record during that time, according to Kopps, and made the statement that if a company has a successful work culture in which everyone creates a safe work environment, everything else in the company likely is good as well.

“That means they care,” Kopps said.

It’s a simple concept: If employees care enough to step forward and correct co-workers after seeing them do something as silly as walking over some unstable pallets or as dangerous as failing to lock out a piece of equipment, they are engaged in activities that go beyond their tasks for the day. They care, and that’s the Holy Grail that thousands of companies look for each day.

U.S. manufacturers have done an outstanding job overall in keeping workers safe. According to the Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS), the median days away from the job for workers that had nonfatal injuries and illnesses in the manufacturing industry for 2012 was nine days. That’s better than several service-oriented sectors, including transportation and warehousing, utilities, and even state government.

Fatalities are another story. The BLS reports that in 2012, 43 deaths occurred in the fabricated metal product manufacturing sector. Thirty-three deaths resulted from a person being struck by an object or some sort of equipment.

These aren’t the days of Upton Sinclair’s

The Jungle

, but there is always room for improvement. One death—or even one injury—is one too many for a manufacturer.

Safety is good for employees and company performance. Requiring visitors to wear steel toe covers isn’t an inconvenience; it’s just doing the right thing.
