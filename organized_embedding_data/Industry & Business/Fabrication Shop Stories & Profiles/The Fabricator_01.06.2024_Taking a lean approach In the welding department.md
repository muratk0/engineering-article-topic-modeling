M

ost metal fabricators have some sort of working knowledge of lean manufacturing, even if they’ve never formally attended a class or earned some sort of certificate. Fabricators realize that eliminating or minimizing any waste in the process involved in transforming metal into parts, assemblies, and products for customers leads to increased profitability and customer satisfaction rates. Call it what you want, but applying lean manufacturing principles in the world of high-mix, low-volume fabricating is a common activity in most companies.

But is it as widely applied across the board on the shop floor? Can these continuous improvement efforts be effective in the welding department where the variabilities from one job to the next can be huge, just like some of the weldments? A welder’s job might involve different metals throughout the day, and the complexity of the work might require a lot of time-consuming setup and maneuvering. Also, the lead times on jobs might greatly differ, requiring detailed planning to be sacrificed sometimes in favor of immediately tackling the work.

While many challenges exist to introducing lean manufacturing efforts to the welding department, they are more likely to be excuses. Waste is anything that doesn’t add value to the creation of the weldment, and waste is always present. It’s just a matter of identifying it and figuring out the best way to address it.

The Fabricator discussed lean manufacturing in the welding department with some experts that have spent a lot of time trying to help welders be more efficient in their work: Jason Becker, host of the Arc Junkies Podcast, owner of Underground Metal Works, and welding consultant; and Brian Engelhardt, plant manager, and Brad Opperman, manufacturing engineer, MERRILL Technologies Group, a Keel company. Their insights offer shops some ideas for getting the most out of their welding operations.

Start Talking to the Welders

"You have to listen to the people doing the welding. The best ideas come from them," Engelhardt said.

MERRILL has more than 700,000 sq. ft. dedicated to fabricating and machining activities, serving industries such as aerospace, defense, heavy equipment, and shipbuilding. It employs more than 100 welders as part of its welding and fabricating operations.

Connecting with all of those welders is a priority. That’s why MERRILL has several different ways that welders can provide feedback.

One of Engelhardt’s favorite tools for interaction are "brainstorming events," where a cross-functional team conducts a gemba walk to observe what’s going on in a work cell or area and engage with the welders. (Gemba is a Japanese word that means "the real place," or in this case, the place where the welding is done.) These events provide instantaneous feedback about issues or ideas that can help improve operations, and often the impact can be just as quick.

Opperman offered up an example of a brainstorming idea that came from a welder working in the facility’s nonferrous welding room. Because the room was located a significant distance from the tool crib, MERRILL worked with its welding supply shop to have a vending machine installed in the nonferrous welding room. The welders got easy access to needed consumables, like grinding disks and gas diffusers, and the company was still able to maintain tracking of consumable consumption.

"These brainstorming events really capitalize on low-hanging fruit," Engelhardt said. "These are typically simple, quick fixes."

Another tool at MERRILL is the "Lesson Learned" app that exists within the company’s ERP software. Every single operator on the shop floor has access to the software and can open a ticket if they have a request or a suggestion. When the ticket is submitted, it goes to Engelhardt, Opperman, certain subject matter experts, and ultimately the launch team, which preps jobs to hit the shop floor. The group meets to discuss the ticket, and any response is shared with the person who submitted the ticket.

Engelhardt said that welders use the Lessons Learned app several times per week. It’s a tool that notifies management that the welders could use a little help, whether it be a request for some bracing to be included in the next kit delivery to a weld cell or an observation that a part might be better off with premachining rather than postmachining after welding.

"This is a tool that we use to not only drive that information back to the engineers, but it also drives that information back to management and leadership," Engelhardt said. "You can start to hear and see where the issues are."

Additionally, MERRILL enjoys regularly occurring meetings, such as a preshift talk where issues related to safety, quality, delivery, and cost can take place. This occurs alongside more structured efforts like regular

kaizen

events, where a cross-functional team comes together to work on a specific production challenge, spending several days in a conference area and on the shop floor, to devise a suitable remedy to the situation. MERRILL wants to ensure that welders and their co-workers have ample opportunity to speak their minds about what can help them to be more productive.

Avoid Rework

One of the killers of production efficiency is rework. It costs time and money to take back the flagged work and repair it, if the customer hasn’t scrapped it altogether. It interrupts production schedules and takes time away from projects for which customers are paying.

This is where a thoroughly defined welding procedure specification (WPS) can make a difference. If the job is done correctly, the WPS should detail the welding process, the weld types, the weld sizes, power source settings, wire size, shielding gas, and other information that removes any guesswork and puts the welder on the path to success.

Becker said that he is familiar with shops that don’t provide adequate prints or work instructions. The prints might have incorrect welding symbols or none at all. The only instructions might read, "Conform with D1.1."

The lack of specifics leaves important decisions up to the welder. That’s not always a good idea. "When you don’t get a weld size, an inexperienced welder might think, ‘How big am I supposed to make this weld?’ So they’ll put more and more metal into the weld, thinking that it’s definitely not going to fail," Becker said. "But that puts a lot more heat input into that part. That puts stress into that joint."

MERRILL is a code shop, so no variability is allowed. A launch team plans for new jobs before they ever hit the shop floor. The team dictates how the fabrication is done, all the way to the tools and consumables needed for the job. When the job is ready for the floor, the welder should have clear instructions on how to proceed.

To stay on top of quality, MERRILL’s certified welding inspectors and nondestructive test inspectors conduct a quality rating for every weld inspection done on the shop floor. That information gets fed back to supervisors and is reviewed on a monthly basis.

Welder audits are done as well. An audit serves as a quality check on the welder’s execution of the WPS. Are they compliant? Are the resulting welds meeting job specifications?

Even with more than 100 welders, Engelhardt said that MERRILL is committed to maintaining a regular schedule of welder audits. If welders are complying with the welding procedures, quality issues—and possible rework—should be minimal.

Keep It Organized

Organization and cleanliness probably stand out as the most obvious symbols of a lean manufacturing work environment. If welders don’t have to search for tools, for instance, they can spend more time doing what the customer pays for—welding.

"Organization is key," Becker said. "A clean shop is a productive shop."

One of the things that Becker did when Underground Metal Works moved into its new Orlando location was run the electrical outlets and air and gas lines from the ceiling. It keeps the floor clean and eliminates a tripping hazard.

In a 5S environment, supplies and tools are put in their correct places where anyone can find them.

MERRILL, likewise, is a big believer in a clean and organized shop. It often has Red Tag Events, where a cross-functional team will walk through the welding areas asking welders to take a critical look at their tools. When these events occur, mostly on Fridays, the team asks welders to look at old tools, consider new tools that might be needed, and to look around for anything else that might be needed or could be tossed. This keeps the welder routinely thinking about what needs to be nearby to be more efficient, what can be useful in improving the act of welding, and what can be moved into storage.

Standardize Where Possible

Standardization is another hallmark of continuous improvement efforts. Less of anything is easier to keep on top of. Even in high-mix manufacturing environments where standardizing can be difficult, it still is possible.

Welders at MERRILL are charged with achieving high-deposition rates for most welding applications. With that in mind, most jobs are specified for use with 1/16- or 3/32-in. welding wire. A veteran welder is quickly going to recognize that 0.045-in. welding wire doesn’t seem quite right for that job and will question it.

Standardization also applies to equipment. Similar tools and layouts help to keep things familiar for the welders and limit the amount of training needed.

Some welding cells at MERRILL can’t be standardized given the complexity and size of some work, but some cells do have some similarities. In these cells, a welder will have access to a jib crane or standard hoist for material handling; a modular worktable, which allows for quick setup of fixtures; shielding gases plumbed right to the cell; and welding equipment. The cell also has a print stand or a print board for them to refer and organize paperwork that comes with the job.

"We also have a focus on 5S [the Japanese lean manufacturing philosophy that stands for sort, straighten, shine, standardize, and sustain], so there’s always a broom and shovel there. We want them to clean up any debris after the welding job is done," Engelhardt said.

Becker also believes that shops should standardize on certain aspects of cell layout. First, he likes equipment on wheels, making it very convenient to reconfigure a workspace should changes be needed. Second, hydraulic welding tables make it easier for the welder to tackle workpieces at different heights, such as sitting rather than standing.

Letting the Welders Weld

Skilled welders are prized assets for any metal fabrication company. That’s why it makes good business sense to let those welders do what they do best. Having those same welders hunting for parts or even the next job is a waste of time and talent.

Becker recalled how at one structural steel fabrication shop he worked at, one person performed fabricating activities just to support the welders. This person fabricated all the end caps, gussets, base plates, and knife plates. That was his sole responsibility. The welders didn’t have to worry about cutting the parts out and punching holes. It was done for them. Becker said that expedited jobs because, after the welders finished one job, they were able to jump on the next one with little worry or delay because everything was there for the job.

MERRILL wants its welders welding, not spending time wandering around the plant looking for the next job. That’s why it has inventory specialists who prep kits for the welders.

"The parts show up. It’s the right quantity. They’ve been checked, inspected, and they are ready to go," Opperman said.

The inventory team acts as sort of a personal shopper for the welder. Someone picks up parts off the burn tables, from the press brake, or from another plant or outside supplier. The parts are collected and kitted. It’s really a bill of material for that build. Not only is the material prepped and ready, but the welder is familiar with what’s arriving. One of the supervisor’s tasks is to brief the welder on the day’s schedule and what to expect with the jobs.

Measure It, Improve It

To properly manage something, you need quantifiable data, and that only comes by keeping track of certain metrics. A gut is best for processing food, not driving business decisions.

MERRILL relies on two primary metrics: project hours and welding activity.

The tracking of project hours—along with other key metrics related to safety, delivery, quality, and cost—are visible for all to see on what the company calls its Green Zone boards. The hours are reviewed on a weekly basis. If actual hours of welding activity exceed budgeted hours, an investigation can take place to see what is causing the discrepancy.

The welders also are charged with logging into the ERP system to record what they are doing during a shift. Welding is considered a "direct" charge, and most other activity is considered an "indirect" charge, according to Engelhardt. Welding time is good because the work being done is work that the customer is paying for.

"But it also lets us know if we may have an issue in an area, such as a maintenance issue. The indirect codes that an operator can clock into give us an idea of what we need to attack and improve it," Engelhardt said.

That’s where a team can come together to prepare a plan for improvement and a schedule of follow-up checks to see if the remedy is working. If those indirect charges return to expected levels, the team can feel assured that the issue has been addressed, and they can turn their attention to something else.

Final Advice

Lean manufacturing entails a lot of different improvement philosophies and tools. For a metal fabricating company that has never really embarked on a formal lean journey, it can be pretty intimidating, even more so if the welding department is the focus.

But the journey of a thousand steps begins with the first one. That’s why even a simple project, like time for a welder to clean and organize a cell, can get the continuous improvement effort going. When welders see that such activities can make their lives easier, they’ll be open to engaging in other such projects.

Engelhardt said that it all begins with the welders. They know the job. They know the challenges. They, more than likely, know the solution to a problem that might leave others scratching their heads. That’s why leadership needs to engage with these problem-solvers.

"Our leadership style is to be visible, be present, and be with the team on a daily basis," he said. "When people feel comfortable, they will tell you what is needed to be successful."
