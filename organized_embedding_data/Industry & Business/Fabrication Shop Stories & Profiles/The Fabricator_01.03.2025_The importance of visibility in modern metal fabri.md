I

t’s not an episode of "Undercover Boss," where a company owner dons a disguise to work among employees, but it was pretty darn close.

Cody Lee, 29 at the time, joined Sidney, Ohio-based job shop and contract manufacturer Everyday Technologies as its lean director in April 2021. The owners of the business, brothers Mike and Bruce Toal, knew that Lee was in the process of purchasing the company from them, but everyone else was kept in the dark. This was a chance for a newcomer to walk the shop floor and ask questions about everything. (For more about Lee’s transition from the commercial construction industry to owner of a metal fabricating company, check out "How one millennial entrepreneur gambled with no fabrication experience" on

thefabricator.com

.)

By August 2021, Lee had closed on the business. After a few months on the job, he also was open to significant changes.

The previous owners were in their 60s and were not investing heavily in the company. For instance, a high-volume powder coating line at a nearby leased facility was OK when it was working, but that was not always the case. Another example was a CO2 laser cutting machine that had 150,000 hours on it, performing well enough to still cut parts, but really lacking when compared to modern-day fiber laser cutting machines.

"It was a Band-Aid approach to things. Just keep things going. There was no real significant investment in the company happening anymore," Lee said.

The first item on the agenda was a state-of-the-art automated powder coating line with a five-stage pretreatment system that was set up at the company’s Sidney facility. (Everyday Technologies also has a facility for its contract manufacturing business in Wapakoneta, Ohio.) This allowed the company to shed one extra lease and eliminate a ton of excessive material handling required to get parts over to the old powder coating facility. Everyday Technologies now has the automated finishing system for its smaller parts and a batch booth for larger parts or when the automated line undergoes a color change.

To ensure the powder coating line is fed consistently, Lee also purchased a 10-kW TRUMPF solid-state laser cutting machine with a compact loading and unloading unit and three new AMADA press brakes, two HRB 1303s and an HRB 2204. In the past three years, that’s almost $5.5 million in capital equipment expenditures.

Even with all of these newfound capabilities, Lee knew his company was still lacking the ability to gather real-time information from the shop floor. This approach to running a business based on hunches and skilled guessing was an obstacle to putting the organization in a position to fully maximize efforts to increase profitability and grow the company.

The Need to Know What Was Happening

Everyday Technologies had an enterprise resource planning (ERP) software package, but it was limited in what it could offer. A part could be quoted, and a job could be created if won, but that was about it. Some general notes could be included on the job router with the basic operational steps. But once the job was released to the floor, it was on its own. The front office could tell that a job was active on the floor, but not really where it was.

In addition to that, the ERP offering had no purchasing, accounting, or inventory functionality. Accounting was done in QuickBooks. Purchasing and inventory was a manual effort, which could prove particularly frustrating for a shop looking to make on-time customer deliveries.

What would happen is that a router would get released, but before it would get released to the floor, it would land on the purchasing manager’s desk. That person would have to make a judgement as to whether enough sheet metal of that particular thickness was on the shop floor or whether it needed to be ordered. The same thinking would apply to things such as insertion hardware like weld nuts. But just because you make an order for raw material or third-party components doesn’t mean it’s going to be there when the job is ready to progress on the shop floor.

"So, you’re really kind of guessing," Lee said. "It was very common for us to have a lot of WIP on the floor because something like electrical boxes were waiting on hinges."

Of course, the opposite of that scenario is the need to carry safety stock, ensuring something like raw material is there just in case the educated guessing might be off a bit. That ties up cash that can be used for more constructive investments or saved to help the company survive the inevitable economic slowdowns.

Lee said that the previous company owners had given thought to upgrading its ERP infrastructure, so the need was recognized. However, nothing was ever really acted upon.

That gave him the opportunity to make his own decision. He elected to go with Plex because of its approach to addressing his concerns about accounting, material planning, and operations oversight. Everyday Technologies kicked off the implementation project in July 2022.

The new ERP system lets Everyday Technologies assign and manage multiple part numbers with one order, permitting schedulers to release parts individually to the floor. This cadence allows the shop floor to work on those projects that are close to shipping and avoid the fabrication of parts that just become WIP.

Getting to Work on the Implementation

Picking an ERP software is the easy part. Implementing it is when the real work begins.

"Everybody has to work together on something like this because we’re not a $100 million company," Lee said.

Everyday Technologies brought in Cumulus Consulting to assist in the ERP migration. The consultants had two big tasks: train everyone at Everyday Technologies on how to use the software and ensure that the ERP had all of the necessary elements to make it functional when it was ready to go live.

Training sessions were broken down along disciplinary areas, such as accounting, engineering, order entry, and purchasing. Core team members from each of those disciplines attended training sessions that covered their responsibilities. These training sessions, of which Cumulus conducted two to three per week, typically delved into the specifics of how to use the software. Lee said the consultants provided homework assignments to those attending the training sessions with expectations that everyone would be ready to move forward at the next meeting.

Simultaneously, Everyday Technologies was building up the software from scratch basically. Lee said that with the previous ERP software, jobs that entailed four parts, for example, would be released to the shop floor at one time and with one part number. This could prove especially challenging if the parts involved different fabricating processes, such as one requiring laser cutting, forming, and welding and another just needing to be laser-cut. In the months after the ERP implementation kick-off, engineers were re-creating already-existing part drawings, breaking them down into individual files and assigning part numbers where they were needed. Even with the part numbers "exploding," Lee said, it was a necessary exercise to ensure smoother sailing down the road.

But this dedication to creating useful engineering files came with sacrifice. Engineers had to continue doing their jobs, in addition to the file creation, but something had to give with the additional responsibility.

"We couldn’t program and quote new work efficiently," Lee said. "So honestly, we kind of missed a lot of good opportunities because we were so focused on this."

To alleviate some of the pressure, Lee decided that only new jobs were going to be processed in Plex, allowing older jobs and material orders to work their way through the older ERP software. Because of this, staff personnel didn’t have to worry about trying to re-create jobs for WIP that was sitting on the shop floor.

So when the day came in July 2023 to flip the switch on the new Plex software, it was kind of anticlimactic. People were still using the old software for existing work, and the slew of new Plex-originated jobs still had yet to materialize.

A couple of weeks later, however, the situation changed. More Plex job orders were being seen. This coincided with a pickup in customer orders. It created a very hectic time as people had to remember just where to go in the new software to find the information they needed to keep work moving, and the shop floor had to get used to applying labels with bar codes to raw material and parts for tracking purposes.

As the rest of 2023 played out, Everyday Technologies became more familiar with using the Plex software and following the steps necessary to support reliable information gathering. Stress levels subsided, and confidence grew among the staff.

Being Able to See What’s Happening

"The ‘aha’ moment for us didn’t come until the following year. We were finally settled in. We started to see the benefits," Lee said. "We’d ask ourselves, ‘Could you imagine doing this with our old software?’"

For the first time, the Everyday Technologies staff had confidence that what was being seen on screen and in reports was an accurate picture of what was happening on the shop floor. The visibility they had was the beginning of the end for the educated guessing game that was once required to run the business.

The first noticeable "win" for the metal fabricator came just three months after the new ERP system went live. The company saw a 20% savings in inventory costs.

Attaching a bar code to all raw material, parts, and shipments allows the metal fabricator to have real-time visibility into its inventory, WIP, and deliveries, something it didn’t have with its prior ERP system.

Plex

Thanks to the Plex software’s material resources planning module, material purchasers confidently acquired the amount of material that was necessary, not what they thought might be necessary. The software is able to take into account what’s on hand, what’s going to be needed over the next week, and how long service center lead times are. Everyday Technologies has gotten closer to a just-in-time delivery scenario for material than it ever has before.

The company also has similar visibility into their fabricating efforts. Before any activity takes place, a scan is required to let the software know what’s happening. When a fabricating process is completed, the system is updated, sometimes with a new label. The labels are a reflection of the trip through the shop: a raw material label is exchanged for a WIP label and ultimately a finished goods label. Front office personnel now can reply with confidence when they are asked what the status of a job is.

With the ability to track this type of information more accurately, Lee said that management is able to make better business decisions because they have faith in the data. Something as important as a material costs-to-sales comparison now is much more powerful because the company is dealing with real data.

Order entry has been greatly simplified as well. Many of the company’s larger customers are utilizing electronic data interchange and sending over orders that automatically get fed into the ERP system, updating material orders, creating bills of material, and generating work orders for the shop floor. Some customers still send over purchase orders, but the Plex user interface permits the quick processing of jobs, allowing an order entry clerk to create necessary work orders, even splitting them if necessary, and get the work instructions released to production. Lee said that Everyday Technologies has gone from three full-time order-entry clerks down to one, and that’s not even a full-time role.

Getting Ready to Grow

When Everyday Technologies was doing its due diligence in investigating its ERP software choices, it visited a shop that used Plex in the Cincinnati area. The company, which offered fabricating and stamping services, generated four times the annual revenue as Everyday Technologies. Lee realized that he would be able to grow with Plex, which influenced him when it came time to make the software purchase.

"If you came into our business right now, you might laugh because we have so much equipment. You also might say that we could be doing way more in sales," Lee said. "Well, I know that. That’s what we’re setting ourselves up to do."

The next goal is to hit $15 million in annual sales. Lee said that he’s confident enough given the previous investments and improvements that have been made in areas like ontime customer deliveries. The internal staff is not the only party noticing changes taking place at Everyday Technologies.

But it doesn’t end there. Lee said that he’s looking to add automated quoting software that will not only speed up replies to RFQs but also would be able to create bills of materials quickly when the jobs are won. That material then would feed right into the Plex ERP.

The desire to automate processes, both in the front office and on the shop floor, is no secret at Everyday Technologies. The plan is being shared with everyone because they all will be integral to the company’s growth, according to Lee.
