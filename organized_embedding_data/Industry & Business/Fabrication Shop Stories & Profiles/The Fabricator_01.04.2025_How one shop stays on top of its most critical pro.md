# How one shop stays on top of its most critical processes: Hardware insertion

By

Dan Davis

With customers in the aerospace, medical, and electrical industries, Midwest Metal Products has a hardware insertion department that is active over three shifts.

Images: Midwest Metal Products

T

he big job of the afternoon has made its way to the hardware insertion department. A laser cutting machine cut the blanks, and a press brake operator worked his magic to add some louvers, creating the bottom of what’s to become electrical boxes. All that needs to be added are five self-clinching nuts and 10 studs to the 100 workpieces.

That sounds a lot simpler than it is. Does the hardware insertion equipment operator know exactly where the nuts and studs are to go? Does the operator have all the hardware for the job? Is it the right hardware? That’s a lot of variables for a step in the metal fabrication process that’s sometimes taken for granted.

Midwest Metal Products, Cedar Rapids, Iowa, doesn’t have the luxury of overlooking any aspect of its hardware insertion program. Since it accounts for a large portion of the company’s production hours on an annual basis, those people placing hardware into workpieces need to be engaged in the process to avoid delays and costly rework.

"We’ve been in business for 60 years, so we’ve gotten pretty good about learning from mistakes that we’ve made," said Paul Warren, Midwest Metal Products’ production supervisor and maintenance manager. "For the most part, we can generally fix everything [if something should go wrong], but we build a lot into the process even before the operator gets a hold of it to ensure a quality part."

Here are five ways that this Midwestern metal fabricator maintains quality in its hardware insertion department.

1. Preparation Makes a Difference

Midwest Metal Products has seen the hardware insertion part of its business grow over the years, not just in terms of quantity of jobs but also in complexity. The work the company does for its customers in the aerospace, medical, and electrical industries contains more features in tighter confines when compared to work from two or three decades ago. Today’s smarter devices and equipment place new demands for those in metal fabricating.

How complex can it get? Warren said his company has processed a job with more than 200 hardware pieces inserted into a workpiece that was still small enough that one operator could handle it.

That’s why jobs, both complex and not so complex, are subject to performance risk analyses (PRA) before they hit the shop floor. Identifying problems before they get to the hardware insertion department is a smart way to minimize headaches in an area that requires plenty of focus.

"With every job that we have coming in, we’ll have our engineering team look at it, and we’ll do a PRA pretty much on every part number. That way we can identify each of the sequences and look at possible areas for failure," Warren said.

When it comes to hardware insertion, the engineering team has a lot to consider. Is the right hole size called out for the given hardware? Can the operator get to the hole with the available tooling? Does tooling need to be made? Is there a specific order of operations that needs to be followed to produce the part?

Finding out those answers and leaning on experience, the team working on the PRA can create an approach to the job that is simplified as much as possible, while still being streamlined enough for efficient processing.

2. Checklists Boost Quality Assurance

Because Midwest Metal Products is ASD9100 and ISO 9001 certified, customers know that the company’s quality processes are solid. They have a plan to ensure poor-quality products don’t leave the shipping dock.

Part of that effort is extensive part inspection. This isn’t just a job for the quality assurance team; the equipment operators are involved as well.

An inspection checklist accompanies every job that heads to the hardware insertion department. The operator is asked to consult the checklist before the job even begins.

The operator is asked to check the hardware, the work sequence, and any additional notes that might be included, particularly if a customer is asking for something that’s atypical. To assist with the preparation, the hardware insertion machine operator can have prints made especially for the process or even look at the 3D model of the part, which has color-coded hardware pieces. The model also can be rotated 360 degrees, if necessary, for a full view.

Once the process is completed, the operator is asked to conduct a first-piece inspection. Warren said that it instructs the operators to look at the part like they are seeing it for the first time, running through the checklist again, but also reviewing hardware installation and looking for any damage potentially caused during the process.

From there, the workpiece then goes off to the quality inspection department. The checklist is consulted again, and the part is inspected.

"Our system is really set up to make sure that we’re getting it done, not only on the first part, but all the way through the process," Warren said.

3. Equipment Operators Need to Be Focused

An operator running one of Midwest Metal Products’ 16 hardware insertion machines can’t be lackadaisical. They need to be engaged to stay on top of the variety of tasks associated with the job on a given day.

"An operator might be working on one to 10 different operations over a shift, going from an aerospace part directly to a medical part," Warren said. "They’ll be working with different hardware, different machines, and different customer specifications, all on a day-to-day basis."

Warren added that a hardware insertion machine operator has to be focused while on the clock. If the mind wanders and the operator is not paying attention, the wrong hardware could be installed or inserted into the wrong hole. The ability to zone in on the task at hand and block out any distractions is a good trait for someone to have in this department.

Operators in Midwest Metal Products’ hardware insertion department play a key role in quality assurance efforts.

4. Hardware Collection Should Be Prioritized

Midwest Metal Products’ hardware insertion department sees a lot of activity over three shifts. To keep up with the work, someone is in charge of hardware organization. Working out of a locked cage, where the company has an inventory of about $1 million worth of hardware, this invidual pulls the correct hardware and tooling for each operation, placing them in bins that are then delivered to the hardware insertion machine operator for the job.

That same cage attendant also is charged with maintaining organization within the storage area. Everything needs to be labeled, and an inventory count kept on hardware and tooling.

For Midwest Metal Products, this role is not associated with inventory or parts management. It’s a key part of the hardware insertion team.

5. Automation Isn’t the Answer for Everything

While the addition of a robot or a cobot seems to be more commonplace in bending and welding departments across the metal fabricating world, that’s not necessarily the case for Midwest Metal Products’ hardware insertion department. The variation of jobs from one customer to the next makes it difficult to justify implementation of such automation.

"If we knew we had a purchase order for 10,000 parts, then we would definitely look into getting a robot or a cobot," Warren said.

Even when the company does have large contracts with a customer, Warren said those parts are not conducive to being automated. Technology still is limited when it comes to replicating a human’s quick decision-making and fine motor skills.

To assist with quality efforts, operators at hardware insertion machines have access to blueprints specifically drawn to detail where hardware goes on the workpiece and to 3D models that can be expanded and rotated.

Final Words of Advice

When asked if he might have any advice for metal fabricators who are looking to improve operations in their hardware insertion department, Warren said it helps to have good working relationships with both internal co-workers, specifically engineers, and the customers.

Inevitably, despite all good intentions, a job will hit the shop floor, and it will become immediately noticeable to those operating the hardware insertion machines that there might be a better way to do it. Maybe a piece of hardware needs to be placed before a bend is made or a fastener needs to be welded in, not pressed. The ability to introduce that type of constructive criticism into a conversation with an engineer leads to everyone getting smarter about part design and efficient flow when it comes to hardware insertion.

The ability to have a similar conversation with a customer is even more beneficial to those involved. The customer learns that the metal fabricator is a source of design for manufacturability expertise, and the fab shop can prove its worth to the customer. A simple design change, such as tapping a hole instead of installing a piece of hardware, can be significant.

The hardware insertion department might be near the end of the metal fabricating value-add process, but perhaps it should be front of mind for those involved in sheet metal parts design.
