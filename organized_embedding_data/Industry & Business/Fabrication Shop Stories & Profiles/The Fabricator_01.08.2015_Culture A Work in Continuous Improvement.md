A

successful fabrication shop wouldn’t purchase a $1 million machine tool and then fail to maintain it, yet some shops are content to let a successful company culture fend for itself in the face of rapid expansion and stressful times when capacity is stretched. In the end, the company culture that developed organically, but later died a natural death, leaving an apathetic workforce, might cost a fab shop thousands in late deliveries, rework, and even lost customers.

With that in mind, I congratulated Jean Pitzo, CEO; Deb Benning, vice president, sales and marketing; and Keith Stout, vice president, operations, Ace Metal Crafts, after a quick visit to their Bensenville, Ill., facility. The company, which came in at No. 39 in

The FABRICATOR

’s 2015 FAB 40 list of successful fabricating operations, is projected to earn $19 million in sales revenue this year and now has 115 employees that work over two shifts. It’s been able to expand by serving a very demanding niche, custom stainless steel parts and assemblies for industries that expect topnotch welding and, sometimes, immaculate finishes. It’s a business that requires a lot of engineering and fabricating talent, particularly in the welding and grinding area.

Pitzo welcomed my compliment, but also acknowledged that Ace Metal Crafts’ can-do culture is not just a coincidence. It’s the result of a continuous improvement effort.

"It’s all built on trust," she said, "which isn’t built overnight."

What are some of the ways that the company helps to build that trust?

1. Invest in the workforce.

During the visit, several workers from the laser cutting area for one of the company’s three value streams were involved in a

kaizen

event in the conference room. The workers looking to eliminate waste in the laser cutting process were still being paid even though they weren’t pushing parts downstream. (It should be noted that this particular lean manufacturing exercise was led by a representative from the Toyota Production System Support Center Inc., a nonprofit entity that educates small and mid-sized manufacturers, local governments, and other nonprofits in the ways of the Toyota Production System. Ace Metal Crafts applied for the opportunity and is one of about 50 organizations that the group will work with this year.)

Pitzo said that investing in the development of team leaders for the shop floor is another area of importance. These individuals are then able to foster the culture with newer workers unaware of what makes the company special.

2. Share the financial information.

Employees know where the company stands in terms of financial performance and what it takes to "make the numbers," Pitzo said. When financial goals are met, employees are paid a quarterly bonus. When the goals aren’t hit, everyone knows where they fell short. Everyone has to work together to contribute to the company’s overall financial success.

3. Make It Fun.

Milestones, birthdays, and anniversaries are celebrated. Employees are members of book clubs. Everyone rallies around the success of Chicago sports teams, particularly the Stanley Cup-winning Chicago Blackhawks. In a newly pro-duced corporate video, a team leader actually confesses to getting butterflies in his stomach when he turns into the company parking lot each morning. Each day represents new and exciting challenges, and employees at Ace Metal Crafts look forward to tackling them together.

It should come as no surprise that the company culture has an official seal of approval from a pretty impressive organization. The American Psychological Association (APA) named Ace Metal Crafts as a winner of a Best Practices Award as part of the organization’s Psychologically Healthy Workplace Award. After submitting a thorough application and inviting outside evaluators to come in and talk with employees, Ace Metal Crafts was recognized by the APA for its efforts to engage employees, encourage work-life balance, invest in growth and development opportunities, and maintain a safe and healthy work environment and workforce.

When a company culture is running 100 percent green-time over multiple shifts, that culture needs to be maintained. If companies wait for the red light to go on, it’s too late.

While the award is nice, the real impact is visible in the company’s success over the years. Pitzo said that it’s no coincidence that the commitment to lean manufacturing following a dramatic slowdown around 2001 coincided with the company’s recent business expansion. The employees have made it work.

Benning said others notice the employee engagement as well.

"If we can get prospective customers out on the shop floor, we pretty much know we can close the deal," she said.

When a company culture is running 100 percent green-time over multiple shifts, that culture needs to be maintained. If companies wait for the red light to go on, it’s too late.
