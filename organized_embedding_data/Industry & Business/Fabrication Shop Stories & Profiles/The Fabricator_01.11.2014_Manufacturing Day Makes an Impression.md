# Manufacturing Day Makes an Impression

From the Editor-in-Chief

Negative stereotypes about metal fabricating are destroyed each time a person gets to visit a modern manufacturing facility

“I

had no idea what manufacturing was,” my 16-year-old son Tate told me as we were winding up our tour as part of a local Manufacturing Day event on Oct. 3. Even though I write about metal fabricating for a living and share stories of visits I make to manufacturing facilities all over North America, he still couldn’t put together just what I was talking about. For him, seeing was believing.

Super Steel, a contract manufacturer in Milwaukee, invited us and others from the surrounding area to see what manufacturing was all about. They hosted nearly 180 students, teachers, and other community representatives, feeding them baked goods and the metal manufacturing gospel.

And the visitors were buying it. In our tour group alone, almost a dozen welding students stared in amazement at the welding tests demanded of new hires at Super Steel. The test for a multipass butt joint on a piece of plate—where each pass is inspected and after the weld is completed, a bend test is done—was just one example that clearly defines the company’s expectations. But these young welders were reminded that they would have plenty of time to prepare and work with a mentor before they would have to stand up for the test. “You just need a commitment to work and a good attitude, and you’ll do just fine,” the tour guide said.

“I’ve been working with manufacturing for 27 years, and I’m more excited about it today than I was 27 years ago.”

—Dirk Smith, Super Steel

Super Steel wants to encourage optimism among these youngsters because the company needs welders—a lot of them. The fabricator is on course to achieve $60 million in sales in 2014, up from $27 million in 2010. It currently has 386 employees, 150 of whom are welders. Dirk Smith, the company’s president and CEO and a former welder himself, said he could bring on another 50 welders if he could find them. That’s why he keeps pushing the “sex appeal and sizzle” associated with metal manufacturing.

“I’ve been working with manufacturing for 27 years, and I’m more excited about it today than I was 27 years ago,” Smith said.

He isn’t alone in his excitement. More than 1,600 companies opened their doors to let the world find out just what manufacturing is all about and how it affects their world. Early estimates suggest that about 50,000 people in the U.S. and Canada had the chance to tour one of these locations. Even the heavy hitters from Capitol Hill showed up to support the manufacturing cause, with President Barack Obama making a stop at Millennium Steel Service in Princeton, Ind., and U.S. Sen. Ron Johnson, R-Wis., taking the podium at Super Steel. There was genuine excitement for an industry that simply doesn’t grab the spotlight that often.

The 16-year-old certainly got into it as he looked at the hulking top section of a locomotive in Super Steel’s rear bay. The sections, which are made from 12,000 separate metal parts fabricated at the company’s 450,000-sq.-ft. facility, are sent to assembly plants in the U.S. and to places like Australia and Brazil, where they are attached to the frames. With that in mind, he then had the chance to see how the plate is cut, formed, welded, assembled, and painted, ultimately creating the metal monster that’ll move the materials and goods necessary to keep the U.S. economy moving forward.

“This is really impressive,” my son said. (While it may not seem like a very in-depth statement, it is actually more than I might be able to get out of him after a day at school.)

As we ended the tour and walked back to the car, he told me he was pretty sure that he wouldn’t be any good at welding, but maybe he could do something else. I agreed that welding is a talent that some don’t possess and reminded him of some of the other people we saw out on the plant floor—the machine operator, the quality assurance technician, and the shipping and receiving clerk. They likely didn’t begin as a welder, but they still found a career path with the company.

On the way home, the conversation drifted back to his real dream—being a drummer in a Kiss cover band. However, when I directed the discussion back to having skills that can lead to a career and a steady paycheck—while he chases the dream during evenings and on weekends—he was not defensive. He understands what manufacturing can offer. He’s open to it.

Others who participated in Manufacturing Day likely are too.
