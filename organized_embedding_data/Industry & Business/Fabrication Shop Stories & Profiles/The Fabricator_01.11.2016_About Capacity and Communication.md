# About Capacity and Communication

[TARİH: 01.11.2016 The Fabricator]

Executive Summary

A shop’s salesperson arrives in the morning, reviews emails, and then checks in with his colleagues—usually estimators and other front-office personnel. He’s the shop’s sole salesperson, and as such he starts making his calls for the day.

He mostly talks to current customers, but he also prospects a bit—particularly to satisfy his bosses, who hired him to hunt for new work in new industries. But landing such work is slow-going.

When he talks with the estimator about why a request for quote (RFQ) was declined, it’s often because the shop doesn’t have the equipment to produce the part competitively, or simply lacks the capacity.

When he talks or emails with customers, he’s often checking the status of a quote. Will the customer move ahead? The buyer doesn’t have an answer—yet. And the waiting game continues.

When he doesn’t win an order, it’s usually because his price isn’t low enough. And he probably could sell more if the shop had more capacity or newer equipment.

When he does win an order, the shop starts with a customer print with typical callouts, but of course shop engineers do need some clarification; and it can take time to get the answers they need.

The previous narrative describes the typical sales experience in metal fabrication, according to the respondents of this survey, except in one respect: A little more than half of respondents don’t have a dedicated salesperson. That’s not surprising, considering that most of this industry consists of small shops, where people wear many hats. At smaller fabricators especially, the person who communicates with customers (often the owner or CEO) is also the one who plays an estimating role and develops quotes.

For the most part, fabricators don’t perform design-for-manufacturability (DFM) services for the bulk of their order volume; and when they do, most say that DFM helps win orders less than half of the time. This isn’t to say there aren’t exceptions. Some shops (including some profiled in this study) do in fact sell their design services extensively and get involved early in product development. But according to this survey, that approach isn’t the norm.

This study reveals a hurry-up-and-wait sales environment. More than half said that sales process delays are usually caused by customers waiting to pull the trigger on an order. But once they pull the trigger, the shop had better move fast.

Whether a fabricator has a sales team in place or not, most don’t expect to hire a salesperson within the next 12 months. This implies that, outside of waiting for customer approvals, sales really isn’t the constraint these days.

Good relationships come from good communication. And without the ability to communicate and understand what’s being sold, a salesperson in metal fabrication probably won’t go far.

So what is the constraint? It’s about capacity. Many respondents said that (besides low price) the reason they don’t win new business is because of insufficient equipment capacity or technology.

Still, the sales process isn’t without its delays, and most of them come from communication challenges—that is, engineers, salespeople, and customers going back and forth to clarify details on a certain order.

This is perhaps why, when shops do hire salespeople, they look for communication skills first, followed by the ability to understand technical information.

Only 18 percent said relationship-building skills mattered most. So do fabricators really think relationship-building isn’t important? Not really. Good relationships, after all, are the

result

of good communication. And without the ability to communicate and understand what’s being sold, a salesperson in metal fabrication—or anywhere else, for that matter—probably can’t go far.

Demographics

"If you commit to me, I’ll commit to you"

About 15 years ago, Jim Lauer changed the direction of Weaver Precision Fabrication & Finishing’s sales strategy—an especially bold move, considering he had just purchased the Akron, Ohio, shop three years before. He ended contracts with two independent sales reps, then sat down with a half dozen of the company’s largest customers.

"I told buyers, ‘I have been spending a lot of time, resources, and money chasing business elsewhere, when there may be a lot more business here. If you commit to me, I’ll commit to you.’"

Ever since, Lauer has focused on winning more work from Weaver’s existing customer base. The sales strategy doesn’t follow the typical narrative of a fab shop under the direction of new, hungry, sales-focused owners. Lauer previously owned a distributorship of safety equipment.

"At the distributorship, I kept calling on companies like Weaver, and it sounded like a lot more fun than peddling safety gloves out of the trunk of my car."

So he ended up selling his stake in the distributorship and, with a business partner, purchasing Weaver in 1998. He spent a year learning the business and discovered what customers really needed, which was in fact pretty straightforward. "They needed good parts, they needed them on time, and they needed them at a fair price," Lauer said. "It’s a pretty basic equation. We’re not splitting atoms here.

"And as I keep telling everyone here, customers pay our bills," he added. "Not just some of the bills—

all

of the bills."

So if customers pay the bills, why not start calling around to search for more? In truth, this pretty much reflected Lauer’s initial strategy. After buying the business, he worked with independent sales reps to help drum up new orders from new customers. But the reps weren’t working out as planned. He ended up spoon-feeding leads their way, and they were only marginally successful.

Lauer didn’t blame them for this, though. Much of it is just the nature of custom fabrication. "It’s a big deal for these buyers to bring in a new vendor," Lauer said. "They’re putting their future in your hands."

Today Lauer is vice president, his wife Marian is CEO, and both are co-owners of the company. Most new customer orders come from recommendations from existing clients and other relationships. And the strategy apparently has paid off. The Great Recession aside, in recent years Weaver’s sales has grown between 15 and 18 percent annually.

But what about diversification? As it turns out, Weaver’s customers are themselves diversified. About half the shop revenue comes from companies in the packaging and material handling industries. These sectors serve a range of industries, from food processing to automotive.

Landing additional work from existing customers (who ideally are themselves diversified) can bring in significant revenue and boost cash flow. And in custom fabrication, healthy cash flow is critical. It allows shops to invest in new facilities and equipment, which in turn can help a fabricator better serve customer needs. This, as it turns out, is exactly what Weaver has done.

The Sales Team

People buy from people

Walker Knox, Business Development Manager

Mid-Atlantic Fabrication & Finishing, Knightdale, N.C.

When cousins Terry Reid and Walker Knox bought Mid-Atlantic Fabrication & Finishing last year, the two had no doubt what their roles would be. A U.S. Marine Corps colonel, retiring as a base commander in Chicago after 30 years of service, Reid

knows

operations management. His cousin Knox

knows

sales.

Although Knox is relatively new to sheet metal, he’s no stranger to technology. He went to school for chemistry and landed a job that specialized in radiation chemistry for shrinkable plastics and polymers. "After a few years, I discovered that I’m a people person, and the technical field just wasn’t for me. So I went into sales for the same company."

In the ensuing years Knox traveled extensively, first selling for the plastics company and then moving on to electronics, telecom, and finally pipe and tube coatings. At that point he started talking with his cousin Reid, and the two saw an opportunity to purchase a fabricator in Knightdale, N.C., not far from their childhood home. It was time for a change.

Now Mid-Atlantic’s business development manager, Knox approaches customers with the fabricator’s niche in mind. As the adage says, among three factors—reliable delivery, highest quality, and the lowest price—fabricators can offer two. Mid-Atlantic chose to focus on reliable delivery and high quality while offering a competitive (but not necessarily the lowest) price.

When Knox visits a customer, he asks a few simple questions. "First, I say, ‘If Mid-Atlantic Fabrication were perfect, what would we be doing that we aren’t doing now?’"

Knox knows how to sell technical products and services—not the most common skill. But why is this such a rarity, exactly? To someone who hasn’t performed sales before, the nuts and bolts of the job seem relatively straightforward, at least compared to the intricacies of bending and laser cutting. A person with years of manufacturing experience should be able to communicate with technical personnel and uncover opportunities, right? Not necessarily.

As Knox put it, "People buy from people. You need to have a personality that people trust and are willing to confide in. I’m trying to get people at our customers to tell me where they hurt. That’s hard to do, and if you don’t have the right personality, it just doesn’t work. I’ve met so many smart, technically intelligent people who just didn’t have the personality that meshed with certain customers."

Having the right personality may be even more important in custom fabrication than in other businesses. In previous jobs, Knox said it was almost a given that salespeople would work directly with not just buyers but also the engineers and product designers.

In custom fabrication, that’s not a given. If a fabricator proves itself as a trusted partner, it may work directly with the customer’s engineers, product designers, and manufacturing managers. Once there, everyone can work together to uncover cost-savings.

Getting to that point not only takes reliable delivery and quality, it also takes some finely tuned relationship-building. As Knox explained, this essentially is built on trust, which can be quickly destroyed by a salesperson with the wrong personality for the job.

The Sales Process

A remote-factory-light

Joseph Villareal, Global Business & NPI Development Manager

Cortec Precision Manufacturing, San Jose, Calif.

Cortec Precision Manufacturing was in trouble. When the semiconductor sector took a nosedive about seven years ago, so did Cortec’s business. It had no choice but to lay off more than half its work force. Those remaining had to take a 20 percent cut in pay just to keep the company alive.

"The sacrifice paid off."

So said Joseph Villareal, global business and NPI development manager. The new-product-introduction acronym isn’t there to make his job title sound more impressive. That acronym has in fact been central to Cortec’s dramatic rebirth. From a low of 55 employees during the Great Recession, Cortec today employs 365 people in its plant in San Jose, Calif., and 110 in Austin, Texas.

Seven years ago, shop managers knew they needed to diversify the customer base. Like many custom fabricators, Cortec had no formal sales force. Still, the company didn’t simply ramp up its sales efforts and start prospecting. In fact, the company wanted to move away from being just another sheet metal fabricator chasing piece-part work.

Instead, managers analyzed Cortec’s strengths. For years the company had fabricated tubular and sheet metal frames for semiconductor manufacturing as well as other sectors—and those other sectors are key. The company’s expertise applied not just to the semiconductor field but also to configurable machine manufacturing across various industries.

The people at Cortec know how to design and fabricate a tubular and sheet metal frame. Of course, this process development knowledge isn’t particularly valuable unless the company is involved early in the product design phase. To that end, Cortec added to its front-office talent, created several cross-functional NPI teams, and instituted a process it calls Early Supplier Developmental Input. It’s all designed to speed a product to market.

"This means we’re at the table with design engineering teams at various companies throughout the world," Villareal said. Central to this process is a risk assessment called the NPI Qualification Plan, which focuses on the future of the equipment build, so companies can qualify other suppliers in lowcost regions. As Villareal explained, "The risk assessment on the front end saves customers huge amounts of investment dollars that were considered project losses before."

When the company began its frame program, though, all wasn’t smooth sailing. "For our first frame program, we fell on our faces," Villareal recalled. "But the customer was willing to work with us. We had to do a first-article about 30 times, because our processes were out of control.

"But once we got the process under control, we were able to document the manufacturing of that frame, and then engage with the customer to send the frame manufacturing overseas. We went to South Korea, and we trained a factory over there that was almost identical to ours."

Cortec essentially shifted its focus to the very beginning of the product lifecycle, to tubular and sheet metal frame manufacturing process development—and not just for the semiconductor sector, but for packaging, food processing, and other industries that need configurable machines. During the development process, the NPI team always considers where production will take place, be it at Cortec or at any other factory around the world.

At this writing the company has launched 237 defect-free frame programs over the past 18 months. Many customers now categorize Cortec not as a sheet metal supplier, but as a "remote factory light."

Cortec still knows sheet metal fabrication. Its equipment produces parts that help prove-out the process. But it’s the

process

, not fabricated parts, that lies at the heart of the value proposition.
