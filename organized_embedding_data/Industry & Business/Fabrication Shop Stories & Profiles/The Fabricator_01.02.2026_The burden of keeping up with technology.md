here’s a lot at stake. There’s a lot of technology out there to be embraced right now with AI and all the different things that we have. It is very challenging financially trying to make all the right decisions and prepare the company to take on that technology."

Allen Mills, president/CEO, NexGen Manufacturing, Knightstown, Ind., made that comment as were talking about his longtime interest in newer approaches to manufacturing, dating all the way back to Windows-based PCs being introduced to shops in the 1990s and his first couple of laser cutting machines. To this day, he still loves thinking about what could be possible, whether it’s new AI-enhanced front office automation or further enhancements to robotic welding cells.

New technology has played a large part in many other metal fabricating companies as well. Just consider The Fabricator’s 2026 Industry Award winner, Dane Manufacturing, Waunakee, Wis. Its own version of a smart factory—with an automated material storage and retrieval system feeding cutting and bending equipment that requires minimal operator involvement—has helped it keep up with almost a 200% increase in sales over the last six years. Dane Manufacturing and others have been able to grow without necessarily having to add throngs of bodies to do the work, which is a good thing because people aren’t lining up to work at fab shops. Technology has made the renaissance of domestic manufacturing possible, and it will play an even more important role in the future.

But it ain’t cheap. Mills made that point as he talked about his own company’s technology investments. When a small fab shop is considering an investment that easily can surpass $1 million for one machine tool, it better be making the right choice.

"The ones that make missteps on those investments, they could be out of business pretty quickly," he said. "There’s a lot at stake."

Mills is serious about what’s at stake at Nex-Gen. When new technology investments are being considered, engineers and shop floor supervisors are part of the discussion. Everyone gets an understanding of what’s possible and what has to be done to get the most out of the investment. These types of capital equipment expenditures aren’t made based on one person’s feeling; an entire team helps ensure the return on investment actually occurs.

The repercussions of a bad equipment investment can set a company back years. A robotic welding cell collecting dust in a corner might have been just a bad decision 10 years ago, but the stakes are much greater today because so many other shops already have automated welding cells up and running. An unsuccessful attempt to make automation work is a victory for nearby competitors that unknowingly are winning jobs because one local metal fabricator can’t match their welding rates.

Accountants recognize the impact that advanced metal fabricating can make in terms of productivity and increased capacity, both of which make it possible to increase sales. Now, they might be uncomfortable with a machine’s price tag, but they eventually come around. What they often don’t understand is the intangible benefit that comes with that expensive equipment. Shops that adopt modern manufacturing technology start to develop a reputation for being industry leaders on the cutting edge of the latest metal fabricating techniques.

Dane Manufacturing has doubled its number of employee since 2021, but even in this aggressive growth phase, it’s been able to keep voluntary turnover to less than 1% per month. Troy Berg, the company’s president/CEO, believes that people want to be part of an organization that strives to be a leader in its industry. A growing company is good for paychecks and internal opportunities for those that want to expand their skills.

"We make an effort to promote from within," Berg said. "Someone can start on the floor running a laser cutter and work his way up to the engineering office, almost doubling his pay."

Those internal opportunities only increase with an expanding business. Sophisticated tools require skilled operators and engineers to keep them running. Again, the financial impact is immediate when these machine tools go down.

Here’s one more thing to consider about technology. If a shop is filled with old equipment built closer to this country’s bicentennial year than its semiquincentennial celebration this year, the owner might have a hard time getting the desired sales price when it’s time for retirement. At this point, that shop is—using some real estate terms—more "rustic" and "charming" than a "turnkey" investment.

Advanced fabricating technology is no guarantee of future success, but such investments do put metal fabricators in a much better position to succeed than others doing what they’ve always done and using the same tools to do it.
