# Demand A moving Target

[TARİH: 01.05.2017 The Fabricator]

The metal fabrication industry

does not need to produce more; it needs to produce more

now

. That in a nutshell is what this study drives home.

Respondents came from a cross section of custom fabrication as well as OEMs, and most said that equipment capacity limitations rarely prevent them from delivering products, even at peak times. In fact, only 19 percent said they had insufficient machine capacity during peak demand periods; 11 percent pointed to supply chain and delivery constraints; but a full 70 percent pointed to labor.

This industry doesn’t use much labor to begin with, at least on an individual company level. As in previous

FABRICATOR

studies, most respondents work at small companies. About 46 percent of respondents said their companies employ fewer than 20 people. These individuals must manage what can be a feast-or-famine business. Almost 20 percent of respondents said that demand varies more than 40 percent between the busiest and slowest times of the year.

Almost half said that they experienced significant drops in demand from at least one major customer during the past three years. And most said this drop didn’t come from a customer moving work to competitors, be they in North America or elsewhere, but instead from declining demand of their customers’ products.

This key finding makes sense when you consider the timing of this survey and the dominance of the small shop. First, the timing: Fabricators over the past three years have ridden the demand roller coaster of oil, gas, mining, and other commodity-related work. When the boom ended, demand for customers’ end products fell.

Second, some operations in this survey sample, particularly very small shops, probably do not have long-term contracts with global OEMs that move manufacturing work around the world. If a major customer pulls work, it’s probably because the end product itself isn’t selling well, or a project (like a construction job) has ended.

To achieve a sustainable, thriving business, fabricators seem to be taking a two-pronged approach. First, they attempt to diversify the customer mix to mitigate demand highs and lows. Second, they strive to respond quickly. That is, they need to produce more now, particularly during periods of peak demand.

When asked what operation causes the greatest challenges during periods of peak demand, respondents gave a variety of answers, and no single manufacturing step truly dominated. Some pointed to welding, assembly, and painting—operations (many of them manual) where multiple parts need to arrive at the right time. Many cited functions that aren’t in the shop at all; they’re in sales, quoting, and estimating. So at least a portion of the labor constraint that 70 percent referred to may not be about the shop floor work force; it may be about indirect labor in the office.

The implication: No one fabrication process is an island. Fabricators are scrutinizing all steps in manufacturing—from quoting to cutting, bending, welding, and shipping—to produce more

now

.
