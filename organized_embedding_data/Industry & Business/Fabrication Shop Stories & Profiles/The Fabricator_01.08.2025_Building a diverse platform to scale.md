N

ot many industries can say they’re dominated by small businesses these days—but metal fabrication can. Operations employing fewer than 50 people make up the vast majority of fabricators in North America. Many have steady customer mixes and long-term relationships. The revenue mix, often highly concentrated, hasn’t changed for years. And they largely operate under the radar. Sales and marketing efforts are pretty much nonexistent. Word-of-mouth and long-term relationships keep work coming in the door.

Shop owners aren’t getting any younger, though. Many are looking for an exit strategy. All this is happening as the reshoring trend—and the hunt for more domestic metal fabrication capacity—continues. This is where Cres Ferrell saw opportunity.

The Right Time to Invest

In 2022, Ferrell had just sold his last venture, a battery testing lab in Gainesville, Ga., and was looking at what to do next. Through his accounting firm and other industry contacts, he noticed a number of small fabrication and machine shops for sale throughout the Southeast.

"There are thousands of these companies out there," he said, "and a lot of them are owned by baby boomers. Within the next decade, many will be looking for a transition."

A mechanical engineer, Ferrell knew the metal fabrication industry, having been exposed to it while attending Georgia Tech in the late 1990s. That’s when, as part of a co-op program, he worked at a bearing manufacturer, which had a small machine shop that supported production. It also outsourced to a number of machining and fabrication operations.

Fast-forward to the early 2020s, when Ferrell and several business partners, including Woodie Eubanks and Mike Naddra, saw a plethora of small fab shops for sale. Could these small operations scale by being part of a larger enterprise, one with centralized marketing, sales, and quoting? Could they capitalize on reshoring trends? They thought so, and with this in mind, they launched ReNEW Manufacturing Solutions in 2023.

Small, Steady, and Relatively Unnoticed

Everyone knows the story: A fabricator grows large on the back of a few or even just one large customer. The fabricator invests heavily to grow with its customer. The customer falters, and the cash flow crunch reverberates up the supply chain. What hasn’t gotten much notice, though, are the thousands of small enterprises that aren’t flashy on the outside but are quite successful under the hood. They probably don’t have much of a web presence. They don’t pay much attention to search engine optimization or social media. They aren’t very automated, and some equipment might be aging. But look at the books, and you’ll see consistently high margins and strong cash flow. Marry that with customers in market niches with consistent demand, long-term customer relationships, and an experienced workforce with low turnover, and you have the bones for a sustainable business that won’t be folding anytime soon.

Ferrell saw many of these attributes in the first shop ReNEW acquired, TIW Machine & Fab in Dalton, Ga., a center for carpet manufacturing. The fabricator got its start serving that carpet and textile business (TIW stands for Textile Industrial Welding), but by the time Ferrell bought it, the shop was heavily concentrated in the railroad industry.

"Those railroad customers really meet our ICP [ideal customer profile]," Ferrell said. "They’re OEMs that have outsourced their supply chain for parts and subassemblies. And the relationships are longstanding. One customer had been with the company for 30 years. It’s a steady business."

Yes, the revenue mix was highly concentrated, but the fact the company evolved over time, moving away from textiles and toward the railroad, exemplifies the flexibility of the fab shop business model. Custom fabrication is by nature horizontal, able to pivot to other verticals as the business climate dictates. Moreover, the dominant customers in railroad offer steady demand; the peaks and troughs aren’t too dramatic, and the business itself isn’t subject to frequent disruption. (Nothing is likely to make railcars obsolete anytime soon.)

As Ferrell explained, all this provided good ingredients that could help get the investment platform off the ground. As part of ReNEW, the shop would benefit from increased software and machine investment, centralized quoting, and the additional capacity that can be spread among numerous shops operating under one brand.

Starting With Sales

Today, just two years after making that first investment, ReNEW has four locations across four states—Texas, Georgia, Ohio, and Pennsylvania. "Over these two years, we’ve looked at more than 200 deals," Ferrell said. "Especially in 2023, there was a good amount of deal flow, so that put us off on our journey."

When considering a purchase, Ferrell and his team focus on shops serving industries that would fit well into the diversified "whole" of Re-NEW. This, he said, sets the organization apart from many outside investors who focus intensely on high-margin businesses. If a shop has, say, a good collection of aerospace and defense customers, it likely enjoys healthy margins that frequently pique investor interest.

ReNEW takes a different approach when looking at companies to buy. As Ferrell explained, the organization of course values healthy margins, but the biggest consideration remains how a shop’s customer mix would fit into the total revenue pie. Revenue diversification is one factor, but the timing of a sector’s business cycle matters too: When one sector is down, another ideally should be up, smoothing out the revenue fluctuations and, ultimately, creating a sustainable business.

Prime examples include ReNEW’s second and third shop purchases. Absolute Machine & Tooling out of Leander, Texas, gave the organization a foothold in the semiconductor market. With Interfuse Manufacturing in Clarence, Pa., ReNEW gained plate fabrication expertise and entered the heavy equipment market.

"They specialize in thick, high-tensile-strength steel and large subassemblies," Ferrell said, "as well as extensive machining on those subassemblies." The operation also now has a 6-kw fiber laser that can cut up to 26 ft., complementing a collection of three other fiber lasers that range up to 10 kW.

Ferrell emphasized that the nature of those customer relationships factors into the equation. A shop might be in a sector that would benefit ReNEW’s overall mix, but high customer turnover is still a red flag. Ultimately, strong, long-term customer relationships matter more than anything else.

The Selling Strategy

As Ferrell explained, after ReNEW acquires a shop, "The first question we ask is, ‘What does your sales and marketing function look like?’ Pretty much every time, we hear, ‘Nothing.’ Usually, the customer base has just been loyal and consistent. There really is no sales and marketing function."

For shops with "good bones," this isn’t necessarily a bad thing. Managers can dive into shop performance to the nth degree, tracking machine uptime, inventory turns, optimal part flow, rework, quality, even on-time delivery. But customer retention is the ultimate barometer. If a shop keeps its current customer base happy and simply relies on its reputation to bring more work in the door, that’s a positive sign that points to a reliable operation—again, good bones.

If the operation were truly subpar or dysfunctional, the best sales team in the world might give a short-term revenue boost, but it wouldn’t be sustainable. ReNEW’s centralized sales and marketing team, though, has good bones to work with.

"Right off the bat, we visited longtime customers and asked, ‘What more can we do for you?’ We want to grow with you. That’s been some of the lower-hanging fruit for us." He added that the marketing side has also helped with SEO strategies and a new website. The goal is to diversify the customer base both company-wide and at individual locations.

Centralized, streamlined quoting has been key. Every plant now is connected to the Paperless Parts platform, and the company now usually turns around quotes in less than a day. The sales team has gotten some of its greatest wins from existing accounts, and the top line continues to grow. This year the organization, now at 190 employees, landed on The Fabricator’s FAB 40 list, at No. 37. In 2024, its revenue exceeded $45 million, and it projects revenue to reach $50 million this year.

The Long Play

Earlier this year, Ferrell talked with a potential customer that, because of the tariff threats, was looking to move production from Mexico to the U.S. Another prospect said tariffs would have to be well beyond 100% for production to move back from Asia. "It’s really been all over the board."

Regardless, ReNEW’s business plan doesn’t hinge on tariffs. Reshoring, though, remains a long-term trend that is sure to extend beyond just one presidential administration.

And at this writing, the organization is doing due diligence on another potential shop purchase. "We now can be selective, to be sure the new acquisition fits within our existing platform," Ferrell said, adding that it likely will stay away from crowded manufacturing niches.

For instance, larger-piece fabrication usually has a higher barrier to entry, requiring large-format lasers, burn tables, and press brakes—hence, the market is less crowded.

Beyond this, internal performance improvement continues. At a recent staff meeting, Ferrell talked about a vision of ReNEW’s performance in the coming years.

"Think about Amazon. We’ve all gotten accustomed to knowing our package is 10 stops away on the delivery truck. When we order from Domino’s, we know when our pizza is in the oven and out for delivery. That’s the expectation, and that’s where we need to drive toward."
