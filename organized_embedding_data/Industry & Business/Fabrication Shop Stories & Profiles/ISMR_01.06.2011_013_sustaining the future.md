# SUSTAINING THE FUTURE

[TARİH: 01.06.2011 ISMR]

THE FABRICATORS & MANUFACTURERS ASSOCIATION, INTERNATIONAL (FMA) is a professional organisation, based in the US, with more than 2,000 individual and company members from 50 countries working together to improve the metal forming and fabricating industry. Founded in 1970, FMA brings metal fabricators and manufacturers together through technology councils, educational programmes, networking events, industry publications and the FABTECH exhibition.

FMA serves members involved in bending; blanking; cutting; drawing; extruding; fastening; finishing; leveling; piercing; punching; roll forming; shearing; slitting; spinning; straightening; stamping; swaging and welding. ISMR caught up with its President and CEO, Gerald M. Shankel.

ISMR: WHAT ARE YOUR PLANS AND TARGETS FOR 2011 AND HOW DO YOU INTEND TO ACHIEVE THEM?

GS Our main goals are to improve the current image of manufacturing and to help our members attract, train and retain a new generation of workers. In the US and Canada/Mexico, the average age of the shop floor worker is 57 and many will be retiring soon. Factory work has this antiquated image of being dark, dirty and perhaps even dangerous and we need to overcome that. The assembly lines of 40 years ago have been replaced by a lot more automation. Today’s manufacturing facilities are bright, clean and reliant on that automation. Manufacturing has changed too – it is no longer endless production lines with rows of people. So, we see ourselves as trying to help change the image of manufacturing to attract a future generation of workers.

To help do this, we have been running manufacturing summer camps for young people (12 – 14 year old age bracket) so that they can experience what modern manufacturing is like. To encourage the new generation of manufacturers, we also provide a scholarship programme and training. We work with local community colleges to provide this type of training. The reality is that only one in four young people in the US graduate from college – we still need to find meaningful work for the remaining three. Some rather alarming statistics in the US report that 30 per cent of people starting 8th grade (13 years old and onwards) do not graduate from high school and around 80 per cent of the people that find their way into our prison system have not graduated from high school. So, we clearly need to focus more resources on secondary and career education programmes here in the US if we are to create productive young members of society. Manufacturing needs people with strong skill levels – it is a wealth-creating industry.

ISMR: WHAT IS YOUR OVERALL VISION FOR THE FMA OVER THE NEXT FIVE YEARS?

GS I want to attract more people into the industry as well as bring them into the manufacturing industry’s organisations to start grooming them for leadership roles. Our goal is to find young people in their twenties who will become the leaders of manufacturing’s next generation. Our ability to improve the image of manufacturing will be key here.

ISMR: HOW DO YOU VIEW THE DEVELOPMENT OF MANUFACTURING AND SHEET METAL MARKETS IN THE US OVER THE NEXT FIVE YEARS? HOW IS THE FMA HELPING TO STIMULATE MARKETS/

## CUSTOMERS AND CREATE OPPORTUNITIES?

GS The US is such a large country that its two largest trading partners are to the north and south of its own borders – Canada and Mexico. When you look at how little of what we produce is going to countries across the globe, the US still has a long way to go to become a truly global exporter. This is still a real challenge for US manufacturers. We try to help members and stimulate international market opportunities, for example through our FABTECH trade show. We want to see particularly smaller US companies look across borders and see what international opportunities are available – reinforcing the idea that any company, of any size, can do business internationally.

We also provide a forum for members to network with each other and discuss these issues. Our LinkedIn group, for example (which is also open to non-members), is used almost as an international partner tool with members of the group helping each other to find international contacts.

We also offer analytical market tools for members to help them understand what market shifts may mean for their business. Our members are mainly small shops with the CEO doing multiple tasks and jobs. They ask us to sift through and analyse market trends and directions to pull out data that will help them directly.

ISMR: WHAT DO YOU FEEL ARE THE GREATEST CHALLENGES FACING SHEET METAL MANUFACTURERS (FABRICATORS AND FORMERS) AT PRESENT?

GS Certainly, attracting new blood to the manufacturing industry is a real challenge. Right now in the US, we have very high unemployment yet there is an estimated 900,000 jobs in manufacturing alone that are unfilled. One of the main reasons for this is a lack of skills. In 1950, 60 per cent of the jobs in manufacturing could be handled by unskilled labour – by 2005, less than 15 per cent were unskilled and that percentage drops a little each year. People now need to graduate from high school and go to a community college or a trade school for additional training so that they can become productive workers for these small fabricating companies. Our focus has been on trying to provide training to help manufacturers become more productive and efficient.

Here in the US, the small and medium-sized fabricators of the past produced almost entirely for the domestic market. This is going to have to change. Recent statistics suggest that this is now changing as more and more US fabricators are exporting goods to international buyers. Fabricators need to see that their competitors are not just the shops down the street but shops all over the world.

US manufacturers face competitive issues such as cheap labour in China. Back in the eighties, Japan was going to lead the world in this respect, now it is China. We are also seeing the rise of India. To survive, US manufacturers must be more efficient, with Just in Time inventories and smaller batch runs. We provide training geared to help fabricators become more competitive so they cannot just survive, but thrive.

We also see technological challenges and changes in the industry such as changing material requirements, more sophisticated advances in control technologies and greater use of design software that represent both opportunities and challenges for our members. With the strong level of price competition in high volume manufacturing, particularly from Asian countries, we also see an eventual shift towards more value added, complex parts production although we still need to retain that volume capacity.

As rapidly as technology is changing, not only do we need to educate a new workforce to replace those that retire but we also need to change the thought process on careers in the existing workforce. A lot of people thought that a job was for life – statistics now show that each of us will have twelve different careers in our lifetime. It basically means that we will need to learn how to be re-trained multiple times. It is part of our educational mandate to upgrade people’s skills as new technology is introduced. The training profile is changing now. In the past, when we offered scholarships, it was to young people in the 18–24 year age range. Last year, half of our scholarships were awarded to people going back into training in their 40s and 50s.

ISMR: WHICH NEW METAL FORMING OR FABRICATION PROCESSES AND TECHNOLOGIES WOULD YOU HIGHLIGHT AS THE MOST PROMISING AREAS FOR GROWTH OVER THE NEXT FEW YEARS?

GS Apart from new technologies such as fibre lasers and press brake controls, our members are finding increased requirements from their customers for documentation of sustainability in their manufacturing processes. This means that re-evaluating their manufacturing processes and becoming greener is almost as vital to their business as purchasing the next new piece of equipment. If they cannot document what they have done to be more environmentally conscious, this has an impact on their ability to get jobs. Our new publication, Green Manufacturer magazine, tries to tell members and non-members how to be greener. We have run several seminars on this topic, too. We are trying to show manufacturers that they can be green and save money.

The larger corporations have always had an interest in supply chain management but now smaller companies are taking an interest in this area too. We have seen recent disruptions in supply caused by the natural disasters in Japan, the hurricane in New Orleans and recent flooding in the US.

Supply chain management is becoming almost as important as having access to steel. If you’re going to have Just In Time delivery, you need to spend a lot of time on supply chain management.

ISMR: HOW DO YOU SEE THE US MARKET DEVELOPING?

GS The news media in the US has still not acknowledged how we have come out of this recession. Nobody denies that the recession is behind us even though there are still problems with the economy. That would not have happened had it not been for US manufacturing and exporting. The manufacturing sector pulled the US out of recession and, specifically, US manufacturing exports were responsible for that. Continued recovery will be based on our ability to export.

Manufacturing has such a great multiplier effect – there are 12 million workers in the US that are directly involved in manufacturing. But, realistically, there are an additional 5–7 people for every manufacturing worker. In the automotive industry, the multiplier effect of every person in that industry is 15 – 18 additional jobs in the economy dependent upon that automotive worker. That illustrates the enormous impact that manufacturing has on the US economy, as a whole.
