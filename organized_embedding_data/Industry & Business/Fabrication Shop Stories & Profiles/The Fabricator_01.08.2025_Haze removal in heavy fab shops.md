eavy fabrication shops don’t look like they once did. Robots for cutting and welding are finding homes on the shop floor. Material handling automation is helping large fabrications move from one process to the next. It’s part of the evolution associated with being a modern manufacturer.

One thing is holding back that transition, however. It’s literally hanging over the industry.

If a heavy fab shop is not properly ventilated, the haze generated from the thermal cutting and welding lingers overhead, a reminder that shop floor workers are being exposed to potentially dangerous compounds. It’s also a warning to those looking at a possible career in metal fabricating that the company might not have the best interest of its employees in mind.

Not a Simple Task

When it comes to cutting plate, whether it’s laser, plasma, or oxyfuel, some sort of ventilation system is usually a part of the system. Fiber laser cutting machines require an enclosure to keep the laser from reflecting off the sheet metal and possibly harming someone’s eyes. Plasma and oxyfuel cutting systems typically have downdraft or water tables to minimize fumes escaping the cutting area.

Welding, however, is another story. Ventilating these fumes is no easy task. Someone working on a 14-ft.-dia., 20-ft.-long tank is not in a position to use a portable vent hood to extract the fumes.

The most effective way to clear that haze, which typically hovers about 17 ft. above the shop floor, is through a ducted system that removes the air from the shop, filters the particulate matter, and sends clean air back into the facility. A properly designed ventilation system with appropriately spaced venting and returns can change the air over 10 to 12 times every hour.

While no one doubts the effectiveness of a ducted ventilation system, some heavy fab shops might balk at it for other reasons. The most obvious one is the presence of cranes, a key tool in moving heavy parts and fabrications. To retrofit a facility with ductwork for ventilation, the ducting has to go above the cranes. If the cranes are too high, it doesn’t allow room for the ductwork.

The other hesitation for some heavy fab shops is the sheer scope of such a project. In some applications in the metal fabricating space, ducts can be 48 to 60 in. in diameter, making them difficult to maneuver in a plant, much less install over expensive machine tools. Of course, the nature of these types of installations varies, but some projects can last a couple of weeks, which many large facilities might wish to avoid.

In recent years, a more modular option has emerged to help heavy fab shops clear out the haze. Compact fume extractors can do the job like ducted ventilation systems but can be moved as floor layouts change and relocated to more effective positions.

Testing the Air Quality

Before any discussion of improving indoor air quality in a metal fabricating facility can begin, everyone needs to know what type of air contaminants are making up the haze.

Internal air quality can be tested with a device that adheres to clothing and captures details of what is in the environment around the shop floor workers. With that information, an experienced industrial hygienist can determine time-weighted averages, or the average exposure that the fabricators and welders have to the fumes.

That’s important because haze is made up of not only gases used in the welding process, but also metal from the workpiece. During welding, the metal is heated above its boiling point, resulting in vapors that condense and turn into fine particulate. Some of these metals have been linked to illnesses and even cancer, so time-weighted averages for welders and other shop floor workers are very important.

Probably the most concerning of these metals is chromium, a component of stainless steel and other nonferrous alloys. As the welder applies the torch to these raw materials, the chromium is converted to what is called its hexavalent state. At this point, the hexavalent chrome, Cr(VI), is considered highly toxic and can damage the eyes, skin, nose, throat, and lungs. Prolonged exposure can lead to cancer. Getting rid of the haze provides a visible recognition that dangerous compounds are being removed from the indoor environment. That not only greatly improves employee safety but also boosts overall morale for those on the shop floor.

Solving the Ventilation Problem

Once the indoor air testing is done, the metal fabricator is ready to meet with the welding ventilation equipment supplier to determine a strategy to create an atmosphere free of the haze and the hazardous compounds that comprise it. The goal is to create enough airflow that 10 to 12 air changes per hour can occur over a shift. Of course, the number of air changes required depends on the amount of welding being done and factors affecting airflow, like large overhead fans.

With airflow targets set, welding ventilation system design can begin. As previously stated, compact fume extractors have emerged as a popular and cost-effective choice to deliver clear air when ducted systems just aren’t possible.

These compact units can be moved with a lift truck and located near the bays where the welding is done. Once in place, they are powered up and ready to start clearing the air.

These fume extractors have a one-piece duct section that sticks up to the height of the thermal layer where the haze lingers. That is the capture point. The fumes are pulled into the filters, where the dust and metal particles are extracted from the air. In most welding environments, HEPA-level filtration is encouraged to capture the smallest particulates.

The compact fume extractors can cover an area of almost 5,000 sq. ft. Strategically placed units across a shop floor can provide ample coverage for a large facility.

Optimizing Extractor Performance

Like a machine tool, a fume extraction system only operates as expected when the manufacturer’s guidelines are followed. If the units aren’t maintained, performance will be less than optimal.

Fortunately, a properly sized fume extraction system doesn’t require constant maintenance. With the right machines in the right places, the proper airflow, and the proper air-to-cloth ratios, filter life could be substantial.

On the other hand, manufacturers that try to save money on the front end and install air collectors that are not properly sized for such a large facility will find themselves changing out filters two to three times a year just to keep the haze from reoccurring.

Not every fab shop has a maintenance staff. But even if a welder or machine operator is charged with fume extractor maintenance, it won’t require too much time. Regularly checking on the filter status and ensuring that the differential pressure inside the units doesn’t build up to outrageous levels will keep the extractors performing as expected.
