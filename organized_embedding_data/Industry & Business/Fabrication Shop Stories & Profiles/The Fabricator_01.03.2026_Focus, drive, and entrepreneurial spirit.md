J

ack Rosemark remembers crawling under an aluminum hull, bucking rivets. His grandfather owned a small business that manufactured airboats, the kind commonly used in the Everglades’ shallow waters. Riveting those aluminum hulls gave him his first taste of metal manufacturing.

Fast forward more than two decades. The founder of Freedom Fabricators in Jupiter, Fla., stands in front of a large press brake and its touchscreen controller, again working aluminum— this time on a part with multiple positive and negative flanges, created with sophisticated backgauging and precision-ground tooling staged across the 14-ft. bed.

Rosemark caught the metal manufacturing bug a long time ago, and his story gives a kind of entrepreneurial "recipe," especially for those looking to get into the precision side of the business. It starts with entrepreneurial drive; involves technical curiosity, focus, and smart technology investment; and ends with a good strategy that prepares the operation for the road ahead.

ENTREPRENEURIAL DRIVE

After high school, Rosemark went to work for Sikorsky’s fabrication and assembly plant, also in Jupiter, eventually becoming senior fabricator. In 2018 he made the jump to Aerojet Rocketdyne, where he helped manufacture the RL-10 rocket engine. Throughout all this, he became versed in Six Sigma and lean manufacturing. He knew about sheet metal, but he also knew about manufacturing efficiency, including the importance of quick changeover and keeping jobs on the move. Any wasted motion meant jobs weren’t progressing as quickly as they could be.

Several years into his career at Sikorsky, Rosemark began working nights and weekends out of his garage. "In 2015, we formally launched Freedom Fabricators. Even today, we don’t have any outside investment. [My wife] Christine and myself own 100% of the company." He paused, recalling those early days: "We saw it as an investment in ourselves. That’s what gave us perseverance."

Rosemark told this story sitting in a new office, in a small industrial park less than a mile west of Sikorsky’s factory and flight development center. The floor today has a waterjet along with new equipment from Bystronic—a 14-ft. press brake, the Xpert Pro. The company also purchased a 10-kW ByCut fiber laser, slated for delivery in April. Both were purchased from Titan Machine Tools in Sorrento, Fla.

"My grandfather had his airboat company, and Dad also was an entrepreneur. He did his own thing. It’s just in my blood, really. I was never going to be happy punching in at the same company for two or three decades."

He then turned to his wife and smiled, recalling Freedom’s early days in 2015. "We had just purchased our house with a garage we could use as shop space," he said. "And before we had a chance to buy most of our furniture, I told her, ‘You know what we need? We need a forklift.’

"I had the mindset that when you’re fabricating metal, you need to load and unload material. So, we found a used fork truck for sale for just $1,500, about four hours north of here."

Next came a small plasma table and—unusual for a garage-shop startup—a small jib crane. "I literally saw-cut the floor of our garage, poured a 4-ft. cube [of concrete] for the foundation, and installed the crane. The critical aspect with any operation is efficiency. I could plasma-cut 1,000-lb. plates on a plasma table by myself. That made the process easy and more affordable for our customers."

Even at that early stage, the Rosemarks were thinking about part throughput—enough to jackhammer a garage floor to install a jib crane. This, combined with a solid foundation in precision sheet metal technology, helped the operation get off the ground. "We have what today is a multimillion-dollar customer who initially found us on a $17 Facebook ad," he said. "There’s no secret recipe. You just need to deliver quality work on time."

FOCUS AND TECHNICAL CURIOSITY

For most of its history, aside from employing an AWS-certified welder for structural and cosmetically critical welding, Freedom had a vertical machining center, an initial-pinch plate roller, and an ironworker for basic punching and shearing—typical equipment for a startup. Last year’s purchase of a Bystronic press brake represented a significant milestone, which Rosemark described by holding up a cosmetically critical part. It had been recently joined with gas tungsten arc welding (GTAW, or TIG). Nearly all of Freedom Fabricator’s welding work involves GTAW of stainless or aluminum, though it also has wire welding capability.

"[Precision bending] gives our welder an easier part to weld," he said. "At the same time, customers are expecting weld quality to be 10 times better than it was in 2015." He added that good welding reduces the need for grinding. "A bad welder makes a good grinder. A good welder, though, doesn’t make a good grinder."

Rosemark then walked back to the press brake, where he pointed out several aspects that have become critical for Freedom’s precision forming strategy. First was the bed width combined with the 3D simulation on the controller, mirroring the offline bend simulation from the accompanying Bystronic’s BySoft Suite software. Long bed lengths have always benefited small fab shops with diverse product mixes, since they can form both short and long bends. That 14-ft. bed also gives ample room to stage various punches and dies.

And with offline simulation software, a complex staged setup isn’t as arduous as it once was, especially if a customer provides a STEP or other 3D CAD file format. The software unfolds the blank and creates a forming program based on the actual tools in use, taking into account die width (which in air forming governs the inside radius) and punch geometry.

"We formed a recent job on ⅜-in.-thick plate on a specific punch and die combination. We updated the bend software, it resized the flat pattern, and we formed the complex part on the first try within 0.012 in. This was an incredibly precise job, and we didn’t have to scrap the first part. We updated the part program, and we were off to the races."

To be sure, every piece of sheet metal and plate has a thickness tolerance, and bending variables can vary depending on the grain direction and other material characteristics. Even so, when processing the first part, Freedom’s new press brake underbends each flange and uses laser-based sensors to dial in the bend angle—no underbending, measuring with a caliper, then trying to reposition the first part exactly to close up the first bend.

The shop tackles multibend parts in which tolerances can easily stack up, as well as challenging parts with holes or other features close to the bend line. Here again, the simulation helps streamline the initial setup. "For instance, if we see a hole near a bend line, we might choose a narrower die opening to compensate," Rosemark said, as long as the required forming tonnage remains below the capacity of the tools and press brake.

Like many small fab shops, Freedom began with a used brake and planer tooling, mainly because the price was right. Trying to achieve tight angular and bending tolerances, though, wasn’t sustainable over the long term.

Now, the company has upgraded to tall precision-ground tools, all of which fit neatly into the Bystronic brake’s large open height and dynamic crowning. The tall tooling and expansive open height make a wide variety of bend geometries possible. The dynamic crowning keeps long bend lengths dialed in across the press brake bed. And the hydraulic clamping streamlines tool changes.

The hydraulic clamping was a strategic choice. As Rosemark explained, the system does reduce the open height by a few inches, meaning that the machine can’t handle extremely deep box bending. "But in my opinion, that tool change efficiency the hydraulic clamping gives you is well worth it," he said. "Do you want to be able to build tall boxes but have slower tool setups, or do you want to set up the other 98% of work you have very quickly?"

THE ROAD AHEAD

In early 2025, Rosemark signed a 20-year lease with his landlord for 7,200 sq. ft. of shop space within a 20,000-sq.-ft. building. "He must have thought, ‘This guy’s lost his mind.’ But then I told him to look around."

This was before the shop’s latest press brake was installed, but even then, Rosemark knew that equipment wouldn’t be easy to move. The electrical and pneumatic infrastructure was in place, as was the air conditioning system—a key differentiator for a metal fabricator in South Florida.

Rosemark has a vision of cellular manufacturing, of blanking centers feeding different value streams, including a high-mix operation alongside one dedicated to production work, complete with bending and perhaps welding automation. The operation also plans to bring in a coordinate measuring machine (CMM) to up its game in the high-precision market.

"My grandfather had his airboat company, and Dad also was an entrepreneur. He did his own thing. It’s just in my blood, really. I was never going to be happy punching in at the same company for two or three decades."

—Jack Rosemark, Freedom Fabricators

Freedom recently achieved its ISO 9001 and AS 9100 certifications. The shop floor is clean, well lit, and its five employees are experienced in precision sheet metal, especially in stainless and aluminum. Perhaps most important, the company is just a mile or so away from some of the largest aerospace players in the country, including Pratt & Whitney, Aerojet Rocketdyne, and Sikorsky.

"If you’re very good at what you do, and you remain laser focused on your niche market, there’s no sense in broadening it," he said.

He made this comment while pointing to one of his brake operators measuring some stainless 304 plate with a nominal thickness of 0.375 in. Turns out, the actual thickness was slightly more, at 0.380 in. He noted the change in the controller, which in turn made slight changes to the bend program automatically.

Everyone who operates the brake is trained to mic the material, [measuring its thickness with a digital caliper]," Rosemark said, noting that these changes "alter the entire bending algorithm."

Measuring sheet thickness isn’t rare, of course, but having it as a standard procedure does help focus Freedom on specific niches. The operation doesn’t practice conventional fabrication, for which most tape measures are accurate enough. It uses precision cutting and forming equipment. Workpieces are measured with micrometers, digital calipers, and (soon) a CMM.

A LEAP BASED ON A PLAN

In the middle of 2020, just as the pandemic was taking hold, Rosemark left Aerojet Rocketdyne to focus on Freedom Fabricators full time. The move might have seemed risky, but Rosemark already had a few critical chess pieces in place to support a long-term strategy.

First was a large food-processing customer that demanded consistent work, especially in stainless and aluminum. Second was a plan for expansion.

He moved away from plasma cutting and toward the waterjet, which could provide the premium edge quality his growing customer base demanded. Those edges didn’t have a heat-affected zone, either, so he knew that, even when he’d upgrade to a fiber laser, Freedom still would find good use for the waterjet.

Finally, he knew just how capable modern equipment was. He pushed the limits of planer tooling and knew precision-ground punches and dies were in his future, as was a precision brake. Drawing from his lean training, he knew welding would be the worst place for a highly variable constraint— and he knew that repeatable welding demanded precision upstream.

Fast forward to this year, and Freedom has that precision upstream processing in place. To make his point, Rosemark pointed to a part with multiple bends that needed to be formed within an extremely tight dimensional tolerance. The angle measurement system moved in, the blue laser light landing on the stainless, quickly measuring the angle despite the material’s protective coating.

The punch descended, underbent slightly, then dialed in to create a perfect angle with the desired radius. The angular and dimensional results were spot on.

"In the sheet metal world, most people don’t talk about ±0.005 or 0.010 in. We’re finding that a lot of companies just can’t dial in their brakes and lasers, so they sub that work out. That’s our wheelhouse, and we’re thankful for that."
