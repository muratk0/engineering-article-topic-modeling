# 5S streamlined and sustained

How SAF keeps continuous improvement rolling

rank Buchholz vividly recalled the "paperwork nightmare" of 5S at SAF’s metal fabrication plant in Winston, Ga. This was 2018, when he managed piles of paper checklists from the company’s 5S audits.

Today, the 5S audits are gone, and so is the paper. Employees at each workstation access 5S checklists on tablets. Even with no paper and no audits, 5S remains as strong as ever. As SAF’s COO, Buchholz continues to work closely with David Alvis, quality corporate representative, who’s worked to implement improvement projects across SAF over the past 15 years.

A standout tool in the lean manufacturing toolbox, 5S stands for

sort, set in order, shine, standardize

, and

sustain

. It applies to all manufacturing models, no matter the product and customer mix. Even so, sustaining the effort can be challenging, and SAF is no Toyota. It’s a distributor, metal finisher, and fabricator with an architectural metal focus (among other markets), with plants in Georgia, Indiana, California, Tennessee, and Texas. Its operations are as wide ranging as any job shop. Considering all this, how has the company kept organized so many years while so many others fail to sustain the effort?

Some of it might have to do with the way SAF implemented 5S over the past decade and a half. Considering how both Alvis and Buchholz told the story, the company’s 5S journey could be looked at in three stages: First,

discover and educate

; second,

embrace and sustain

; third,

streamline and let go

.

Discover, Educate, and Steal

Around 2010, the powder coating department supervisor at the time initiated an informal 5S effort, and within months, people already were seeing the results. After taking some 5S classes at Georgia Tech, Alvis continued the work that the painting department had started. By 2011, he helped launch a formal 5S initiative and expanded the effort incrementally, department by department. He took pictures—lots of pictures. He witnessed people spending their days looking for tools. He saw plenty of duplicate items, too, as tools thought to be lost turned up after another was purchased.

"The program evolved gradually," Alvis said. "We talked with people about the issues they were having, then developed checklists. Once we saw what didn’t belong in a workspace, we then asked, ‘OK, where do they belong? Where do we put these items?’ We started putting tools in specific locations, such as consolidating certain tools on a cart. And over time, we developed shadow boards."

At each step along the way, Alvis (and eventually department leads) asked front-line personnel what exact set of tools they needed during a shift. "If it was a tool they needed every day, once an hour, they needed to have it within arm’s reach. Other tools could be put on buggies, so they could be moved to a workstation as needed," Alvis said, adding that the entire process dealt closely with worker efficiency and ergonomics. Any unnecessary motion was scrutinized.

He gradually repeated this process in different departments, but as he did so, challenges cropped up almost immediately. Workers in unorganized areas began walking over to the "5Sed" departments and taking their tools. They were easy to find, after all.

The "tool stealing" did have a few positive consequences. "It actually helped move things along," Alvis recalled, because it helped identify in-demand tools. Workers took tools from other areas often because they were simply better tools for the job. Over time, the effort helped standardize tool selection over all departments on the floor.

David Alvis opens the "coffin," a cabinet on wheels that holds segmented brake tools.

All along the way, Alvis talked with the team and developed checklists. Key to worker buyin, he said, was focusing on what he and SAF managers could do to make life easier on the front lines. Most important, the team allowed (and still allows) time at the beginning and end of each shift for the 5S checks to take place. Everything had to be put back in its place, ready for the next operator. Today, operators spend 10 minutes at the beginning and end of each shift to complete their 5S and manufacturing readiness checklists.

Embrace and Sustain

To keep the momentum during the ensuing years, SAF conducted 5S audits, where teams from other areas would score specific attributes of a workcell’s manufacturing readiness. He started with standard 5S audit templates, widely available online, then customized them to suit SAF’s operation.

The exercise spread ideas throughout the floor and sparked healthy competition. Employees discovered better ways to organize, including shadow boards with tool-shape embossments cut on the shop’s router—an easy way to make the "shadow" permanent and clearly visible for years. They also expanded the boards to include not just hand tools but also common check gauges and templates.

Moreover, they embraced the fact that not every 5S-related idea works. Alvis pointed to some radius templates staged by the plate roll. At one point, those templates covered most of the work that passed through the machine. But the mix of work has changed, and the radius templates haven’t been used in more than a year. No matter. As Alvis pointed out, ideas that don’t pan out should never foster "5S-justdoesn’t-work-here" attitudes.

Streamline and Let Go

At some point, Alvis and Buchholz began to see the 5S program take hold and, in many ways, sustain itself. Even so, the system was showing some cracks, especially when it came to all the paperwork. Every department had files of past checklists, hidden from view and pretty much useless. They helped workers keep their spaces clean, but they didn’t help managers and planners with timely information. The paperwork took time to manage, and it wasn’t time customers were paying for.

To tackle that paperwork headache—and to streamline the overall initiative—SAF took a two-pronged approach. First, they examined the 5S and manufacturing readiness program in its totality and, in lean manufacturing fashion, looked for waste. They started with the audits. In the beginning, audits proved extremely valuable; they helped spread "5S thinking" throughout the plant. Now that 5S thinking had become the norm, though, audits became a bit tiresome. People ranked specific attributes of certain workstations, but the differences were minor. Attributes in the "sort" category in the bending area might get a 4.5 rating, while the powder coating area might get a 4.4. People were splitting hairs. SAF eventually phased out the audits, which eliminated the associated paperwork.

"As time went on, the checklists became simpler too," Buchholz said, mainly because workcells had become so clean and standardized, they simply didn’t have that many elements to check. "For instance, you check for ‘sorting,’ but with everything ‘sorted,’ we really didn’t need to check for that," he said, explaining they just combined the checklist elements under "set in order" and "shine."

In SAF’s experience, at least, detailed 5S checklists helped people question everything, take nothing for granted, and "learn to see" the disorganization in their work areas. Over time, however, as 5S became second nature, the checklist itself could be culled and simplified.

The second prong of SAF’s 5S streamlining effort involved software. Alvis began with a free platform, Google Docs, and used that to feed data to a touch-friendly, web-based user interface, which SAF developed in-house.

Alvis and his team rolled out the system all at once. The last thing they wanted was to manage two 5S recordkeeping systems—one software based and another on paper—at the same time. It would have made the paperwork issue more challenging than it already was. Most important, it would have made life harder for people on the floor.

The transition happened, bugs were fixed, and life at the shop went on. Today, when workers arrive, they pick up the tablet and, after a few taps, access their 5S and manufacturing readiness worksheet. As sources explained, because 5S had already been so deeply ingrained into the company culture, the paperless transition occurred smoothly and without much fuss.

When press brake operators arrive to start their shift, they open the tablet and tap to see their workstation. They then run through a detailed checklist. They ensure all tools on the shadow boards are accounted for; calibrate the machine; check the hydraulics; and ensure the tools they need are ready. Checking all the items turns the brake workstation’s color green on the manufacturing readiness chart. Both the operator and machine are ready to produce. They then follow a similar checklist at the end of the shift, verifying all tools have been returned to the shadow board and certain items in and around the workstation are in place, ready for the next shift. Completing this again turns the workstation green on the manufacturing readiness chart.

Enter Ergonomics

Alvis pointed to the "coffins"—horizontal stainless steel tooling cabinets on casters—staged by the press brake area, each holding tools shared among several brakes.

One coffin holds a large radius die that spans the length of a press brake bed. Open the coffin, and you see the tool on rollers that can be raised and lowered by turning a hand crank. The worker raises the tool to the bed height, then slides the tool onto the machine—no heaving, no danger of dropping the massive (and expensive) tool.

"This is where 5S merges with safety and ergonomics," Buchholz said.

Outside this, tools are color coded to correlate to specific machines or jobs, or where an upper punch is always mated to a lower die—especially critical when they’re stored with similar looking tools.

Focus on Value

Multiple SAF facilities now are making their way down the 5S path, and a healthy competition remains between plants. "The Indianapolis plant looks incredible," Buchholz said. "They’re doing some elements of 5S better than we are. It’s great to see."

Today, SAF’s 5S initiative has matured to the point where every action employees take—the check sheets, the placement of everything, the safe and efficient retrieval of heavy tools—correlates directly to manufacturing readiness: that is, a work center can spring into action at a moment’s notice to fulfill a customer order.

That’s

what customers pay for.
