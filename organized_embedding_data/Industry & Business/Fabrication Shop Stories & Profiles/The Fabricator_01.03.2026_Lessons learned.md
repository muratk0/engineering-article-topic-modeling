# Lessons learned

[TARİH: 01.03.2026 The Fabricator]

Tech Talk

‘Hey, we should buy a laser’

E

ight years ago, my brother Jacom and I were building a motion platform for virtual reality. You’d sit on our machine, put on a VR headset, and fly an airplane or drive a car while the motion platform moved to make it feel completely real.

It was a fun side project, but sourcing parts was tricky. We weren’t the best kind of customer. Our design required dozens of unique parts in single-unit quantities, and they definitely wouldn’t convert to high volume. We also were cost-sensitive. This was a passion project, funded by our discretionary income. If that weren’t enough, we really wanted parts fast, within just a few days.

Nobody seemed motivated to make parts for us. I get it now, but at the time I didn’t understand why. So, in frustration, I texted Jacom and said, "Hey, we should buy a laser." I reasoned that if we couldn’t buy parts, we could instead buy a laser and make parts ourselves. I said, "We can run the machine on the weekends to pay for it."

Of course, I had no clue what a laser would cost, or where to source metal, or how to run a fabrication business. As they say, ignorance is bliss, and necessity is the mother of invention. We needed laser-cut parts, didn’t know any better, and bought a laser. We then invented our own way to make it work.

It’s been eight years to the month since I sent that text to my brother. Our growth has exceeded my wildest expectations. We are still relatively small, and still a young company in a space dominated by generational fabricators with decades of manufacturing experience. Still, our perspectives have changed, and I find myself reflecting on what we’ve learned.

SPEED EARNS MARKET SHARE

Our customers care deeply about speed. In 2018, we launched with a standard lead time of seven business days, which put us at about half the lead of our regional competitors. We quickly dropped that to five days standard. As we grew, we realized that we had systems that would enable turnarounds as fast as same day: order before noon, have parts in-hand tomorrow. We delivered completed parts faster than many shops might quote them. We charged a lot more for that kind of speed. For several years, those rush fees accounted for literally half of our net income.

That tells a compelling story about how much our customers value speed. But in late 2024, we changed our model: We reduced our standard lead time from five days to just two for simple parts. Same-day shipping is still available for a premium, but fewer customers choose it, because the standard lead time is so short. The number of customers paying for extra speed instantly dropped by a whopping 75%.

sandsun/iStock/Getty Images Plus

That was a painful change, but something magical happened when we made the switch. Our pace of growth more than doubled overnight. We started acquiring new customers faster, and existing customers started ordering more often. It’s hard to prove causality, because we’ve been improving our service in other ways as well. But the drop in rush revenue perfectly aligns with our accelerated default lead time.

The real miracle is that despite losing 75% of our rush revenue, our overall revenue growth accelerated by 100%, and our net income and margin in 2025 was higher than ever before. Not everyone is willing to pay extra for speed. But when speed is the default, people are significantly more likely to buy.

VERTICAL INTEGRATION ENABLES NEW BUSINESS MODELS

We also learned that speed is hard. Fast turnaround requires excess capacity, and in a capex-heavy, labor-heavy business, that has a real cost. It also requires more working capital. If you want to promise same-day or even nextday shipping, you have to keep the inventory in stock.

Altogether, a business structured for speed will have higher capital requirements to support growth. Meanwhile, you have to walk a narrow line that balances growth, capacity, and price. Grow too fast, and you suddenly can’t promise fast turnaround anymore. That’s a problem if speed is your big differentiator. On the other hand, if you add capacity too fast, you lose capital efficiency or, worse, profitability. The bigger your company is, the easier this balancing act becomes.

Aside from these structural challenges, speed is just logistically challenging at scale. When you provide prices instantly, online, you tend to collect lots of small to mid-sized jobs. These might be simple individually, but across thousands of SKUs, they create chaos.

We didn’t start with a master plan to fix this, or even with any real understanding of what the problems would be. It just so happened that I have a software background, so when we encountered logistical pain, we solved it with inhouse software. Since we grew the company around high-mix, high-scale constraints, we learned how to deal with them. I honestly don’t think our business model would be possible absent our internal software tools. We "vertically integrated" the tech stack to support our own operation.

It has cost us a lot over the years. Today, we spend more than $1 million a year on software development, both to support existing systems and create new ones. But it’s worth it.

THE ECONOMICS OF SOFTWARE DEVELOPMENT

Once you develop software in-house, you are forever a software company. You don’t just develop a tool and use it forever without ongoing support. You’ll need to fix bugs, augment features, and evolve your tools as your business grows. AI is making this easier and cheaper, but even so, it can be hard to justify new permanent overhead.

What I’ve come to realize is that in manufacturing, software’s cost is justified not just by improved quality of life, but as a way to convert variable costs into fixed costs. In our case, we’ve used software to automate office overheads. First and foremost, we automate quoting and manufacturability checks. We employ zero estimators, but we can quote more than 100,000 parts per week without straining our systems. Absent this automation, our high-mix model would require that we grow a quoting team with revenue. But with software, the cost is fixed. The more we grow, the better the economics, as the fixed cost is amortized across many thousands of new customers. Crucially, better unit economics allow us to pay our operators higher wages.

The same economics work for any variable cost that can be automated: purchasing, scheduling, receiving, inventory management, customer communications, shipping, invoicing, receivables. These are all expensive processes that tend to grow with revenue, and especially with high-mix revenue. Software can either eliminate or streamline every one of these cost centers. It can be expensive up front, but the payoff shows up when you scale.

THE MARKET IS BIGGER THAN WE EXPECTED

Eight years ago, we thought we were building a garage shop to support a hobby. Today, we employ more than 100 people, support tens of thousands of customers, and are growing faster than ever before. We never imagined that there would be such market pull for fast metal fabrication services, but it’s clearly an idea whose time has come.

We aren’t even the biggest player in our space. Not by a long shot. The two giants with similar online-first models are Xometry and Proto Labs. According to their public filings, they together collect over $1 billion in revenue a year, though they offer many more services than we do. Our most direct competitor, SendCutSend, is a private company and has usually not disclosed revenue figures, but recent announcements the company has made put it at something like two or three times our size. Whatever the case, the combined scale really highlights how much people value speed and simplicity.

It can be hard to nail down the total size of the market for on-demand, online-first manufacturing. The challenge is that for many fabricators, the market is defined by position in the value chain, not by product categories as defined by NAICS codes. Fabricators serve hundreds of industries and thousands of product categories, and usually in fairly low volumes per vertical. So if the total market size is tens of billions of dollars, it doesn’t mean that digital fabricators can capture that much volume. Put simply, big automakers and other OEMs are not placing volume orders through web portals. I’ve personally estimated the addressable online-first market size between $5 billion and $10 billion, and growing. But it’s anyone’s guess.

IT’S STILL EARLY

The simple fact is that tightly integrated, online-first manufacturing hasn’t existed for all that long. One of the longest-lived companies in this space, Proto Labs, has only been around for a little over 20 years. My business and others like it have been around for less than 10.

I believe that we’ll see an ongoing shift toward digital-first business models. This shift will be driven by buyer expectations in the "Amazon era," net new demand enabled by accessibility, capacity vacuum as more traditional shop owners retire, and geopolitics.

When we jumped on this train eight years ago, we didn’t have any idea where we were headed. Today, we understand the shape of the market much better. We still don’t know what will happen. Nobody does. But we suspect that it will be a wild ride indeed.
