How investors are shaping the metal fabrication industry

A

perfect storm has gathered over the custom metal fabrication business for years. So many are nearing retirement and looking for an exit strategy. Younger generations—many of whom came of age when globalization really ramped up in the 1990s and early 2000s—aren’t interested in buying, so outside investors like private equity (PE) are stepping into the fray.

Opinions about private equity vary, but no one can argue that they’re making an impact on metal fabrication. They’re creating new kinds of businesses with an array of different manufacturing technologies: sheet metal and machining combined with plastic injection molding and extrusions; specialty aerospace and defense manufacturing combined with shipbuilding. The combinations seem endless, and they’re also opening up new opportunities within key growth markets: renewable energy, defense, medical, data centers, warehouse automation, and more.

What will the custom metal fabrication landscape look like in 10 years? For some insight, The Fabricator spoke with Vince Pappalardo, managing director, and Hubert de la Vauvre, director, of investment bank Brown Gibbons Lang & Co. Their message: Investors are looking to build companies with diverse, sustainable revenue streams, and the end markets a fabricator serves are becoming more important than ever.

Geography and Scale

"Investors see metal fabrication as an industry that’s still very fragmented," Pappalardo said. "There’s a value to being a one-stop shop. Now, you can have a salesperson go out and say, ‘We can fabricate, weld, powder coat, and do everything you need before final assembly.’ There’s sales synergy and back-office synergy. When you step back and see where the industry is, there’s just a good growth story available."

Much has happened over the past decade when it comes to consolidation, and de la Vauvre described the principal drivers. First came a custom fabricator’s ability to scale, both in volume and (especially) geographically. This is where close scrutiny of a shop’s competitive position entered the picture—and here, the geographic footprint matters. A fab shop with a small footprint has a greater chance of excess revenue concentration. Exceptions exist in certain areas of the country, of course, but by and large, if a fabricator serves a short list of large accounts that happen to be nearby, chances are that other shops in the area have a similar list. And even if they don’t, a fabricator with an ultralocal customer base is likely to have fewer large customers and, hence, a higher revenue concentration.

Investors are looking for fabricators that have expanded their geographic reach. This could be through prior acquisitions, but even more valuable, de la Vauvre said, is having manufacturing processes that serve high-growth industries with high barriers to entry—where customers demand certifications and highly tuned processes.

A shop might have advanced laser cutting, panel bending, even laser welding, but those off-the-shelf technologies look less strategic if they aren’t utilized in a unique way—be it multiprocess cells with cross-trained employees, value streams dedicated to specialized work, as well as design assistance and other forms of customer collaboration.

Automation in Context

"Automation is still very important to investors," de la Vauvre explained, "but you still need the scale and geographic footprint. End market diversification matters too, and automation today looks especially attractive. But that ability to scale still needs to be there."

Consider a fabricator that has just invested in welding automation, yet the processes and technology around welding still restrict those robots to certain kinds of work. Meanwhile, the manual welding area employs a team of older workers who lay down clean beads and are true experts at their craft—no weld grinding here.

Still, who’s going to take their place when they retire? What internal training and knowledge-sharing is in place? And given the shortage of welders, can that operation be scaled? Fabricators might automate after landing a certain order, but then what? Can the automation be repurposed?

Automation looks attractive to investors when it can help fabricators scale, both from an employee and customer perspective. For employees, a new machine might make a job easier and more ergonomic. From the customer’s perspective, good use of automation is a sign of predictable throughput. At the same time, if the automation investment isn’t matched with a strategic business development strategy—specific market sectors, geographies, or a combination of both—the ability to scale might not be there.

Process Diversity

Years ago, if a shop bought a laser, work just flowed in the door. Job shops that provide only laser cutting are still out there, but their potential for scaling up isn’t what it used to be.

"If you do sheet metal fabrication only, that’s often less attractive. But if you also do precision machining and other specialized processes, that’s seen as part of your ability to scale," de la Vauvre said, "and it will add to your valuation."

Process diversity dovetails directly back to that potential to tackle in-demand end markets with high barriers to entry. This increases customer "stickiness," makes for less transactional customer relationships, and can anchor a shop for future growth.

The Role of End Markets

Historically, fabricators have dealt with a growth ceiling of sorts. They might reach $10 million by serving a handful of large accounts (which maintain a steady cash flow) along with a diverse "long tail" of low-quantity work—an area that might have high profits, but the demand is also highly variable. That can be an issue when you’ve invested millions of dollars in equipment.

Fabricators might attain local or even regional reputations for reliability and quality, building or buying additional plants. But at a certain point, revenue growth plateaus. That can be because they lack several ingredients needed to scale. Being part of a larger organization, the thinking goes, would add those needed ingredients.

Investors see these businesses and ask how they can add value to a larger entity. That could happen through sheer size and number of locations, unique capabilities, and, especially now, established relationships with key end markets they’re trying to reach.

Beyond process variety (fabrication, machining, and even other processes like plastic injection molding), automation might bring half a turn or even an entire turn (1X) on the EBITDA multiples offered, de la Vauvre said, adding that "now, the right end market portfolio can be huge, adding a turn to two turns."

Investors are trying to put organizations together that are greater than the sum of their parts, Pappalardo said. "By putting smaller companies together, you can get scale from a size point a view, you can get scale from a process capabilities point of view, and when you move up in EBITDA, you get expansion in the multiple. You might be able to buy a small company at five or six times EBITDA, but by the time you put something bigger together, it might be worth six or seven."

He added that specific end market presence is really driving up valuations. That’s because those markets provide the demand that in many cases make all the other pieces of the valuation puzzle come together. A company might have amazing process diversity, robust automation, and an incredibly diverse customer portfolio, yet it lacks deep relationships in a growth industry that could propel the company to the next level.

Conversely, a fabricator that’s highly concentrated on a high-growth sector might have serious potential. "An investor might buy a shop with a narrow set of capabilities, but with a deep relationship with a specific market," Pappalardo said. "Roll that relatively undiversified customer portfolio into a larger entity, and all of a sudden, you’ve got end market diversification, diversification of capabilities, and ultimately a more stable business."

So, what makes an attractive end market portfolio? That depends on the investor, but as Pappalardo explained, many good ones involve several components. First is the "general industrial" segment, which can incorporate a broad range of accounts. Think of that as the "index fund" of metal fabrication. "That tells investors that, if they buy, they’re taking an economic risk versus a sector risk."

At the other end of the risk spectrum is (no surprise) oil and gas, an industry notorious for its boom and bust cycles. Still, having oil and gas consume a slice of the revenue pie isn’t always a bad thing. Those booms can help build a strong business, as long as the shop has sufficient revenue diversification to mitigate the downside risks.

Outside this, penetration within certain high-growth sectors have been piquing investor interest. "In recent history, warehouse automation has been the apple in everyone’s eye," Pappalardo said.

"We’re seeing much higher multiples driven by aerospace, defense, and medical devices," de la Vauvre said, adding that the differences can be stark. Today, a fabricator with good market penetration within "hot sectors" can sometimes drive multiples into the double digits.

"There are big jumps in multiples out there," de la Vauvre said. "That said, because everyone is looking for these businesses, I do think multiples will contract a little bit. So, the next play might be to broaden the scope, meaning many are seeing more value in that general industrial space."

Both cautioned that chasing sectors to build potential multiples isn’t a good strategy. Ultimately, it’s about building that well-diversified customer portfolio. Markets with continually increasing demand also grow more competitive. Concentrating on just one piece of the puzzle can be a recipe for quick growth followed by a painful bust.

Good Systems

Travel the country, and you’ll see some large and medium-sized fab shops that tend to get a lot of attention. There remains, however, an incredible number of very small businesses. Many are owned by entrepreneurs, some quite young, who plan to stay in the business for decades. Others, though, have owners who have spent a lifetime in the company. They built a solid business, but the next generation isn’t interested in taking the reins. What now?

De la Vauvre described one PE-owned fabricator that was looking to sell to another outside investor. The investor representative visited the shop, which had two plants on opposite ends of town. The company had plenty of capabilities, which isn’t unusual. After all, many enter this business because of the sheer fascination with manufacturing technology. All that mechanized wizardry makes it fun to come to work (and, for that matter, visit a show like FABTECH). But business processes and software systems were lacking.

"There wasn’t much cross-selling or synergies between the two plants," de la Vauvre said. "They even had some good end markets that they served, but the systems weren’t there. This left the buyer thinking, ‘How much work am I going to have to put into this?’"

In this case, the EBITDA multiple offered was very low, and ultimately the transaction didn’t go through. That said, every case is different—and good back-end systems can make a world of difference. The first sign a shop has good systems is having some basic information ready, like a quality-of-earnings report detailing how the organization accumulates revenue.

"Having reports ready for the buyer signals two things," Pappalardo said. "One, you’re serious about doing something. And two, they’re going to get involved with something where they at least have an idea of what the end result will be. They don’t want to get into a situation in which the accounting systems are so rough that they can’t figure out what the real earnings are. Now we’re in a quagmire. And it still happens."

De la Vauvre added, "You’d be surprised, but that remains one of the biggest issues for many of the deals out there. It’s a tough situation, especially in our data-driven world."

He added that this is why today’s buyers take a serious look at how a business integrates both software and machinery automation. Machinery automation provides predictable capacity; software automation—IIoT and MES (manufacturing execution system) platforms, production control software, and ERP tailored for high-product-mix work—gives the ability to measure what’s

really

going on.

Sometimes investors might look at machinery automation as a good sign, and not just for predictable capacity and the ability to scale. If a fabricator has invested in high-end machinery, it’s more likely to support those investments with back-office software. "At least what we’ve seen more recently, if a company is highly automated, they have a well-tuned office operation as well," de la Vauvre said. "You really can’t make use of that highly automated floor if you don’t have a good office operation. Sure, there’s sometimes a disconnect, but that’s becoming rarer these days."

The Importance of Operational Excellence

Many companies just can’t afford the most expansive software systems, but as Pappalardo explained, that isn’t necessarily a negative in investors’ eyes—especially if they have a good gut feel of operational excellence on the floor.

They see clearly where parts are coming from and where they’re going, and they notice a mature 5S program: tools are organized, floors are clean, workers are engaged. "Many roll-ups use an operating partner to get a gut feel by walking the floor," Pappalardo said. This can jumpstart an acquisition process that culminates in that smaller company being acquired by a larger firm with advanced software platforms. "This can bring these companies into a new world of understanding about where earnings are coming from."

Conversely, a shop with good numbers and yet a poor shop environment will spur many questions, Pappalardo said. It’s not always a dealbreaker, depending on a fabricator’s other attributes. Shop environments can be improved upon, after all. Regardless, investors want to know where profits are coming from, and the potential for operational chaos is never a good sign.

About the Multiple Arbitrage

Sources emphasized that these are just some of the factors investors consider. The management team as well as employee skill and engagement enter into the equation too. Investors are looking for a deep bench of talent—not necessarily a shop where growth is driven solely by the personality and sheer willpower of the founder.

Also, not every outside investor has a short-term goal of selling the firm within a handful of years. But for PE and similar investors, the multiple arbitrage really is the name of the game: Buy low and sell high. In truth, that doesn’t sit well with everyone. Stories linger about painful strip-and-flips or acquisitions that didn’t go as planned.

That said, success stories abound as well. Time will tell how all these investment plays pan out over the long term, but the fact remains that age escapes no one, and owners are looking for exit strategies.

The sustained interest from outside investors does imply that the industry has serious growth potential, especially if an organization targets the right sectors. A fabricator can have incredible processes, make great use of technology, and be the most polished operation in the world—but the ride can still be rough without consistent, predictable, and

growing

customer demand.
