S

mall private businesses, custom metal fabricators included, usually fall into one of two categories: a

lifestyle

business or a

growth

business. A lifestyle business works around the owner’s lifestyle, like the number of hours owners want to work and the amount of profit they need to live how they want to live. They’re usually not interested in growth beyond what they can manage on their own or with a small team.

In contrast, a growth business focuses on making the company bigger and more successful over time. Run as an investment, these businesses aim to generate wealth and establish value for the owner upon exit, planned

or

unexpected.

Growth-oriented businesses create a legacy. They continue to generate wealth without the owner being present, which of course is attractive to a buyer or, for that matter, anyone else running the organization after the owner leaves. They generate value through profits that are used to reinvest in the business, increase wages, reward shareholders, and offer career growth for employees.

When it comes to succession planning, a quality growth company simply has more options. The owner could sell to a next-generation family member, a group of employees, or through an employee stock ownership plan (ESOP). The owner also could sell to another company or outside investors.

If you want to build a growth-oriented business, you’ll likely run into some challenges. The transformation is never easy, and here is where checklists can help. M&A banks, business brokers, and consulting firms develop and use checklists to determine the value of a business—and for good reason. At their most basic, they give an objective view of company performance to ensure you’re building a growing, sustainable, profitable business.

Know that what follows is a subset of what you may find being used in the investment community. Even so, focusing on these will have the greatest impact on your company’s ability to scale and sustain itself over the long term.

1. Markets and Industries

I’m no stranger to metal fabrication. For years, I helped grow Skilcraft, an established contract fabricator based in Kentucky, into a sustainable, growth-oriented enterprise.

My journey began in the early 2000s. When I learned about Skilcraft, I saw a business with serious potential, as did my business partners. Skilcraft’s founders established the contract metal manufacturer in 1965, and they’re still remembered for the capabilities they pioneered and the contributions they made to employees and the community. In 2004, the founders wanted to retire. They had no succession plan and no leaders within the business who wanted to purchase it.

We bought the fabricator and continued to steer it toward that growth-oriented model. Why? We wanted to create a sustainable business, increase its value, and, even more important, have a positive impact on our employees and the community.

Early on, we focused on what remains the greatest barrier to a custom fabricator’s growth:

geography

. When you ship steel more than 250 miles, costs rise, and soon you simply can’t compete. It’s a big reason why most fabricators tend not to grow beyond $10 million in annual revenue.

To break the barrier, you have three choices: You can acquire a company more than 250 miles away, expand your capabilities, or focus on servicing new markets. At Skilcraft, we chose the last two.

This included tackling the first item on the checklist—

markets and industries

. We researched industries that prioritize quality, certifications, certain capabilities, and services that were as important as the part itself. This would help make geography a nonfactor.

At Skilcraft, we found that medical, space, and aerospace industries allowed us to break geographic barriers. Yes, we had to put new systems, processes, people, and capabilities in place, but all that work allowed us to grow. We shipped products globally within the aerospace market and nationally in other markets. At long last, we had broken that 250-mile barrier.

Moreover, the systems, processes, and certifications we added became barriers to entry for competitors. Sales grew by 100% over five years, our customer base diversified, and now we could serve markets with high barriers to entry—all positives when it comes to company valuation.

2. Product and Service Capability

Skilcraft was a well-known regional fabricator in Kentucky for 40 years before I became president and CEO. We were good at precision components and volume production. To scale, we moved away from job-shop work and became a contract manufacturer of precision components for OEMs. This move required many things, but excellent quality, on-time delivery, and capacity were critical for success.

We expanded our services to include design for manufacturability, reducing costs and improving quality through design. As we started building complete fabrication assemblies, such as X-ray machines for airport security and freezer cabinets for the medical industry, we began shipping products to customers outside our region. This was a game-changer and helped us surpass that $10 million revenue "ceiling" in metal fabrication.

3. Competition

The competition you have within a 250-mile radius greatly affects your ability to scale. To overcome this, know your capabilities and the capabilities of your competitors. With that insight, find products and capabilities that competitors cannot or simply won’t do.

At Skilcraft, our general industrial fabrication business had 10 times as many competitors as our medical and aerospace fabrication business within our geographic region. Once we developed our capabilities and broke through that geographic barrier, we saw competition drop off significantly.

I stopped worrying about competitors at that point, since we now had capabilities they simply didn’t have. From that point onward, we focused on being the best solution for our customers and, for prospects, the best potential partner.

4. Customer Base

Your customer base should be well defined and aligned with your overall business strategy, but also well diversified and profitable. You don’t want to take all work under the sun and try to be everything to everyone. At the same time, you don’t want to become too reliant on a handful of large accounts.

The contract manufacturing model that we implemented at Skilcraft forced us to evaluate our customer base. We reduced our customer base from 150 to fewer than 30 accounts. Longtime employees thought I was crazy. They asked me, "Are we really going to cut a 50% gross-profit customer?" But when we dug down into the revenue and uncovered the

true amount

of work it took to service that customer, we realized that, in fact, we weren’t making money.

If we saw no upside to bringing up an account to a certain level of business, then yes, we cut them. We prioritized certain industries, revenue levels, and margins. Having a smaller number of customers allowed us to service them at a higher level, utilize resources efficiently, and scale with these customers faster than if we were trying to be all things to all 150 customers we had originally.

We focused on customers with long-term relationships and established revenue and margin targets within specific industries. This helped us build longer-lasting customer relationships and foster a good customer base that, year over year, produced good revenue and profit.

That said, with our renewed focus on contract manufacturing, we did struggle with revenue concentration. No single customer should account for more than 20% to 30% of revenue. Exceptions abound within certain industries, and Skilcraft certainly had them, but the goal of well-diversified revenue should always be there.

High revenue concentration can be dangerous. A heavily weighted customer might move to another supplier, go out of business, or become aware of the leverage they have and try to drive down your margin. I had each occur during my time at Skilcraft, and major challenges arose every time.

5. Systems

Information technology (IT), enterprise resource planning (ERP), and customer relationship management (CRM) systems are essential. At Skilcraft, we went through three ERP upgrades and two IT system upgrades. I was proud of our efforts. We organized top-notch, cross-functional teams and moved forward only when we weren’t onboarding new clients or undergoing other significant events.

Still, to be honest, I hated every upgrade. They were

incredibly

disruptive—but also incredibly necessary. After all, these systems were critical for managing production, ensuring quality, meeting cost and profitability expectations, and providing customers with production visibility.

Good systems help scale your business like nothing else, but when implemented improperly, they can bring you to your knees. We experienced one ERP implementation that went wrong. It forced us to deliver late and hit us financially.

Remember, you and your team

know

what you need to run your company. Consultants can help implement, but their goal is for you to use the system as they designed it—not necessarily how you need it to run your business. New methods that ERP upgrades can bring will help you move forward, but consultants do not know your processes well enough to be prepared for problems.

6. Processes

Both for production and the office, processes need to be documented, quick and easy to learn, and repeatable—that is, every time you follow the process, you get the same result. Without good, well-defined processes, scaling gets difficult.

At Skilcraft, we had specific processes for jobs and for managing the company. We developed them around information gleaned from resources like the Traction Entrepreneurial Operating System (

eosworldwide.com

) and Scaling Up (

scalingup.com

). We kept tabs on our key performance indicators (KPIs) regularly; held daily huddles; and performed weekly, quarterly, and annual strategic reviews. All these helped ensure everyone was working toward their goals and pulling the rope in the same direction.

It took us a few years to develop a robust set of management processes. When we did, we were able to scale effectively and with a little less pain. More than once, we

doubled

production volume from one year to the next, and good processes enabled us to do it.

7. Intellectual Property

If you’re in custom fabrication, you probably don’t own the design IP (intellectual property) of the products you build, but you

do

own the production processes. Yes, purchased machines aren’t your IP—but the way you put them together, from laser cutting to bending to powder coating,

can

be IP. Know when you have it. This helps you protect it and price work accordingly.

At Skilcraft, we developed specific fabrication processes for customers, making it difficult for them to move their business elsewhere. These represented our IP, and protecting it added value to our business.

8. People

Small businesses often have trouble finding and retaining people, and Skilcraft was no exception. In general, larger companies can offer better pay and benefits. What they can’t always offer, however, is

opportunity

. If someone wants to engage, learn, wear multiple hats, and take on new responsibilities, a well-run small company can be a good place to be.

At Skilcraft, we chose to invest and develop our team. We gave people opportunities to learn and take more responsibility. We were very intent on creating a culture that helped employees to feel appreciated and empowered. Most important, they knew

where we were going

as a company and where they could contribute.

We worked with high schools and technical and vocational schools. We trained everyone: operators and material handlers, engineers, the quality staff, quoting, sales, customer service. Our talent pipeline came through local vocational programs and internal training. Within seven years we grew from 40 to 175 employees. That growth spurred more career opportunities and increased how much people could earn. Word got around, too. People knew that, if they wanted a rewarding manufacturing career, Skilcraft was a good place to be.

We aligned our organization to the products and capabilities we offered to our customers and staffed them accordingly. We focused on areas like welding, engineering, ERP-related roles, quoting, and quality. Attracting the right people is always a challenge. But having the right culture—one that values investing in employees and providing new opportunities—makes growth easier.

9. Leadership

When it came to leaders—including executives, managers, and supervisors—we first looked within the company, then recruited outside talent. Most important, we

invested in training

. Using a local university that had an established leadership training program (one of the best I’ve ever encountered), we trained people who demonstrated a desire and capability to be future leaders.

The program required a four-month commitment with frequent time out of the facility. It covered subjects such as behavioral and motivation profiles, supervision practices, strategic thinking, time management, conflict management, and interpersonal skills. That significant investment helped us all lead in similar styles, communicate well, and build a good culture.

10. Profitability

Every checklist item so far carries weight when valuing a business, but I can assure you, profit carries the most weight of all. A healthier EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation, and amortization) brings a greater valuation, which in turn opens your business’s future to a more successful transition for everyone, including shareholders, employees, and the community.

At Skilcraft, we established an earnings target to reach within five years. I kept a running valuation model each year and reviewed how our EBITDA was doing. It was yet another way of making sure our actions were generating value for shareholders and positioning the company for success beyond us.

Good, sustainable profitability fuels growth; allows you to invest in capital equipment and hire additional people; and maintains financial ratios and covenants with your lending institution. If your bottom line isn’t growing with your top line, you can run into trouble.

Some say that setting profitability targets is the wrong way to establish a growth goal. They argue that you need to first establish your "why"—that is, the purpose of your business. If you next define the "what" and "how," profits will follow. I agree with having a "why, what, and how," but profits

must

factor into the equation. After all, we are in business to make profits, and profits allow the "why, what, and how" to be realized.

About Legacy

You know the saying:

If you aren’t growing, you’re dying

. It’s a true statement, as long as you go about growing in the proper way, one that’s sustainable beyond the working life of the owner. Again, it’s about establishing a legacy. A growth-oriented company is positioned for new ownership that will continue the success that the founders and employees created.

At Skilcraft, we had a vision, established revenue and earnings targets, and took a 40-year-old business down a new path. We green-fielded a second facility dedicated to a specific industry that brought the company worldwide brand recognition. We created jobs, provided opportunities for our employees, and created value for shareholders. We received various awards from state and regional organizations as well as several customer awards from global OEMs. Ultimately, we created a contract manufacturer that could transition to another owner who, as part of a larger corporation, could continue to build the business. To the founder’s legacy, we added our own.
