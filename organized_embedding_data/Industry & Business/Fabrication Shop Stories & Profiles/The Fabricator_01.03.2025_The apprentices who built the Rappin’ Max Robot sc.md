# The apprentices who built the "Rappin’ Max Robot" sculpture

N.Y.-based apprenticeship takes unique approach RAFAEL to developing future fabricators

Rafael Guerrero

N

ot often do you run into an 18-ft.-tall robot, but you just might at Columbus Park in the Brooklyn borough of New York City.

"Rappin’ Max Robot," created by artist Eric Orr in the 1980s, is often credited as the first hip-hop comic book.

In 2024, Brooklyn-based Welder Underground reimagined the comic book’s title character as a large steel sculpture that weighs approximately 8,000 lbs. It has resided in the park since late October and will remain there through mid-April before moving to Paris.

The "Rappin’ Max Robot" sculpture was the inaugural project of Welder Underground, a unique six-month apprenticeship that debuted last year with six apprentices. Its objective is to teach young adults how to weld and fabricate through the building of large-scale public art and to entice the next generation of skilled tradespeople with opportunities in large, visible work that can be admired for years to come.

A Unique Apprenticeship

Marc and Adina Levin founded Collab, a fabrication/design studio, about 15 years ago. Currently, the 14,000-sq.-ft. studio with 25-ft.-tall ceilings houses CNC machines, water jet capability, rapid prototyping, laser cutters, and 3D printing, and can accommodate everything from welding to cutting to woodworking.

"When we started, we always thought there would be an opportunity to take the skills we employ on a daily basis to earn our livings to create apprenticeships programs, where we could teach those skills to young people so they could have an employable future," said Marc Levin.

Since its inception, Collab has found ways to teach, train, and pay young adults through project-based learning opportunities in areas like woodworking and robotics.

Levin, though, noticed how quickly people were drawn to welding just by handing them a torch.

Six apprentices worked on the "Rappin’ Max Robot" sculpture with help from in-house staff, local mentors, and instructors.

Brooklyn, N.Y.-based Welder Underground is a six-month apprenticeship opportunity for young adults to learn how to weld and fabricate through public art. The program’s first project was an 18-ft.-tall steel robot sculpture.

Images: Welder Underground

Apprentices worked 40 hours a week for six months as part of the Welder Underground apprenticeship.

"Welding feels like a superpower," he said, adding that it gives people the ability to put pieces of metal together.

When deciding how to best use the studio space, Levin said, "When I noticed all these kids took to welding so quickly, we saw that this was the future."

The Levins and Collab launched Welder Underground in spring 2024. For six months, the apprentices spent 40 hours a week at the shop, earning $15/hour. The first eight weeks were dedicated toward training, learning about safety, and learning how to weld. Once training ended, the apprentices were ready for hands-on work, developing technical and soft skills.

The apprentices came in with no welding experience—the only expectations were that they be ready and willing to work, to learn, to be safe, and to show up on time.

"We learned that young people, if inspired, the ones who thought little of themselves and their abilities, realize they have many more talents and abilities if they just apply themselves," Levin said.

Leaving Their Mark

As for Welder Underground’s first big project, Collab had connected with Orr as part of the studio’s involvement with The Hip Hop Museum, slated to open in New York in the near future.

Levin said they liked Orr’s "Rappin’ Max Robot" and thought it would be great to turn it into a 3D character.

Following their training, the apprentice group worked with two instructors, two mentors who are metal sculptors, and internal staff to bring the large steel sculpture to fruition.

"The concept of building something that can stand and live in the world, that these young people can point to for the rest of their lives and say to themselves and others that ‘I built that?’ It’s very meaningful," Levin explained. "We all want to leave our mark; we all want to have some relationship with the world we live in. And that relationship can be deepened by some connection with something physical that stands."

The finished product has garnered positive public reaction. Welder Underground shared footage of the sculpture being built and unveiled, and those interviewed used words like "unbelievable," "surreal," and "monumental." Even Orr weighed in, calling it "absolutely amazing."

"It’s a great portfolio piece. … They’re not just learning to do something out of context, they’re learning to do something by building something that will exist somewhere," Levin added. "Project-based learning is a deeper way to learn something."

Who, What Is Next?

Welder Underground selected its first group of apprentices through word of mouth and recommendations, said Levin. Of the six apprentices, two have since been hired full time by Collab; one went to work for a shelter in maintenance and building repair; one began another apprenticeship elsewhere; one went into clothing design; and another became a freelancer.

The success and publicity of the first group has raised the interest for the second installment. The next group of apprentices—who reported to the studio in March—will be larger, going from six to 10.

"We’ve built enough of a reputation here in New York where we’re getting lots of applications for this next [group]," Levin said.

The next group also will be working on a sculpture, and Welder Underground hopes to bring in welders and mentors like last time to provide input and assist with skills development. What that next project will be, though, is a secret, Levin said.
