Artist Sharon Agnor shares the stories behind her sculptures

RAFAEL GUERRERO

Sharon Agnor stands beside her sculpture, "Vancouver Volunteers." Agnor is an artist based out of the Pacific Northwest.

Images: Sharon Agnor

E

ach of Sharon Agnor’s sculptures tells a story, with topics ranging from the big and broad to things that are more personal to the Vancouver, Wash., artist. Take her work on the campanile tower in nearby Washougal, Wash. Sixty pieces of glass and stainless steel help tell the history of Washougal, showcasing significant events and landmarks. The sculpture’s name, "Two Rivers Heritage," was inspired by how the smaller Washougal River meets the Columbia River.

These pieces wrap around the bottom 25 ft. of the 40-ft.-tall tower.

"As [visitors] walk around the sculpture, they can learn about the town. … This is a really wonderful way to kind of make [the campanile tower] more of a focal point in town and make it more of a place to visit, especially if you have tourists or out-of-town guests," she said.

The sculpture was installed in late August, and a formal unveiling was scheduled for Sept. 6, at the time of this writing. It’s the largest project Agnor has worked on so far and required her to partner with an engineer and contractor.

Unlike her other projects, she opted to use CNC cutting rather than her hand-held plasma cutter.

"When I cut with a plasma cutter, I have to grind all the edges. I’m trying to save my joints a little bit, so for this size of a project, I’m definitely happy to have it cut [this way]," she said.

Another piece, the 7-ft.-tall "Vancouver Volunteers" sculpture, tells the story of the volunteers in the Vancouver, Wash., community. Installed in 2023, the three-dimensional V-shaped piece is the centerpiece of the city’s Volunteer Corner mini-plaza.

Her largest sculpture is made of stainless steel and glass and wraps around a campanile tower in Washougal, Wash. Ahead of the installation, Agnor and others cut wooden versions of the sculpture pieces and wrapped them around the tower to see how the final version would fit.

Agnor fabricated this project with stainless steel and glass and added color to the sculpture by using automotive pinstriping paint. It weighs roughly 150 lbs., explained Agnor, with air primarily surrounding an angle iron armature inside the V.

"The work she does is really interesting, combining glass with metal and making these sculptures," said Owen Zentil, owner of the Metal Supermarkets location in Portland, Ore. Agnor and other artists like her frequent the store for materials and find odds and ends for their projects, he added. "She’s partnering with local cities and putting artwork out in downtown areas where people can enjoy it."

Then there is "Walking Warrior," a stainless steel/glass/bronze piece that once stood in downtown Hillsboro, Ore. The human-size sculpture tells the story of Agnor’s Stage 3 breast cancer diagnosis and how she was "leaning in, moving on, and not getting stuck" with the aftermath of such a life-altering moment.

"You come to a point where you have to make a decision on what your life is going to look like," Agnor said. "It’s really easy to get stuck in that medical world where you’re just paranoid about it coming back and you’re super alert to aches and pains. You have to decide, ‘Is this how it’s going to be for me now, or am I going to move forward?’"

Agnor said she is fine now, adding that she has "residual stuff from radiation and chemo." She is still able to work and sculpt, which is what is important to her.

"You know, I’m doing what I’m able to do, what I love to do, and it’s a blessing to me to be able to work," she said. "I work as much as I can; when I am not doing well, I just take a break.

"I want to work as long as I can. As long as jobs keep coming, I’ll keep applying for things and just see how far it goes."

A Late Bloomer

Agnor, 72, has always had an eye for art. But she was a late bloomer when it came to bringing artwork to life, taking her first art classes at a local community college in her late 40s. She described herself as being "pretty mediocre" at some of the art mediums.

But then she saw a flier for a new sculptural welding class. At first, the class intimidated her for a few reasons, such as learning how to use welding equipment or the fact there weren’t many women in the class. But as time passed, Agnor grew comfortable working with metal.

"I really started to love it. I started to love metal because of what you could do with it when you put it together," she said. "[Metal sculptures] can be very big and very strong, but I could do it. I could make it happen. And as a woman, that was pretty empowering."

Most of her work can be found in Washington or Oregon; she did finish a piece for a botanical garden in Indiana this year.

Agnor’s shop has MIG and TIG welding machines, a propane forge, anvil, plasma cutter, oxyacetylene equipment, slip roller, cutoff saws, tile cutters, equipment for glass, and air-powered tools.

"I started so late, and I remember feeling a little bit jealous of younger people," said Agnor. "Whenever I see a young artist, someone like in their 20s, I just think, ‘Oh, you have so long to learn.’ … For someone in their 20s, I say just go for it.

"It’s really a gift to be able to create something and have a material that is so wonderful to work with," she added. "Everybody has that gift of creativity—it’s just different for everybody."
