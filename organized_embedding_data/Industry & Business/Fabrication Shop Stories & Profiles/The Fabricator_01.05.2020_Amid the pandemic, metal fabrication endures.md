# Amid the pandemic, metal fabrication endures

Special Report COVID-19

The industry isn’t booming, but it isn’t down for the count either

From Staff Reports

ow quickly the world changes. In early March Dan Zimmerman, business development manager at Laserfab, was at the Fabricators & Manufacturers Association’s Annual Meeting in San Antonio, Texas. Business was booming at the 22-employee fabricator in Lebanon, Pa. A few weeks later he and other managers were wondering whether they were going to shut down completely—not from financial insolvency, but because, in a scrambled reaction to a global pandemic, the state government was still refining its guidance as to which businesses were critical and which weren’t.

As it turned out, Laserfab very much fit into the former category. At this writing (in early April) the fabricator was laser cutting and bending components for hospital beds.

staff reached out to various operations in an effort—from a business activity standpoint, at least—to take the industry’s temperature. The verdict at this writing (on April 14), at least based on anecdotal evidence, is that metal fabrication isn’t feeling great, but it isn’t sick either. Some companies are suffering from sharply lower order volumes, but others serving critical supply chains are showing extraordinary resilience.

Travel, retail, and certain parts of manufacturing might be virtually shut down, but bright spots remain. Much of metal fabrication occurs in the job shop and contract manufacturing world, and in these spheres a well-diversified customer base has become the industry’s saving grace.

Hiring Restaurant Workers

Consider Prototek, a custom metal fabricator with plants in New Hampshire, Wisconsin, and California. Conditions can change in a hurry during a crisis, but as of mid-April at least, the operation remained busy. It even hired a few people who’ve been laid off from restaurants and other customer-facing service-sector jobs. "Business is still very strong, and buyers are placing a lot of orders," said PJ Swett, vice president of operations.

The company serves a diverse supply chain and does a fair amount of business in the defense and medical supply chains, so it’s been deemed an essential business. It has a good supply of masks and gloves, and they’re in touch with customers and vendors (especially in the medical supply chain) to ensure there’s enough to go around. Everyone on the floor is maintaining a safe distance. They already were at a safe distance in the cutting and bending departments, of course, but the operation has had to spread out workers in other areas, particularly grinding and assembly.

Prototek’s customer mix shifts continually—lowvolume and prototype work is its niche, after all—and it’s a trait that many custom fabricators share. That adaptability will come in very handy in the coming weeks and months, and it shows just how resilient the custom metal fabrication business model really is.

Fabricators Step Up

Fabricators are stepping up to meet the needs of the crisis head-on. Consider Maryland-based Marlin Steel, which has ramped up its operations to supply test tube racks for COVID-19 testing facilities. As the company stated in an e-newsletter, "These test tube racks were made possible by our team’s diligence and quick turnaround, as we raced around the clock to fill an emergency test tube rack order and get these critical products manufactured and shipped to a large health care provider, where they will be used to save lives, expedite testing, and help combat the spread of COVID-19."

Then there’s Pioneer IWS, a fabricator in the Amish community of Dalton, Ohio. Running off a natural gas generator, the shop has all the characteristics of a modern precision fabrication operation, from laser cutting to press braking and robotic welding. As of mid-April the company, which produces flexible workstations, had retooled to produce thousands of field hospital beds destined for Ohio, California, New York, and elsewhere.

Shops Apply for Financial Assistance

It’s tough to generalize business conditions in metal fabrication, even in normal times, and conditions shop managers are reporting now are more varied than ever. Moreover, just because a shop remains operational doesn’t mean it couldn’t use financial assistance. Many fabricators have gone to their banks to apply for the Small Business Administration’s Paycheck Protection Program, a key component of government stimulus. It’s aimed to give loans to small businesses of 500 or fewer employees. The loans can be worth up to 2.5 times the average monthly payroll costs (up to $10 million) to cover payroll, interest, and rent. If at least 75% of the loan is used for payroll expenses, and if head counts and salary levels remain stable, the loans will be fully forgiven.

FMA Stands up for Fabricators

Metal fabrication is a critical link in the country’s essential manufacturing supply chains. That said, not all local and state governments have been on the same page. That’s a real problem, and it’s one that manufacturing trade groups, including the Fabricators & Manufacturers Association International, aim to address.

In late March Ed Youdell, FMA’s president and CEO, wrote to President Donald Trump regarding the essential nature of metal fabrication and its importance to helping put an end to the COVID-19 pandemic. Youdell is now asking metal fabricators and manufacturers to share a similar message with the nation’s governors.

"As you lead this country’s efforts to defeat the COVID-19 virus, please consider the men and women who work in the metal fabrication industry an essential part of the resources and effort needed to push back and ultimately eliminate this terrible disease," Youdell wrote. "Any prolonged work stoppage of job shops across the U.S. will only slow efforts to defeat and eliminate COVID-19."

Communication Is Paramount

"We take it day by day, and the news changes every day," said David Berdass, president of Bermo Inc., Circle Pines, Minn. "I try not to go on the floor so much, and neither does my plant manager. But I keep employees informed with regular postings every few days or even every day. We tell everyone the orders we have, what’s changing, and what we’re seeing down the line. And I tell them we appreciate what they’re doing. I make sure they’re not left on an island, where they don’t know what’s going on."

Steve McDaniel and David Kennedy are following a similar strategy. A little more than six years ago the two founded US Duct, an industrial ductwork manufacturer in Kernersville, N.C., an operation that found itself in a situation that demanded quick action: a large HVAC component for a local hospital. The team met the challenge at a time when many operations in the area had temporarily closed.

"We realize how essential the work that we do is for maintaining health and safety for the people who rely on our products," said Kim Williams, director of marketing. "Steve McDaniel, our company president, has said that this is a historic time, and how we choose to respond will be the story that we’ll be telling our grandchildren in the years ahead. He asked the question, ‘What’s the story you want to tell?’"

Currently the most popular stories sound extraordinarily dire. "I wouldn’t call this a great recession or even a great depression. It’s a great paralysis, because for the first time that I can think of in recorded human history, you’ve had a literal standstill of large parts of the major economies of the world, which have literally just stopped functioning."

So said CNN anchor Fareed Zakaria in a mid-April interview with the producers of TED Talks. And yes, for large sectors of the customer-facing service economy, Zakaria’s comment is pretty much spot-on. But go back a few links on the supply chain and the story changes.

No doubt, manufacturing is suffering. Demand from big sectors like automotive has fallen off a cliff. But fabricators carry on serving the needs of medical, defense, and other critical sectors. For those with strong balance sheets and enough financial resources to weather the storm, the opportunities during the eventual rebound could be tremendous.

Back at Bermo, managers have helped space out operations, especially in grinding and assembly, to ensure everyone follows physical distancing protocols. Equipment is wiped down regularly, and staff are given gloves and other personal protective equipment so that they can stay safe.

"And obviously, we have policies in place if people have fevers or other symptoms," Berdass said. "We have monitors in place." At this writing, no one at Bermo has tested positive for the virus.

Before Minnesota locked down and closed schools, Berdass met with company leaders to develop a scheduling strategy. They were prepared to change shift hours and even ramp up weekend work to accommodate people’s schedules and parenting requirements.

"By the time the schools closed, we had no requests," he said, though he added the company would gladly accommodate. "Everyone has come to work, and no one has complained. I had expected to implement a hodgepodge of work schedules to accommodate employees, but so far we haven’t had to do that."

"Accommodate employees" is the key phrase there. It’s always better to face challenges together, something that custom fabricators are keenly aware of. The shop floor depends on good information from the front office (job travelers with no surprises, for instance). Everyone looks up- and downstream. The process chain—cutting, bending, welding, grinding, coating, assembly—is only as good as its weakest link. People work together or not at all.

Beating the virus demands physical distance, but not social distance. Teamwork, timely information, and openness have never been more important, and in many places across the industry, all three seem to be alive and well.
