The potential of scaling up in metal manufacturing

TIM HESTON

his year’s FABTECH show in Chicago broke exhibit-space records. The early September event drew more than 40,000 people. Optimism seemed to be everywhere, despite broader concerns about tariff uncertainty, weak job numbers overall, and inflation.

Joe Hogg sensed the optimism too. The president and founder of Inflexion Financial, a Charlotte, N.C.-based management consulting and M&A firm has analyzed recent acquisition deals throughout the metal fabrication arena. "Given the data we’ve seen in public markets, there’s going to be a lot more interest from investors to get involved in the metal fabrication sector, especially from private equity," he said. "Now, with the backdrop being so positive for U.S.-based manufacturing, and the increased worry about what could happen to supply chains if something were to happen in East Asia, there’s going to be a renewed interest to get deals done."

The sector has its share of headwinds, at least in the near term. Many fabricators reported coming off a slow summer as certain sectors put major projects on hold. Now, as more are adapting to a new reality around trade and tariffs, more projects are indeed moving forward.

Of course, our politicians give us a range of uncertainties (such as a potentially prolonged government shutdown). And struggles in certain sectors remain, as the so-called "vibesession" persists. Some shops have reported down orders in lawn and garden, commercial trucking, and especially agricultural equipment. Farmers are being hit hard by the trade war as China stops buying many agricultural products. Combine decreased demand with bumper crops, and you have an industry that’s not ready to boost capex anytime soon.

So, why all the optimism at FABTECH? Because some sectors continue to race ahead with some unprecedented levels of demand—most notably AI, data centers, and the power generation to support it all. The sector has ramped up demand extraordinarily quickly, and investment isn’t abating yet.

Second, modern contract fabricators have developed some seriously robust business models—and Mayville Engineering Co. (MEC) is a prime example. With so many plants across the eastern half of the country, and with penetration into so many markets, MEC has become an industry bellwether. The fabricator has a diverse mix of revenue sources and industries it serves, yet until this year it didn’t serve the data center market in a serious way—which is why the acquisition of Accu-Fab LLC, a fabricator in North Carolina and Illinois with heavy exposure to data centers, made a world of sense.

According to a press release announcing the purchase, "Accu-Fab brings to MEC a track record of profitable growth and operational excellence. … During the three years ending in 2024, Accu-Fab has generated an average adjusted EBITDA margin of 20.2% compared to MEC’s average adjusted EBITDA margin of 11.2% during the same period."

On Sept. 15, MEC submitted an SEC filing that showed just how strong Accu-Fab’s financials were—and just how rapidly the fabricator was growing. Between 2023 and 2024, revenue grew from $36 million to $61 million; at the same time, net income

quadrupled

, going from $2.1 million in 2023 to $9 million in 2024. That slice of publicly released financials shows just how profitable a well-run metal fabricator can be, if it has the discipline to instill continuous improvement practices and build an internal operational structure to scale up and meet demand quickly.

But it also shows why the current boom times might not lead to a repeat of the dot-com bust. Accu-Fab was highly concentrated in a highly profitable sector—but no party lasts forever. Combining forces with a large, highly diversified contract fabrication operation makes a world of sense.

More broadly speaking, Hogg said the move is showing outside investors just how much opportunity there is in the metal fabrication space. With the high expense of high-powered fiber lasers and automation in general, the barrier to entry within certain metal fabrication niches is a lot higher than it once was.

The industry will always have a "long tail" of operations serving niche sectors. But in some areas of the market, the ingredients for further consolidation are there. Combine these with the generational shift—with boomer-generation owners retiring and looking to sell—and the metal fabrication industry overall might be on the verge of significant change in the coming years.
