Reshaping Fabrication

Smart use of information allows fabricators to scale

T

he FAB 40 not only shows revenue trends in custom metal fabrication but also reveals strategies at companies that operate under the radar, away from popular and even general business media.

Progressive fabricators are breaking down walls that hinder communication. They’re spreading knowledge of manufacturability to customers and other players in the supply chain, and they’re considering new approaches to automation, from part sorting to autonomous mobile robots.

They’re also working to make every individual on the payroll more valuable by focusing on work that customers pay for. Customers don’t pay for longer-than-expected setups; for sorting parts; for rework or hunts for missing work-in-process; or for miscommunication about manufacturing details like weld fixturing, press brake tooling, and material consistency and quality.

Most notably, companies in this year’s FAB 40 are making good use of software, especially those platforms that offer ease of integration and metrics in real time. Such visibility leaves few inefficiencies hidden. Once those inefficiencies are discovered, communication commences, challenges are overcome, and the virtuous cycle of improvement keeps rolling.

No 9: Momentum Manufacturing Group

Matthew Smith has seen a lot of change during his tenure in metal fabrication. He joined when the organization (then known as NSA Industries) had one metal fabrication plant in the hills of northern Vermont. That operation now is part of Georgetown, Mass.-based Momentum Manufacturing Group (MMG), which has 14 locations and more than 1 million sq. ft. of manufacturing space throughout New England.

Smith, now chief commercial officer of the group, reported that sales are slightly off this year, especially for consumer goods like lawn and garden and exercise equipment. One exception is the semiconductor sector. "That area has certainly stayed very, very busy," he said, "which of course makes sense, considering all the investments going on."

One constant at MMG is change. Spending on exercise and lawn care equipment skyrocketed during the pandemic, and now there’s a pullback—but for how long? MMG needs to ramp up quicky, and to that end the organization implemented several significant initiatives.

First, it has brought automation integration in-house. "We’re implementing automation ourselves now," Smith said. "We’ve got an implementation team that targets certain processes."

He added that plenty of outside integration firms have helped MMG over the years, but many of those firms today are very busy with other, often very large projects. At the same time, the pace of change has accelerated significantly in recent years, and the fabricator simply needed a faster way to automate.

"In a recent project, we’re taking a 20-year-old machine, and we’re automating it," Smith said, adding that one cell today actually involves a mobile robot moving parts from a lathe to a mill and finally (for some parts) into a fixture for a coordinate measurement machine (CMM). If the CMM detects a measurement that needs an adjustment, it reports the error back to the machines that make the part, which then makes the necessary adjustments.

Beyond this, the automation team is looking to automate some of its panel bending machines with pick-and-place robotics, and is considering the use of autonomous mobile robots (AMRs). "We’ve run some tests, and we’ve found it’s able to find the quickest path, accounting for obstacles in its way," Smith said.

As the automation team grows, the company is looking to add more expertise in control languages, to get different machine controllers to "talk" with robots and other automation elements. A key issue has been developing interfaces between disparate equipment, like between a press brake, a panel bender, and robots handling parts.

That said, not every technology project requires complex integration, and this includes a certain kind of factory production monitoring system that MMG recently installed. Called Guidewheel, the factory operations software platform tracks uptime and overall equipment effectiveness (OEE) not through control interfaces but instead by reading the electrical signals that go into the machine. If a machine uses electricity, the system can connect to it.

"Consider a press brake," Smith said. "You’ve got the base power when the machine is on and idle. But then you bend, and you have different power levels. The system measures the fluctuations in power, so it knows when the machine is actually running. It’s very basic; it takes about 10 minutes to install it on a machine, but yet it gives you very good information."

MMG has the system integrated with about 40 machines so far, tracking the cycles and giving baselines for improvement. The frequency of the power fluctuations can reveal a lot—like any setup issues on the brake (the ram strokes infrequently), and it can uncover hidden best practices, like an expert operator who’s able to process parts faster than his or her peers. And it can uncover areas for needed technology investment, be it a new machine or automation.

Smith added that more acquisitions might be on the horizon for MMG, as well as new organic growth opportunities from some major OEMs. But whatever is in the fabricator’s future, flexibility—and moving fast—will be key.

No. 12: Maysteel Industries LLC

Peek at some of the technology at Star Precision Manufacturing outside Denver, and you can see why Maysteel Industries made the move to acquire the precision fabricator in January of this year. Star invested heavily in automation; turrets and press brakes have auto-load capabilities and automated tool changes. And its customer mix complements and augments Maysteel’s existing core markets, including data storage, utilities, and renewable energy.

As sources at the company explained, automation isn’t all about robotic cells and automated tool changes, though they are a big part of the equation. Another important element is software, especially platforms that facilitate communication and analysis throughout the enterprise.

"For instance, we’re automating the feedback we give to our various team members. We use an HRIS [human resources information system] system that is also connected to our ERP system, so we can get real-time feedback to employees."

That was Kevin Matkin, Maysteel CEO, describing some of the newly implemented information systems that’s helping the almost 90-year-old metal fabricator scale. This includes increased focus on enterprise-wide analytics using Microsoft Power BI.

"Our continuous improvement group has really focused on Power BI and has been behind implementing a lot of it," said Lindsay Ojeda, director of marketing and new business leads. "We use our business intelligence platform to help us understand how we [can] make better decisions to serve our customers. We can see our productivity levels, our on-time delivery, what has shipped, and what hasn’t. The dashboards are fantastic."

"Our continuous improvement folks have really championed putting this information in front of the appropriate people," Matkin said, "from the leadership team down to production supervision and the shop floor."

The idea is to spread best practices. Say a welder in one plant achieves a certain productivity level for a certain job, as recorded by the ERP and presented by Power BI analytics. The fabricator today has plants in Wisconsin, Texas, Mexico, Virginia, and (with the Star Precision acquisition) Colorado. Such geographic diversity has obvious benefits for customers, but myriad locations can make communication a challenge. Another welder or welding team at a different plant welds the same job. Cycle time at one plant might be much shorter—but why? Real-time data tracking can help answer that question.

"Our COO is now bringing those kinds of information together," Matkin said, "and we can develop best practices from there."

All this helps get everyone on the same page, which for Maysteel has become more important as the fabricator works to broaden its services, such as expanding its project management arm to focus on not just delivery but also the installation of products in the field.

"We’re really expanding into the project management arena," Matkin said. "It’s about the detailed tracking of information and being able to communicate it all efficiently between us and the customer on very large projects with multiple stakeholders and simultaneous activities."

The expansion of Maysteel’s project management group began about two years ago, and the effort is shaping business development. Plenty of jobs don’t require broad project management, but these days, even piece-part or subassembly work for OEMs require more communication than ever.

Complementing this is the company’s broader efforts to build and maintain what Matkin called "a defined set of diverse markets with tailwinds." Data center, infrastructure, energy storage, and renewable energy in general are all prime examples, though that mix might evolve in the years to come as the fabricator grows organically and through acquisitions.

"We need to be sure we’re focused on the right markets," Matkin said. "This is an ongoing effort. It’s not something we can decide and be done with it."

Complementing that market analysis is, of course, technology, including the automation of both information (with software) and the actual fabricated workpieces that flow through a plant’s value stream.

Matkin put it this way: "If we want to grow business, we not only need to be flexible and agile but also scalable. Technology gives us a chance to do that."

No. 17: Standard Iron & Wire Works

CEO Brian Hazelton joined Standard Iron & Wire Works in February, and he’s new to the contract metal fabrication world—but in many ways, he’s not new at all. He spent years at OEMs like Briggs & Stratton and Winnebago Industries. He had worked with Standard Iron as a customer, and now he’s leading the company, a large contract fabricator that operates in two very different spaces: a production division that serves OEMs as well as a division on architectural metals, a sector the company was founded on almost 100 years ago.

The two divisions operate in different arenas when it comes to the nature of production flow. Architectural Metals (which includes a railing system product line) is project-based, with varying demands on fabrication capacity, while the OEM side (Contract Metals) ships a steady production of parts and subassemblies. Sales and design teams operate separately. Because customer demands in each area are unique, "we really do treat them as separate businesses," Hazelton said.

Still, both use similar or identical metal fabrication technology, so the resource-sharing opportunity abounds. For instance, architectural metals manufacturing has dedicated cells in certain plants, but the cells can be used for contract manufacturing if needed. "At present, they’re set up as dedicated operations," Hazelton said.

Moving from the OEM to the contract fab world, Hazelton brings fresh perspective. First, he appreciates the diversified business. When some sectors are down, others are up. This year, for instance, Standard Iron is seeing a slowdown in the agricultural equipment market, especially on big-ticket items likely affected by interest rates. At the same time, power generation, infrastructure, mining, and related industrial markets keep going at full throttle.

In January 2022, Owner Resource Group, a private equity group based in Austin, Texas, bought a majority interest in the company. "They’ve been very supportive," Hazelton said, "not just organically, but looking to see how we can grow with the right acquisition. A good match has to make financial sense, but from a cultural standpoint, we’re looking for companies that follow servant leadership models. If you don’t have a good cultural match, it’s very hard to get [the combined organization] pulling in the right direction."

He added that Owner Resource Group’s investment didn’t involve any turnaround or massive change in direction. The move was more about building off what was already a successful company. "Historically, they’ve looked for successful businesses that needed helped growing," Hazelton said, adding that the Demeules family, which founded the business in 1930, remains highly involved. Rich Demeules, Standard Iron’s previous CEO, is on the board.

Over the past few months, Hazelton has been reviewing the fabricator’s new product introduction (NPI) process, looking for areas to streamline. "We’re already good at it, but I think we can be even better." One key aspect, he said, is transparency and communication. "Is everyone onboard? That’s the key for a good NPI process, to give all operational teams visibility upfront so that the factory knows what they’re going to build, the supply chain knows what they need to buy, and the engineers can start strategizing. Does the business need a dedicated cell? Can we automate this? It’s about doing more on the front end and not just taking a job on and trying to figure it out later.

"Again, we’ve done a good job with this, but we can always get better at it. If we’re going to grow this business, the lifeblood is going to be new product development."

No. 21: Miller Fabrication Solutions

When CEO Eric Miller learned of an opportunity from an ag equipment supplier that required manufacturing capacity between September through February, he found it slotted in nicely among other work at Miller Fabrication Solutions, which usually goes into high gear during the spring and summer, making parts for next season’s offerings for construction equipment suppliers. The new work complements opportunities in power generation, too, another area the fabricator sees significant demand.

"Historically, sales used to be one of our greatest bottlenecks," Miller said. "Now, our pipeline now is huge. To be honest, we’ve never been in this situation to see this level of demand."

The Brookville, Pa., fabricator ramped up its investment in sales and marketing in 2018. MFS’s production business has long sales cycles, so it can take years for a project to come to fruition. Discussions around orders in power generation, for instance, began in 2022, and the first products now are hitting the shop floor. Today, those initial sales investments are paying off.

A portion of new sales relate to MFS’s growing capability in heavy fabrication. Miller described one prospect who visited, observed how clean and organized the operation was, noticed the heavy machining and plate cutting equipment capacity on the floor, and initiated a large order there and then. "There just aren’t that many of us who have this kind of heavy-duty capacity," Miller said.

Marketing for the company now is focused less on new customer acquisition and more toward recruitment. The fabricator has the orders to grow, now more than ever. But like everyone else, it needs technical talent to sustain its current growth over the long term, as well as new technology that can help employees add more value.

Nowhere can this be seen more than in MFS’s new Pine Creek, Pa., facility, a brownfield site the fabricator purchased in 2021. "We’re trying to build automation into that facility from minute one," Miller said, "and be more productive with our labor."

The idea is to build a facility with fewer people wielding hammers and manually taking parts out of nests from plasma cutting tables and lasers. "Sorting is an important job, but there’s no one paying us to do that work," Miller said, "and it’s not particularly rewarding work."

By the end of the year, company leaders hope to remove denesting entirely from the plant floor. In May, a TRUMPF TruLaser Center 7030 with automated part removal began production. Dynamic pins under the cut blanks push them up to mate with grippers, which stack parts on pallets.

Not far from that will be a Messer plasma table with a so-called Smart Crane, able to lift heavy parts out of nests (separate from the plasma cutting table, so sorting and cutting can happen simultaneously). The crane then will place them on a conveyor toward a deslag system. The first time an MFS employee touches a part will be after it’s been cut and labeled (via an inkjet on the plasma table or alternative method), sorted, and deslagged.

"We’ll be able to sort almost 93% of our plasma-cut parts automatically," Miller said. "The only parts we won’t sort automatically will be those that are just too big for the automation to handle."

The automation allows MFS to scale with its production work. The aim is for raw material to come in one side of the plant and for fabricated kits to exit the other side. Those kits will either go directly to customers or to other MFS plants for final assembly or processing.

The Pine Creek plant also will give better opportunities for its existing workforce. "We can now make their positions a profit-center job," Miller said. "No one is paying us to sort parts, but they are paying us to cut, bend, machine, and weld parts."

Environmental cleanliness plays a role here, too, including noise and air quality. Noise levels never exceed 85 dB. Not only is the laser fully enclosed, but so is the plasma table. Any grinding happens with a hood nearby, to minimize dust. Wooden pallets are kept to a minimum; most products are transported on returnables. And as necessary, plates will be blasted before the primary cutting operations, to eliminate the need for smaller blast booths for individual parts.

Miller and his team have a vision of building a highly automated workflow, complete with automated guided vehicles (AGVs) transporting parts from blanking to bending and welding and beyond. That won’t happen immediately, of course, but it’s on the path.

"We’re just getting started [at the Pine Creek facility]," Miller said. "I’m sure we’ll run into challenges. But we’ve got a team of people working to overcome them."

No. 24: Lou-Rich Inc.

Price, on-time delivery, quality, customer service, and collaboration: Those attributes have been top of mind for Lou-Rich Inc.’s customers, at least according to voice-of-the-customer surveys the contract fabricator has been conducting for the past several years.

"Underneath that collaboration umbrella, we have design for manufacturability. Our team does some comprehensive work, looking at designs and coming back with suggestions. Our customers really appreciate that."

That was Lee Gulbrandson, general manager at the Albert Lea, Minn.-based contract metal fabricator, adding that those surveys have helped define why customers buy from the operation. The company also has used survey insights for content marketing efforts, be it evergreen content online or targeted advertising. "Ultimately, it’s less about sales and more about being helpful."

Marketing doesn’t focus on what Lou-Rich can do but instead on what current and potential customers can do to improve their outsourcing experience. This includes that all-important design for manufacturability (DFM), be it in laser cutting, bending, welding, or machining.

The company’s content marketing efforts are even being tied to machine utilization, with ads and other material showcasing certain technologies that happen to have open capacity. "We know it’s a tough ask and a hard line to connect [between machine capacity and marketing]," Gulbrandson said, "but we’re headed in that direction."

Marketing comes in many forms, of course, and it needn’t be digital. The best marketing, in fact, not only triggers orders but also builds relationships, which is just what happened when Bruce Mullenbach, national sales manager, sent a detailed proposal to a customer in Milwaukee. Presented over a Teams meeting, the proposal wasn’t just a simple bid but instead a detailed analysis of the project, including DFM suggestions that would help reduce cost and speed delivery.

"They were so impressed, they asked him to visit and train their young engineers about DFM," Gulbrandson said.

"We ended up presenting to about 30 people," Mullenbach said, adding that he and a team of experts spent an entire day covering the gamut of manufacturability issues: cut quality and achievable tolerances in laser cutting; how a radius forms in air bending on a brake; why flange length requirements are what they are; why holes near or on bend lines usually aren’t a good idea; and why sheet metal tolerances in thickness and hardness really matter (a different thickness gives you a different radius, which changes the elongation and everything else). They covered fixturing and distortion effects in welding, powder coating requirements, and milling and turning.

"We literally presented for about eight hours," Mullenbach said. "They had questions, and we had a team of technical experts who could answer those questions. These experts have been with us 20, 30, some even 40 years, and they’ve seen everything. We really had a great collaboration that day."

Mullenbach conceded that the team didn’t get a chance to cover every aspect of DFM. "We could have spent eight hours on press brakes alone," he said. Still, the event likely won’t be the last, either. And it will likely spur future collaborations.

Theoretically, customers and prospects could use that newfound information to work with cheaper competitors that perhaps don’t provide so much help. Still, the cost of not sharing that information is probably greater. After all, engineers are retiring, and vast quantities of knowledge and experience are exiting the workforce. Collaboration helps stem the brain drain, and in this industry, at this juncture, nothing could be more important.
