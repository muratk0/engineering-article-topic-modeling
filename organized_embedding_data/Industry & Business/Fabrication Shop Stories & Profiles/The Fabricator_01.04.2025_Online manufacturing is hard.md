# Online manufacturing is hard

[TARİH: 01.04.2025 The Fabricator]

Tech Talk

Instant quoting is just the beginning

CALEB CHAMBERLAIN

T

his is the age of immediacy. It’s now possible to order most products online and have them in hand within a day, sometimes same day. Amazon-enabled speed has become so normal that we usually don’t think about it. It’s easy to forget that extreme accessibility and speed is a new phenomenon.

I remember when mail-order catalogs were the state of the art. You’d fill out a paper order form, mail it with a check, and maybe see your order in a couple weeks. But today, young adults are entering the workforce having never known a world without Amazon.

Speed and simplicity are now the status quo. It’s natural that customers increasingly expect the same from manufacturers, but that creates interesting challenges, especially for shops accustomed to working at a more sedate pace.

A Different Kind of Business

Compared to manufacturing in general, an online retailer like Amazon is obviously a different kind of business. That’s true across the board. Online retailers tend to be transactional; manufacturing tends to be higher touch and relationships based. Retailers optimize for logistics, inventory management, and distribution; manufacturers lean more heavily toward optimizing production flows. Retailers generally serve many thousands of customers; manufacturers often scale on the backs of a handful of large accounts. Retailers often serve consumers; manufacturers tend to serve businesses.

But as manufacturers take lessons from Amazon, the lines start to get blurry. Many companies, including my own, have integrated online instant quoting and self-serve ordering tools. At first glance, this might just seem like a simple way to optimize quoting. But the moment anyone on the internet can get an instant price and place an order, you’ve opened the door to a whole new way of running a manufacturing business. You’ve stepped into a world where you’ll now serve consumers; your prices are now publicly available and instantly comparable to other online-enabled vendors; you’ll tend to collect hundreds or even thousands of transactional one-off jobs; and because people can interact online, just like they might with Amazon, they will start expecting you to perform faster than ever.

This mixing of worlds creates an all new kind of business: one having all the complexity of manufacturing but also serving thousands of high-mix, low-volume, transactional customers who expect their parts delivered perfectly within 24 hours for cheap. Just like Amazon. It’s a challenging mix, but it also creates new opportunities. As I write, no single U.S. company has taken this new business model to its logical conclusion.

Be Careful What You Wish For

Many manufacturers prefer to have a handful of high-touch relationships with high-value customers. Recurring production is the name of the game. This deliberate strategy helps amortize expensive sales, bidding, engineering, and office overheads across lots of parts. These manufacturers often have to be selective about whom they serve, because high overheads make low-volume and one-off jobs unprofitable.

An online quoting and ordering tool attracts a different kind of business. At OSH Cut, 90% of the SKUs we produce are only ordered once. Customers come back and order again, but they are ordering different parts every time. And they’ll often order in quantities of one.

Absent office automation, it’s impossible to scale this kind of high-mix work. Automated quoting opens the floodgates for high-mix, low-volume work. But orders still need to be processed and often changed on the fly ("Hey, can you add this other part, and change a material for me?").

Material needs to be purchased, jobs scheduled, parts reviewed for manufacturability, machines programmed, scrapped parts remade. Every overhead associated with fulfilling orders remains, but now you are processing thousands of unique part numbers every week. Manufacturers accustomed to large jobs may find that their office overheads—usually fine for their high-value, recurring business—end up unscalable and unprofitable for small online jobs.

Meanwhile, rapid turnaround service creates interesting inventory challenges. If a customer places a rapid-turnaround order for a single 5-in. part and you only have full sheets on hand, you’ll create remnants, which you’ll have to track and reuse later. Laser loading automation won’t handle the wide variety of remnants created in this way. Meanwhile, data entry, labeling, and tracking sheet metal remnants is hard to do at scale without specialized software automation.

Collecting payments also becomes challenging. Instead of invoicing a handful of large, stable customers, you’ll now be collecting from hundreds or thousands of individuals and businesses. Cards decline; terms invoices go unpaid.

Because your online quoting portal is open to the internet, your addressable market will break normal regional boundaries. Shipping will become a major part of your operation (at OSH Cut, we ship 90% of what we produce; half of that is freight). Streamlining shipping operations will become important, but it also exposes another big risk that businesses might not ordinarily consider: sales tax liability.

Sales Tax Can Kill Your Business

Since you are shipping parts all over the United States, you’ll become responsible for collecting and paying sales tax across over 13,000 jurisdictions, each with its own tax rates and rules. In the landmark South Dakota v. Wayfair case in 2018, the U.S. Supreme Court ruled that states can require businesses to collect sales tax from out-of-state sellers. That includes manufacturers.

Once your business starts shipping across state lines at scale, you’ll be required to charge the right sales tax based on which of the 13,000 jurisdictions parts ship to; then track, file, and pay state sales taxes as often as monthly. I’ll say with candor that interstate sales tax is completely broken in the United States. It’s literally impossible to manage without custom software integration, especially for manufacturers.

Failing to properly collect and report state sales tax can have business-ending consequences. A few years ago at OSH Cut, we discovered late that we had crossed economic thresholds in many states without realizing it.

We corrected the issue and filed retroactive returns for all missed periods, and then we wrote software to automate collection and payment. It cost us $300,000 in uncollected back taxes, penalties, interest, and professional consultation. As bad as that was, it could have been far worse. This risk exists for any business that ships across state lines, but automated online quoting brings the problem front and center, because anyone, in any state, can visit your website and place an order. Sales tax compliance is a ticking time bomb for any online manufacturer.

Synergies at Scale

As complex as it can be, every problem is solvable. It mostly requires customized software automation to streamline bidding, design for manufacturability, planning, logistics, programming, production, shipping, billing, and tax compliance. Once that’s done, the benefits really start to emerge.

Aggregating lots of small orders allows high-volume economies of scale to be applied to all the small orders individually. That yields lower costs, which can produce a combination of better gross margins for the manufacturer and lower prices for customers.

Scaled capacity also benefits lead times. Demand volatility grows a lot slower than average demand. The bigger you get, the faster you can fulfill orders without having lots of excess capacity. In the limit (as the number of orders gets very large), every small to mid-sized order could be turned around in less than 24 hours at virtually no extra capacity cost.

The possible end state is compelling. As online, on-demand manufacturing becomes more commonplace (and as more volume becomes aggregated across fewer manufacturers), it’ll become increasingly cheaper, easier, and faster to get custom parts made here in the United States and shipped to your door. That’s an outcome I can get behind! But it takes a lot more than throwing an instant quoting engine on your website. When you do that, you’ll get what you wanted— lots of online orders. That’s when the real work begins.
