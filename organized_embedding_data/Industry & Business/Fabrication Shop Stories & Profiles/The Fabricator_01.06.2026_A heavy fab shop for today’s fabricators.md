E

ven before current U.S. military activity in Iran, the U.S. shipbuilding industry has been under heavy scrutiny by those in the federal government. Despite the investment of billions of dollars, the private sector has been unable to keep pace with the government’s desire to add more ships to the U.S. Navy fleet. The U.S. Government Accountability Office recently reported that some ships are as many as three years behind schedule.

Two of the main obstacles holding back manufacturers are "industrial decay"—what some in the defense industry call old facilities and antiquated methods—and simply finding enough people to fill manufacturing roles key to producing the ships. These challenges have existed for a while but only grow more frustrating for Pentagon officials with each passing year.

Intercon, Riverside, Mo., is a small part of the Navy supply base, but it’s an important one. The company produces key components for propulsion and power generation systems for destroyers, aircraft carriers, and submarines.

Intercon started in 1958 as a repair shop for heavy construction equipment. In the 1960s, it was doing repair and overhaul work on heavy dredging equipment for a related business, and that eventually led to designing and building marine winches.

In the 1980s, Intercon started producing articulated tug-barge couplers, devices that allow an ocean-going tugboat to connect to and push a barge. The couplers are critical to moving fuel supplies up and down the coastlines, from Shemya, Alaska, to Lima, Peru, on the Pacific Coast and all through the Gulf of Mexico, the Caribbean, and the entire Eastern Seaboard.

In the 1990s, Intercon began producing main reduction gear housings for Navy destroyers, specifically the Arleigh Burke class of guided-missile destroyers. Today, the defense work represents an ever-growing portion of the production being done at the company’s 135,000-sq.-ft facility.

Mike McCoy, Intercon’s president, has steered the company increasingly toward defense work to offset the ebb and flow associated with its markets for marine winches and tug-barge couplers. The defense work involves heavy fabrications, most weighing between 20,000 and 80,000 lbs.

CHANGING OF THE GUARD

As the product mix has changed for Intercon over McCoy’s last five years at the helm, so has the workforce. Like other metal fabricating businesses, the company had its share of 50- and even 60-year-old workers before the pandemic. But when they faced the potential dangers associated with the COVID-19 virus, many of the older workers elected to retire. This left Intercon with some personnel gaps, and finding talent to replace those experienced workers was a challenge.

"Being in heavy fab, the reality is that trade schools have never really served this space. They just don’t teach people to weld 8-in. bevels," McCoy said. "So it’s never been the case for us that we were able to go out and necessarily pluck people from a trade school and they were going to come in on day one with the skill set that we needed.

"It used to be the case that it was easier to find people either with experience or a trade school education, where you could then train them to do what you needed them to do," he added. "But the pipeline is much smaller than it used to be."

In the years after the pandemic, Intercon has turned to technology to find ways to improve its productivity and quality, both of which suffered when years of institutional knowledge walked out the door with the retirees. Key investments have proven helpful in getting the company where it needs to be to further grow its defense business. (Today, the company employs 125 people with an average age of 41.)

The capital equipment investments also have given McCoy a chance to reimagine how a heavy fab shop should operate. From a macro point of view, suppliers need to rethink how they approach fabricating and machining if they are to catch up on delivery of ships to the Navy, so new technologies that boost throughput are helpful. From a more localized perspective, that new technology helps to modernize a shop floor that then can act as a recruiting tool for people curious about a career making things—in this case, really big metal components.

"When I joined the company 15 years ago, I looked around the shop, and it looked dated in the way that every heavy fabricator I’ve ever visited looked," McCoy said. "My commitment to myself and to my team is that when I retire in 15 to 20 years, I want the shop to look modern. If I retire in 2040, I want the shop to look like it belongs in 2040."

CLEANING UP WELDING AREAS

In the past, Intercon personnel liked to describe the company as a job shop. As McCoy learned the business, he struggled with that interpretation.

Intercon certainly was a high-mix manufacturer, and the volumes were limited. But many of the jobs were repeat orders, and even with a volume of two or four per year, the workpiece sizes made them considerable fabrication projects.

McCoy wanted that reality to guide the company’s capital equipment investments. What could be flexible enough to help deliver productivity gains and improve quality and safety with these different projects, while simultaneously being robust enough to stand up to the rigors associated with heavy-duty fabricating?

In early 2025, Intercon purchased two WeldPro 360 weld booms from Andersen Industries for one of its welding areas. These booms take the welding power sources off carts and put them at the end of a double-articulated arm. The 10-ft.-long primary arm can rotate 192 degrees around the main pivot point, providing coverage within a 56-ft.-dia. semicircular welding zone. The secondary arm, 8 ft. long, is able to rotate 360 degrees about the end of the primary arm, which helps it reach dead zones that single-articulated, straight-arm booms struggle with.

One of the things about the weld booms that really appeals to Intercon personnel, according to McCoy, is the ability to route gas hoses, air hoses, and electrical cables inside the arms, which has eliminated very obvious tripping hazards.

"When I lead a tour now, it’s a lot easier to get around because those booms keep things off the ground," McCoy said. "They generally just make it a more pleasant place to work, which again is helpful when it comes to recruiting and retaining talent."

One of the biggest changes with the booms is the ability to use a 500-lb. drum of welding wire, which sits at the base of their support beam, instead of a 33-lb. spool of wire. Welders no longer having to change out those spools, saving stress on their backs and letting them spend more time welding. When a drum needs to be changed out, the welder calls over a forklift for the job.

McCoy said the welders have found the booms easy to work with. The weld process controls can be found at the end of each boom arm, eliminating the need to walk back to a cart where the power source might otherwise be located. The booms are designed with bearings in the pivot joints, allowing the arms to be moved easily, and once stopped, the arms hold firmly in position.

McCoy added that the welders like the booms so much that the company purchased four more only three months after the first two booms were installed. It’s thinking about adding another six.

In the meantime, Intercon continues to take steps to use the booms to deliver more efficiencies. Because it is making concerted efforts to use more solid wire instead of flux-cored wire, the company has equipped two of the booms with dual wire feeders. (While Intercon would like to use more solid wire in its defense-related jobs, the Navy takes two to three years to approve new welding procedure specifications.) Early results indicate that welders are able to spend more time welding instead of moving welding carts, switching out wire spools, and working around messes of hoses and cords. Because of this, the management team plans to upgrade the other four booms with dual wire feeders soon.

"What we’re doing is looking at all the time that we are taking highly skilled welders and asking them to do something other than welding, whether that’s moving a welding machine or changing out a wire spool," McCoy said. "We want to minimize that nonproductive time and remove menial activities from our employees’ plates."

A CERTAIN TYPE OF WELDING AUTOMATION

Typical industrial welding robots don’t make a lot of sense for Intercon because of the large size and limited volume of fabrications they produce. But that doesn’t eliminate all welding automation from consideration.

Intercon purchased a welding cobot from Vectis Automation last year to handle the welding on two large shipping fixtures. The fixtures had a significant volume of long, multipass welds that weren’t all straight. The cobot could be trained relatively quickly to knock out those welds.

"It was a fantastic experience for us," McCoy said

He added the cobot justified its purchase on that first job. It’s literally been rolled out to tackle similar jobs in the subsequent months.

"I come back to saying that this is for boring welding. A good welder is not interested in an 8-ft. weld where they just go pass after pass after pass," McCoy said. "They want to do the more interesting work."

Buoyed by the success of the first cobot, Intercon purchased another one in early 2026 to weld parts associated with the tug-barge couplers. The job involved a lot of confined-space welding, so the company had been farming out the parts for nearly 40 years out of concern for welder safety.

But outsourcing really didn’t remove the risk from the activity; it simply pushed the risk to someone else’s facility.

"After we had this experience with this first cobot that we bought, we started looking at it and saying, ‘Well, what if we could train the cobot to weld in a confined space?’" McCoy recalled. "The welder could train the cobot, then back out of the fabrication, hit the go button, and let the cobot do the confined-space welding while the welder is on the outside looking in."

The cobot enabled Intercon to bring the work back in-house, eliminate the risk, and maintain quality results.

"We put a GoPro on the end of the cobot, and I watched it weld in this confined space without any operator in that confined space. It was fantastic."

A MODULAR APPROACH

When you walk through the Intercon shop, you’ll notice Bluco modular fitup tables in several of the work areas. They go hand in hand with access to tablet computers for the welders.

With their grids of predrilled holes on the surface, the tables make it possible to quickly create fixtures for fitting and welding. Using the tablet for guidance, a welder can use standardized clamps, pins, and accessories that fit directly into the tabletop to create custom fixtures. There’s no need to go searching for a fabricated fixture that might be residing on some hard-to-access shelf elsewhere in the facility.

The tablet also provides directions for building the product. The welder is not left trying to make out welding symbols on a difficult-to-read printout.

"That’s really compelling when you bring someone in," McCoy said. "If they’re younger and they’re coming out of a trade school, it shows them that we’re not just thinking about today, but we’re thinking about what we need to look like 10 years from now."

It’s easier to turn a small boat around rather than a large ship, but both start with a commitment to go in another direction. Intercon is finding success as it continues on its journey. Perhaps that bodes well for its supply chain partners in the shipbuilding industry.
