# The tariffs are here. Now what?

[TARİH: 01.04.2018 The Fabricator]

Biz Talk

Smart purchasing can help a fabricator cope, even thrive

By Tim Heston

Read more from Tim Heston at

www.thefabricator.com/author/tim-heston

T

he FABRICATOR

’s Leadership Summit, part of the Fabricators & Manufacturers Association (FMA) Annual Meeting held March 7-9 in Phoenix, Ariz., was timed well. It’s not often you get to sit in an audience of metal fabricators, metal distributors, and steel company executives and talk about trade policy that changes day by day, hour by hour. Speaker John Packard of "Steel Market Update" kept his phone handy on the off chance that news might change during his presentation.

Views varied depending on political bent, personal perspective, and place of employment. Regardless, no one can argue one simple fact: Barring exclusions for our North American neighbors (at least at this writing), tariffs on imported steel and aluminum

are happening

, and all U.S. metal fabricators must deal with that reality.

Alan Bear’s clients are dealing with the reality rather well. Bear is president and CEO of Minnesota-based Bear Market Intelligence, which advises manufacturers on materials purchasing strategies.

Last year Bear advised his clients to buy, buy, buy, and today his clients are reaping the benefits. In 2017 all economic indicators pointed to increased demand. Mix that with the Commerce Department’s protectionist thinking, and you have a recipe for skyrocketing materials prices. Bear didn’t know for sure whether tariffs were coming, but he wasn’t surprised when they did.

"Prices are going to stay elevated for quite some time," he said. "We’re going to see metal prices higher for the balance of 2018."

Many at the summit worried about inventory levels and material availability. "Some have pointed out that steel inventories are high," Bear said, "and when you look at pipe and tube, it’s true, those inventories are high. But when you look at sheet and plate, the inventories aren’t as high. And there’s a very long mill lead time right now."

The situation with aluminum is particularly complex. "With the tariff on aluminum, you’ve got to remember that there isn’t much domestic capacity remaining," Bear said. "Smelters have been announcing restarts. There’s a lot of action on the nonferrous side, and pricing power is going to be sustained for some time, because you won’t have enough supply to bring the price down until the new domestic production is brought online."

How should a custom fabricator manage going forward? First, Bear suggested that, if a shop can afford to do so, now might be a good time to bite the bullet and increase raw stock inventory, particularly if the potential for a material shortage is looming. Purchasers should talk to service center representatives and pay attention to mill production rates and lead times. If lead times are getting longer faster than production rates are rising, shortages of some type may be coming. If a shop has limited room, it can work with suppliers on warehousing agreements, where a fab shop owns the material sitting on the supplier’s floor.

Shops may not get the best deals at this point, but paying the current rate on material is better than having no material at all. "I tend to shy away from just-in-time inventory during times like these," Bear said. "The fabricator who has material will ship the product."

And now is an especially bad time to be stuck without material. Companies that buy fabricated products are hedging, too, pulling the trigger on orders before prices ascend farther.

Second, strategic spot buying—catching a good deal when it’s available—can be a fabricator’s friend. As Bear explained, this allows a fabricator to move carefully or even avoid passing price increases on to core customers. "The best thing [for fabricators] to do is to honor the metal supply contract, and spot-buy [material] alongside of it when they find material at a better price, to essentially leverage a cost-averaging activity for themselves." Some material prices may be low, some high, but on average, they’re lower than the competition’s, which of course gives a fabricator a big advantage.

He added that all this purchasing means a fabricator must outlay more cash and extend the time raw stock sits in inventory, which often helps a cost-of-cash analysis. Moreover, strategic spot buying on good deals "will take away some of the burn when you’re not getting as good of a price as you want."

Giving a key account a break on material now, particularly considering the pricing turmoil, could make a strong customer relationship even stronger. "If you ding your customers as soon as pricing changes, there’s no loyalty to either side, and a relationship without loyalty is never advantageous," Bear said. "Better relationships mean you get better information, and better response when you ask for a favor."

In an odd way, the metal pricing volatility gives fabricators another way to gain a competitive advantage: strategic purchasing. Smart purchasing allows shops to ofter a lower price, and a lower price is value any customer immediately appreciates. With material available and manufacturing processes refined and efficient, the fabricator also can deliver quality product quickly.

"Price and product lead time for a small fabricator are essential," Bear said. "If competitors need to wait two or three weeks to get a piece of material, and your shop has it, you’re going to get the job. That boils it down to an equation that’s usually too simple. But when we get into this type of [materials] market, some of the solutions are in fact really that simple."
