FABTECH Mexico 2016 Brought Best of Manufacturing to Mexico City

Highest number of attendees to date explored new technologies, educational sessions, and met with industry experts

FABTECH

®

Mexico, the leading manufacturing event in Latin America for the metal forming, fabricating, welding, and finishing industries, brought the latest innovations in manufacturing to Mexico City’s Centro Banamex on May 4-6, 2016, and broke attendance records by welcoming over 12,500 attendees and 550 exhibitors during the three-day show.

Spanning 146,000 net square feet of exhibit space, the eighth edition of FABTECH Mexico provided attendees with the opportunity to see live demonstrations, explore the latest manufacturing and welding technologies, meet with industry experts, and attend educational sessions. Educational sessions covered technical topics such as automation, cutting, forming and fabricating, lasers, lean management, software, stamping, and welding.

"The growth of FABTECH Mexico over the past few years has been impressive," said Mark Hoper, FABTECH show co-manager. "Giving businesses and customers the opportunity to come together at one place to learn, network, explore new products and technology, and make lasting connections is what has made this show the largest of its kind in Latin America."

The annual show comprises four co-located shows: FABTECH Mexico encompasses metal forming and fabricating as well as tube and pipe technology; AWS Weldmex features welding technology; METALFORM Mexico highlights metal forming and tool and die technology; and COATech focuses on finishing technology.

The show rotates each year between Monterrey and Mexico City. The next event will be held May 2-4, 2017, in Monterrey. To learn more about FABTECH Mexico, visit

fabtechmexico.com

.

About FABTECH Mexico

FABTECH Mexico along with the established AWS Weldmex, METALFORM Mexico, and COATech is Latin America’s largest collaboration of technology, equipment, and knowledge in the metal forming, fabricating, welding, and finishing industries. The event provides a powerfully aligned hands-on, face-to-face business growth experience. It is made possible by its five co-sponsors, all of which represent the varied and diverse makeup of the manufacturing industry: SME, Fabricators & Manufacturers Association Intl. (FMA), American Welding Society (AWS), Precision Metalforming Association (PMA), and Chemical Coaters Association Intl. (CCAI).

Nothing Gets the Juices Flowing Like Steel

No matter what the commodity or product is, there are always those that consume it and those that produce it, and these two sides are rarely in sync. Take the oil sector right now. The constant refrain is that low per-barrel prices for oil have been hurting the U.S. economy now that we are one of the world’s major producers. That is true for those in the business of producing that oil, but for those that consume the oil and the products made from it, this drop in price has been a windfall. Drivers who were spending over $4 a gallon were suddenly paying less than $1.50. Farmers faced less expense in the planting season, airlines saved money and bought new planes. Whether the price decline was a good or bad thing was a matter of perspective.

The same issue exists for steel. The U.S. steelmaking sector is competing with countries that have far lower production costs and engage in all manner of trade subterfuge to gain an even greater advantage. This has prompted the U.S. to go after the countries that are selling steel to us at less than it presumably costs to make it. China is at the top of that list, but it is far from the only one. This kind of action makes everyone feel confident that the U.S. is looking out for its steelmakers. But what of the steel users?

This is the other side of the issue. As one of our readers points out, the price of steel in the U.S. is on the rise — hot-rolled is up by 49 percent and cold-rolled is up by 46 percent. The consumers of steel are paying more and more, and that places these manufacturers at a disadvantage against companies that can access cheaper steel. U.S. steelmakers are currently producing some 1.7 million tons per week at a 71 percent capacity level, and U.S. manufacturers are consuming 132 million tons annually. This leaves a substantial gap between domestic capacity and consumption, and that gap means that manufacturers are at a severe disadvantage against foreign competition.

The goal is some kind of balance that allows the domestic steel producers to stay in business and even expand capacity but at the same time allowing manufacturers to get access to the steel they need at the prices that allow them to stay in business and compete. A blanket ban on steel from other countries may not be the best way to accomplish that goal.
