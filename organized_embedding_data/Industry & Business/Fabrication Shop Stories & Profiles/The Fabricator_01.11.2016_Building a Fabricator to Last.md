# Building a Fabricator to Last

[TARİH: 01.11.2016 The Fabricator]

How to create long-term strategic value in metal fabrication

By Tim Heston

J

oel Strom recalled visiting one particular custom fabricator that seemed to have stellar profitability. A lot of things seemed to be going just swimmingly, at least on paper. Operating profit, EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation, and amortization), and net income all looked extremely healthy. But then he visited the shop floor.

Shop leaders and supervisors seemed to be scurrying around, barking orders, asking where this job was, or why that job was late. He saw work-in-process everywhere. And he saw old, poorly maintained equipment.

"They told me that they basically muscled jobs through the shop."

Strom, managing partner at Scottsdale, Ariz.- based CKS Advisors, had seen variants of this situation before. He once owned and ran a job shop himself, and he concedes that he’s made mistakes like everyone else, so he can empathize.

Entrepreneurs, in metal fabrication and in most other businesses, usually launch their companies by hitting the ground running, landing orders, and, in effect, "muscling" jobs through. The business founders then land more orders, and annual revenue keeps climbing year after year.

Eventually the shop grows so large that salespeople no longer have the company infrastructure—the operational processes and procedures, management, customer service, and associated documentation—to support them. Soon people burn out, and quality and on-time delivery suffer. Expediting becomes the norm. As Strom put it, "Eventually sales decline to where the level of support really is."

This is why when people value a business, they don’t just look at revenue and earnings. The books show current financial performance, but they aren’t the best predictor of how a company will perform in the future.

In truth, a business is valued on the basis of many factors, and Strom divides them into four areas: the market, financials, operations, and strategy.

The Market

Getting paid for a big order may help a business make payroll, but that order alone doesn’t set the company on a successful trajectory. That, Strom said, comes from a company’s reputation—or, more precisely, how the market perceives the company.

Strom added that many companies make the mistake of staying under the radar and keeping a low profile. Managers don’t participate in local or national industry groups; they don’t network; they don’t market or advertise in any way.

"I often ask shop owners, ‘How well are you known in the industry?’ And many tell me, ‘We like to keep a low profile because we don’t want the competition coming after us.’ That’s the wrong answer when it comes to creating strategic value. If you are the outstanding company in your region and in your industry, people will buy from you over somebody else. That makes your company’s value higher. But if you run the best company that nobody knows about, your company value will suffer."

Business cycles rise and fall. And on occasion, for economic or technological reasons, a business sector changes for the worse permanently (think electronics manufacturing in the U.S.). But more often than not, when a sector declines, those with poor reputations fall by the wayside first, sometimes very quickly if it’s a crowded market. The players with good reputations pick up market share and emerge from the downturn stronger than ever.

So what builds that reputation? That’s where the other three factors come into play.

The Operation

This entails the structural part of the business, and scrutinizing that structure often starts by asking questions.

Are there documented work procedures and quality systems?

Are there procedures for periodic inventory cycle counts?

Are bills of materials (BOMs) accurate?

Do engineers, estimators, purchasing personnel, and others have defined methods to work effectively together to process and schedule an order?

Does a shop have a reliable scheduling method, especially to handle the highly variable custom fabrication environment?

Are documented training procedures in place, or do some areas rely on tribal knowledge to get the job done? Are tools and machines organized and labeled for optimal part flow and quick changeovers?

Is the shop safe?

Is a management structure in place with decision-making authority, or does only a handful of top managers (or just the owner) call the shots?

Are people given the tools they need to succeed?

All this really boils down to one fundamental question: Have the owners invested sufficiently for long-term success? As Strom explained, all these factors may not have an immediate effect on the numbers for next month, but they make the business sustainable and, perhaps most important, scalable. The structure is there to support the fabricator’s growing sales. If the company founder still muscles jobs through the shop, it becomes difficult for a company to sustain long-term growth.

The Strategy

A company’s strategy is essentially its focus, Strom said. No matter how good its financials, its reputation, or its operational structure, a company without focus may experience difficult times ahead. No fabricator can handle every type of job, and it can be easy to forget this in the face of short-term financial gain.

As a former job shop owner, Strom knows this all too well. His company specialized in low-volume, high-mix, high-precision work. Rarely did an order exceed 1,000 pieces a month. Then one day, Strom uncovered a huge opportunity: A company submitted an order for 5,000 pieces a month. The customer was large, financially sound, and paid on time. It seemed like the perfect opportunity to increase revenue and take the business to the next level.

"After six months of suffering, missing due dates, buying a new machine—which we never did get to work just right—and losing our shirts, we handed the tooling back to the customer," Strom recalled. "It was all a big mistake."

Why? It’s because Strom’s company had an operational structure for specific kinds of low-volume work. In that sandbox, his job shop had an outstanding reputation, and the financials were sustainable. Jumping outside the sandbox—without first scrutinizing the company’s structure and reputation—cost in the long run.

A company’s strategy should define what it does best. It could be a proprietary process or technology. It could be a type of sheet metal part or a range of complicated electromechanical assemblies. It could be a focus on extremely specialized processes, like laser or electron beam welding.

A business that makes a lot of money today might not mean it will make a lot of money tomorrow. And when valuing one’s business, "tomorrow" should take center stage.

"If a very small business has a well-established, really strong niche, that focus can increase the business value tremendously," Strom said. "That’s because they know who they are, they know what they do, and they know where they’re going."

Strom added that one niche may not be inherently better or worse than another. It’s the level of focus on that niche that really counts. For instance, Strom works with two custom stampers that at first glance have very similar operations. But visit their shop floors and you’ll see how starkly different they are.

One focuses on precision, low-volume work; the other focuses on high-volume, low-precision jobs. Just because the jobs are "low precision" doesn’t make it a bad business. What matters is how the company’s reputation, structure, and financials support that high-volume, low-precision strategy.

Customer and industry concentration plays a role here too. It’s easy for custom fabricators to grow with excessively high revenue concentration, with most sales coming from just a few customers and just a few industries. A fabricator may dominate a niche, but if that niche has only a handful of players, the business strategy, although very focused, may not add much to the company’s value.

At the same time, it’s not just the number of customers that counts. Strom recalled one shop that had dozens of major customers offering different products and services. "But after researching it a bit, we found that most of these customers were either owned by or were supplying the same large, multinational company. Realistically, 85 percent of that shop’s business was related to that one large company. That wasn’t the greatest situation."

Strom added that "industry concentration is not as bad as customer concentration." For instance, a shop serving just two industries, like material handling or packaging, which happen to be very broad, may not adversely affect company value as much as a shop that’s serving just two large customers. "And, of course, the quality of the customer is definitely a component," Strom said.

The Financials

Strom recalled working with a fabricator and stamper with more than $32 million in annual revenue, and yet the company had no formal financial statements besides its tax returns.

"They in fact were showing a nice, big bottom line," Strom said. "But the value of the business was reduced because of the way the business was run. A buyer would look at it and say, ‘I’m going to need to cut that bottom line in half to turn this into a sustainable business’" with adequate financial controls and an operational structure.

How does a fabricator actually grow to this size without a working balance sheet? As Strom explained, much of it has to do with the "cash in/cash out" focus of a lot of startups. That is, the cash for an order comes in, and the cash goes out to pay for people and machines. The founder doesn’t put together a formal balance sheet simply because he has no need for a third-party financial audit, either for banks or investors outside friends and family. This, of course, isn’t sustainable.

When a shop does have a formal balance sheet, Strom said that he never looks at the numbers without considering the other three pieces of the valuation puzzle. A company’s balance sheet may have outstanding net income, and yet the shop floor may be full of old equipment, and the people may be overworked and underpaid. Alternatively, the balance sheet may look great, but the company may have no operational benchmarks in place that connect actions on the shop floor with financial performance.

A Starting Point

All this just provides a foundation for building strategic value, Strom said. Other factors adding or detracting from that value really hinge on the company and the circumstance. For instance, those who acquire companies—be they strategic buyers, financial buyers, managers, friends or family, or any combination—will ultimately set the value of the business when they agree on a purchase price. And that price can vary depending on the timing and situation.

Still, Strom added that "cleaning up the house to sell it" is really just short-term thinking, and it isn’t a good reason to focus on strategic value. In the long term, managing by valuation improves operating results and EBITDA.

"Most important, it gives the owner options," he said, adding that with a strong business, owners can smoothly pass the business down to family members, managers, or, if need be, outside parties.

Regardless, the foundation of strategic value, Strom said, really boils down to sustainability. A business that makes a lot of money today might not mean it will make a lot of money tomorrow. And when valuing one’s business, "tomorrow" should take center stage.
