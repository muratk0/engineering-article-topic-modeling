From The Editor-In-Chief

A fabricating company that fails to organize the chaos isn’t serious about attracting and retaining new hires

M

y nephew was a perfect candidate for a career in manufacturing. In fact, he worked for a company that had fabricating operations, but that didn’t last too long.

Graduating from high school was a big deal because being in high school wasn’t a big deal for him. It was someplace he had to go by law, and his parents weren’t going to let him sleep his days away and play video games at night. Sports had been an enjoyable outlet for him, but as he watched his classmates grow faster, stronger, and taller, participation in a varsity sport became a little problematic. By the time he was a senior, he just wanted out, and in May 2018 he got his wish.

Reality was about to set in, but he was ready. He didn’t mind working hard and earning money. For someone still living at home, an entry-level wage can make you fill like a Rockefeller.

A family friend got him an entry-level gig at a nearby manufacturing company. He did a lot of sweeping and cleaning up, but he didn’t mind. He showed up on time and did what he was told. However, he couldn’t help but notice what was going on around him.

Being a manufacturer that had strict deadlines for product delivery, this facility environment was stressful. My nephew noticed it as he was asked to hustle with final delivery preparations and witnessed numerous "discussions," where voices were raised and accusations flew.

He told me of a day when a leader on the manufacturing floor just walked out of the facility after an especially loud vocal altercation. That manager did not return the next day.

When I asked my nephew what he thought after witnessing such an event, he replied, "It was pretty funny." Gallows humor is a family trait, so the response wasn’t that surprising. His resulting job change wasn’t either.

A father of one of his peers got him a job as an entry-level laborer for a fabricator of heavy-duty parts and structures with the goal of working his way up to a crane operator. Of course, he spent the first two weeks on the job doing landscaping and basically acting as a gopher for the people working in the maintenance yard, but he still showed up early every morning and put in his hours until it was quitting time. For the time being, he’s on a career path.

I share this story because as metal fabricators work to persuade a new generation that manufacturing is a worthy career pursuit, others are doing damage to the cause. Think about what my nephew and his parents have to say about his first employer after high school. If they are generous, they might call the environment "unorganized" or "freewheeling." But they aren’t writing advertising copy, so their statements are going to be a lot more negative. Also, they aren’t likely to recommend that manufacturer as a place of employment to friends or family.

That type of word-of-mouth can be devastating for a company in a community, particularly one where job-seekers have multiple opportunities. With the U.S. unemployment rate at 3.7 percent as of July 2019, most manufacturers are not in a position of power when it comes to trying to find skilled people willing to work for the wages being offered.

I think the story has a silver lining for metal fabricators, however. While my nephew is not working for the chaotic fabricator, he did land in a comfortable place where the adult who got him the job acts as a mentor. He also has an idea of the job that awaits him if he puts in the work and performs well.

That scenario is easily replicated in metal fabricating companies, even in ones that don’t have a formal human resources department. Somebody just needs to be involved with bringing along the rookies on the shop floor and have frank discussions with them about potential growth opportunities.

In the 2019 edition of the "What Keeps You up at Night?" survey,

The FABRICATOR

asked its readers if they would recommend metal fabricating as a career choice to someone in their own family. Out of the almost 100 responses, 77 percent said "yes." Metal fabricators for the most part love what they do, so much so that they wouldn’t think twice about having their family be a part of it. That enthusiasm should be shared with those outside of the shop walls.

Additionally, the effort to boost the image of manufacturing shouldn’t be sabotaged by those companies that are overwhelmed by chaos and fail to organize their processes so that they can improve customer service and create a more comfortable work environment for employees. They are doing themselves and their manufacturing peers no favors.
