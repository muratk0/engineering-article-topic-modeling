# Morphing to meet demand

[TARİH: 01.06.2025 The Fabricator]

Biz Talk

Fabricators pivot like few other businesses can

TIM HESTON

A

s I report on the FAB 40 each year—our list of some of the largest and most successful custom and contract metal fabricators in the United States—I’m reminded just how various FAB 40 companies are. You can’t pigeonhole them. They morph and move to meet new customer demands. They shape themselves to meet the market, which is perhaps why the custom and contract metal fabrication business model is so resilient.

Consider two very different FAB 40 companies, R-V Industries and Rock Run Industries. The former serves diverse markets, the latter is extremely focused—and both are successful.

"We want to be the mutual fund of fabrication. We want to be diversified. So, when outside factors hit, like the current tariff situation, we’re not concentrated in any one industry or geography."

So said Kirk Putt, president and CEO at R-V Industries in Honey Brook, Pa. The company name comes from the founders, Ronald Putt and Victor Dodd, not the recreational vehicle market. The company doesn’t service the RV market at all, in fact—but it serves plenty of other sectors. "When one vertical goes down, another goes up," Putt said. "Sometimes it’s by necessity, sometimes it’s planned, but the model has really helped us grow and maintain stability over the years."

Driving growth now, Putt said, is new demand in power generation as well as work associated with new chip manufacturing plants. Power plants slated for closure are now staying open, and some are expanding. The new digital age, it turns out, is extraordinarily power hungry.

R-V will likely never delve into job mixes that require most shop employees to perform repetitive work. In fact, when it does find employees with experience, they’re usually looking for less monotony and greater challenges—something R-V can provide. The complex work has attracted many students from local trade schools, as well as a fair number of students studying welding engineering.

"We focus on future opportunities here," Putt said. "If the company isn’t growing, the opportunities—for you to earn, grow, and attain the skills you want to move forward in your career—don’t happen."

Since 2017, the company has had a long list of acquisitions that expand the kinds of machinery and specialty industrial equipment R-V fabricates for various industries, from pharmaceutical manufacturing to paper mills.

Rock Run Industries has taken a different approach—with a laser focus (metaphorically and literally) on the recreational vehicle industry. The company launched in 2007 out of a garage, and over the past 20 years it has grown into a highly automated operation. Today, the company has four material handling towers and seven lasers feeding a host of downstream processes.

"We started as a hobby shop. But piece by piece, building after building, we’ve grown until where we are now, where we have 150 employees and between $45 million and $50 million in sales every year."

That was Brandon Esh, marketing manager at the Millersburg, Ind., operation. He added that the shop has diversified to some degree, serving new opportunities that come in the door, but the organization hasn’t actively sought to diversify its revenue.

The reasons boil down to location and deep specialization. Elkhart County is home to more than 80% of the world’s total production of RVs. "In the 18 years we’ve been in business, we’ve only had two years where we haven’t increased sales by about 20% to 30%."

Recent investments also include a tube laser. As Esh explained, Rock Run purchased the system to help address some significant opportunities that arose in the structural space. "We can cut up to 12- by 12-in. shapes and 16-in. round," Esh said. "We’ve cut some massive extrusions and structural shapes. That work dried up, though. Still, we pivoted. We used that tube laser to expand our capabilities to serve our parts-making capabilities for the RV industry."

The fabricator won some work cutting tubular parts, but it also redesigned some parts slightly to increase throughout and simplify part flow. For example, some sheet metal parts that used to be laser cut and then formed on the brake are now done-in-one on the tube laser.

Few think of buying an RV if the economy is down or interest rates are up. The industry is notoriously cyclical. How then has Rock Run grown so steadily? According to Esh, the shop has been slow just six months over its 18-year history. The fabricator had to cut hours, but that’s about it. It didn’t dare lay off anyone.

"The RV industry once again is pretty slow overall," Esh said, "but we’re still extremely busy. So now, we really feel obligated to take care of our core customers. The overhead that goes into diversifying while we’re still busy is hard to justify. If our history teaches us anything, even if the RV industry slows down dramatically, we’re still their priority fabricator."

Esh conceded that the company may well need to diversify down the road, and company leaders are strategizing ways to redeploy resources to serve another industry. As its tube laser cutting experience shows, however, being able to pivot quickly has tremendous value.

Rock Run’s story proves that there’s no one way to grow a metal fabrication enterprise. Diversification can work, but so can specialization. One common thread remains: the ability to pivot quickly to uncover new opportunities. Doing so in ways competitors can’t, a metal fabricator can build a business that lasts generations.
