# Make way for the kids

[TARİH: 01.09.2024 The Fabricator]

Editor’s Corner

A new generation entering metal fabricating means a new way of thinking

Dan Davis

I

n many ways, Blake Hamar is a lot like other metal fabricators.

He’s pretty conservative in running his Fort Worth, Texas-based businesses, Cali Raised LED, a manufacturer of aftermarket truck accessories, such as bumpers, auxiliary lighting, catalytic converter shields, molle panels, and skid plates, and Apex Fabworks, a job shop. He doesn’t believe in overextending himself when it comes to buying new equipment. ("If you can’t afford it, don’t buy it," he said.) Also, he is more likely to add an additional shift and a few good hires to keep up with fabricating activity before simply buying a new piece of equipment.

But in one way, he’s nothing like a majority of today’s metal fabricating business owners. He’s only 26. (You can read more about his story in "Shining the light on unfulfilled needs" on page 136.)

"The thing that I’ve noticed that’s interesting is every one of these manufacturing companies that I’ve met [since moving to Texas] is one of two things. It’s either a guy that started a really long time ago, and the owner is in his 60s. Or it’s a guy who’s running it that’s in his 40s or 50s, and it was passed down to him from like his dad or his grandpa," Hamar said.

"There aren’t a lot of people getting into it that I’ve seen like at a younger age, which is crazy, because it can be such a good business. There will always be a need for people to make stuff," he added.

What Hamar lacks in wisdom that comes with age he makes up for in what he called "life experiences." In high school, he was an early pioneer of something he described as "retail arbitrage," buying goods at retail outlets known for carrying close-out and marked-down goods and then selling them online for more than what he paid. When he took that same digital marketing and online sales success and applied it to LED light products for trucks and then fabricated bracket systems for those lights, his business career took off. Now he’s running multiple shifts in his fab operations, which employ 40 people.

Hamar also isn’t a gear head. He didn’t spend countless hours working on engines and tricking out his truck for off-road adventures. He’s more of an entrepreneur. He sees a need and works hard to try and fulfill it.

"It’s funny because I don’t like off-roading. I have no desire to take my truck and get it dirty," he said with a laugh. "That’s also another reason why we’re successful, because this isn’t my passion and that burns a lot of guys out."

What else can you learn from this whippersnapper?

• He cross-trains his entire workforce. Hamar said that he likes to hire welders and then introduce them to other processes in the ensuing months. A welder will go from working with welding robots to operating a press brake to running the laser cutting machine. He said such an approach builds loyalty and improves an employee’s work ethic because each day brings new challenges and experiences, eliminating the boredom that comes with doing the same thing over and over.

"There aren’t a lot of people getting into [metal fabricating] that I’ve seen like at a younger age, which is crazy, because it can be such a good business. There will always be a need for people to make stuff."

— Blake Hamar, Cali Raised LED/Apex Fabworks

• Hamar hires young people. He figures the average age on the shop floor is about 25. The manufacturing newbies have no issues listening to him, even if he is only 26, and they are open to guidance. Hamar can train them in the ways that Cali Raised LED and Apex Fabworks operate. Additionally, Hamar offers lifestyle advice, such as Friday afternoon financial discussions on topics like buying a car and establishing credit.

• Hamar isn’t afraid to let his employees seek out new opportunities. "I’m like, if you get an opportunity to go run another shop, go do it," he said, adding that he has three former employees running fab shops in the Dallas-Fort Worth area.

Before you assume that Hamar thinks he has it all figured out, he doesn’t. What he does know is that he’s constantly having to figure it out. He’s open to the realization that there’s always more to learn. For example, he said he’d put the tube assemblies fabricated by his team up against anyone’s, but he admitted that he likely scrapped thousands of dollars’ worth of tubes in learning how to produce those assemblies.

Hamar and his peers are slowly beginning to make their presence known in the metal fabricating industry. They do have their opinions, but they also are open-minded when presented with good points. They understand what good quality means and what can happen when shortcuts are taken. They are a product of an instant-gratification world, but that means they like to eliminate waste.

The upcoming generation is very different from the ones that preceded it, but that’s not necessarily bad. The kids are alright. The metal fabricating industry is going to be in good hands.
