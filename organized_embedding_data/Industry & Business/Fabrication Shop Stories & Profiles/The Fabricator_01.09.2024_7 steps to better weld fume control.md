# 7 steps to better weld fume control

W

hen Tracy Branch began working in safety at metal fabrication facilities, she started to spot the small fab shops along the highways and byways around Houston. On occasion she witnessed an all-too-familiar sight: blue haze.

"I sometimes felt like I should stop and share something with those folks!"

As senior EHS consultant at Texas Steel Fabrication Safety Consulting, Branch focuses on welding safety, and welding fumes in particular. That blue haze characterizes many small fab shops and even a few large ones. It’s usually avoidable, at least with the right fume mitigation investment. More than that, the haze exudes that dark and dirty atmosphere that those in manufacturing recruitment try to avoid. "This isn’t your father’s manufacturing industry," they proclaim. Touring a shop, prospective employees look up and see a thick cloud of blue haze hanging in the air.

Really?

Over her 33 years as an environmental health and safety (EHS) officer in manufacturing and metal fabrication, Branch has several key insights as to why, even today, so many fab shops are far too full of fume.

Fume mitigation techniques abound, of course, and if you’re headed to FABTECH this year, you’ll see plenty of them: traditional local fume collection, either overhead or at the source, near the arc; systems in which fume collection is built into the welding gun itself; as well as sophisticated plantwide fume mitigation that can make the entire facility a more pleasant place to work.

As an EHS consultant, Branch makes recommendations, fully aware that not every shop has the same budget. That said, the desire is there to make the weld shop a better, safer place to work—an aim that, as Branch explained, can be fulfilled by the seven steps that follow.

1. Educate, Achieve Buy-In, and Monitor

"Employees and even employers are not aware of what the real hazards are," Branch said. "They might be aware of metal fume fever from zinc, and perhaps a few other basic things. And they know about the dangers of hexavalent chromium in stainless steel. But mostly, they don’t realize that even the water fountain in that shop might have chromium in it. Or it might have manganese. They’re just not aware of the hazard."

The first step in any weld fume program is to describe the actual hazards. Every welding consumable has a spec sheet. When welders apply heat, they release chemicals residing in the consumables and base metal into the atmosphere. There, they can be inhaled and, in some cases and at certain levels, cause serious harm.

"There’s a limit as to how much you can breathe in before it becomes harmful to your body," she said. "For each one of these chemicals, they have limits."

After education, the monitoring starts, proving who is over a designated permissible exposure limit (PEL). She shows the weld team a standard industrial hygiene monitoring device—basically a pump and filter media—attached on the welder’s lapel near the breathing zone to, in essence, "breath in" the same air as the welder.

"We get eight-hour, time-weighted data from this," Branch said. "We send this to a lab, which gives you a concentration simulating what the welder is breathing." The system is placed outside the helmet simply because the helmet itself can create an inaccurate reading. The air outside, not inside, the helmet is what matters.

"We don’t want the reading to be influenced with different helmet styles," she said. "We want the measuring device outside, so we know what the actual exposure is."

She added that here, independent third-party consultants sometimes have an easier time conveying the importance. The employer has compliance top of mind, and employees want to work in a safe environment—but when they hear about the importance of safety from those selling safety-related products, they of course question the advice.

Everyone has biases, but third-party consultants aren’t selling products, so they’re less likely to be wedded to one solution over another. This sometimes can make buy-in easier, both from managers skeptical of someone who has an agenda to sell something, and from workers skeptical of someone telling them they should take the time to be safe. If welders don’t buy into the concept, improving weld safety becomes an uphill battle.

2. Monitor Weld Practices

When measuring PEL, Branch always observes the welder’s motions, arcon time, and other tasks. Certain welding jobs produce more fume than others.

"Very slight changes in behavior can change a welder’s exposure level to fume," Branch said. "One welder might work on pipe, and fitting time might be very low, so he or she actually has more hot time than another welder who might be working on big structures. In that case, it takes a long time to get everything fitted up, so the hot time is less. They’re both welding all day, the same base metals, and using the same weld wire, but because their activities are slightly different, their exposure levels are very different."

The details matter here, and Branch takes numerous pictures to document what exactly the welders do during a shift. Are they welding a pipe that’s rotating on a positioner, or are they welding overhead? Are all welders doing different tasks, or is everyone working on similar products? What positions are they welding, and what processes? From a fume perspective, a gas tungsten arc operation looks much different than a fluxed-cored wire weld.

She might take a few pictures, take notes, go elsewhere on the floor, then return an hour or two later. Is the welder working on the same or similar product in the same weld cell? Or is it a different product, perhaps in a different area of the plant? "You need to make sure you monitor tasks that are representative of all the tasks and roles they play in the fab shop."

A single monitoring session lasts about eight hours, which usually is enough to form a baseline for a fume mitigation plan. That said, if readings are quite inconsistent with industry norms, or if she catches any other related abnormality, she might revisit the team on a later date for a second reading.

She added that attaining a good reading helps set a good foundation for everything that comes after. It’s the "current state" of welding fumes. Basing a plan on incorrect or incomplete information—like one that doesn’t consider all the activities welders perform or the true arc-on times for the products they weld—can throw a wrench in all the fume mitigation efforts to come.

Also, when Branch returns for follow-up readings, having all those job details—where welders were, what they were welding, when and how—

really matter.

If the fume exposure readings come up entirely different, Branch compares the current notes with her notes from the previous reading. Sometimes, even small changes in the job can change fume exposure to a significant degree.

3. Know What Makes a Quality Weld

"We could just put some big fans to increase circulation," Branch explained, "but how much is that going to impact your weld quality?"

The current weld environment might be coincidental or purposeful. The weld cell might be positioned for good part flow and away from any breeze, to maintain the integrity of the weld pool.

Attaining this insight sets the foundation for potential options. Any safety apparatus that makes a welder’s job significantly harder makes fume collection (and perhaps the overall safety strategy) an afterthought in the minds of employees.

4. Analyze General Ventilation

Fabricators and weld shops operate in different environments and have different capex budgets. Not everyone can afford the most advanced fume mitigation technology on the market.

So, once Branch educates the staff, achieves their buy-in, and knows what welders need to achieve a quality weld, she analyzes general ventilation. She starts with changes that don’t require any investment or even welder technique. Changes could be as simple as just moving a weld cell to an area with better air circulation and keeping roll-up doors open for ventilation—as long as the situation doesn’t hurt weld quality (as discussed in No. 3). The idea, she said, is to get people thinking about air movement, and arranging weld cells not just for productivity but also for optimal welder safety and comfort.

Getting below the PEL is, of course, the target. The aim is to get excess fume away from the breathing zone, to bring exposure under the PEL. Ideally fumes should be evacuated safely and completely to the outside.

(Note also that this step might occur early on, before welder monitoring, if obvious ventilation improvements can be made without sacrificing weld quality.)

5. Consider the Fume Mitigation Options

After tackling the low-hanging fruit, Branch presents options for fruit hanging higher on the tree (that is, those that require some capex). Local extraction options include on-the-welding-gun extraction, hood capture systems, downdraft workbenches, robotic welding fume control, and more.

The technologies continue to advance here—and again, many will be on display at FABTECH in Orlando this year. On-the-welding-gun extraction methods, for instance, are a lot more flexible and compact than they once were.

6. Measure Again

Such mismatches can reveal themselves during a follow-up fume monitoring session. "Sometimes, we find that the mitigation techniques aren’t really helping," Branch said. "So, we reexamine the situation to uncover what really is inhibiting us from capturing the fumes. Here, you want to take pictures and document the process. What’s preventing us from capturing or removing the fumes we need to, to make this operation safe?"

Here is where welder observation, and simply asking what welders need to perform quality work, really matter. A weld shop might install a downdraft table only to find that welders rarely turn it on, due to the airflow’s effect on shielding gas coverage and overall weld quality. The solution might involve training or an alternative fume mitigation technique, but a downdraft table that’s never turned on isn’t doing anyone any good.

Something similar might happen with overhead units with excessive draw that might affect the shielding gas. Again, training might be the issue, and perhaps new ventilation technologies are called for, but as Branch emphasized, the numbers don’t lie: If a monitoring session measures potentially harmful levels of fume,

something

needs to change.

"We need to find that balance between the device that’s supposed to offer safety and the quality the welder needs to achieve. If the device interferes with that, they won’t use it."

Regarding local fume extraction with source-capture arms, perhaps the system doesn’t have enough draw, or the welder might not be moving the ventilation along with the weld—a particular issue with large workpieces with long welds. "Welders are not going to stop welding to move the ventilation system," Branch said. "Depending on how long the [weld] is, they get away from the system. So, we might see that at the end of a [source-capture arm], the circular end piece isn’t working. Can we change the shape of it to something that is more in line with the weld length? Does the arm itself need a few more elbows to allow the welder to position the ducting correctly for the weld at hand? Does the unit have more than one arm, and if so, can you position them in different places, to ensure complete coverage throughout that long weld?

"Solutions vary, but the bottom line is, the fume mitigation ductwork needs to be in a position that can capture the fume from the beginning of the weld to the end."

She added that focusing on the source-capture arm alone might not yield the best solution. The idea is to think of the entire operation: the welder position, the position of the work, the types of welding being performed, and more. Budget constraints might not allow for the best solution, but observing the situation and asking questions at least brings the problem to the fore, so shop leaders can discuss options—rather than just ignore the issue altogether.

7. Repeat

Metal fabrication businesses change. New welders are brought onboard. Product mixes evolve, as do the welding and fume mitigation challenges. For all these reasons, the fume mitigation effort—as with any initiative related to health and safety—should continue in a virtuous cycle.

And as Branch explained, the first step—proper training—should never be glossed over. In fact, it usually has the greatest return on investment. "Education is always valuable. If you thoroughly train welders to understand the potentially harmful effects of fume exposure, often they themselves can make small adjustments that we may not even see. They may adjust their body position so they’re not directly over the weld."

Small changes can make any fume mitigation investment even more effective. After all, the weld shop environment can be ever-changing. If welders tackle the work with the fume in mind, soon, certain best practices become automatic.

Eventually, the

entire approach

to fume mitigation becomes automatic. The shop culture changes. The blue haze dissipates. And when the next generation starts to consider whether they’d like to pursue a career wielding a welding torch, they can visit a clean, well-lit shop; experience well-circulated, clean air; and breathe easy.
