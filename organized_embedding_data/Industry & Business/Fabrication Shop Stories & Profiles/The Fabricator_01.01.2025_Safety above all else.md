# Safety above all else

[TARİH: 01.01.2025 The Fabricator]

Biz Talk

One fatality is too many

TIM HESTON

This isn’t your father’s manufacturing, but you can still find some scary stuff out there. And thanks to internet videos, much of it is in plain sight, there for the world to see.

F

or years, industry advocates have been trumpeting the fact that this isn’t your father’s manufacturing. Shops today are cleaner, safer, and more high tech than ever. Manufacturing technology has become truly astounding, creating a very different industry—one based more on problem-solving and less on grunt work. The dark, dirty, and dangerous days of manufacturing are long gone.

Recently, however, some troubling news has rumbled throughout the precision sheet metal fabrication business. OSHA investigations are underway—including one involving a fatal accident in which a worker was crushed while in the laser cutting work envelope. The Food and Drug Administration (FDA), which regulates laser machinery, has issued warnings. Specifically, it published a "red list" of imported machines that fail to meet certain standards.

In late November, Ed Youdell, president and CEO of the Fabricators and Manufacturers Association (FMA), released the following statement: "A safe and healthy workforce is necessary for a vibrant metal fabricating community. That’s why FMA is working with the FDA to ensure that fabricating equipment being sold in the U.S. meets all the safety requirements necessary to minimize the risk of injury to shop floor personnel. For example, a worker should never have to worry that the safety glass on a laser cutting machine isn’t actually rated to contain the laser beam."

This year, new FDA rules (specifically, Laser Notice No. 56) that align with the latest International Electrotechnical Commission (IEC) standards are in effect. According to a statement from Eric Rock of Chicago-based Rock Trade Law LLC, these rules "place a heightened focus on global harmonization and safety. The shift marks a significant change in how laser products are evaluated and ensures greater consistency in their design and use worldwide."

This isn’t your father’s manufacturing, but you can still find some scary stuff out there. And thanks to internet videos, much of it is in plain sight, there for the world to see. Sure, ignorance might be to blame sometimes. Hand-held laser welding and cleaning technologies in particular are relatively new. Many shops—especially those that don’t have a laser cutting machine and an experienced laser safety officer—simply don’t know about the dangers. But ignorance isn’t bliss in this case. When hand-held laser welding and cleaning happens out in the open, with no light-safe enclosure to be found, some irreversible eye injuries could occur.

To put it diplomatically, manufacturers worldwide operate in very different environments when it comes to machine safety—especially in laser cutting. Worldwide, the way people design and use fiber laser cutting machines hasn’t been consistent, and that brings up some serious safety concerns. Again, the internet has some scary stuff, including videos with fiber laser cutting machines operating without enclosures.

FABTECH, North America’s largest sheet metal fabrication tradeshow, has increased its vigilance recently, with the event’s laser safety officer continuing to step up enforcement. "We want all attendees and exhibitors to remain safe. So, when people do not adhere to the safety standards, we will require them to shut down their machines, and they will remain inactive during the duration of the exhibition."

That was Steven Glover, speaking with The Fabricator last year before FABTECH 2024. He added that "if a laser operating at high power or an unsafe wavelength were to penetrate an unprotected eye, it can lead to permanent eye damage. … That’s why this is a serious subject. Attendees deserve to be safe, so we need to ensure that they are safe."

Knowledge is power. The more people know, the safer they can be—which, incidentally, is why FMA recognizes shops with exemplary safety records every year, with its Rusty Demeules Award (honoring longtime former FMA volunteer Rusty Demeules).

Part of that knowledge has to do with how people perceive what’s truly "safe" and what isn’t. Looks can be deceiving. A shop full of new machines isn’t inherently "safer" than an operation with decades-old equipment. The same applies for machine safeguarding. An operation with apparently robust safeguards can be extraordinarily unsafe if they’re installed incorrectly or people find ways to override them. This isn’t your father’s manufacturing, but the laws of physics haven’t changed. Robotic arms, gantries, material handling shuttles, and other automated systems operate blindly, and terrible things can happen when they’re energized when they shouldn’t be. Where the gross negligence resides is for the regulators and legal system to decide. Still, we can’t pretend that safety problems don’t exist. One fatality is one too many—and a quick search through OSHA inspection records shows there’s been more than one fatality in the precision sheet metal business over the past three years.

There’s a good reason why so many FMA safety award winners talk about instilling a safety culture. "Culture" encompasses everything within the enterprise. Everyone knows what the best practices are, and incentives are aligned to promote safe behavior. If people see unsafe behavior, they say something. These best-in-class operations also implement manufacturing technology strategies that incorporate safety training, operator expectations, and—not least—a thorough review of equipment. In other words, they put employee safety above everything else.
