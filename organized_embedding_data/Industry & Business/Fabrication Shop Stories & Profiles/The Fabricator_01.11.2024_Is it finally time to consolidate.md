# Is it finally time to consolidate?

[TARİH: 01.11.2024 The Fabricator]

Tech Talk

Metal manufacturing is notoriously fragmented; will technology change that?

Caleb Chamberlain

T

here’s been a lot of talk about consolidation in manufacturing, particularly in aerospace and defense. Investors and outsiders to the industry often describe manufacturing as fragmented, filled with many small mom and pop garage shops with inconsistent process, pricing, and capabilities. They argue that it’s time for someone to modernize the industry.

Some high-profile companies have been funded on this premise. They might succeed. But I believe the manufacturing value chain is far more complex and nuanced than most people realize. Manufacturing has resisted consolidation for many reasons, and where consolidation was possible, it has mostly already occurred. Technology will undoubtedly change how companies position and scale, and exciting changes are taking place right now. But manufacturing as a whole isn’t going to be remade under the leadership of a single company.

To understand why, let’s look at why industries consolidate in the first place. In the early 1900s, there were hundreds, even thousands, of automakers in the U.S. Today, only a handful matter. Why? And why hasn’t that kind of consolidation happened across manufacturing?

I believe two primary factors must exist for an industry to consolidate and remain consolidated: 1) industry homogeneity and 2) market power derived from consolidation.

Industry Homogeneity

Homogeneity is a necessary condition for consolidation, because it allows standardized processes to be developed to support scale. If an industry produces a highly varied set of products or services, consolidation is much harder.

For example, in industries like consumer goods or logistics, homogeneity is high because core operations can be scaled and streamlined (like with Amazon’s warehousing and shipping network). But in industries with high customization or variability, like specialized engineering or bespoke manufacturing, homogeneity is low, making it tough for a single company to do it all efficiently.

This is one of the factors that make manufacturing historically difficult to tackle: It’s not homogeneous. Product requirements in one vertical might differ substantially from another, to say nothing of sales relationships, distribution, and operational and logistical requirements. It’s hard to scale a company that makes parts from napkin drawings, but there are a bunch of contractors who need exactly that service. For that niche, both locality and high-touch turnkey service are key. Meanwhile, companies that are optimized to make precision metal parts for rockets aren’t going to look twice at a napkin drawing for a piece of custom flashing.

Market Power Derived from Consolidation

For an industry to consolidate and remain that way, consolidation also has to create market power. By "power," I’m referring to Hamilton Helmer’s "7 Powers: The Foundations of Business Strategy." If you haven’t read the book, you should get a copy and read it, because it’s incredible. To summarize, Helmer defines "power" as a set of conditions that can give your business persistent differential returns. There are seven powers: scale economies, network economies, counter-positioning, switching costs, branding, cornered resource, and process power.

To qualify as a power, a condition must meet two high bars: It must "yield material improvement to cash flow" and it must prevent competitors from doing the same. If there’s no barrier to prevent competition, then new companies will see your margin and smell opportunity.

Going back to automakers, one obvious power advantage earned by consolidated players is scale: Mass producing and selling automobiles is hard. It requires huge capital expenditures, networks for distribution, brand recognition, buying power, and manufacturing expertise. As volume became concentrated across fewer companies, it became increasingly difficult for smaller manufacturers to compete at scale. As we’ve seen with modern players like Tesla, it’s possible. But it’s extremely difficult to challenge a scaled incumbent.

Meanwhile, the automotive industry is very homogeneous, which allows companies to scale in the first place. It’s difficult to build the infrastructure to make millions of cars, but once you’ve nailed it, you can sell into an extremely large market without dramatically changing the product to serve other verticals.

Mass consumer products lend themselves well to this kind of consolidation. But it’s worth noticing that where homogeneity breaks down, so does consolidation: GM isn’t known for tractors and heavy earth-moving equipment, for example, although it has tried to get there through acquisition. Winning in consumer automobiles doesn’t necessarily translate to other markets, even though ostensibly it’s all just "manufacturing."

The Sandbox

In a recent podcast, The Fabricator magazine’s Tim Heston talked about a company’s "sandbox," a simple example of the space manufacturers play in. I like that analogy. I’d say that consolidation can happen inside a sandbox, and the boundaries of a sandbox are defined by homogeneity (I can do everything in this sandbox well), and power (competitors can’t easily challenge me here).

Manufacturing has, at least to date, resisted consolidation because manufacturing isn’t just one industry—it’s a collection of technologies serving numerous industries. And inside each industry, there are tons of variation in value-added capability, sales channels, and customer relationships.

There’s even more complexity not shown: While Figure 1 shows where manufacturers might be positioned vertically and horizontally, it says nothing about differentiating factors like sales models (high-touch vs. self-serve), regional constraints (shipping heavy metal gets expensive), technological integration, deep engineering, branding, quality, pricing, and lead times. Overall, Figure 1 provides a picture of how and why the metal fabrication "industry" might be fragmented. Even in the small subset pictured, there are hundreds of ways of doing business.

A job shop might capture a narrow slice of the full value chain, scaled horizontally across many industries and geographies. But to expand and differentiate, a job shop might push downward in the value chain, offering welding, finishing, and assembly services in a particular niche.

Turnkey services differentiate a shop’s offerings, providing margin opportunity. Automakers (themselves very consolidated) might work with contract manufacturers, which have carried specialization to its logical conclusion and learned to service one specific industry extremely well.

On the far right, we have an example of a vertically integrated OEM. This is a company that has insourced manufacturing and distribution of its own product. By executing well, vertically integrated companies can reduce costs and provide a highly differentiated customer experience compared to nonvertically integrated competitors.

On the top left, some service centers have expanded downwards in the value chain, offering more value-added services to improve margin and better serve particular customers. When service centers push into value-added services in this way, they compete directly with their own customers. This doesn’t feel great for shops that have to buy from their competitors.

Still, there’s a limit to how far service centers can take this, because industries lack homogeneity. Service centers can’t serve every industry or even every customer within a single industry. This prevents scaled service centers from expanding downward to absorb the entire market.

The same thing can be said of mills. I’m aware of at least one mill that makes aluminum sheet metal and sells to service centers in the normal way. But it also makes aluminum road signs. I’ve captured that dynamic by extending a narrow vertical segment down the value chain for mills in Figure 1.

Why wouldn’t a mill just expand vertically downward until it owns everything? The answer is that there’s just too little homogeneity. It couldn’t be good at it. But it can certainly pick a relatively narrow slice in one vertical and own it end to end. A mill that produces a finished product will either enjoy incredible margins or be able to price so low that no downstream company can possibly compete, all other things being equal.

Of course, that won’t stop another company from making super-customized "road signs" and selling them to consumers as decorations. Even in the road sign market, the companies at the top of the "food chain" (so to speak) can’t completely own a product category.

How Tech Might Enable Change

With this picture in mind, you just have to ask: What exactly do people mean when they say they are going to come in and "fix" manufacturing and save the world? What’s their new tech-enabled sandbox going to look like? And once they’ve carved out a new sandbox, what new power is going to prevent other companies from doing the same?

Remember, if there’s no power, a market won’t stay consolidated. It’s frankly not enough to jump in and say, "I’m going to use software to execute better." Come on. The world is full of extremely smart people, and the manufacturing space is filled with scaled-up companies highly motivated to improve their own margins with technology.

Being smart and knowing how to write software isn’t a power. In fact, in his book, Helmer makes the very explicit point that good execution is very much not a long-term value-generating strategy. It’s more like table stakes.

Having said that, technology is definitely changing manufacturing by making it possible to expand the boundaries of market sandboxes. Shops still need operators who know what they’re doing, but modern controls and software have made machines much easier to operate. The outcome of this trend will be twofold: OEMs will be better able to bring manufacturing in-house, and job shops will support a wider suite of services without compromising customer experience.

Software also enables an entirely new kind of business, one that has integrated software to serve a particular need across a wide range of markets. Quick-turn providers with automated quoting (which includes my company along with several others in the U.S. and Europe) fall into this category.

Other companies use software to take rather the opposite approach: Instead of optimizing around generic value-added services, they optimize to serve specific industries extremely well. These companies form in response to poor customer experiences offered by legacy players and are often led by industry outsiders who see a better way.

Build Your Sandbox

Despite all this, manufacturing isn’t about to be completely transformed by industry outsiders who can write software. Sure, some consolidation will happen, but there will still be hundreds, if not thousands, of sandboxes carved out horizontally and vertically in the supply chain. There’s a lot of opportunity and risk out there for everyone, but no single player is going to absolutely dominate the metal manufacturing space.
