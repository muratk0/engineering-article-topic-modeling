# What’s behind the powder coating defect?

W

hen a powder coating is applied and the cured finish reveals a visible defect, the process is not yet finished. In fact, the investigation is just beginning.

Rework is something with which every metal fabricator can identify and works to avoid. It’s that much worse when it comes to the powder coating finish because it’s the last step in the value-added process before the part is shipped to a customer.

If a shop is lucky, the defect is caught early on, and only a couple of parts have to be stripped and reworked or simply tossed and replaced with quality parts. Either way, however, new parts have to be recoated, and scheduling has to be adjusted to accommodate the work.

Not all shops offer powder coating, but an increasing number have added the service in recent years as they look to stand out to potential customers as a one-stop shop for fabricating, forming, and finishing. For those that don’t powder coat, they likely have worked with a custom coater for their finishing needs. In both instances, quality coatings keep everyone on schedule and in the good graces of customers.

The Fabricator spoke with three powder coating experts, each representing a key step in the powder coating process, about not only avoiding defects but identifying possible causes if defects are present. Not all metal fabricators are finishing experts, but they do understand how the right processes can contribute to quality production. Luckily, that same type of thinking applies in the powder coating department as well.

Potential Problem Areas

Raw Material.

Before the discussion focuses on cleaning and prepping parts for the application of the powder coating, Mark Mortensen, president of All-Color Powder Coating, Oregon, Wis., wants to talk about the actual material to be coated. If you think it’s the same material that was being purchased 20 years ago, this custom coater wants you to think again.

Because more metal is made from recycled content than actually being created from scratch, more impurities have worked their way into the material. This has especially been noticeable in aluminum, Mortensen said.

"Typically, with more recycled content comes more contaminants, which can migrate to the surface and cause some of these defects," he added. These defects most commonly reveal themselves in pinholes, holes or pores that appear at the surface as contaminants look to release air or gas during or after the curing cycle, commonly referred to as outgassing.

Obviously, material with mill scale can pose a problem, but that’s something a powder coater can expect. With material quality, it’s more of a guessing game.

Mortensen said that oxidized edges, created when sheet metal is laser-cut and oxygen is used as an assist gas, have become less of a problem in recent years. (Edges with oxidation present adhesion issues for those applying powder coatings.) Most metal fabricators realize that they must use another assist gas, such as nitrogen, to bypass the oxidation process or rely on some sort of mechanical finishing process to remove the oxides from the edges prior to coating.

The Powder.

The metal might not be the only material that a metal fabricator has questions about. What about the actual powder coating material?

"Not that we don’t have errors, but we do have a pretty rigorous quality control process," said Michael Withers, business development manager, architectural powder coatings and thermoplastics, at Philadelphia-based Axalta Coating Systems, about the company’s powder manufacturing process. "We’ll have raw material in the hopper to feed the extruder, and someone will grab a sample there, take it to the lab, extrude it, grind it, and spray it in the lab to confirm colors and test to ensure that the mechanical properties are specific to that product."

That all comes after years of development work, creating powder coatings that deliver the right color over the long term, durability, and corrosion protection. By the time the material is being made, powder coating suppliers are confident that their production recipes and processes are going to result in powder that will meet expectations, if everything else goes right in the finishing department.

"When I first got into powder coating, it was kind of a joke, but it was also real. If there were a problem, the powder guy would point at the pretreat guy and the pretreat guy would point at the powder guy, with the customer in the middle, not knowing how to fix the problem," Withers said. "It’s not like that anymore. In fact, it hasn’t been like that for a long time. They all work collectively to solve a problem now."

With all that said, any company that is involved in powder coating should be serious about storing the material. The powder should be stored in a cool, dry, and well-ventilated area away from heat, ignition sources, and direct sunlight. This helps to avoid degradation and clumping of the material.

Pretreatment.

Prepping metal parts to receive a powder is simple in theory, but it can get pretty complicated based on the different delivery methods and stages that a cleaning might require. Basically, the pretreatment stages not only clean the metal, but they also prepare the metal surface for adhesion, offer increased corrosion protection, and enhance the final coating.

Pretreatment chemicals can be applied via a spray nozzle, either manually or automatically, or parts can be dipped into tanks filled with pretreatment chemicals, which are agitated to ensure the parts are fully exposed to the different chemical agents. That is then followed by some sort of rinse. In some instances, another round of pretreatment exposure and rinsing can occur. When that cycle is complete, the parts have to move through an oven to get dried prior to receiving the powder.

A powder coating finish that flakes off is likely a sign that the part wasn’t cleaned thoroughly prior to the powder being applied, the part wasn’t cured according to the correct specifications, or the powder was applied incorrectly. Flaking is just one of many powder coating defects. Eliminating these types of mistakes requires a commitment to maintaining consistent operations and an engaged finishing team.

"The definition for quality parts is consistency," said Brian Korecky, district sales manager for Bulk Chemicals, Reading, Pa. "If you don’t have that good consistency in maintaining the pretreatment chemicals and water quality, you’re not going to have very good quality."

Korecky said having trained line supervisors and operators can go a long way in ensuring that the pretreatment line is operating as it should. It’s not like a press brake, where changeovers from one job to the next are a regular occurrence. The pretreatment mix is likely to remain consistent as it has been specified to work with the part mix being processed in the finishing department. If the finishing team can ensure that the pretreatment chemical balances are where they need to be and temperatures for the pretreatment delivery and the dry-off ovens are set, the parts should be in proper shape to receive the powder coating.

Water quality is another area to keep an eye on, according to Korecky. A reverse osmosis or a deionized water system helps to keep the total dissolved solids down, making rinsing much more effective in terms of leaving a clean surface to be powder coated.

Application.

Applying powder coating is not difficult, given that the electrostatically charged powder particles gravitate to the surface of the metal parts, but that doesn’t prevent defects from happening. Poor application practices can lead to too little coverage, too much coverage, and even defects like orange peel.

"A lot of this can be self-corrected," Mortensen said. "You set the guns up correctly and use a consistent spray pattern, and a lot of defects can be resolved fairly easily."

Smart powder application guns have emerged as well to help with consistent powder application. Sensors provide an indication as to just how far away from a part the gun is, and it can adjust the kilovolts up or down to ensure even powder application. Withers said that this type of technological advancement is especially helpful in dealing with the defects that might arise from back ionization, where a collection of charged powder particles actually end up repelling each other rather than just making their way to the grounded surface.

Powder Coating Defects

It’s one thing to know what parts of a powder coating operation might be the source of defects, but do you have a good feel for the different types of defects?

Pinholes.

These little holes in a powder coating finish are the result of outgassing. Causes for this type of defect can be moisture that has not been completely dried off the metal surface prior to entering the powder coating booth or air trapped inside of a casting that escapes as it passes through a curing oven.

Obviously, eliminating moisture and gases are important, and an oven can help with that. Mortensen stressed prebaking parts, if necessary, as a reliable way to reduce outgassing issues.

Another thing to keep in mind is that moisture might not necessarily be a result of a pretreatment rinse—well, at least not directly. Withers said that he was familiar with a situation in a shop where steam released from pretreatment equipment would hit a stream of heated air during colder weather, pushing the moisture down on hanging cleaned parts about to enter the powder coating booth. Those droplets caused resulting pinholes.

Craters.

Also called fish eyes, a crater is a large cavity in the coating. It sometimes can extend down to the surface.

Craters are typically the result of poor surface preparation or some sort of contaminant. Withers said that he once investigated a powder coating line that was experiencing issues with craters. The pretreatment and application activities looked good, so he took a walk around the shop. He noticed one set of welding curtains nearby that differed in color from the rest. When he asked why a welder had different colored curtains, the welder said he had purchased a silicone cleaner from an automotive part store and used it to clean the protective barriers.

"Silicone is a finishing process killer," Withers said.

Incomplete Coverage.

As previously stated, electrostatically charged particles can work against each other, preventing them from landing evenly on a metal surface. This is especially true when it comes to enclosed areas, like the inside corners of boxes. This phenomenon is called the Faraday cage effect. These charged particles like to take the path of least resistance, and that’s why they’ll adhere to the edges and walls before these tight interior corners.

Mortensen said changes in the way the powder coating is applied can help overcome the Faraday cage effect challenges. While a powder coating technician’s first thought might be to crank up the power to force the material into that confined space, Mortensen said the opposite actually works better.

"I would argue that the most successful solution is turning the voltage down and kind of fluttering the powder in there," he said. "Let the powder do the work."

He added that it’s helpful to powder coat those challenging areas of a part first, while the powder attraction is greatest, before coating the rest of the part.

Additionally, in the case of the application gun emitting a light spray pattern, Withers recommended checking the ground connection. Grounding done close to the application booth results in better powder coating transfer.

Film Buildup.

Improper application can lead to defects such as sagging, where excessive powder droops because of gravity; and orange peel, a bumpy or dimpled appearance, much like the skin of the popular fruit. A more disciplined approach to powder application is the most direct method to rectifying these defects. Tools like film thickness measurement devices also act as a user-friendly quality tool to keep the powder coating process within specifications.

Withers said that those types of gauges might seem like an extravagance, but their cost pales in comparison to the cost of excessive powder coatings being applied to metal parts. If the customer specification is 2.5 mils thickness for the powder coat and the powder coating application is resulting in 3.5 mils, that could be tens of thousands of dollars lost on excess that the customer doesn’t expect.

"If you don’t understand the impact and you haven’t done the math, you don’t even realize that you are spending that money," Withers added.

Chipping.

One of the most hard-to-ignore powder coating defects is chipping. When large flakes of the finish just break away from the metal surface, it can be a jarring sight.

More than likely, poor cleaning of the part or improper powder application is the culprit. Both contribute to poor adhesion to the metal substrate.

A good ground is necessary for the metal parts to receive the electrostatically charged powder particles. Something as simple as a hook with too much powder accumulation can diminish the grounding effect and adversely affect powder application efforts.

RossHelen/iStock/Getty Images Plus

Poor material handling of the finished part at times results in the discover of this type of defect. As a part bangs against another part or strikes a heavy object, a chip might fly off.

Dialing in the pretreatment process, keeping an eye out for possible contaminants that might emerge during the powder coating cycle, correcting powder application issues, and being sure that the parts have been properly cured are all possible solutions to this particular loss of adhesion issue.

Yellowing.

A defect more commonly found in lighter colors, yellowing is a breakdown of pigments, which results in a washing out of the color. Withers said that this can occur in darker colors as well, but the miscoloring obviously will be darker, not so much a yellow.

More than likely, the cause of this defect is overcuring, exposing the powder-coated part to the high-temperature heat of the oven for longer than prescribed.

The remedy for this ailment is adhering to the job data sheet, ensuring the parts are cured according to the coating recipe. Also, when the conveyor line is shut down, someone needs to ensure that no parts are stuck in the oven or that the oven is set to a low-fire mode.

Troubleshooting Tip

Of course, that’s not all of the possible powder coating defects that a line technician might see. That’s why it’s important to take steps to try and prevent the defects from occurring.

Korecky suggested that to maintain consistency in pretreatment and powder coating application performance, a line supervisor or a technician should conduct an audit before every shift. That person can check to see that each stage of the process is where it needs to be to produce quality finished parts.

Things that the audit should check, but are not limited to:

Line speed, if an automated system

Dwell time of parts in each stage

Titrations, which should be checked at least once per shift

Conductivity of the rinse water

Performance of the rinsing stage

Proper grounding

Time and temperature in the dry-off oven

Condition of application guns

Spray patterns

Applied film thickness

Temperature of the curing oven

Time in the curing oven

Condition of the fire detection system

Final Advice

Sometimes a process of any kind is only as good as the people involved. Powder coating is no different. Someone applying the powder coating or a line technician that is engaged and committed to doing a good job is very likely to deliver quality parts on a consistent basis.

"Utilize your vendors, whether it’s your chemical vendors, powder vendors, or the equipment side of things. Use them to help you with your staff training," Korecky said.

He also recommended the training offerings from the Powder Coating Institute, which offers Powder Coating 101 and 202 workshops throughout the year.
