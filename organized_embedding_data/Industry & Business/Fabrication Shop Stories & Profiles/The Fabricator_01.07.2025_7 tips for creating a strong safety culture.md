# 7 tips for creating a strong safety culture

[TARİH: 01.07.2025 The Fabricator]

Safety

The winner of FMA’s 2025 Rusty Demeules Award for Safety Excellence reveals how a focus on safety helps to empower a workforce

By

Dan Davis

A

metal fabricating and forming company with no recordable injury or illness cases or lost workdays related to a recordable injury or illness might consider its mission complete for the year. Any safety professional, however, will remind you that there is no finish line in this journey, and actually resting on laurels is a good way for an organization to get lackadaisical about maintaining its recent safety success.

Steel Craft’s Milwaukee Stampings Division had no recordable injuries or illnesses and no lost workdays or restricted duty cases that could be related to such incidences in 2024. They also appear to be maintaining that same success rate in 2025.

In 2024, the company’s recordable incidence rate (total recordable injury/illness cases multiplied by 200,000 and divided by total employee hours worked) was zero, an improvement over the last two years (2023: 1.59 and 2022: 4.41). As a result, the metal forming company has seen its workers’ compensation modification rate, a key component insurance companies use in determining workers’ compensation premiums, fall to 0.93 in 2025 from 1.25 in 2024.

This documented performance helped to earn Steel Craft’s Milwaukee Stampings Division the 2025 Rusty Demeules Award, the Fabricators and Manufacturers Association’s highest honor in its safety awards program. The award, named after the late Lawrence Thomas "Rusty" Demeules, who served as president of FMA’s Safety Committee from 1989 to 1996 and was president and owner of Standard Iron & Wire, recognizes one company each year that excels beyond OSHA recordable incidence rates and demonstrates an established safety culture.

"This award really meant the world to us because we’ve spent a lot of time getting that safety culture in place. We weren’t always at this level," said Jill Corsten, the company’s general manager.

It was not an overnight transformation into an award-winning, safety-first organization, but it didn’t take decades either. It was just a matter of listening to employees, being consistent with the safety commitment, and keeping people engaged in the effort. This is how Steel Craft’s Milwaukee Stampings Division does it.

1. Prioritize the Areas That Need Improvement

When Environmental Health and Safety Manager Kurt Gardner started at Steel Craft in 2022, splitting his time between the company’s fabricating division in Hartford, Wis., and the stamping division, he wanted to get a feel as to what was working and where there might be room for improvement. Safety training was a regular occurrence, but gaps still remained.

"What we ended up going with is trying to get a baseline of where we were. By looking at all the data, we wanted to learn what were the injuries and where they were occurring," said Gardner, a safety professional with more than 30 years of manufacturing experience.

In his role as an environmental health and safety manager, Kurt Gardner sometimes has to man the grills for the luncheon celebrating another year without a lost-time incident.

Banners with employee signatures symbolize the group effort involved in ensuring that Steel Craft’s Milwaukee Stampings Division had no lost-time incidences the last two years. Images: Steel Craft’s Milwaukee Stampings Division.

One of the early focuses was on lockout/tagout. (Lockout describes the use of a lock on machinery to prevent it from being started. Tagout refers to placing a label on the machinery that reminds others not to start up the device.) Gardner recounted that many shop floor team members thought that lockout/tagout practices were only for the maintenance crew, who would shut down any of the company’s 11 stamping presses for inspection and upkeep. But press operators also spend time cleaning out the presses as part of their regular duties, so that lockout/tagout practice made sense for them as well.

That observation and others led to more conversations about lockout/tagout and expanding its uses, leaning on leaders to share how these safety practices are protections for anyone working around the presses.

Everyone celebrates another year of avoiding work-related injuries at Steel Craft’s Milwaukee Stampings Division with a company-wide luncheon.

2. Partner With Those on the Shop Floor

"When you support the employees on the floor and the leads, they buy into it," Gardner added.

That involves the management team knowing and backing the supervisors and the leads on the shop floor, who then engage employees for their ideas and feedback. That exchange of ideas becomes fodder for daily team meetings and discussion items for communication boards on the floor.

"It’s cool to walk by and see a change on those boards," Gardner said. "You find out that something was identified as a risk or hazard, the team talked about it, and a change was implemented."

Over the past couple of years, Steel Craft’s Milwaukee Stampings Division has incorporated the STOP program to empower employees as guardians of a safe work environment:

S—Stop.

Any employee has the authority to stop unsafe work.

T—Take.

Employees are expected to take ownership of unaddressed hazards and unsafe work practices.

O—Observe.

Everyone is asked to observe, recognize, and address unsafe conditions and behaviors.

P—Promptly.

Reports on near misses and first-aid incidents need to be made promptly.

"They aren’t afraid now to stop what they’re doing, ask questions, and get others involved to make sure we handle things in the right way, rather than having somebody take a risk or a chance," Gardner said.

3. Include Safety in Most Discussions

The best way to keep safety at the forefront is to make it a part of almost any conversation. The consistency helps to build familiarity with the focus, and people come to expect that part of the conversation.

"All of our meetings—our lead meetings, our supervisor meetings, and our plant meetings—start with safety," Corsten said. "How have we performed? Were there any recent occurrences? Were there any observations made?"

These types of discussions occur multiple times per day. There are the morning SQDC (safety, quality, delivery, cost) meetings where the shop floor teams discuss safety concerns and any observations that need to be addressed; the daily production meeting with the managers, supervisors, and leads where the safety-related metrics from the day before are discussed; and daily gemba walks that allow members from the operations, engineering, planning, quality, safety, and human resources teams to walk the shop floor and have discussions with production about items on the SQDC boards.

Layered process audits are assigned and completed by departments every month, and each audit has a safety section. This is another method to reinforce that safety practices are being followed. Corsten added that any issues that are identified as a result of these audits are addressed immediately by the supervisor and the department lead.

4. Talk About Near Misses

At Steel Craft’s Milwaukee Stampings Division, near misses are not treated like prosecutions. They are learning experiences.

"We failed. We had a near miss," Gardner said. "But we failed safely. Nobody got hurt."

With that in mind, the employee involved with the near miss is expected to share his experience with his co-workers. During this "safety stand down," as Gardner called it, the employee talks to the team about what happened, why it happened, and what needs to be done to prevent further safety concerns.

"Rather than the blame game, let’s put it on the table and discuss this. Let’s all learn from each other," Gardner said.

5. Keep People Engaged in the Safety Effort

Corsten recalled that just under two years ago she was part of a safety-related video made by the company’s own shop floor employees. It was part of the STOP program launch.

The short video was meant to capture the types of unsafe incidences of which employees should be cognizant. It also happened to be a new way to get folks engaged in the safety effort.

"It’s trying new things," Corsten said. "When one thing doesn’t work, you try something else."

In another example of boosting safety awareness, Steel Craft’s Milwaukee Stampings Division includes safety as part of its continuous improvement activities. Promoting the need for new ideas at town hall meetings, management collects suggestions from the shop. Corsten said that over time more than half of the suggestions have been safety related.

To encourage continuous improvement suggestions, a $25 gift card drawing is held each month and is given to an employee who contributed an idea. Also, once a continuous improvement idea project is implemented and closed out, a cost avoidance review is done, and the originator of the idea receives a Visa gift card that reflects a percentage of the cost savings.

6. Don’t Stop Pushing for Improvement

Much like continuous improvement efforts, the pursuit of a safe work environment really has no ending. Even with two years of no lost workdays, Steel Craft’s Milwaukee Stampings Division is still pushing forward with initiatives that aren’t tied to regular safety practices.

"We’re now starting to look at safety observations. How many did each department submit?" Corsten said. "We’re actually going to Pareto-chart those safety observations to see if any department stands out. That’s where we might need to focus on next."

7. Watch Out for the New Folks

In today’s manufacturing world, a new hire is not only likely to be new to the company, but also to manufacturing. That type of inexperience can put an individual in risky scenarios—if left to his own devices. Steel Craft’s Milwaukee Stampings Division recognizes that and works to keep that from happening.

After an initial safety orientation, any new hire meets with a trainer, the safety officer, and a supervisor from the shop floor. That person is then paired with a mentor, who basically acts as a sherpa for this new person exploring the manufacturing world.

That new hire also is asked to wear a safety vest that stands out on the shop floor. Everyone immediately recognizes that a rookie is amongst them, and they might want to keep an eye on that person.

"We want to make sure that person is doing everything he or she needs to do and that it’s done safely," Gardner said.
