NO. 2: CADREX MANUFACTURING SOLUTIONS

Cadrex has a short history, yet for the contract fabrication space, it’s extraordinarily significant. Owned by a private equity firm out of Chicago, Cadrex’s story starts in 2021. Formed after numerous acquisitions over just a few years, the organization today employs more than 2,000 people in 21 locations across North America. It made $540 million in 2025—a record— and it’s expecting to close in on $660 million in sales this year. Not bad for a five-year-old.

"We’ve stuck with our long-term plan. We have three major end markets. We have ICT [information and communication technologies], which are your hyperscalers, your network computer and storage companies that are driving AI and machine learning. Then we have very strong market growth in our aerospace, defense, and medical business. We’re making big investments to support us from both a capacity and capability standpoint."

That was Matt Crichton, chief commercial officer, who added that company management is looking at the current environment with a long-term perspective. "Yes, we happen to be in the right markets at the right time. But a big part of who we are is being able to execute on our long-term strategy."

Plenty of fabricators operate as part of some kind of holding company. They get the power of scale when it comes to purchasing, health care, and technology investment, but they maintain the individual company brands. Cadrex took an entirely different approach, putting its acquired companies under one brand and communicating a single go-to-market strategy. A salesperson visiting a prospect might have certain areas of expertise but represents Cadrex as a whole.

Central to this was a focus on a strategic combination of end markets— specifically, ones with significant growth opportunities (like ICT), as well as arenas that tend to run counter to broader economic cycles, like medical devices and defense. Next, Cadrex married these markets with the unique technology mix created after it acquired 11 firms: punch/laser machines, blanking and bending automation, robotic press brakes, stamping, machining—the list goes on. And, like others in the FAB 40, the organization offers extensive assembly and supply chain management.

"The challenging part is to apply our capabilities at scale across the platform," Crichton said. "To solve this, we really concentrated on capacity planning and demand management. We need capacity in the right place and at the right time to deliver on time."

This sounds logical, but digging into the weeds gets complicated. "For instance, at one location, we might have abundant punch/laser combination machine capacity, but no press brake capacity at all. And then there might be some outside processing. How do we manage that? We look at the big picture."

He added that OEE (overall equipment effectiveness) is just one data point. Capacity of the entire job routing plays a role too. Moving a job to a plant with ample laser cutting capacity, only to ship it to another plant with press brake capacity, wouldn’t make sense. Machine capabilities vary too. A plant might have plenty of press brake capacity, but the brakes themselves might not have the bed lengths, tools, or tonnages available to handle the job in question.

"We’ve learned to focus on long-term customer partnerships," Crichton said, explaining that Cadrex didn’t just immediately take on increased demand without forethought. Sure, OEE metrics might say an operation has immense excess capacity—but customers pay for shipped products, not incredible machine utilization. "We could have brought work in, filled available work center capacity, and jammed work through by blunt force. But that won’t work in the long run. Over the past few years, we’ve really focused on how we can operate in a thoughtful way. [Specifically], we have built the processes and the culture to deliver on volatile demand."

This includes prototyping and new product introduction processes, which recently became much more collaborative. "Speed is the currency in our market now," Crichton said. "Our customers need to get their products to market faster than their competitors. That’s really the value we’re delivering to the marketplace. Cross-functional teams work to move a project forward. We avoid working within independent silos."

Yes, Cadrex has immense metal fabrication capacity. But capacity doesn’t mean anything if customer promises aren’t kept. As Crichton explained, their key competitive advantage "will be thoughtfully planning production for speed and agility. We’re not 100% there yet. But ultimately, I think we’re building a process to scale for the future."

NO. 3: O’NEAL MANUFACTURING SERVICES

Like others on the FAB 40, OMS has seen significant upticks in demand, especially in the data center sector—but that’s not the only hot spot.

"New opportunities are still funneling in. That’s made us very bullish for the remainder of this year and into the following years. And the sales cycle is long. Anything we see today might not materialize into a purchase order until next year. We don’t want to take our foot off the gas, so to speak."

That was Russell Bagby, OMS’s CFO, who added that some data center orders have demand extending out until 2030 and beyond. Natural gas and power generation have been hot spots, too, as well as construction equipment. Ag equipment has been sluggish, "but we hope that ag business to come roaring back at some point."

As data center work continues unabated, the question about how sustainable that work will be always arises. Are we in a bubble? Whatever happens, Bagby said, the contract metal fabrication business model can adapt. "If data center work comes to a grinding halt, I guarantee there will be some other sector looking for capacity. We need to be nimble and diversified."

Bagby described three initiatives to support that strategy. First is reinvestment in manufacturing technology. "We believe we need to average at least $1 million a year per location in capital expenditures over the next five years. In total, that’s between $8 million and $10 million a year. That includes faster machines to meet surging demand, or new processes we don’t have. The idea is to add new customers without necessarily needing to add significant head count."

Second is advanced information management. OMS has a variety of AI and software initiatives, including one that involves cameras and safety monitoring. "We want our people to be safe on the floor. We don’t want injuries, of course. So now, we are piloting a software program that looks for PPE violations, like safety gloves. The system sends an alert to a supervisor."

He conceded that this does sound "a bit like Big Brother. But we don’t want to admonish someone. If the pilot program is successful, it’s there to improve our safety culture, driving those near misses and injuries out of the operation."

In the office, OMS has implemented AI-based tools to assist with order entry for new part programs, including part numbers, descriptions, and job attributes. "The solution gets us 70% there, and manual inputs can take us the rest of the way."

All these initiatives—capex, safety, information management—aim to meet the needs of a demand forecast that looks a lot different than it did just 12 months ago. Like other large fabricators, OMS receives regular forecasts from its largest customers, especially those in ag and construction equipment. And as Bagby explained, those forecasts look a lot different than they did a year ago.

"Throughout 2025, it was common for us to see negative numbers," he said. "Around November and December [of 2025], those forecasts turned positive. And for every month since then, we’ve seen those forecasts increase."

Will demand continue? "We see uncertainty out there. But we had the tariff uncertainty, and we navigated that. And now we have the uncertainty connected to the Iran war. We’ve seen what’s going on with the price of oil and the potential ramifications. But for us, they haven’t materialized yet, and we still see some incredible opportunities."

NO. 9: MOMENTUM MANUFACTURING GROUP

The New England fabrication giant has focused on commercial work for decades. Warehouse automation continues to be a hot spot, and commercial work is as strong as ever. Today, however, the company is broadening its focus to cover new markets.

The organization is headquartered in Massachusetts, but a principal fabrication facility remains in St. Johnsbury, Vt. Overseeing the market-focus expansion is a lifelong Vermonter, Matthew Smith, who’s been with Momentum for 38 years. One new market is (no surprise) AI infrastructure.

"We’re now really focused on data center opportunities, be it the infrastructure, cooling systems," Smith said. "Again, we continue to do a lot of commercial and industrial projects. But now, we’re also focused on aerospace and defense work."

These last sectors have pushed the operation to achieve cybersecurity certifications like CMMC (Level 2, at this writing) and industry-specific certifications like AS 9100. The new focus also has spurred the fabricator to expand its quality, customer service, and engineering groups. "It’s a transformation, no doubt. We’ve learned a lot, but it’s going very well, and we’re seeing some good opportunities."

The customer-mix expansion has also pushed Momentum’s automation initiatives. These include new investments in robotic welding, robotic press brakes, and blasting. Smith added that these investments involve two key drivers. First is flexibility; second is ensuring employees add as much value as they can and are freed from manual work that few (if any) want to do anyway.

Manual blasting is a prime example. Until recently, Momentum has relied on workers wielding media blasting guns to prep the surface of a relatively high-quantity product family. For this project, the fabricator relied on its internal robotics team, but it also tapped into some next-generation talent.

"Here in Northern Vermont, several local high schools have really embraced local manufacturing. They want to give kids some practical experience and not just have them sitting in front of a computer screen."

Students and the internal team worked together to implement a robotic system that today automates a significant amount of small-part blasting work at the Vermont plant. The system now is performing a manual job that few wanted to take on manually.

"Manually blasting these parts took 15 to 20 minutes," Smith said. "We’re now blasting them in three minutes. The throughput this system gives us is just amazing."

When it comes to flexible automation, Momentum’s current collection of robotic press brakes is a prime example. The fabricator’s robotic brake capability includes automated tool changes and flexible programming designed to handle a wide range of parts. Some automated brakes handle large parts almost exclusively, while others handle small ones.

Still, Momentum had a range of part geometries that didn’t take full advantage of all the tool- and gripper-change flexibility of its existing robotic brakes. This led the company to design a custom brake cell that uses only a limited set of tools, where the bend sequence could be optimized for a relatively narrow range of part geometries.

"Our internal team actually designed our new press brake cell around a defined range of work, not for a specific customer," Smith explained. "Instead, it’s designed to operate best around a specific work envelope."

Momentum’s forming strategy now helps the company get the most out of its automation investments. Robotic brakes designed to handle a wide range of parts—a small system for the very small and a large brake for the very large—offer immense flexibility, especially with automated tool changes. Meanwhile, a custom-designed brake handles a narrow range of common part geometries that share common attributes and toolsets.

As Smith explained, all this gives Momentum the best of all worlds. First, it has bending cells that employ automated tool change. "For those cells, changing over from one part to another is incredibly quick. Part volume just isn’t as critical as it once was." At the other end of the spectrum is that new cell that optimizes throughput for a specified part range.

The overall approach handles the part-mix reality of contract metal fabrication. You have high-quantity orders combined with ranges of similar parts from different customers, as well as a "long tail" of disparate parts. Automation can’t handle it all, but it can help free the constraints and, perhaps most important, make the most out of an organization’s skilled labor.

NO. 11: NIC GLOBAL

NIC Global’s roots go back to the late 1990s, when Steve Simon and Simon Ventures LLC purchased three custom fabricators in the Seattle area. In 2001, the company was incorporated as National Industrial Concepts, then as NIC Global a few years later. Its legal and financial headquarters remain in the Pacific Northwest, but it also has a major presence in Tennessee, with its operating headquarters and a large plant located northeast of Nashville, along with thriving locations in Texas and Ohio. Its newest plant, in Spokane, Wash., opened in 2023.

The family-owned fabricator serves a wide variety of customers, with a focus on three core markets: heavy truck, power distribution and infrastructure (which now includes major investments for data center support), and medical devices. The West Coast operation focuses mainly on the energy and medical device segments, including ultrasound machines and various other mechanical assemblies. It’s work that includes a healthy dose of supply chain management, coordinating the delivery of myriad components that are integrated into a final assembly in NIC’s plants in Woodinville, Wash.

"Heavy truck remains the largest piece of the pie, but we’re increasing our presence in power generation too. We support customers who keep the lights on: smart grid technologies and power distribution. We already had a nice growth trajectory in these areas, but obviously, over the past few years, we’ve seen dramatic increases in demand driven by products that support data center expansion."

That was Troy Wood, NIC Global’s president and CEO, who explained that the fabricator’s customer mix has shaped a complementary manufacturing network across NIC’s footprint. Each plant handles a wide variety of work, and the network is built to flex—jobs are routed to wherever they fit the customer best. The West Coast operations bring particular strength in mechanical assemblies and supply chain integration for medical and energy customers, while the Tennessee, Ohio, and Texas plants are well-suited to heavy truck and power distribution work.

"Our customers look for good sheet metal fabrication, but to be honest, they can get good sheet metal in a lot of places," Wood said. "For us, it’s the complex fabrications and assemblies that really drives our growth."

The operation works to perfect its final assembly to ensure smooth delivery to OEMs’ final assembly lines. "For instance, we sequence critical assemblies to feed truck plants, minimizing inventory and space requirements. And it’s not just about sequencing. We have error-proofing strategies and technologies, including vision systems, that ensure we deliver good parts and subassemblies at exactly the right time. Reliability is everything."

Wood pointed to NIC’s hardware insertion and riveting stations across the assembly areas, where tool-mounted sensor technology validates every fastener as it’s installed. The sensors confirm that each piece of hardware is present, correctly seated, and set to spec, and they tie directly into hardware counting, so quantity and placement are verified in the same step. Vision systems supplement the sensor data at select stations as a second layer of confirmation.

"We’re validating quality as we go," Wood said. "Every fastener, every rivet is confirmed at the station before the part moves on."

A similar strategy is built into one of NIC’s welding cells, where a camera ensures all clamps on the fixture are closed before the operator initiates the program and the carousel rotates the part into the work envelope. "Different process, same approach," Wood said. "Verify before you proceed."

Tying it all together is NIC’s internally built manufacturing execution system, which connects all machines, sensor validations, hardware counts, work instructions, and routing data "into a single source of truth on the floor," Wood said. "Operators see what they need at the moment they need it, and data flows back automatically for traceability and continuous improvement."

"We’ve got talented, dedicated people on our floor," Wood said. "Our job is to make sure they have the right information at the right time so they can do what they do best."

NIC’s model is built for production at scale— the rhythm, repeatability, and integration that OEM assembly lines demand. "Prototyping and low-volume programs are a growing complement to that core," Williams said, explaining that such work "often serves as the front door to longer production relationships with the same customer."

He pointed again to complex assemblies, staged and ready for shipment to a customer. A consistent manufacturing rhythm pervades an operation synchronized with its OEM customer base. Components that went into that assembly flowed through stamping, CNC punch presses, laser cutting, bending, welding, hardware insertion, and final assembly—work that draws on the full range of capabilities NIC’s core customers value.

"We’re at our best when we’re a true extension of our customer’s operation," Wood said. "We’re looking for long-term partners who value the complexity we can take on and who want a manufacturing partner that grows with them."

NO. 16: GENERAL STAMPING & METALWORKS

The South Bend, Ind., company has a new tube laser, punch laser machine, and tube bending machine, along with a new 650-ton press brake, for heavy forming. All this complements its expansive collection of laser cutting machines, press brakes, welding automaton, and more. On the stamping side, which serves as a high-volume complement to its fabrication business, General Stamping & Metalworks has a new transfer press too.

"And we now have more salespeople than at any time in the history of the company."

That was John Marshall, GSM’s president, adding that the organization has stepped up its sales game. In fact, managers see a finely tuned sales strategy as central to GSM’s success, not least because of all the demand for capacity that now exists.

The company plays in a range of sectors, some of the hottest being energy storage and solar. "These can be on-site power storage modules, sheet metal components for cabling assemblies at solar fields or power storage facilities. The work is wide-ranging, and, of course, data centers are driving the need."

GSM continues to serve the heavy truck, lawn and garden, and construction equipment players. "We see a key trend there," Marshall said. "OEMs are looking to maximize their assembly line throughput, rather than invest capital into metal forming equipment inside their buildings."

The trend is shaping GSM’s technology reinvestment and sales strategy. Equipment upgrades at GSM continue, as well as capability expansions: hence the fabricator’s new tube laser and rotary-draw bender. It also continues to use its 5-axis laser for work that needs trimming or hole-cutting after tube bending or trimming stamped parts. (Cutting those geometries before forming would create oval holes during bending or distort features too close to the bend line.)

"Because we have specialized machinery, our customers do not have to send work to different supplier locations to, say, have a 5-axis laser trim some part or cut a hole in a curved component."

Manufacturing capability is one side of the coin, though. The other side of the coin is a good sales process designed to bring in work that fits GSM’s core strengths. And as Marshall explained, managing the sales process becomes more important as revenue grows. As a contract fabricator scales, work that’s not a good fit sends ripples of uncertainty into an already complex, high-product-mix part flow. And that part flow is becoming only more complex as OEMs offload more fabrication and assembly to their supply base.

"We do utilize an AI-enabled CRM [customer relationship management] platform," Marshall said. "There’s no magic to our CRM or even the sales process we developed. Results come from diligent sales leadership and following a proven sales process. You need to know where you’re at with the customer—whether you’re first and goal or somewhere else on the field—to keep from being lost with the start/stop/delays that happen. It’s about having the right people with the right experience. We’ve recruited great people, and we are honing our playbook so that we are more professional and responsive."

GSM has systems in place to absorb demand spikes, the most straightforward being a fiveday schedule. "We plan for a five-day schedule each week, and we intentionally do not plan to run the additional two days," Marshall said. "That represents capacity we can sell into and use as a buffer for a product launch ramp. Last year, there was a period where we ran most weekends (seven days a week) for about four months in a row to help a customer deliver on their commitment."

Scaling a high-product-mix operation isn’t easy. Even so, GSM and others on the FAB 40 are blazing a trail.

NO. 25: PRINCE SERVICE & MFG.

The middle-Georgia contract fabricator has had quite a decade. In the 2010s, the operation— then known as Prince Precision Products— gained a reputation as an aggressive technology adopter, especially for laser cutting. Since then, the owner of Prince sold the company to Eric Williams, the owner of Unified Defense, another contract fabricator in middle Georgia.

"I actually looked up to Prince for years. They were a benchmark for me. When I retired, I wanted my company to be as big as theirs, with all the equipment they had. I realized it took them three generations to get there. I didn’t want to wait that long."

Williams launched Unified Defense in Byron, Ga., in 2016; grew the defense manufacturer; and developed a close relationship with Prince in nearby Macon. When the owner of Prince was looking to retire in 2022, Williams saw an opportunity. "The owner valued the way we worked together, and he was looking for an exit strategy. It really was one of the biggest opportunities of my life."

Today, Prince Service & Mfg. and Unified Defense are under common ownership—Williams serves as CEO of both organizations—but they operate in separate plants and go to market as separate brands. (The revenue reported for the FAB 40 represents Prince Service & Mfg. only.) As Williams explained, the Prince brand assures the customer base that it will remain a diversified contract fabricator.

The Macon plant has expanded significantly in just the past few years, with a renewed focus on automation. Years ago, Prince’s early adoption of CO2 lasers set the operation apart. The company has since upgraded to fiber lasers, of course, but today, just having a fiber laser isn’t much of a differentiator. Prince is now looking to differentiate itself with an aggressive capex strategy that focuses on advanced automation. "Today, we’re try to automate the positions that we don’t want people to make careers out of," Williams said.

Denesting is a prime example. The company has invested in a parts sorting and stacking system that lifts extremely large, heavy parts off the table and stacks them neatly onto pallets. And in the coming months, the fabricator will be pushing aggressively into blank-to-bend automation. Specifically, the plant will have a punch/laser combination machine feeding a panel bender, as well as a laser cutting system that feeds directly to a robotic bending cell.

Williams added that press brake and panel bending operation "are great career paths, but you can only achieve so much throughput manually." Besides, the production work flowing through that automation doesn’t make the most of those talented operators, who can add the most value fabricating lower-quantity part geometries that really can’t be automated effectively.

At the same time, automation really hasn’t diminished the need for skill. "The systems we’re implementing require a lot of upfront preparation and programming," Williams said. "But we’re not a job shop. We have the volumes that suit this machine. In fact, our customers in the ag equipment space as well as the data center space have been ordering very high volumes." He added that, like everyone else, data center work is one of Prince’s hottest markets.

If an order is optimized for automation, it can flow through cutting and bending without anyone touching the product. Downstream, Prince has implemented welding cobot automation, and next year it plans to invest in robotic welding cells designed to handle extremely large components. "We hope for those robots to eliminate several time-consuming steps for some huge parts we make," Williams said.

He added that Prince isn’t planning to eliminate all its manual welding. In fact, the company plans to launch its own welding school later this year.

Those in the welding department won’t be fighting difficult fit-ups. Precision equipment and automation upstream increases consistency downstream. And considering the demand trends related to data centers and beyond, high throughput and quick delivery have never been more important.
