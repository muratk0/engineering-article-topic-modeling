t’s not a typical day in the shop when you get to hear "The Star-Spangled Banner" sung prior to a U.S. senator delivering a speech, but Oct. 2 is no longer an ordinary day in U.S. manufacturing.

That first Friday in October is now reserved for Manufacturing Day

SM

, a day set aside for manufacturers and other organizations that serve the industry to open their doors and show the general public what modern manufacturing is all about and possibly change some people’s perceptions. In this third edition of Manufacturing Day, more than 2,500 organizations sponsored an event. (The Fabricators & Manufacturers Association, the parent organization of

The FABRICATOR

and its sister publications, is a founding producer of Manufacturing Day.)

On Oct. 2 I was in a hotbed of fabrication action known as Waukesha, Wis., visiting Miro Tool & Mfg. Inc. U.S. Sen. Ron Johnson, R-Wis., made a stop to tour the facility and speak to the company’s employees as part of a full day visiting manufacturers in the Milwaukee area.

Sen. Ron Johnson, R-Wis., speaks to Miro Tool & Mfg. Inc. employees in Waukesha, Wis., on Oct. 2.

Following an opening prayer and a stirring rendition of the national anthem by Jenni Zielke, Miro Tool’s human resources manager, Johnson addressed the company’s management and staff. His remarks weren’t focused on trying to entice young people to consider a career in manufacturing, as they were at an earlier stop, but instead focused on the importance of small businesses, just like Miro Tool.

"These are great jobs working for great people," Johnson said. "People can make a good living and support a family."

The politician speaks the truth. The Bureau of Labor Statistics reports that the average weekly manufacturing wage, as of Sept. 15, was $833.66, which is almost twice that of the average retail wage of $448.20. That’s a good wage for folks who don’t have a college degree and don’t want to sit behind a desk all day.

In fact, small manufacturers comprise the manufacturing backbone of this country. The Manufacturing Extension Partnership, a federal agency within the National Institute of Standards and Technology charged with assisting these small manufacturing businesses in improving their operations, believes that 99 percent of all manufacturing businesses are small companies. They also account for 8.2 million jobs, or 73 percent of all manufacturing employment.

Miro Tool is a perfect example of why these small manufacturers are so important to supply chains. Jeff Brown founded the business in 1988 as a tool and die shop that also worked on fixtures and gauges for other nearby manufacturers. By 2000 Brown realized that he could escape the cyclical nature of toolmaking by taking on production work. That’s when he added the company’s first laser cutting machine and started investing in stamping presses.

Today the shop is in a position to help customers with orders of almost any size. It can build hard tooling for runs of 50,000 parts, or it can laser-cut 100 parts for a hot job. For something in between, it has even developed low-cost tooling that involves hand-transferring a part from one station to the next.

"Our willingness to change has brought us to a point where we can grow with our customers," Brown said.

Miro Tool has 42 employees and offers fabricating, forming, and machining services. In March 2015 it upgraded its laser cutting capability with a new Mitsubishi ML3015 eX laser cutting machine attached to a nine-shelf material tower. The company is looking to work its way back to reach the sales levels of 2012, a record-setting year, after a major government contract ended in 2013. The business is doing well.

Miro Tool is an example of a manufacturing operation that should be the face of Manufacturing Day. The owners walk the floor of their business every day and know the employees by name. When they talk of family, they typically mean it because a couple generations of one family probably works for the company. These small-business owners see their companies not solely as cash-generating entities, but as important members of the local community and the larger manufacturing economy.

It should come as no surprise that only days before Johnson shared his admiration of Miro Tool and small companies like it, Caterpillar announced 10,000 layoffs. Unfortunately, that type of news from large manufacturing companies is common. If anything, the Great Recession reminded small manufacturers that they should keep their key personnel. Layoffs are a last resort, not a reaction to poor sales in the last quarter.

Small manufacturers make America better. It shouldn’t take a day of recognition for others to notice that fact.
