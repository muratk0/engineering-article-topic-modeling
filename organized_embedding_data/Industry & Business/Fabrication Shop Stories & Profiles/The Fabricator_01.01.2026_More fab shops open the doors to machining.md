achining and sheet metal fabrication are two distinct disciplines. That’s why machine and fab shops exist, each dedicated to metalworking processes that are very different from each other.

While that still exists for the most part, more metal fabricating companies are expanding their machining activities. These businesses aren’t interested in becoming machine shops, but they do want to remain valuable suppliers of metalworking services to their customers. So when the current customer asks if a fab shop can handle machined parts or take on more machining work, the metal fabricator usually says "yes."

The Fabricator talked to two metal fabricators about their newfound commitment to machining and what they’ve learned from their experiences.

GROWING WITH OPPORTUNITIES

Ludlow Manufacturing Inc. (LMI), Waukegan, Ill., has been machining parts for customers since 2013. LMI offered that service to support random requests from customers for machined parts; it was hardly a strategic part of the company’s growth plan.

Three years ago, that changed dramatically. As domestic manufacturing came roaring back after the pandemic, LMI started receiving more requests for machined parts, a majority of them attached to orders for weldments.

"I’m a salesman at heart, so it’s hard for me to say ‘no,’" said Todd Ludlow, the company’s founder and CEO.

With the increase in business, LMI also recognized the need to control manufacturing activity. Relying on subcontractors meant potential delays in part delivery and possible quality issues.

In the last three years, LMI has committed to a robust investment in capital equipment, more than doubling its number of machining centers. Today, it has 20 machines—eight vertical machining centers, six horizontal machining centers, two 5-axis Swiss machines, and four lathes.

Probably the most important change, however, was the purchase of a machine shop two years ago. Only one machine was brought over to LMI’s Waukegan facility.

"I got a guy who is a programmer, a setup guy, and an all-around machinist," Ludlow said.

Having someone with that machining experience allowed LMI to pursue its expansion of machining activities with confidence. No one was under pressure to learn how to program a new machine, much less learn a whole new metalworking discipline. A skilled machinist leading the department kept manufacturing rolling even as new machines were added and machine operators had to be educated on how to run them.

"Tapping and drilling—anyone can do that work," Ludlow said. "High-end machining, like what we’re doing for our weldments, that’s where you need the expertise."

LMI’s machining department currently has seven people working during the day and five at night. That could increase soon as machining has emerged as a key factor in growing the company.

"Without machining, we would not have grown like we have," Ludlow said. "We would’ve grown, but it probably would have been about a 10% increase instead of 35% [in 2025] and 25% to 30% [in 2026]."

Ludlow added that he is more focused on LMI’s welding department right now. Because he can’t find welders, Ludlow has invested in six robotic welding cells over the last two years, and he’s interested in adding two more.

A welding department that is able to process more parts likely means more business for LMI’s machining department because that’s the business Ludlow wants. The company makes a lot more money on weldments that require machined parts than on simple orders of machined piece parts.

As for the future, Ludlow said his interest in new fabricating technology has him thinking what might be possible with a key investment, such as a large horizontal mill. That’s not going to happen any time soon, but it’s an example of how one metal fabricator is thinking more about machining than he has done in the past.

MAKING MACHINING WORK

Scott Hartrick, president, Dynamic Metal Innovations (DMI), Emmaus, Pa., went to an ERP software users’ conference in 2024 and started chatting with an East Coast fab shop owner. At that point, DMI was thinking about jumping back into machining after doing it for a short time from 2019 to 2021, during the early days of the company’s existence. Hartrick’s fellow conference attendee had very strong feelings about a fab shop undertaking machining activities.

"I remember him saying, ‘Don’t ever start a machine shop. Go out and buy one, because it’s so much easier,’" Hartrick recalled. "He admitted that it’s easier said than done, with an obvious need for capital, but he also said that he had tried to start one in his shop, and he just shut it down. It was just too much."

By that time, however, DMI was already getting ready to get back into machining to satisfy ever-increasing customer demands. Aerospace customers wanted weeklong lead times at the very most, and that was too tight for DMI’s machining subcontractors, which delivered outsourced work with two- to three-week lead times.

So in 2025, as DMI moved into a new facility, it jumped back into machining with the purchase of a new 3-axis vertical machining center. DMI also hired a machinist to run the equipment.

This was a leap of faith for Hartrick because, as a self-described "sheet metal guy," he didn’t have a deep knowledge about machining. In the past, if someone had a question about a sheet metal part design or something related to the laser cutting machine or welding equipment, he could offer up some assistance.

The new endeavor got off to a "rocky" start, according to Hartrick. The person who handled programming for the sheet metal machines also knew a bit about programming a CNC machining center, but he and the machinist weren’t always on the same page when it came to program and tooling choice. Additionally, no one was really overseeing the machining activity, which led to some miscommunication issues.

"By this time, we were like, ‘Let’s get a guy in here,’ and we really hit the jackpot with a machine shop supervisor we hired," Hartrick said. "He’s a really passionate guy, and he’s even got a 5-axis and horizontal [machining center] in his own shop."

The new hire had been working in his own family’s machine shop but wanted to try something new. Now, he does all of the machine programming and is helping to grow the machining side of DMI’s business.

DMI also has expanded its equipment lineup. It now has a twin-spindle horizontal turning center, a 3-axis bed mill, and a knee mill with a retrofitted 2-axis CNC on it. At its Brownsville, Texas, facility, DMI has a 3-axis bed mill as well. It plans to add a slightly smaller version of its 3-axis vertical machining center to the Texas facility and a 5-axis mill to the Pennsylvania shop in the coming months.

"Since we’ve had our supervisor come on board, it’s been very reassuring. We now have the confidence that we can move forward and grow this department," Hartrick said.

DMI’s aerospace customers like the parts being provided to them. In fact, they are willing to throw more machining work to the shop when it gets more capacity, according to Hartrick. It’s just a matter of figuring out what equipment makes the most sense and where it’s going to be located.

In the meantime, Hartrick said that despite the early bumps in the road, the journey to adding machining capabilities has been a positive experience for DMI.

"Machining will make any sheet metal shop better because it does force you down the path of better inspection equipment and better processes," he said.

As of late 2024, DMI is now ISO 9001- and AS9100-certified. It also has a coordinate measuring machine, a linear height gauge to help it with the high tolerances that come with aerospace customers, and a new optical scanner that can collect part features and compare them to a PDF in a matter of minutes. The latter is expected to help speed up first-article inspection by more than 75%.

"When we’re talking to prospective customers that we want to work with and they hear that we have this type of measuring equipment, it does create a sense of confidence in the customers that we can do this work," Hartrick said.

In the coming months, DMI is going to try and grow its machining side of the business, which currently accounts for about 20% of the company’s overall revenues. They don’t have the real estate to add more machines in Pennsylvania, so Hartrick said he’s hoping they can add a second or even a third shift to add more capacity.

Hartrick hasn’t learned all there is to know about machining, but his early suspicions about the complexity of running a machining business have turned out to be true.

"High-mix [manufacturing] is not too bad in sheet metal. I’m learning that it’s a lot more effort to do high-mix machining," he said. "You can put 100 unique parts on a laser nest in 10 minutes, but it’s a lot harder to program 100 unique machined parts. It’s very different."
