I

t’s an expensive lesson to learn for some metal fabricators, but it’s inevitable: Automation doesn’t make up for poor processes.

If anything, automation just aggravates a situation because the velocity of production increases. Instead of a couple of parts that need rework or scrapping because of poor instructions on a router, a metal fabricator could be looking at a bin full of parts with the wrong sized flange or lacking laser-cut holes. And that’s probably not a one-time headache. If the root cause of the problem hasn’t been corrected, the headache is going to come back on a regular basis.

Counterpart Inc., a metal fabricator in Brookings, S.D., has invested heavily in automation since 2018, when it installed an automated storage and retrieval system with 200 shelves for raw sheet material or even fabricated parts. Connected to that material storage system are advanced punching and laser cutting machines, each with parts sorting and organizing capabilities. The company also has three robotic weld cells.

Of course, this automation works well only when the job instructions are correct. Even the most advanced machinery isn’t going to stop a job to ask, "Are you sure this is what the customer wants?" Automation can do a lot, but it needs human intervention to get the most out of it.

"What Counterpart and any other third-party manufacturer knows is that the difficult part isn’t producing the parts; it’s making sure you’re producing the parts to the customer specification," said Adam Longtin, a senior manufacturing engineer with the metal fabricating company.

For 10 years, Counterpart has been using its new-part process to ensure that customers are getting the parts they specified on the purchase order. The process brings together representatives from engineering, quality, operations, and scheduling. They all get to see the request for quote and chime in with questions and suggestions.

What’s the design intent? What sort of tolerance needs to be held at the press brake? Are all the necessary weld symbols covered? Can the part be assembled in the most logical way, or is another approach needed? All of these questions and more are shared during this meeting of the fabricating experts. Everyone has the chance to have their voice heard.

"We can put our heads together so that we can get these questions out if there are any," Longtin said.

At this time, a router is created. This signifies that the drawing has been approved and material reviewed. Machine programs are created. Quality creates a method to ensure job specifications are met.

Once the router is approved, the work order is ready to go. The shop floor should have a plan for successful production of parts.

It sounds like a simple approach, but that doesn’t make it commonplace. Too many companies still have siloes where engineering doesn’t actively communicate with the shop floor and the shop floor doesn’t feel empowered to offer up observations that can make a difference.

Longtin said that collaboration has been one of the big benefits of this part onboarding process. Everyone’s input is encouraged, which shows people how valuable their knowledge is. In some instances, some of those shop floor experts, like those responsible for offline press brake programming, have become key parts of the front office team.

This cross-functional team meeting also has paved the way for successful implementation of automation. When Counterpart added a new punching machine in 2024, it had a lot to consider because the technology had changed quite a bit since the last time the metal fabricator had purchased such a machine. Longtin had conversations with supervisors and machine operators, all of whom were more than willing to share their perspectives.

"What do we think about a tool changer? How many tools do we need the machine to hold? What do we think about skeleton-free processing?" Longtin asked his shop floor colleagues.

"You get to talk about the potential benefits directly with them, and it’s fun because then they’ll have the cons as well," he added.

With all the talk of automation, does it make sense to automate the new-part process as well? That currently is a big discussion item for the company.

Longtin compares it to using Amazon. Some people just want to go to a website, place an order, and wait for the package to arrive. That’s not the most ideal basis for a metal fab company to establish a relationship with a customer, but those are the expectations for some.

"Anything we can do to shorten that lead time for you but still capture the same level of completeness [of job information] is something we’re interested in. We’re actively working on that now," he said.

People make the process work. They’ll make the automation upgrade work as well.
