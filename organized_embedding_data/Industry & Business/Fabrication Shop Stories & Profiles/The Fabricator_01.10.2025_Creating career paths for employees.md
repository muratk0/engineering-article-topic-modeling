# Creating career paths for employees

[TARİH: 01.10.2025 The Fabricator]

Shop Management

How Special Products & Mfg. builds its culture of learning

By

Dan Davis

Employees of Special Products & Mfg. are routinely reminded of the company’s core values. One in particular—learning—has played an important part in the metal fabricating company being able to scale as it takes on new customers and work.

T

anya Lance, whose parents both worked in Texas manufacturing, followed a familiar path when she joined a manufacturer of network infrastructure equipment after earning her mechanical engineering degree. What she didn’t anticipate was becoming part of a training model that would transform her career trajectory—taking her from engineering into leadership roles such as human resources manager and ultimately to her current position as materials manager.

That unusual career path occurred at Special Products & Mfg. in Rockwall, Texas, and it’s not unique to Lance. Nora Harding, SPM’s executive vice president, considers it a key part of developing "leaders at every level," which she sees as incredibly important to the company’s ability to attract and retain talent, as well as sustain growth and success. With that type of preparedness, people can assume new roles when needed and be nimble in reacting to changing customer demands or market dynamics.

In Lance, Harding saw a person with great problem-solving skills and an ability to create processes to support initiatives and collaborate with others to sustain those efforts. What she wanted next for Lance’s development was experience in assessing people—being able to discern where a person fit in the metal fabricating organization not only now but down the road. She wanted Lance to learn how training and mentoring could help shape people for roles for which they had no idea they might be a fit.

"Tanya said, ‘There’s no way I’m going to be an HR manager,’" Harding recalled. "I saw it in her. I just had to provide the opportunities for her to grow into it. She needed that to go to the next level."

Lance said she’s grateful for the experience. "I’ve been able to build both my family and my career while continuing to grow and develop, thanks to our SPM philosophy of ‘preparing the team of tomorrow,’" she said.

Laying the Foundation

Harding started her own career at SPM as a human resources manager, arriving in 2011 after serving in similar roles for other companies. She played an important role in the ensuing years in breaking down silos, democratizing decision-making, and boosting training for the metal fabricating company. (The story of SPM’s transformation, which culminated with the company winning The Fabricator’s 2025 Industry Award, can be read in "Leaders at every level," The Fabricator, February 2025, p. 28.)

Her goal during this time was to prepare the company so it could grow, while simultaneously maintaining expertise and processes that would make SPM a suitable supply chain partner for metal fabricating services. She was charged with preparing the workforce to be the "team of tomorrow."

"What we did was really focus on culture first and create one that embraced training," Harding said.

In her previous roles before SPM, she witnessed how coaching and training helped people develop leadership skills. During that time, she also recognized that people have to be open to expanding their skill set.

"You can have people who are here just to come to work and go home—and those team members are needed for steady operations," Harding said. "But when we started to change the culture to focus on training, people were like, ‘Whoa! They’re asking more of us. I want to do more.’

"As a result, SPM saw many team members step forward to receive the training, and the result has been a flexible workforce."

A Plan for Everyone

Every person hired at SPM receives a training program. As part of that program, the new hires learn their job responsibilities, their expectations, and the skills necessary to do that job.

Lance said that finding the right trainers to assist new hires helps to increase the likelihood of positive skills development. What might be surprising to an outsider, however, is that these trainers aren’t necessarily the technical experts in their departments. Successful trainers might not know all there is to know about a subject, but their strength is in the soft skills—coaching, communicating, collaborating, and teaching. This fosters a safe learning environment where knowledge is shared and applied by the trainer and trainee.

"We had success in our technical training with our welders, grinders, and press brake operators," Lance said. "We’ve been able to take team members that come in with little or maybe no direct experience in those departments, partner them up with a trainer, and train them—providing them a road map for future development."

If all is going well after six months, the new hire and the supervisor discuss what’s next, finding out what might be of interest to the new hire or where he or she might be a good fit. Harding said the latter is important because supervisors need to be thinking about an individual’s next job—what skills can help an employee grow while also making them more valuable contributors to SPM’s success.

"The reason it’s been successful is because we’ve picked the right people to do the training," Harding said. "At first, we chose technical experts, but we realized they weren’t always the right fit. We had to change our strategy."

As an example of how such career planning can make a difference in employee turnover and overall morale, Harding shared a story about SPM’s grinding department. It’s often an overlooked part of fabrication, but it’s necessary in many instances, as customers look for parts to be deburred and edge-rounded. SPM had two longtime employees in the department, but they were joined by what seemed like a never-ending carousel of new employees.

Harding said that she and the plant managers talked with the veteran grinders and identified the skills that the grinders needed to separate themselves from those new to the job. They came up with three classifications for grinders: beginner, intermediate, and expert. Anyone classified as a beginner needs a shadow as they begin their work. Intermediates can work on more complex jobs, and experts are those who also can be trainers. As employees move along the job path, they get commiserate pay increases.

"There used to be constant turnover in that department, but now everyone is well trained. Job satisfaction is now increasing in that area," Harding said.

But it doesn’t have to end there for the grinders, she added. For example, after touching up welds over the years, perhaps one of the grinders might want to develop welding skills.

"Can you imagine how great a grinder could be as a welder?" Harding asked. "They see bad welds, and they have to clean them up. They know exactly what a poor-quality weld looks like."

A Constant Focus on Skills Development

It’s one thing for an organization to talk about having a culture that prizes skills development, but how does SPM remain focused on the goal? Harding said that’s part of her job as leader of the organization, stressing the importance of having everyone ready to step up and fill new roles when they become available.

"It’s all part of succession planning. Each leader has one," Harding said. "They’re responsible for making sure the people in their plan get the right opportunities, projects, and training to support their career path."

When conversations occur between Harding and her leadership team, these are not just discussions about those in the room. They also are exploring the skill sets of those who report to the leaders.

Lance said that leaders learn to communicate with their team, so that they can understand what motivates them. That helps to put them on the right career path and increases the odds they stay focused on their development. Sometimes a person isn’t quite sure what makes sense because they can’t see his or her own potential.

"We’ve had great coaching on how to talk with team members, how to delegate to them, and how to get to know what drives them," she said. "Some team members love new opportunities. Some like praise and recognition. Some like knowing that they are the ‘go-to’ people."

Harding shared the story of how SPM is installing a new 12-kW fiber laser cutting machine in its machine shop facility in Fate, Texas, just down the road from its Rockwall headquarters. The normal facility conversations took place as the installation team reviewed all the items required for the transition. However, SPM leadership took it one step further and reviewed the change management required from a team perspective: What did the presence of the laser cutting machine mean to the team in Fate, and how could the leadership team get them excited about the new opportunity?

Those early discussions, which included the SPM machine shop team, created an environment where they were not only excited for the installation but also to learn how to run and program the new equipment, according to Harding. With the machine shop team knowing what to expect and buying into the change, SPM can expect quality results and a faster timetable to operation because the whole team wants to see it successfully implemented.

The Payoff

The SPM leadership team comprises nine individuals from different areas of the company. Harding stated that with them being committed to developing and keeping tabs on the career paths and skills development of their direct reports, the leadership team is effectively "multiplying" themselves. Expertise is being developed at all levels of the organization.

That leads to nimble and effective decision-making. Issues that might fester because no one really addressed them are resolved quickly. People can step into roles to fill gaps where needed. Communication and the implementation of proven work processes keep surprises to a minimum.

"Everyone says they need more people," Harding said. "But I tell them, ‘Show me how you’re using the technology, how you’re leveraging the systems in place, and how productive your team really is. In most cases, productivity issues can be solved if you look at your training.’"

Introducing fabricating technology to the shop floor is an exercise in avoiding surprises at Special Products & Mfg. The leadership team works diligently to prepare potential machine operators, supervisors, and other members of the shop floor team about what the new machine tool means to the company and what sort of impact it’s expected to have.
