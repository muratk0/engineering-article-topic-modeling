# A united front for metal fabricating?

[TARİH: 01.02.2025 The Fabricator]

Editor’s Corner

Economic realities get in the way of small and medium-sized shops coming together to create vibrant manufacturing partnerships

DAN DAVIS

T

his month’s feature "Leaders at every level," highlighting Special Products & Mfg. (SPM), The Fabricator’s 2025 Industry Award winner, provides you an idea of just how the metal fabricator has gone about creating a culture where employees are empowered and actively contribute to the company’s growth plans. Leading the charge has been Nora Harding, who first joined the company as human resources manager in the fall of 2011.

Harding has spent most of her time since being named the company’s director of operations in 2018 and then executive vice president in 2020 focused on internal matters, ensuring the right people are in the right roles and working to improve communication among all levels in the organization. She admittedly hadn’t spent a large amount of time outside the walls of the company’s Rockwall, Texas, headquarters, but she aimed to change that.

Of course, one of her first stops was a FABTECH in Chicago. What could be a better place for someone to learn about the latest in metal fabricating technology?

After a long day at the tradeshow, she decided to step out of her comfort zone and head down to the hotel bar, where the real scoop on industry trends and gossip could be heard. The conversations were so fruitful with the metal fabricators that she met—all men, by the way—that she repeated her post-show plan.

"I went every night to that bar at FABTECH, and I’m so glad I did," she recalled. "I met so many cool people who were doing so many innovative things."

She talked with her peers about machine tools, manufacturing concepts, and even books. What they really didn’t get to explore was possibly visiting each other’s shops. That’s where the newfound friends felt they had to draw the line.

The concern isn’t unusual. Most metal fabricators are concerned about who might be walking their shop floors, particularly if they are a local competitor. The last thing a metal fabricator wants is an interloper walking past a pallet of metal parts, catching a customer name, and then calling the customer, soliciting them for metal fabricating services.

But in the minds of those who feel like there is more than enough work to go around and who see a benefit from learning and working with others—even those down the street—they don’t understand such close-mindedness. United we stand, divided we fall, right?

"Shared best practice knowledge is powerful," Harding said. "We have many competing factors that are making an impact on our industry in the U.S. Let’s focus on them together and fix them because we want this work to stay in the U.S."

This country finds itself in an interesting position because societal change shows no signs of slowing down. With that change comes ample opportunities for metal fabricators.

Consider the proliferation of the computing sector, where data farms are needed to support not only the current digital economy but also future artificial intelligence applications and data mining to support cryptocurrencies. Then there’s the advancements needed in energy generation to support those data farms, along with modernization of the energy grid to quench that future thirst for power.

Large companies recognize this. That’s why they are on purchasing sprees, gobbling up metal fabricating and machine shops. They know that increased manufacturing capacity gives them the opportunity to serve industries starving for fabricating services—and make handsome profits at the same time.

Meanwhile, small and medium-sized fab companies might have trouble competing for those same jobs because of the scope of the work. A united front with other similarly sized shops, however, can mimic the offerings of a larger company. But for that to happen, a certain amount of trust among the partnering companies has to exist, and if one is afraid to open its doors to the others, that trust is hard to build.

Harding added that close relationships with other fabricators, particularly those nearby, offer other benefits, such as subcontracting opportunities. Also, local manufacturers coming together to support and influence area vocational education programs is always a positive.

SPM is not alone. Harding said that she has struck up a relationship with an Ohio metal fabricator, who has visited her shop, and she plans to visit his in the near future. The benchmarking of operations has proven helpful in determining where SPM stands out and where opportunities for improvement might exist. Even simply having someone to chat with about the fab business has been beneficial, according to Harding.

SPM has discovered that there’s power in collaboration—both on the shop floor and with other metal fabricators. Others would do well to follow its lead.

If you are looking to meet and chat with other metal fabricators, think about attending the Fabricators and Manufacturers Association (FMA) Annual Meeting in Chandler, Ariz., Feb. 25-27. It’s the perfect opportunity to meet other shop owners and managers of metal fabricating companies in a casual setting, while simultaneously learning more about the business trends and technological changes affecting the industry.

If you can’t make the trip, also consider joining an FMA Users’ Group, a small collection of fabricating companies that get together on a regular basis to tour each other’s facilities and learn from each other. Visit

www.fmamfg.org/get-involved/users-groups

for more details.
