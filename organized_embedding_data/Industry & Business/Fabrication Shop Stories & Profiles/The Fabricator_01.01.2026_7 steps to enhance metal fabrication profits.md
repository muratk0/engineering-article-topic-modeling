# 7 steps to enhance metal fabrication profits

any in custom and contract metal fabrication evolve in a similar way. They launch as a job shop, initially focusing on building relationships and growing with as many customers as they can. The customer base grows as the fabricator’s reputation builds.

Over time, though, not every customer relationship remains a fit. The fabricator ends up bending over backward to meet customer demands. Operators tear down and set up to handle expedited orders; engineers manage the umpteenth design revision; shop culture goes downhill. Leadership sees this happening and knows some changes need to be made.

But what kind of changes? It may be time to reassess the customer base but not before a fabricator has real insight into what jobs are

truly

profitable, as well as which customers and what kind of work fit into an operation’s long-term growth strategy. That was the message from Jake Rohrer, manager at Wipfli Advisory LLC in Milwaukee, during a presentation at FABTECH 2025, held in early September in Chicago.

"If we don’t understand profitability, how can we expect to make good investment decisions? How can we coach others in the organization if the most foundational piece of our business is misunderstood? That’s why we need to show and visualize profitability by various dimensions. These can involve the type of parts being processed, the material type, the customer, work center, plant location, and more."

Rohrer’s insights can be grouped into seven steps that help guide a fabricator toward a greater understanding about profits. In essence, identifying

true

costs and

actual margins

can show where an organization needs to improve and, not least, help guide it toward the kind of work that drives success.

1. ALIGN INCENTIVES

The industry focuses on asset utilization for a good reason: Machines are expensive, and if they’re not producing, they’re not giving their expected return on investment. Thing is, customers don’t pay for stellar machine uptime; they pay for shipped orders. If, say, operators and department supervisors are incentivized maximize machine uptime, they might produce the largest batches of products they can to minimize job changeovers—even though doing so creates excess work-in-process and prolongs overall lead time.

As Rohrer explained, "When working with those key performance indicators, we need to make the best decisions as a team while still supporting the global metric."

He added that this can vary depending on a fabricator’s product mix and customer demand trends. Local efficiency metrics shouldn’t run counter to overall efficiency and throughput. Put another way, metrics need to be aligned so that everyone rows in the same direction.

2. FOCUS ON WHAT FABRICATORS CAN CONTROL

As Rohrer described, a fabricator looking to improve profitability shouldn’t focus on "outside elements it can’t control. And in job shop environments, they can’t control material costs, and they can’t always control outside processing costs," especially if an OEM requires a shop to use certain approved vendors. "Instead, we want to focus on the cost of value-adding activities."

This includes the time while machines are producing and welding arcs are welding. It also includes how quickly a work center can adapt to changes in demand.

Imagine a fabricator with documented setup procedures, organized tooling, and the ability to change over to another job within minutes. Technicians program and simulate offline as much as they can. All job information is updated continually, with well-established revision controls. And when processing orders, the shop ensures every question at every station along the job routing is answered. No one should be running to the front office for clarification.

3. STREAMLINE AND AUTOMATE THE DATA COLLECTION

Any improvement needs to be grounded in reality— what’s really happening, from estimating and order processing through the entire job routing on the floor.

"People need to trust what’s being recorded on the floor," Rohrer said. "If you retroactively record your labor time against your work orders at the end of the day, you know you might have errors or misattributed time. That could be detrimental to the way you understand the true amount of labor that’s going into producing an order. That’s why tools like IIoT [Industrial Internet of Things] platforms are so valuable."

Basic observation and training in continuous improvement come first, of course. Setup procedures should be standardized and documented across all shifts, tools should be organized, and no one should be spending their shifts wandering and searching for tools and materials.

Still, a custom fabricator could have dozens or even hundreds of different orders flowing through the shop at a given time. In this environment, waste can remain hidden. Say a press brake job requires an operator to walk to the other side of the plant for a special tool. The job doesn’t come up regularly, so people don’t think to question the clock-in and clock-out data from the ERP. They just assume the job requires more time.

An IIoT platform, though, tracks when the press brake ram is

actually stroking

. Correlate that to clock-in and clock-out information from the ERP, and software uncovers a lot of unexpected machine downtime. Move the tool closer to the point of use, and the job can be done a lot faster and cost a lot less.

"Moreover, IIoT dashboards can show workcells with the most unplanned downtime, and show, say, the top three reasons why certain work centers are down," Rohrer said. "Is it an operator skill issue? A scheduling issue? Absenteeism? This helps us target specific areas."

Any profitability analysis starts with understanding the true cost of value-adding activities. This requires robust data collection and a focus on flexibility. Yes, a challenging customer might require the shop to tear down a setup, but how long should job changeovers

really

take? Quick changeover can mitigate those ripple effects of an unexpected schedule change—and make a seemingly challenging customer perhaps not so challenging after all.

4. PUT OVERTIME IN CONTEXT

Rohrer added that understanding true costs also requires putting overtime in the right context. A shop floor might have extraordinary throughput, but people need to work overtime to achieve it. "And, of course, we need to pay more for labor that is working overtime."

This, he said, is why any cost analysis needs to look at value-adding dollars as they relate to full-time equivalents (FTEs). FTEs convert all employee-paid time—money paid for temps, full time, part time, and overtime—into a single full-time number, creating an apples-to-apples comparison for job costs.

5. FOCUS ON THE VALUE CREATED

The sales-per-employee metric in metal fabrication can be useful, but it can also be deceiving.

Margins take center stage here—not just margin percentage, but also the margin dollar amount. The more volume a job has, the lower its margin percentage usually is, and the more margin dollars it usually produces. A low-profit customer can still be extremely valuable if it produces a lot of margin dollars for the fabricator.

Some operations can seem to squeeze a lot of revenue out of every employee, but in truth, the shop processes a lot of high-cost material and might rely heavily on outside service providers and component suppliers.

This, Rohrer said, is why it’s important to focus on the

value-adding activity

the operation provides. "We try to look at value-adding dollars over full-time-equivalent employees."

Put another way, for every dollar a shop spends on payroll, how many dollars does a fabricator get in return? This removes material and outside processing costs which, again, are often outside of the fabricator’s control.

"We also consider overhead as well as direct labor into that calculation, so we can really understand the costs," Rohrer said.

This can involve scrutinizing how an organization assigns specific costs. Direct and indirect labor play key roles, of course, but so do fixed overhead costs (there whether the facility is running or not) and variable overhead (like gas and other consumables for welding or laser cutting, and other elements that change with increases or decreases in production). Put simply, accounting maneuvers shouldn’t hide true costs of a certain job.

"We don’t want to just peanut-butter-spread the overhead," he said. "We want to itemize the overhead accounts based on the relevant cost drivers."

6. KNOW THE ESTIMATED AND ACTUAL MARGINS

If a fabricator knows its true costs and allocates them appropriately to each job, it also knows its true margin. Of course, predicting costs perfectly isn’t realistic.

"In our experience, we typically see between a 12% and 15% difference between what’s reported on the income statement and the estimated margin," Rohrer said. "If we see just a 3% difference, that’s world class. If the difference is more than 20%, that’s rough."

Uncovering the source of the discrepancy can involve scrutinizing estimating practices, material pricing, cost allocation methods in accounting (including overhead), and OEE (overall equipment effectiveness) production monitoring.

"We’ve seen a lot of cost complexities arise with tariffs and supply chain issues," Rohrer explained, adding that supply chain uncertainty might mean a fabricator needs to carry more material or component inventory to meet customer demand. Material price hikes need to be accounted for too. Not passing all those costs on to the customer will, of course, affect margins.

The closer estimates can be to actual costs and expected margins, the better. If estimates are wrong, "it’s better to be consistently wrong," Rohrer said. "If everything is consistently overstated or understated, it’s still directionally and proportionally correct. You can still make decisions on that information."

If the difference between expected and actual margins changes unpredictably, that’s a red flag. When this happens, fabricators need to dive into the data to uncover the root causes behind those variances.

7. CONDUCT A PRODUCT MIX ANALYSIS

This is where the rubber hits the road. The typical product-quantity analysis plots quantities on the Y axis and customer orders (products) on the X axis. In a typical mix, a handful of large customers comprises about 80% of the sales, while the remaining 20% provide the "long tail" of low-quantity orders.

Margins take center stage here—not just

margin percentage

, but also the

margin dollar

amount. The more volume a job has, the lower its margin percentage usually is, and the more margin dollars it usually produces. A low-profit customer can still be extremely valuable if it produces a lot of margin dollars for the fabricator.

"Those low-quantity orders need to be highly profitable [with a high gross margin percentage]," Rohrer said, "or they need to be considered what we call a ‘grow account’ for strategic purposes."

All this sets an objective framework for a sales team to shape and grow the customer mix. The analysis also prevents the fabricator from parting ways with customers that might be demanding, but still are profitable and a good fit for the shop’s overarching growth strategy.

At times, though, paring down a customer base can make sense. "The sales team might feel sentimental toward certain accounts," Rohrer said. "They might enjoy working with a certain customer. But administratively, they might be difficult to manage. In fact, the sales team might be misallocating their time to the wrong sources."

A long tail of low-quantity work can throw a wrench into part flow, especially in the office. As Rohrer explained, for one recent Wipfli client, a custom metal fabricator with $65 million in revenue, just 78 customers made up 90% of the value-adding dollars the company earned—out of a total customer base of 723.

In this case, it might be impossible to service them well all at once. Even so, Rohrer explained that the way to manage this situation depends on the shop’s goals and overarching business strategy.

Regardless, the decision to cut ties with a customer should be made based on objective truths. Relationships matter, but so do healthy margins. After all, it’s difficult to maintain healthy business relationships when the business itself fails.
