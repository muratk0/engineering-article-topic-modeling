# How to target new customers for metal fabricating services

[TARİH: 01.11.2024 The Fabricator]

Shop Management

A hypothetical case study provides a game plan for a shop looking to diversify its customer base

By

Dan Davis

Metal fabricators looking to target a new market in a bid for customer diversification can start their intelligence-gathering efforts by targeting and learning from a lead customer, a company that is a leader and is known for its operational excellence. Since these types of organizations are always looking out for new ideas and successful partners, they more than likely would welcome the chance to learn more about a metal fabricating company pitching their services.

H

ugh G. Bends Inc., a family-owned architectural metal fabricator, has been manufacturing and installing metal components for commercial construction projects for more than 25 years. A recent purchase of a laser tube cutting machine, however, has it rethinking the overall business.

The laser was purchased to help with cutting tube parts and to shorten overall lead times. Placing tube in a rigged-up fixture for a 2D laser cutting machine to do the cutting wasn’t necessarily meant to be a long-term solution, particularly as jobs required more laser-cut parts. That’s when the purchase of the new 6-kW fiber laser was made.

But with the new cutting capacity, company owners realized that the machine was not going to be operating a full 24 hours. In fact, a second shift could be added to cut parts for new customers requiring laser-cut tube parts, and if things really took off, automation to handle material loading and unloading could be added for lights-out operation.

Bends had an idea to chase new customers, but how would it go about doing so?

Don’t Be Intoxicated by Market Size

Mark Coronna is founder of The Practical CMO, a company that offers marketing expertise in the form of consultation or even as a fractional executive. He has worked with clients in fields like banking, logistics, and metal fabricating, and many of those assignments were related to finding new customers in a bid to grow sales.

He began by saying that a company like Bends is in a good position because it has a customer to subsidize a new capital equipment investment like a laser tube cutting machine. He is not a fan of a metal fabricating company investing in new equipment without a plan to attract customers.

For Bends, he recommended starting with two questions:

Is the market big enough?

Is the market good for the company?

"A market that’s valued at $2 billion is noticeable, but that doesn’t really help you. It might draw your attention, but honestly, the lure of big numbers can get businesses off track," Coronna said.

"The second question though, that’s a much more important question. That’s the one that takes a lot more work to address."

This really gets into whittling down the forest to just a few trees. Bends, for example, thought that sporting and athletic goods (NAICS Code 339920) might be a good target for customers, but in reality discovered that the focus should be on exercise equipment manufacturers, which required tubing for machinery supports. Additionally, exercise equipment continues to be in demand as the younger generation, who ate apple slices instead of McDonald’s French fries after soccer games, grows older.

What’s the Best Way to Learn the Market?

Bends has found the exercise equipment market to be worthy of its focus. After all, it’s a $100 billion market now, and according to Fortune Business Insights, it’s expected to grow to $202 billion by 2030. What’s the next step?

"I like the idea of what I call a ‘lead customer,’" Coronna said, "which is one customer that you think is representative of the market. Cultivate a relationship with that company, and you can really use them to help you refine your go-to-market message."

This kind of relationship provides the opportunity to not only learn about the overall market, the exercise market in the case of Bends, but also what individual companies in the space are confronting on a daily basis. A simple conversation can provide a quick summary of real-life opportunities and struggles for these companies.

"Go find somebody who is deep in the market and who is operationally excellent," Coronna said. "These are the people who are always looking for something better, faster, and cheaper."

With that in mind, Bends targeted Fitness Shack, one of the largest manufacturers of muscle-specific fitness equipment stations in the U.S. As expected, someone in purchasing was interested in hearing from a company that supplied tube parts, particularly if it could be consistent with on-time delivery. Apparently, that was an issue with the current supply base.

Coronna added that cultivating a relationship with one or two lead customers provides more than just an education on the market. When all is said and done, a manufacturer can have one or more solid references that it then can share with other companies in this market segment. For a company with not a lot of real history in a new market, these types of references can go a long way in providing confidence to a new customer target.

Of course, a company like Bends shouldn’t limit itself to just establishing a connection with lead customers. It should take time to visit a tradeshow, a very cost-effective way to touch base with people from all corners of a market segment.

With market intelligence in hand, a company is ready to take the next step of creating a value proposition. But before this can happen, a project lead needs to be appointed.

Coronna said that in the past he has seen two-person teams, comprised of one person familiar with the new market and the other a veteran of the company, do very well in these roles. Knowledge of the new market is an easy-to-recognize plus for a leader, but what does someone who’s been buried at a desk or on the shop floor bring to such a role? That person is going to know if what’s being promised is possible or what needs to happen to deliver on such a promise. Relationships are filled with occasional disappointment; a manufacturer doesn’t want to start off a new relationship in a new market with manufacturing delays or products that don’t meet specifications.

Time to Construct the Value Proposition

Bends’ representatives, a newly hired salesperson from a contract manufacturer that had done extensive work with exercise equipment manufacturers and a 10-year veteran of the company’s engineering team, conducted several conference calls with the Fitness Shack team and even enjoyed some face time with R.U. Sweddy’s, a manufacturer of fitness cycles and rowing machines. A trip to the International Exercise Exposition helped to round out their view of Bends’ role in this potential new market: It was an ever-evolving, growing, and welcoming provider of metal fabricating services that could assist with design for manufacturability (DFM), consistently meet quality expectations, and deliver on time.

All of those service elements were to become part of Bends’ go-to-market strategy. This value proposition, as Coronna described, helps a new customer to understand why it should listen to the new entry to the market. In this case, Bends had a pretty solid selling proposition for anyone in this exercise equipment segment.

If the work with the lead customers is mined correctly and enough time is spent talking with people in the industry, a metal fabricator should have a good feel as to why its offerings would stand out to new customers in a new market. Those conversations also can help prioritize the offerings.

For example, Bends, like any metal fabricator, knows that quality parts delivered on time is something that’s always valued, but the company found out that exercise equipment manufacturers need more than that. The segment is very competitive, so any cost that could be removed from any product could help improve margins or make for more competitive pricing. That’s why DFM had to be part of the value proposition. Luckily, Bends had a couple of young technicians who took to the laser tube cutting machine and had been aggressive in learning about what it could offer. The metal fabricator was confident it could help to rethink how certain tube-related parts were fabricated and look at other parts that could be consolidated into one tube component.

"You need to consistently repeat your value proposition to reinforce your message," Coronna said.

Any metal fabricator could buy a laser tube cutting machine, and Bends knew that. However, it was the internal processes and technical talent that were going to make it possible to win over new customers.

Time to Generate the Leads

The value proposition is done. The work with the lead customers has generated good references. It’s time to go after the market.

This won’t be the typical broad-based approach to business-to-business marketing, where generic messaging is sent out to hundreds or even thousands of companies. This is an account-based approach, where the message is tailored to a select group of companies that share a profile with the lead customers.

"Once you understand who your ideal customer is, the marketer’s job is to go out and find more of them," Coronna said. "This is not just about more in numbers. It’s finding companies with the same level of quality and opportunity as the lead customers."

Learning what made sense from its relationship with Fitness Shack and R.U. Sweddy’s, Bends knew that it wanted to work with fitness equipment companies that are looking for supply chain partners and not just a build-to-print metal fabricating shop. Bends wanted the kind of relationships in which its customers welcome the company’s ability to offer DFM advice.

With that in mind, Bends narrowed down a list of about 10 other similar companies in the fitness space, and its team targeted specific messaging for those customer targets. The Bends website was altered to promote its design and engineering capabilities, as well as its metal fabricating prowess. Images of parts for exercise equipment applications were included.

From that point on, any targeted marketing efforts could be tracked with website visitor information and a special tollfree phone number created for the effort. The Bends marketing leads also ensured that sales efforts were personable, timely, and worthwhile. The conversations uncovered potential pain points, and because of investigatory work done to learn the market, Bends salespeople were able to offer fabricating approaches that could help customers.

The addition of new fabricating capabilities provides the perfect opportunity to launch a campaign to add new customers to the existing lineup.

"It’s always about quality over quantity," Coronna said. "I understand who I want to do business with, and I’m pretty sure that I can find other people that look like that."

Don’t Give Up

Bends knew that developing a strong customer base in the exercise equipment market was going to take longer than a few months. Being an unknown entity to these companies, Bends realized it needed to spend months developing trust with these new parties.

"When it comes to business-to-business marketing, it can take 12 to 15 touches to cultivate a new customer," Coronna said.

If a metal fabricator has done its homework and understands where it can fit in its new market, the leads eventually turn into quoted projects and additional work. It can just take a while to come to fruition.

Bends eventually saw its success, but it didn’t necessarily happen right away. Because it believed in the preparation it put into the marketing project, it knew it would pay off. As with most things in life, proper preparation prevents poor performance.
