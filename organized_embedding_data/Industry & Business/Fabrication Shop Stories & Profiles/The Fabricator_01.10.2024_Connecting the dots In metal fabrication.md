R

ecent FABTECHs, including this year’s held in Orlando, Fla., have started with an undercurrent of change. The exposition floor shows metal fabrication at its best: incredibly productive fiber lasers; the latest in blanking automation; more press brakes with automatic tool changes and intelligent control; process simulation to eliminate those shop-floor surprises; cobots in welding and elsewhere, offering simplified programming; and production control and related software showing true machine uptimes, utilization, and opportunities for improvement.

Certain approaches to improvement represent a real sea change in metal fabrication. Local efficiency numbers for specific work centers are now being looked at within the broader context of overall throughput. Fabricators rely on improvement tools (lean concepts, 5S, process analysis) that they’ve used for years, but they now can boost those efforts with the unprecedented pattern recognition capability of artificial intelligence (AI).

All this involves not only scrutinizing what happens within each machine, those direct manufacturing activities; but also what’s happening in between them, the

indirect

tasks that support (and sometimes hinder) smooth job flow, from the moment the order is released until it ships out the door.

A Look at the Metrics

Advancing technology has helped fabricators navigate market demand after a tumultuous few years. According to recent financial and KPI surveys from the Fabricators and Manufacturers Association, sales growth this year has been softening after a dramatic fall in 2020 (14% decline on average) and a big rebound in 2021 (35% increase). The sales-per-employee metric has been on a tear too. Four years ago, the average from FMA survey respondents was $214,653; today that number has grown to $273,206. Material costs contribute to that, but so too does increased productivity. With better technology, the same number of people produce more and add more value.

But another metric has remained stubbornly low year after year, even at some top-performing custom fabricators:

on-time delivery

. Among FMA’s survey respondents, on-time delivery averaged between 77% and 84% over the past four years.

To be sure, these are averages. Some fabricators do offer world-class on-time delivery levels, but some suffer from below-average delivery too. Considering all the technology on offer, where do these delivery challenges come from?

This is where the big, connecting-the-dots picture comes into play. A laser might cut at incredible speeds, but what if someone forgets to order material, a fork truck driver doesn’t show up, or a poor-quality material warps and stops a laser head from cutting? Or, even if everything goes smoothly, what if curing time in powder coating remains your constraint—and your overall throughput doesn’t rise, despite increased blanking capacity?

Not every job has the same manufacturing constraint, of course. Even so,

actual

machine utilization—when gleaned from Industrial Internet of Things (IIoT) platforms, be they from machine OEMs or third parties—often ends up being shockingly low. The revelation reveals true production potential, but also shows that, at least for much of the time, everything isn’t going to plan.

Identifying the Indirect Processes

What’s behind those shockingly low utilization metrics? Before jumping to conclusions, some objective fact-finding is in order, ideally focused on a repeatedly ordered product or product family—that is, the 20% of the work that creates 80% of the value (the 80-20 rule).

An indirect process analysis

entails every indirect task—that is, tasks performed any time a tool or machine

isn’t

working on the material (see

Figure 1

). Engineering creates a bill of materials, assigns part numbers to the job, and determines the work centers to be used. They coordinate with purchasing to ensure material is available. They program the parts and create nests.

The analysis serves several purposes. First, it reveals low-hanging fruit. For instance, a manager might observe that operators spend two minutes looking for the next job. Two minutes doesn’t sound like a lot, but that adds up to an hour every shift, two hours every two-shift workday, and 10 hours every workweek. When you start looking at the big picture, the little things really add up.

Second, the analysis identifies unnecessary tasks. Say a once-challenging job requires operators to inspect every part. Now, though, thanks to operator training and perhaps new technology, the job isn’t so challenging. If the order has run for months or even years without issue, is that 100% part inspection

really necessary

? It might be, or it might not.

Regardless, the indirect process analysis reveals every task that’s being done so that each can be addressed. As the saying goes, you can’t improve something you don’t measure. The indirect process analysis goes one step further: To improve, you need to

identify

everything that’s really going on.

Many challenges have come from reduced batch sizes. As customers order smaller quantities more frequently, the mix of work rises on the floor. All the while, those indirect processes were initially implemented back when part mixes were less complex and batch sizes were larger.

Breaking down activities at various work centers can help reveal why real uptime numbers are so low and help guide improvement efforts.

"As we see a reduction in lot sizes, those indirect costs become very different." That was Walter Kampitsch, manager, product automation at TRUMPF Inc., who works with the company’s Smart Factory consulting group.

He added that the indirect process analysis works in concert with a

multimoment study

detailing what machines and operators are doing (see

Figure 2

). The effort usually reveals various factors that contribute to a machine’s low uptime. Part of it is simply the sheer speed of modern machines, especially (of course) fiber lasers, which is why so many fabricators have made the jump to automation, especially when processing thin sheet metal. Operators simply can’t keep up with the cutting head.

"We want to reveal how tasks interact, to synchronize the entire system," Kampitsch said. "It makes no sense to have a highly productive machine that spits out parts like crazy, but no one can process the parts coming out. The objective is to ship, not over-produce. That’s why it’s so important to look at the big picture."

That big picture involves more than just machine speed. Operators might be programming, handling material, setting up a new job, and denesting a previous job. But a large chunk of their time might also be spent on

paperwork

and simply

waiting

. This again is where connecting the dots between all the value streams comes into play.

"Every activity involving paperwork can be a source of mistakes," Kampitsch said. "Say employees enter raw material into the warehouse. That person fills out the thickness, chemistry, and dimensions. When doing so, they fill out the wrong chemistry. The error might not be noticed. It might cut just fine on the laser, and it might even weld fine too. Then, in assembly, someone notices the error. Consider all the value that employees upstream thought they were creating. All that value is now destroyed."

One error in the office or upstream process—a missed email, incorrect material being ordered, misplaced raw stock—can send ripple effects far and wide. So can unconnected back-end systems that require manual typing or writing onto a paper job packet. Unintelligible handwriting causes issues, but going paperless alone doesn’t solve the problem. In a fab shop, fat-fingering an extra zero on a laptop can have far-reaching consequences.

Identifying all those indirect activities also reveals the true value of design for manufacturability (DFM). For instance, what if a four-piece subassembly could be formed as one piece instead of being welded? The benefits might seem obvious, but the decision might not always be straightforward. What if forming the four-piece subassembly as one part creates a challenging press brake setup? Would bending four simple pieces and welding them be a more reliable approach?

As Kampitsch explained, not necessarily, especially when you look at all the indirect tasks needed to track multiple part numbers. "Remember, you need to maintain drawings for each piece. When you combine pieces into one design, you have fewer part numbers, and the part has the same functionality. In the end, you’re much better off."

Swim Lanes

All these analyses reveal what’s really going on, from order to ship. They also reveal why a part might have a four-week lead time, yet the laser, brake, welding robot, and powder coating line might spend mere hours

actually processing

the part. Sure, it needs to be scheduled with dozens or even hundreds of other jobs. But it also spends a lot of time waiting—as work-in-process on the floor or in someone’s email inbox waiting for a reply.

As shops start tracking unexpected downtime and delays, the reasons behind them can seem overwhelming. Material might not be available, or perhaps changeovers or job sequencing might not be optimized. Jobs might be released with incomplete information, leading to issues like forms on a part that can’t be made with available tooling or rework caused by material distortion. It can all seem like a spreading wildfire, but tackling it head on without further analysis can sometimes turn into a quixotic effort, a futile game of whack-a-mole.

It also doesn’t get to the heart of the issue: an overall lack of coordination— between suppliers, customers, and departments across the shop floor and around the office. When people just keep their heads down and operate blindly, jobs can move forward and take two steps backward.Errors are being corrected, but the root causes aren’t being addressed.

Here’s where the

swim lane analysis

comes into play (see

Figure 3

). These detail what tasks happen when. Every lane (horizontal bar) is a job function, department, area of expertise, or manufacturing step.

"When building these, it helps to get everyone who participates in the process involved," Kampitsch said. "Many times, people don’t understand what other people do to get a job out the door. When you graphically put it on the wall and map out every step, the results can be very revealing."

Once the swim lanes enter the floor, each part number switches lanes (another manufacturing step) in a linear fashion; cutting has to happen before bending, which happens before welding and powder coating. In the office, however, there’s no reason why certain tasks can’t happen concurrently or in different sequences.

Mapping out the entire flow in swim lanes can help streamline flow while still looking at the big picture. The best improvements shouldn’t cause a big splash and throw the swimmers in other lanes off course.

Where Is That Job, Really?

Production control and IIoT platforms can enhance such analyses even further, as can real-time locating systems showing exactly where every job is on the floor at any given time. The goal is to eliminate wasteful searching and optimize flow of disparate jobs throughout the shop.

"You don’t get paid for the time spent searching for parts, and these systems help you reduce that waste."

That was Kartik Iyer, director of the TRUMPF Smart Factory in Chicago, who spoke at the FMA Annual Meeting earlier this year, describing how wireless technology—on automated guided vehicles, manual carts, or any other material handling device—can communicate directly with production control software. Beyond simply identifying where parts are, "these systems can implement geofencing, which triggers events when parts move from one zone to another."

For instance, a cart of cut blanks moves from the laser to the press brake area. As soon as the cart reaches a certain area, the locating system signals that the parts are ready for the next operation downstream. Set up in the right way, such systems could allow for all sorts of indirect activities—job paperwork, machine program downloads, and more—to occur automatically in the background.

Harnessing the Power of AI

Predictive maintenance, IIoT, and locating systems give fabricators more useful data than ever before. Good analyses can turn that data into actionable information—and here, AI will play a leading role.

"AI is not magic. It’s good at recognizing repeating structures and patterns out of big data. Whenever you have repeating actions and processes, AI is of interest."

That was Stephan Mayer, TRUMPF’s CEO for machine tools, who spoke at a press conference earlier this year. The executive detailed new ways machine tool providers connect and support fabricators, who in turn use AI to better serve customers.

Some of this delves into machine condition monitoring and predictive maintenance. "For instance, if the laser heats up faster than expected, or if there is excessive light around the beam profile, dirt in the system might be reflecting light." Mayer added that those service reports go into a database where, again, AI can recognize patterns and suggest ways to detect issues well before they appear in a sheet metal workpiece.

Perhaps most significant, the algorithms behind AI aim to make automation more flexible. Mayer described instances in which AI-driven tools suggest optimal part nests for certain shifts—tools that become especially powerful with automated part removal. Fabricators invest in such automation so denesters needn’t spend hours removing and sorting parts after a lights-out shift. The last thing they want, though, is for the automated parts stacking system to catch on a skeleton and shut down in the middle of the night. Some parts might be better suited for the day shift, while others could be run unattended at night. Here, AI can suggest the best approach.

Such innovations, Mayer said, make automation "truly flexible." In one sense, AI is the last piece of the improvement puzzle. Fabricators work to improve quality and DFM, making parts more accurate, repeatable, and ripe for automation. Still, the real world is the real world. The unexpected occurs, and until recently, the algorithms driving automation simply couldn’t adapt to every possible error. This reality, however, is starting to change.

"AI has a certain tolerance for inaccurate data," Mayer said. "It can still develop a solution. This increases the robustness of automation tremendously."

Tuning a Fabricator

Metal fabrication involves optimizing complex patterns created by both direct and indirect processes—essentially, every task that helps (or doesn’t help) get a job out the door. Multimoment studies and swim lane diagrams help people see the big picture, recognize patterns, and develop ways to improve.

AI automates the pattern recognition to take the optimization even further.

How well does this part shape, cut from a specific material and thickness and grain orientation, lift out of a skeleton, form on the brake, and weld at the welding cell?

Looking at all the data at once, AI could help answer these questions and more. The ripple effects run everywhere. Every act has a reaction, and the better fabricators predict them, the more successful they can be.
