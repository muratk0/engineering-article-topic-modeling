By

Dan Davis

Management at CDH Custom Roll Form believes that safety is not just about slogans and meetings. It’s a core value that plays a part in everything the company does.

C

DH Custom Roll Form has gone five years without an accident. That’s five years of zero OSHA recordable injuries and zero days away, restricted, or transferred cases. That’s an amazing feat considering huge steel coils routinely move throughout the 80,000-sq-ft. facility, where five roll forming lines run daily and heavy tooling is switched out of presses and roll formers. All of that metal and heavy equipment hasn’t caused a single injury.

While pleased with the safety performance, CDH Custom Roll Form is not satisfied. Some even suggest that the new unofficial slogan about the company’s safety performance is "Five More Years to 10."

That’s not an audacious goal for a company that has created a manufacturing environment where no safety director is needed. Everyone is charged with keeping an eye out for unsafe practices, and they are actually incentivized to report them. Safety literally has been a priority for the company since its opening day, even before the first employees were hired.

This company’s safety performance and the environment that helped to foster it have earned CDH Custom Roll Form the Fabricators and Manufacturers Association’s Rusty Demeules Award for Safety Excellence, an honor that recognizes companies that go above and beyond OSHA standards to build and sustain outstanding, employee-driven workplace safety cultures.

BUILT-IN FROM THE VERY BEGINNING

The Warren, Ohio, company is a joint venture between ClarkDietrich, a large manufacturer of cold-formed steel framing building products, and Hadley Industries, a U.K.-based producer of cold-rolled profiles. The new company, launched in 2019, provided ClarkDietrich with a facility that could produce highly engineered profiles in high volumes and gave Hadley a foothold in the North American market.

Since the company was a startup, managers could set things up as they wanted. With that in mind, safety was priority No. 1.

"We have engineered and implemented safety-focused technologies and enhancements with safety as the top priority, ensuring risks are mitigated through design as well as behavior," said Dan Ross, operations manager.

A roll forming line is an encapsulation of several fabrication and forming activities. It begins with a stand onto which steel coils up to 10,000 lbs. are positioned so they can feed the next station, a leveling machine that straightens out the coil and removes deformities. From there, the coil accumulates some slack so that it can provide a consistent feed to the punch press. Slack accumulates again as the coil exits the press and before it feeds into the actual roll forming section.

During roll forming, the material is passed through consecutive sets of shaped rollers, with each section incrementally bending the metal until the desired cross-sectional profile is achieved.

Martin English, CDH Custom Roll Form’s technical manager and the directory of technology for Hadley Industries in the U.K., said the company built three of the lines for the new facility from the ground up. Two others were refurbished. The work called for Hadley Industries’ British engineers to consult with OSHA standards to ensure they were meeting U.S. safety guidelines as the lines were built.

"As part of the process, we had to confer with the OSHA standards. In the U.K., we work with the EU machinery directive, which is similar to OSHA," English said. "But we found that the OSHA standards in some regards go further."

In particular, OSHA-specified lockout/tagout—placing a lock or device on a machine to prevent it from being turned on and applying tags to notify others not to turn the machine back on—is not really part of the safety culture in the EU. English and his fellow engineers liked the idea so much that they are now adapting it into Hadley systems being installed in other parts of the world.

Back in Ohio, CDH Custom Roll Form management wanted to build on those OSHA standards. It wanted to include single points of disconnect for all energy sources.

"If you make it easy for the operator to employ these disconnects, they’ll use them," said Jack Pacalo, president. "In addition to the single points of disconnect, we developed a system where maintenance had their set of locks. Every operator has their own designated lock," Ross said. "We also have lockout procedures at every piece of equipment that indicates hazards, where the energy sources are located, and where the lockout points are located for each of the different types of equipment."

The Rusty Demeules Award for Safety Excellence is the highest honor in the Fabricators and Manufacturers Association’s safety awards program. It was created to recognize one company each year that excels beyond OSHA recordable incidence rates and demonstrates an established safety culture. The award is named after Rusty Demeules, who worked for his family’s metal fabricating company, Standard Iron and Wire Works, in Monticello, Minn., while also serving as president of the FMA/CNA Safety Committee from its inception in 1989 until 1996.

With these individualized locks, there are no surprise machine power-ups, even during shift changes. The roll former has transition locks on which notes can be written to explain to those on the next shift why the machinery is shut down and the potential hazard being investigated.

Ross added that English and his co-workers even incorporated light curtains into the roll forming lines, providing another safety barrier. This occurred even before employees began filling open roles in 2021.

GETTING EMPLOYEES INVOLVED

As the company began hiring, safety remained at the forefront. Establishing a safety-oriented culture would be the foundation and feed the success of other areas, such as quality, customer service, and operational excellence.

For CDH Custom Roll Form to achieve its goal of an accident-free workplace, it needed employees to be engaged and involved in holding each other accountable. This wasn’t just about attending meetings and staying awake during presentations. They needed to participate in a meaningful way.

"Employee engagement is extremely important. We encourage feedback," Ross said. "For instance, we have a near-miss program that we encourage employees to report conditions or processes that have potential hazards that could result in an injury or illness.

"We also have built that into an incentive program where participation drives changes," he added.

Employees are expected to submit near-miss reports. If they don’t, it can affect bonuses.

Pacalo said that the manufacturing team missed a "max" bonus several months ago because not all participated in submitting reports. It was a smaller bonus than what was possible, and that did not go unnoticed by those on the shop floor. During the next reporting period, management got 100% participation.

Pacalo added that while everyone is aware that they should be reporting unsafe work practices or potentially dangerous situations, this does not evolve into a witch hunt. It’s a continuous improvement exercise. Actions can be corrected. Processes can be improved.

"But if someone is demonstrating that they cannot work safely, you have to protect them and you have to protect the rest of your workforce," Pacalo said. "And we will discharge somebody for it."

"Now, we’re not punitive. We coach. We go through a progressive discipline. But you know that, if they’ve decided that they can’t adapt to the team, the team’s not going to adapt to them."

Safety inspections are another part of this proactive approach, and once again, employee participation is directly tied to the company’s tiered wage advancement program.

Part of this inspection program is an internal safety committee comprising volunteer team members who conduct monthly facility safety audits, evaluating equipment, processes, and overall compliance. Part of these activities include monthly and annual crane inspections, routine forklift reviews, and pre-operation checks on all equipment. All new equipment and processes are analyzed for job safety before they go live on the shop floor.

ASSISTING THE HUMAN EFFORT

Employee safety is not just left up to good intentions and healthy habits at CDH Custom Roll Form. Key investments have been made in safety-focused technologies to assist with the mission.

Recently, the roll former added advanced laser scanners to the coil loading area of its lines. These scanners can monitor a work area and activate an equipment shutdown should personnel enter an area that is supposed to be off limits.

This is an example of how the right safety measures actually can lead to increases in productivity. Because the laser scanners can serve the same purpose as guarding, a cage structure isn’t necessary around the coil handling area. Personnel can stage coils without the need to open a gate, which in the past would force the entire roll forming line to shut down.

CDH Custom Roll Form also has added crane hoist safety systems with visual light indicators that show personnel where to stand to minimize the chance of them being injured if the hoisted load comes crashing down. The safety system also has audible alerts to notify others nearby that a heavy load is being moved overhead.

This safety tool proved incredibly useful one evening recently when a team leader was training two new employees on an off-shift. A 10,000-lb. coil was being moved with a crane and the strap broke. The coil crashed to the ground, but no one was injured. The supervisor had been instructing the new employees to be outside of the visible outline projected on the floor.

"Nobody was injured. Everybody was in the right space," Ross said. "We gave them all $50 gift certificates."

But this wasn’t just one safety tool that kept injuries—and possibly even worse—from happening. CDH Custom Roll Form follows suspended load guidelines, practices the 45 degree rule (personnel stand at a distance away from the suspended load so that their line of sight to that load forms an angle of 45 degrees or less), and uses no-touch sticks to keep people’s hands from possibly becoming ensnared in straps.

Of course, remediation of the safety hazard is the best way to prevent something similar from happening again. CDH Custom Roll Form had a case where a raft, a section of tooling for roll forming that can be slid into and out of the line as needed, was being lifted out of the area, but a hold-down was not removed. As the raft was lifted, the holding clamp broke, which could have led to the load becoming unstable and possibly injuring someone.

No one was injured, so the next step was to figure out what had gone wrong. The entire plant was shut down, and the investigation started. Investigators found that the hold-down was just overlooked. It blends in with the rest of the tooling on the raft.

Today, that hold-down is painted yellow so it stands out much more clearly. Additionally, two people are required to sign off and validate that a raft is ready to be moved before the action takes place.

"The amount of investment made here has never been in question by ownership and board," Pacalo said.

THE LEADERSHIP HEARS ABOUT IT

That’s probably a good thing, because the environment that CDH Custom Roll Form has created not only encourages employee engagement with safety activities but also with others in the organization. And that’s not limited to shop floor supervisors.

"They hold us accountable too," Pacalo said. "You’ll get a question like, ‘Hey, I turned in this report, so what’s going on with it?’"

He recalled a chop saw that had been used for cutting material to size that was selected for replacement. A safety audit had determined that even though the equipment had done the job adequately for years, a more modern saw would further reduce the risk of injury by eliminating the constant up and down motion required to pull the saw down, providing better guarding for the operator should a blade break, and decreasing the amount of airborne hazards emanating from the cutting process.

The new equipment was scheduled for purchase, but it couldn’t come soon enough for the saw operators, Pacalo said. When the board of directors visited (as they do for most of the safety recognition events), the operators welcomed them—and then asked when the saw might be arriving.

"We are a cohesive team, and we’re transparent. That goes a really long way when we have our monthly safety meetings or KPI meetings. We don’t withhold information," Pacalo said.

A WORTHY AWARD RECIPIENT

Five years without an accident is a remarkable achievement for CDH Custom Roll Form, but almost as incredible is that the company did it while doubling its workforce in the past couple of years. It currently has about 40 employees.

Inexperienced employees are often likely candidates to be involved in a workplace accident because they simply don’t know the lay of the land and they might be unfamiliar with the work as well. And different contractors enter and exit the facility frequently to work on projects like equipment installations. No matter who they are or how much experience they have, they are all held to the same standard: Work safely or don’t do the work.

If past outcomes are any indication of future results, achieving 10 years without an accident doesn’t seem out of the question for the roll former. Ross made a solid case in his application for why CDH Custom Roll Form should earned the Rusty Demeules Award for Safety Excellence this year—and maybe again in the future:

"Our organization embodies a true safety-first culture, where results are not achieved by chance but through intentional leadership, innovation, employee engagement and a deeply embedded commitment to continuous improvement. This sustained performance and dedication are what set us apart and make our organization a deserving recipient of this safety award."
