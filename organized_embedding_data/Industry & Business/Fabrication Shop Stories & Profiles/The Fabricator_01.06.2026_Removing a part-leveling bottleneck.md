A

ny manufacturer would like a metal fabricating services provider to be on the level—honest and trustworthy. In today’s automated world, however, "on the level" takes on a whole new meaning.

Original equipment manufacturers still demand that the metal fabricators in their supply chains be reliable, but they also literally require parts that are leveled. They don’t want metal parts that are slightly warped or out of spec because they need to fit precisely into fixtures for automated welding processes. The days when skilled welders would finagle an unlevel part into position in a fixture with a shim or just cover up a larger-than-expected gap with extra filler metal are done. Welding automation demands precise parts, and OEMs are demanding leveled parts from their metal fabricator suppliers.

O’Neal Manufacturing Services’ Ambridge, Pa., facility has seen this type of customer shift with its own longtime partners—and even with its own internal welding operations. After years of making investments to optimize its laser and plasma cutting operations, the company had work-in-process in front of its manual leveling operations—a sign that something needed to be done.

"Nothing was more frustrating for me than to have our lasers cutting with full automation and we couldn’t get parts flattened fast enough," said Gus Cassida, OMS’ regional general manager. "We were starving our secondary operations."

This was no small bottleneck for this fabricator of thick metal parts. Cassida estimated that about three-quarters of the parts under 0.5 in. processed by the OMS Ambridge plant needed some sort of corrective flattening.

OMS had been relying on a large hydraulic cylinder on a gantry bridge to correct the unlevel parts. An operator would have to bolster the part off the ground or off a table, position it, and have the hydraulic cylinder bend the part past its plane in hopes that it sprung back into the right position. It wasn’t the most efficient operation, but it worked for years. But as those years passed, it became harder to find people interested in doing the job.

"At the end of the day, we just couldn’t keep throwing bodies at an inefficient process," Cassida said.

To optimize its leveling process, OMS purchased a FlatMaster 88 200 from ARKU in 2025. This machine is similar to three other machines that are in place in OMS’ Greensboro, N.C., facility. The machine can process materials up to 79 in. wide and 0.04 to 1.1 in. thick.

WHAT DOES THE MACHINE DO?

To the untrained eye, most of the sheet metal and plate coming into the OMS facility looks flat enough. However, that doesn’t reveal the true story behind the stress that exists underneath the material’s surface.

The sheets delivered to a metal fabricator contain internal tension, also referred to as residual stress, that results from activities after the steelmaking process. For example, curvatures can be introduced to a coil; coil set is a curvature that appears in parallel with the rolling direction, and crossbow appears across the width. Also, as a metal strip is coiled, the top surface is stretched, introducing more tension, while the bottom is compressed.

Workers use a straight edge to check the flatness of a part that has been processed through the roller leveler.

Tension is relieved in the sheet metal when heat from the laser beam engages the material’s surface during the blanking process. The heat reduces the material’s yield strength, allowing the material to give in to the residual stresses introduced from material manipulation during coiling.

One of the ways to remove the tension from the material is physically alternating bends past the yield point of the material until tension is relieved. At that point, the material is likely to remain in its flat state even if downstream activities, such as welding, take place.

The ARKU FlatMaster 88 200 machine uses rollers to remove the stress from the metal and return it to a flat state. Metal parts are fed onto a conveyor that feeds the machine, and they then pass through multiple sets of upper and lower rolls, which are offset from the machine entrance to exit. This means that one roll always dips between the two opposite straightening rolls.

That alternating bending introduces counter forms that help the material work its way back to a flat state. As the material is fed into the machine, a curvature is generated that is greater than the strongest curvature in the nonlevel blank. This begins the process of eliminating the stresses in the material. By the time the material is close to exiting the roller levelers, the alternating bending is minimized, and the uneven sections have sprung back to a flat state.

BYE TO THE BOTTLENECK

Once the leveling machine was up and running, it helped OMS remove about two weeks’ worth of work-in-process.

"Within a matter of weeks, our constraints were no longer with flattening," Cassida said.

Now one operator per shift works the machine. That operator is able to feed the machine and organize parts as they exit. He also provides quality checks on the parts when a job is initiated.

"Training for operators was a lot faster than we anticipated," Cassida said, "because I think the system itself is a lot more intuitive than a lot of other pieces of equipment we have.

"There are some parameters in there that the operator has to adjust," he continued. "For instance, he might be running 500 of the same parts, but if he starts to see parts that are coming through the ARKU that aren’t 100%, he can quickly make corrections inside those parameters."

To get the machine running on a new job, the operator needs to provide it with some basic information, such as material grade, yield strength, and material thickness. From there, the operator can make adjustments quickly if early part runs suggest some tweaks might be required.

Not only has OMS removed a bottleneck, it now has enough capacity to tackle the parts load for the foreseeable future. This also includes parts shuttled over from the plasma cutting table.

With that capacity available, OMS might offer leveling services to others in the metals processing industry, according to Cassida. After all, OMS isn’t the only metal fabricator with customers looking for high-precision, stress-relieved parts.
