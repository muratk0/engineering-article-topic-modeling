# First line of defense: Small and medium-sized shops

[TARİH: 01.04.2026 The Fabricator]

Editor’s Corner

The federal government is making success difficult for the metalworking shops that make up the bulk of domestic manufacturing activity

By

Dan Davis

T

he U.S. Department of War, formerly known as the Department of Defense, dropped bombs on Iran on Feb. 28, so obviously, things are pretty busy at the Pentagon right now. Unfortunately, U.S. metal fabricating and machine shops need the attention of the Pentagon for a few minutes to clear up some supply chain issues. Simply stated, if this administration wants to maintain a strong national defense, it needs to know that a new cybersecurity standard threatens a good portion of the shops that make up this nation’s metalworking base.

Cybersecurity Maturity Model Certification is a tool the Pentagon is using to verify that contractors serving the nation’s defense industrial base are in a position to protect sensitive unclassified information. This certification, known currently as CMMC 2.0, is a transition from a self-attestation model, which relied on personal verification, to a framework in which compliance is verified through self-assessments or independent third-party audits.

In particular, defense officials are looking for protection of what the department calls "controlled unclassified information": sensitive but unclassified information that requires safeguarding. This could include technical drawings, engineering data, or financial records.

While the goal is universally recognized as a need, shops in the industrial base are wondering if there’s a better way to get there. Those that produce parts for defense-related applications are no strangers to the extra paperwork and bureaucratic headaches that come with that work, but the added expense of meeting CMMC requirements is steep—so much so that some of these small companies run the risk of going out of business if they lose these jobs because they don’t have the proper certifications.

"As the owner of a small machine shop serving defense and aerospace industries, we already face a long list of compliance burdens and costs: annual ITAR registration, ISO 9001 implementation and audits, JCP registration. The list goes on. For companies that win seven-figure contracts, these new CMMC 2.0 costs are manageable, but not for small shops," wrote David Smith, owner of Motive Machine Works, Lancaster, Pa., in a LinkedIn post in late February.

"I am here to tell you that large primes and even medium-sized primes do not make much of any kind of prototype/R&D work. They subcontract the work to small shops across the country like mine. The issue is that many customers claim that ‘price doesn’t matter,’ but in reality they often shop for the lowest bid; if another vendor is a few percent cheaper, they’ll choose them.

"Now I’m being told to spend $30,000-$50,000 implementing/preparing for the audit and maintaining a $50,000 audit cost every three years in perpetuity?"

Obviously, things are pretty busy at the Pentagon. But if this administration wants to maintain a strong national defense, it needs to know something: A new cybersecurity standard threatens a good portion of the shops that make up this nation’s metalworking base.

Without those small shops, the defense industry loses flexible and often very cost-competitive links in the supply chain. Jesse Vodvarka and his father, Joseph, of Vodvarka Springs in Clinton, Pa., provided an example in a video posted to the web in mid-February.

Joseph described a defense-related job that called for 14,000 springs that would cost him about $0.14 to $0.15 each to make, but he quoted $0.11 to get the job. After the contract ran out, someone undercut him for that same job, quoting about $0.06 each.

Years later, that same spring came up for a quote and the price had gone up to $1.40 each. Joseph considered quoting it for $1 each (with a volume of 8,000), which was "ridiculous," but decided not to quote as it wasn’t worth the risk of running afoul of the new CMMC regulations. Jesse said that contract was later awarded for $4.44 per piece.

"Where we’re at right now, it’s a fiasco," he added.

Of course, that’s just one small example shared by one small shop. But with the Pentagon budget ballooning close to $1 trillion in 2026, it’s not hard to believe that a lack of competition for metal parts can lead to prices that are more expensive than they have to be. Small shops are where the value is to be found in the domestic manufacturing base.

This presidential administration that is so keen on building back domestic manufacturing needs to recognize that small shops need help. Cybersecurity certification makes sense, but the costs are too high. Whether through grants or support through entities like the Manufacturing Extension Partnership, shops need assistance in meeting the costs associated with attaining and maintaining their CMMC credentials.

War is big business. If the U.S. government wants it to be a more cost-effective business, it needs to ensure that fabricating and metalworking shops of all sizes can compete—and stay in business doing it.
