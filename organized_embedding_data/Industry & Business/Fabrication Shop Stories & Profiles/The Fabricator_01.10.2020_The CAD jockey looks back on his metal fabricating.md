After running a fab shop, Gerald Davis found a comfortable landing spot as a designer for hire

By

Dan Davis

Gerald Davis, who at the time owned a precision sheet metal shop, had his first Precision Matters column appear in

The FABRICATOR

’s January 2001 edition.

Davis delved into some of the issues of running a fab shop during his "Wise words from the mountaintop" series of columns that ran in 2004 and 2005.

Y

ou might know Gerald Davis as the author of

The FABRICATOR

’s Precision Matters column. You might not have known that he was the founder of one of the first SolidWorks user groups in the U.S. and was an owner of a metal fabricating company in Denver for many years. His past has given him a perspective that many don’t have as they enter the metal fabricating field as a young person—computing experience—and that experience let him see what might be possible in the 1980s and 1990s in terms of design and communication when others didn’t.

Davis picked up that computing experience in the U.S. Navy in the early 1970s. He worked on computers with magnetic ferrite memories, described by Davis as being like "little magnetic doughnuts."

Davis’ father, Ralph Davis, was a machinist by trade and always liked building things, such as his own gold mining machine that he actually put to use. He still needed a day job, however. Ralph Davis broke out on his own in 1966 in an eponymous business. Later he joined forces with Bernie Baron, a naturalized U.S. citizen from Germany who had a deep knowledge of sheet metal fabricating and a fever to build a business. Davis Sheet Metal, later known as DSM, was a business that would one day grow to about $4 million in sales and employ as many as 75 people.

But it did not reach those levels of achievement with the same two business partners. Baron and Davis split ways in 1983 over ownership questions. That provided an opportunity for Gerald Davis, who had served as a sort of apprentice to Baron when he was younger, to come onboard and assist his dad. Gerald had been out of the Navy for about three years and had yet to find his true calling in the business world.

"The great thing about the split was that it created the opportunity for me to create a career and apply my computer skills," Davis said.

Gerald Davis and his wife Mary eventually took over ownership of DSM and ran it for several years before dissolving the company in the mid-2000s. At that time Davis was shifting his focus to designing parts and end products for clients and leaving the metal fabricating to others.

Needless to say, those experiences made Davis a must-read columnist in the pages of

The FABRICATOR

since his start in 2001, whether he was writing about running a fab shop or organizing documentation while CAD modeling. Davis reflected on his time as a fabricator and his involvement with computers and software in a recent interview.

The FABRICATOR:

Were you an early adopter of CAD at DSM?

Gerald Davis:

I was the beneficiary of seeing a lot of different companies that were already using CAD, like Hewlett-Packard had their minicomputers running at the time. I forget what their CAD system was called, but they had 3D CAD way before anybody else. It was not that good. AutoCAD running on a Tandy was so much better. So there was a lot of CAD out there, but there was a lot of bad CAD.

I was lucky to be sitting in a seat where people were feeding it across my desk every day. As an estimator, I was looking at all of this stuff. As a salesman, I was going out, making proposals and visiting the shops, walking through IBM and looking at all the modern tools they had. And then I go visit another shop that was doing it literally with a spreadsheet and a calculator. So I got to see a lot of stuff, and it was just accidentally I trained.

FAB:

In what other ways did your shop embrace modern computing power at the time?

Davis:

Ralph and Bernie had invested in a little PDP-8 computer from DEC [Digital Equipment Corp.] to run a paper tape for their CNC stuff. So it was around 1979, and they paid tens of thousands of dollars for that PDP-8. It sounded great. They had a little computer, but it wasn’t as smart as the thermostat in your own house.

So I was able to replace that with all kinds of different technology. We leaped forward in CNC programming ability. We had the internet going by 1996, which not many people had at that time. I was even running my own emails, which was an error.

We had a lot of technology at that time. My wife was even writing a relational database system way before anybody else. We had pretty good estimating software, but we were so obsessed with family life and the nitty-gritty of keeping the bankers at bay that we really could not see the opportunities that were around us.

As Davis began focusing his career on part and end product design, he also changed the focus of Precision Matters to CAD and 3D modeling.

In learning about the finer points of 3D modeling over the years, readers also got a lesson in fabricating some nice items, such as this grill from a series of 2010 columns.

We were getting phone calls from people who saw us on the web, where we showed all of our equipment. "Where can I get a brake like that?" they would ask. I would say, "Yeah. We don’t sell brakes, but I’ll bend the metal for you." They would say that they could bend their own metal but wanted to buy that particular brake. I would tell him to go talk to the distributor.

We probably should have been selling the brakes. We probably should have sold my software.

FAB:

You see some metal fabricators nowadays hiring software developers to create custom systems and interfaces for their own use. Did you do any more software development besides the estimating software?

Davis:

We saw the estimating software as secret and proprietary, and we wouldn’t share it. So it became something that Mary and I could support, but nobody else could do it. When we had 50 employees, we didn’t have time to code. We had to go out and find a different solution.

I made the decision to buy a product called Visual Manufacturing. It was an SQL-based estimating system that cost just under $100,000. We spent a year training people and flying them all over the country. It was quite an investment to get the thing up and running. It did become our estimating system that we ran for probably 10 years before we closed the business in the mid-2000s.

FAB:

Do you find it interesting that you have spent a large portion of your life working in the metal fabricating industry yet still had the chance to work with computers over that same time span?

Davis:

Let me tell you a little bit of a long story. When I was apprenticing for Ralph and Bernie, when they were working in the garage, building fireplace parts, they had a little Di-Acro leaf brake. Bernie would set it up, and he would show 14-year-old me how to bend a piece of copper. I stuck it in there, clamped it down, lifted up the handle on the brake, and measured it, making sure it was 90 degrees. Then I picked up the next one and went on with the job. Bernie taught me how to follow instructions.

Jump forward seven years, and now I’m in the Navy and boot camp. The drill instructor shows me how to fold a T-shirt. So I did all my shirts. The drill instructor, meanwhile, went around showing everyone how to fold T-shirts and yelling at them because they couldn’t do it right. He came back and looked at my pile, and it was like something you take a photograph of for recruiting purposes. They were perfect. He was just as floored. "How did you do that?" he asked. "You showed me how to do it, and I repeated what you did," I said. The drill instructor then said I was the educational petty officer, and he told me to go sit over in another room. I spent the rest of boot camp pushing paper while everybody else was out doing pushups and marching around.

Then probably with that same kind of attitude—"If you can tell me what to do, I’ll do it"—I ended up in the intelligence center on an aircraft carrier, where I learned about running computers. I then went back to the family business with that same attitude, and it led to a career in sheet metal.

Everything’s related somehow. It was all preparation so I could write articles about it, I guess.

FAB:

How did your writing career with

The FABRICATOR

start?

Davis:

One of the metal fabricating competitors in the Denver area was a guy named Carl Roberts. He wrote a little article that appeared in the

Denver Post

, and he was saying that it’s a good idea to have community vocational schools. His company’s name appeared at the end of the article. I thought that I better do that too. At the same time, I was receiving

The FABRICATOR

, which was a giant back then, hard to miss. There was an editor who asked readers to share their life experiences with the magazine. What was it like to run your job shop? So I sent in an article, which I think was about shearing, and it got accepted. I then contributed another one a little while later on punching.

Somebody said, "Would you like to do some more articles?" and that turned into Precision Matters. This was a marketing ploy for the company. I thought people would see my articles and subliminally think about my company. I also loved to show the articles in the glossy presentations without the ads. We’d purchase those and have them for the customers to see. But people were extraordinarily unimpressed with that kind of glam presentation. That didn’t work at all.

So I gave up on that, but I still enjoyed writing the articles. I also found that my team of employees read the magazine like they were devoted to it, but they would not listen to me. There’s a whole lot of Precision Matters that was just written for the benefit of my employees.

But I also wrote them for my customers too. They’re lectures.

FAB:

In 2004 you started a series of columns called "Wise words from the mountaintop" in which you sought business wisdom from a tiny guru living in the nearby Rockies. We have received feedback on those articles as recently as last year. Did you think they would have as much impact with readers as they did?

Davis:

As Kate [Bachman, the current editor of

STAMPING Journal

] and I were working on those series of articles, we were both surprised that we were getting reader feedback. It was actually being received pretty well. We got to the end of the year and wondered what we could do with the collected prose. So I tried to package it into a book, thinking there was something profound there.

I’ve read it again though years ago, and I discovered it’s actually a sort of an ode to the end of the business. It was a recitation of all of my errors, as opposed to actually definitively knowing what the right thing to do is. If you went to the University of Illinois and got an MBA, you would expect that with that degree you would have learned about business things like cash flow and ledgers.

I didn’t get that. I don’t have an MBA. But I feel that my life experience in business, through a series of errors and survival, gave me some understanding of this. That didn’t mean that my understanding was anything that people should copy, but it did give them something to refer to.

FAB:

If you could rewrite that same series of columns, would you write anything differently?

Davis:

All I could do would be to recite mistakes that I made and say, "Don’t make the same mistakes." I viewed my employees as entries in a computer program that predicts costs. When the employees didn’t behave as predicted, which they never did, I’d have a problem. I tried to approach them as a programmer. I’ve always been a programmer and an engineering type; do what you’re told and repeat it. Again, that works great in computers and works pretty well in the military. It’s horrible when you’re trying to manage a crew of people.

So I sent in an article, which I think was about shearing, and it got accepted. I then contributed another one a little while later on punching. Somebody said, ‘Would you like to do some more articles?’ and that turned into Precision Matters."

FAB:

The internet contains such a wealth of information, but do you think it has the same value as real-world experience?

Davis:

When I bought my first house, I really felt like I’d become an adult. It was a commitment, and I signed on for that 30-year mortgage. Of course, I had no clue how long 30 years was. It was just the willingness that I had. I wanted to have a house, and I’m going to do this. So I’ll make that promise. Here’s my signature.

Whether you are in the sheet metal trade or product development, whatever kind of thing you’re in, you’re in for the same kind of commitment. You’re going to go for it. Young people who have that kind of willingness to sign on and to commit can go further and faster with the internet, but they still have to have the courage to go forward with insight and to put their own blood into it.

I think that vocational training should include a little bit of that. When I was a kid, I went through a program called Junior Achievement. I didn’t learn a heck of a lot from it, but over the years it was still a reference point that I could relate to when I started running a business. You build a product, and you sell it. That kind of training should be a part of the vocational training or high school as well. Having kids go through college and hoping that an MBA will deliver that same training, that’s too aspirational.

FAB:

If you could go back and advise a younger Gerald Davis just starting out in the metal fabricating business, what would you tell him?

Davis:

I think back to a moment when I was sitting in my dad’s office with a couple of guys from Hewlett-Packard, and they said to my dad, "Would you like to grow your business?" I was vice president at the time and just listening to the conversation.

And my dad said, "No, I actually think I probably like this company the way it is." I heard a thunderclap in my head when he said that. I knew something was wrong, but I just sort of sat on it and didn’t say anything. All these years later, I know if I had stood up and said, "Yes!" we could have become the sheet metal shop for Hewlett-Packard.

There was another moment in life with Advanced Energy, which later became listed on NASDAQ. They were just starting off, and I drove up and visited with them. They gave me a pile of drawings to quote for their factory, which was still under construction. We actually met at a Wendy’s. I didn’t really believe them. It seemed like a lot of hot air, and I didn’t quote on it. If I had quoted on it, I could have possibly become the king of sheet metal.

These are the regrets of opportunity. But at least I had that opportunity, and I gained the insight to know that they were lost opportunities. I think that’s hard to find on the internet, even though the information is out there.

So I’d tell him, take advantage of the opportunities. When something is knocking on the damn door, answer it. So many people don’t get that.
