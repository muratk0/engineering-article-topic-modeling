# OSHA wants to publicize reported injury information

AROUND WASHINGTON

Manufacturers claim such data needs to remain confidential

By Stephen Barlas, Contributing Writer

Manufacturers are none too happy with the Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA) proposed rule that would require them to provide injury and illness data to the agency—data that could become public.

“Of significant concern is the public release of information that our business treats as confidential commercial information—employee hours worked,” said Robert Kersey, president, Rochester Metal Products Corp.

OSHA made the proposal last October. The new rule would require employers with 250 or more workers to submit to OSHA every quarter the individual entries on their OSHA 300 logs and the information entered on each OSHA 301 incident report. OSHA would then post the data on its public website after redacting only injured employees’ identifying information. The proposed rule also would require employers with 20 or more workers in designated industries to submit information electronically from their 300A annual summary forms to OSHA, which OSHA also intends to publicize.

All of that calculation is now done on paper forms, which are never submitted to OSHA, except in the case of inspections or companies that have to submit the forms every three years because they have been labeled high-injury workplaces. The latter represents about 1 percent of companies completing the forms.

OSHA’s intention to get the data from companies and make it public has provoked significant opposition from the manufacturing community. The Freedom of Information Act exempts from public disclosure “a trade secret or privileged or confidential commercial or financial information obtained from a person,” said Joe Trauger, vice president, human resources policy, National Association of Manufacturers (NAM).

“NAM and its members believe employee hours worked on the OSHA Form 300A is confidential business information, because that information gives insight into the state of a business at any given time and creates a competitive harm,” Trauger added.

OSHA representatives argue that other federal agencies post establishment-specific health and safety data with personal identifiers, including names. For example, the Mine Safety and Health Administration (MSHA) publishes coded information pertaining to each accident, illness, or injury reported to MSHA on Form 7000-1. Reported information includes employee gender and age, as well as narratives associated with specific accidents/injuries for a particular year.

Trauger said the fact that other federal agencies release employee information does not necessarily make OSHA’s desire to do so legally permissible. He added that the agency is abandoning its historical approach of applying a balancing test and provides no detail on how it will protect an individual employee’s privacy rights.

If the rule becomes final, some think it might prove to be a disincentive for reporting injuries. Jerry Call, CEO of the American Foundry Society, is in that camp. Currently OSHA encourages employers to record all possible qualifying incidents, counseling that those which turn out to be outside the reporting requirements can later be stricken.

“With quarterly reporting, employers are unlikely to record close cases because, in many instances, striking them later may be impossible as the information has already been reported and posted publicly by OSHA,” Call said.

Peg Seminario, director, AFL-CIO’s Safety and Health Department, has a solution to that. She said she wants OSHA to add provisions to the final rule that expressly bar incentive programs, discipline programs, or other employer programs, policies, or practices that discourage workers from reporting injuries and bar any adverse action against an employee who reports an injury or illness.

“The failure to include such a prohibition and enforcement mechanism in the final rule has the real potential to lead to increased actions to suppress injury reporting,” she said.
