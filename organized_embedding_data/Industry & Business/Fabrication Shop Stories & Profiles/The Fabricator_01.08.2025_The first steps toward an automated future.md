O

ne of the impediments of growth for a metal fabricating company is room. An additional shift usually can be added, but sometimes an organization just doesn’t have the space for more inventory, machinery, or other value-added activities.

It’s no coincidence that as manufacturing in the U.S. has rebounded since the pandemic, so has commercial construction. Opportunities are aplenty, and manufacturers need the space to add capacity for today and tomorrow.

Miller Fabrication Solutions, Brookville, Pa., recognized its own need for additional manufacturing space when it announced its intent to purchase a 186,000-sq.-ft. facility in early 2022. It already had two existing facilities in Brookville and a third in Homer City, Pa., but no real room to plan for the future. This additional facility, called Pine Creek, would allow it to expand its parts production business, while simultaneously providing management a blank canvas to chart out what the company’s automated future might look like.

The existing industrial infrastructure of Pine Creek was well suited for the 62-year-old company’s vertically integrated approach to fabricating and necessitated relatively minimal renovations. Miller plans to use its new facility to expand component parts production and execute its future automation vision, according to company President Eric D. Miller.

"This increase of more than 35% shop floor space will give us the scale to consider how automated devices, autonomous vehicles, and other technologies can streamline production beyond what we have currently," Miller said at the time.

Today, that expansion is complete. Pine Creek is now being set up to consolidate most of the cutting work being done at its other facilities. The first step is plasma cutting.

A New Approach to Plasma Cutting

"Part of this vision is automation and establishing world-class manufacturing and state-of-the-art processing," said Rich Steel, Miller Fabrication Solutions’ director of lean manufacturing. "So, as we were looking at new burning systems, we were interested in automated material handling and parts sorting."

The metal fabricator approached Messer Cutting Systems with ideas, and the cutting technology developer pulled its automation partner, Sieger Astor, into the conversation. There weren’t many similar types of installations in the manufacturing world where part sorting of plate was at the core of the cutting operation, but both Messer and Sieger Astor did have some experience in this area. In this case, however, Miller Fabrication Solutions would be one of the first in North America to have this type of setup. After several months of planning, the plasma equipment was delivered for installation at the Pine Creek facility in early spring this year. Commissioning of the line took place in May.

The company now has a plasma cutting system with two Hypertherm 400-amp plasma torches and a table that can accommodate a 96- by 240-in. sheet. The system primarily cuts between 0.625- to 2-in.-thick material, according to Steel.

What might be considered unusual by many shops is that the actual plasma cutting is done inside of an enclosure. While most domestic fabricators might associate this type of arrangement with laser cutting, enclosures are much more common in places like Europe, where workplace regulations regarding worker safety are much more stringent than in the U.S.

"We can run both 400-amp plasma torches, and you can be right beside the machine and talk at a normal level, even with hearing protection in, and hear just fine," Steel said.

Unlike its other two plasma tables, which use water to minimize cutting fumes, this new Mess-er unit has a downdraft system. In combination with the enclosure, the downdraft system eliminates the escape of any fumes.

Material is fed into the plasma cutter via a two-pallet system. While one pallet is inside the enclosure for cutting, the other is outside awaiting a load from the system operator.

When the cutting process is over, the pallet delivers the parts and skeleton to a table for automated part extraction. Two smart cranes identify the cut parts, extract them from the skeleton, and place them on a conveyor that feeds an automated de-slagging machine.

Steel said that the entire changeover—from parts and skeleton drop-off, to the new gantry with raw material entering the enclosure, and the other gantry returning to accept the next load—takes about four minutes. System operators were using the equipment for plasma cutting parts before the automation went live, and it took them almost 27 minutes to complete the changeover. It’s making a big difference.

Automating Deslagging

Miller Fabrication Solutions had invested in machinery for slag removal on plasma-cut parts in 2023. The EdgeBreaker 5000 MAX was the second such purchase from ARKU, which provided an EdgeBreaker 4000 in 2017. (The metal fabricating company also added a FlatMaster 120 for leveling and stress relieving parts in 2017.)

The EdgeBreaker 5000 MAX was a good fit for the new plasma cutting system because it didn’t require parts to be flipped, as was the case with the EdgeBreaker 4000. The smart cranes drop the parts onto the conveyor, which feeds the machine.

As the parts enter the machine, they engage with two sets of deslagging stations. Each station, one for the top of the material and the second for the bottom, is an arm that has multiple rotating discs with deslagging pins, also referred to as slag hammers, that knock the slag and large burrs off the material. Assisting in this effort is the fact that the arms move from side to side, ensuring complete coverage of parts being sent over the roller conveyor.

The machine is designed to process materials up to 4.92 in. thick and up to 51 in. wide. Steel said that the machine could see parts as long as 15 ft. in some instances.

He added that the deslagging machine is not having to work as hard as originally expected. With the company’s plasma cutting experience and the new equipment, technicians have been able to dial in the jobs so that minimal slag is evident in most applications. The machine, however, still provides a valuable service in that no one has to manually grind down that slag or even chip it off.

"The first time a human touches a part from raw material is after it’s processed through the 5000," Steel added.

The plasma cutting system and the deslagging machine do not have the ability as of now to communicate with each other. Because of that, the deslagging machine has to be adjusted for the particular parts widths coming off the plasma table. Steel said the Edgebreaker operator can adjust the machine while part handling is occurring.

Future Possibilities

The automated plasma cutting cell has room for expansion. The new system has enough capacity that Miller Fabrication Solutions plans to take one of its older plasma cutting systems offline at the end of the year.

Steel said that the cell could accommodate two more cutting machines, with the likelihood of the equipment being added as needed. Time will tell if the next system has two plasma cutting torches, a plasma and a laser torch combination, or two laser cutting heads.

The company also might need a new deslagging machine should production numbers grow. Steel added that the company might look to add conveyors that would make it easy to move certain plasma-cut, deslagged parts straight to the FlatMaster for stress relief and straightening.

There’s always room for improvement, after all.
