anufacturers and the trade associations that represent them have made the looming skills gap a major issue over the past decade. Apparently, those efforts are starting to pay off.

In a recent study from PricewaterhouseCoopers and the Manufacturing Institute, a third of the 120 U.S. manufacturers surveyed said that they have had no or little difficulty in hiring talent. Another 44 percent reported only "moderate difficulty" in filling slots.

Also in that "Upskilling Manufacturing: How Technology Is Disrupting America’s Industrial Labor Force" study, about 60 percent said they anticipate that a skills shortage is going to be an issue in the next three years. (Twenty-nine percent of that number already think it exists.) Numbers from the Bureau of Labor Statistics lend credence to that fear as only 263,000 hires were made in manufacturing in May 2016, leaving approximately 90,000 jobs unfilled.

One of the obvious ways to address this dearth of workers with advanced manufacturing skills is to reach out to the next generation at universities, community colleges, and vocational technology schools. That’s one of the goals of the Smartforce Student Summit at the six-day International Manufacturing Technology Show (IMTS), which starts Sept. 12 at McCormick Place in Chicago.

IMTS officials expect students, educators, administrators, and parent chaperones from almost 500 organizations to attend the event. (In 2014 the Smartforce Student Summit had representatives from 34 states participate, which was double the number of states represented at IMTS 2012.) Smartforce participants will have the chance to visit 50 hands-on exhibits.

As part of that direct interaction with manufacturing technologies, students can participate in three distinct challenges at the tradeshow:

1. Design It! opens the door to the world of product design on CAD/CAM software packages (see

Figure 1

).

2. Make It! has students designing key components for a 3-D printed miniature rocket and then testing it to see how it works.

3. Build It! has preregistered teams of students and educators taking on professional fabricating technicians in building a CNC waterjet cutting machine (see

Figure 2

).

WARDJet, Tallmadge, Ohio, is the sponsor of the Build It! segment of the program. Students will fabricate one of the company’s WARDKit waterjets, which is a package of components that fabricators can purchase for assembly at their shops, and the professionals will construct an E series waterjet, which is very similar to the WARDKit models. At the end of the build, one of the educational institutions will have a chance to win the waterjet that the students built.

If you see people feverishly assembling something at IMTS, realize that they are in the midst of a contest to build a waterjet cutting machine. Teams of students and educators from universities and community colleges will be pitted against technicians from WARDJet, shown in the picture assembling a waterjet, to see who can build the machine tool the fastest.

"You could consider this technology somewhat in its infancy in terms of industry adoption. But we are seeing an uptick in interest," said Benjie Massara, a WARDJet product manager. "Even recently we are seeing some high schools that are getting these machines now that the technology is at a good price point. They are transitioning their programs from a wood-based shop to one that has CAD/CAM packages and working with machine tools."

Attendees who register for a conference program also receive access to the show floor. Some of the conference tracks this year are the IMTS 2016 program, which is centered around issues such as improving productivity; improving part quality; and developing a stable, competent workforce; Trends in Advanced Machining, Manufacturing and Materials (TRAM) aerospace conference, which focuses on transferring advanced manufacturing technologies to key players in the aerospace sector; and the Additive Manufacturing Conference, which brings together manufacturing professionals who are interested in additive manufacturing for production parts.
