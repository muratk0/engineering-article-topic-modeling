ot all small welding shops have a formal plan for company growth, but they need to be ready should more customers start throwing work their way.

Luckily, one-shift operations with a handful of employees can start doing some basic things to prepare to successfully add new people and take on larger-volume jobs down the road. In essence, small welding shops can plant the seeds for a culture that fosters employee engagement and empowerment—having everyone working together to fabricate quality products that are delivered on-time and in a safe manner.

The process involves some basic lean manufacturing principles that are practiced in larger organizations to maximize the value they deliver to customers and do so efficiently. While this may seem intimidating to small weld shops, they are likely already using these principles in one form or another—just not as routinely as needed to make business growth as painless as possible.

GETTING THINGS IN ORDER

In many aspects of life, a thorough cleaning can do a lot of good. The same goes for shops.

A lean expert would consider this reorganization a part of 5S, which originated in the Toyota Production System. The five-part framework is tied to creating a space for everything and putting everything in that place.

5S comprises these elements, with the original Japanese words included:

1. Sort (Seiri)

– Determine what’s necessary and ditch the rest.

2. Set in Order (Seiton)

– Take these essential items and find homes for them, ensuring that it’s obvious where they go so they can be easily returned.

3. Shine (Seiso)

– Clean the work area and maintain equipment to promote production efficiency.

4. Standardize (Seiketsu)

– Create rules and visual guidelines so that everyone can stay consistent in eliminating waste, organizing the work area, and keeping areas clean.

5. Sustain (Shitsuke)

– Commit to making these tasks into daily habits and consistently work to seek out other improvement.

Lean manufacturing experts consider 5S to be a foundation from which a lot of other continuous improvement activities can be put in place. A clean and orderly environment opens the doors for a lot of other positive activities to take place.

"It’s from this strong foundation that you’re going to be able to 1) work safely and 2) you’re more likely to produce quality products. 3) Then you’re more likely to deliver on-time to your customers, and 4) you’re going to be doing it with a high degree of productivity," said Jim Floyd, a technical specialist with the Illinois Manufacturing Excellence Center, a part of the MEP National Network. Floyd also has 17 years’ experience implementing and leading lean manufacturing and continuous improvement programs in manufacturing facilities.

Of course, 5S is much more than taking 10 minutes before the end of a shift or workday to tidy up the work area. But that daily cleanup is a good starting point.

From there, a shop’s owner, manager, or lead supervisor must make the case to everyone else that the team needs to take cleanliness to the next level. It might be harder for some to accept, but that’s when they need to understand how 5S practices can make their shop lives better.

"Sometimes when you come into their space and expect them to flip a switch and become neat, tidy, safe, and productive, that might be hard for them to do," Floyd said. "We’re all wired differently."

Floyd said that’s why it’s important to paint a clear picture of what can happen when 5S is fully implemented. Clutter is gone, which eliminates trip hazards and helps people to find what they are looking for. Storage is clearly marked, allowing people to find tools and gear quickly and be able to return it without misplacing it when done. Inefficient practices like work-in-process in an aisle or a hydraulic leak can be identified easily because such abnormalities stick out in a clean shop.

In the end, people feel proud when working in a clean and organized shop. That’s when employee engagement begins to really take shape.

THE DAILY HUDDLE

There aren’t too many welding shops or metal fabricating companies where a shift or day doesn’t start off with some sort of discussion about what’s ahead. This is where people learn what needs to be worked on and share other details, such as an absent co-worker or an offline machine. For some shops, the morning conversation might be just a small catch-up moment in a busy day.

Floyd said this type of interaction is particularly important for company leaders. This "daily management," as Floyd called it, allows those leaders to get in front of others in the company and interact with them.

"Those on the shop floor want to see how the leadership team is supporting them, so it needs to start from that cultural standpoint: how does the leadership team view themselves and the organization, if they view themselves as servant leaders to the organization, making sure those frontline team members, who are the real value adders in the organization, are producing what the customers are actually paying for," Floyd said.

If the shop floor feels supported, that unlocks another level of engagement. And the best way to take advantage of that is for the leader to ask questions, particularly open-ended ones that require a thoughtful answer. That allows workers to share their stories and offer up their unique perspectives about what’s happening on the shop floor.

SHARE THE NEWS

If a shop wants everyone working toward the goal of doing what’s best for the customer and maximizing production efficiency, they need to know how they are doing. Floyd calls for a "scoreboard" of some sort that allows the team to know if it is "winning."

The scoreboard in most places might be something as simple as a whiteboard. In more sophisticated manufacturing environments, it might be monitors that provide real-time information on key performance indicators.

Those KPIs happen to be the points that determine whether the team is meeting expectations. Floyd said that the metrics for a metal fabricating or welding operation typically revolve around safety, quality, delivery, and cost. If an organization is stringing together zero days missed because of a work-related injury, minimal instances of rework, near-perfect on-time delivery, and performance within cost expectations, it’s looking pretty good in terms of customers satisfaction and profitability.

Small and midsize companies may not necessarily have the means to track all of these different factors, and leadership might not have the time to analyze any information gathered. But even though a shop’s small size might prevent it from acting like a much larger manufacturer, it still can focus on an important metric and incorporate that into a daily team meeting.

For example, safety is important in any shop, particularly small ones, where any absence is greatly felt. The team can take time to talk about a near-miss accident or an observation someone had about an unsafe work condition.

If safety is less of a concern because a shop historically has avoided accidents, perhaps it can focus on on-time delivery. The team can discuss the status of projects, identify potential obstacles to meeting a deadline, and reflect on what went right to get parts out the door earlier than expected.

In both of those examples, the shop floor team should have the opportunity to contribute to the conversation, Floyd said.

"It’s their chance to look at the KPIs and have a discussion about what do we need to do potentially to make improvements and what’s getting in the way of success," he explained. "They’re the real subject matter experts. They’re the real change agents."

This type of discussion pulls the shop floor into decision-making, creating a scenario where workers can have an impact on business success. That type of engagement creates a culture that wants to improve. Change is not as intimidating because everyone is working together.

LET’S TAKE A WALK

It’s hard to find a welding shop where the owner isn’t on the shop floor doing what built the business in the first place: welding. That’s something even leaders of the biggest companies can appreciate. It’s important to go out and see what’s going on.

Floyd recounted how Taiichi Ohno, the father of the Toyota Production System, used to drag his leaders out of their cozy offices and told them to stand on the factory floor and watch. That way they could understand what was actually taking place and not rely on spreadsheets and second-hand commentary.

For many shops, this can be a scheduled walk-around (or a Gemba Walk in lean manufacturing parlance), where the leader hits the floor to chat with team leaders in an area of the shop floor where KPIs are posted. It gives everyone another opportunity to talk about factors that are important to the company’s overall performance.

The walk also can be informal. It could be a stroll to observe manufacturing practices, look for waste, and converse with those on the frontline. Conversations with frontline employees can provide insight that simply might not be available in emails or notes buried in work instructions.

SUSTAIN THE CULTURE

Cleanliness, communication, and continuous improvement set the stage for production efficiency. The challenge is sustaining the environment once it is established. That can be easier said than done, particularly for lean shops.

"Where sustainability becomes a challenge is when there is a disconnect between the leadership team and the value adders in the organization," Floyd said. "Let’s say that an organization had a 5S workplace and gotten really organized, and it’s running well. But then we’re in a part of the organization’s lifecycle where it’s growing and sales are hot and heavy. The organization is running overtime, and we’re getting the message to go, go, go.

"It’s sometimes during these situations where an organization will drift away from its good behaviors and good habits that were learned but are now getting pressure in some degree to not spend time doing."

What might seem like a commonsense move to focus on productivity over all other things can have lasting ramifications to a continuous improvement culture. Employees may begin to question leadership’s commitment to real change, prompting people to refrain from buying in to future projects. Employees can feel slighted, realizing that a metric like safety may be minimized in the name of increased production.

"Organizations will fail to sustain because the team will stop hearing the message from their leaders," Floyd said. "Because they don’t hear that message, they might take that to mean that it’s no longer important."

What’s easy to kill is hard to grow. Once a winning culture is in place, an organization wants to maintain that. Trying to relaunch a lean manufacturing culture with a group that has already seen it die once can be very challenging.

READY TO GROW?

Not all welding shops have a desire to grow. That’s OK. There’s no doubt that a lot more responsibility comes with an ever-growing manufacturing company.

But that responsibility doesn’t have to burden one person. More people are able to lift a heavier load than any one single person.

That’s the beauty of incorporating lean manufacturing principles into a small business. People become much more than people that simply perform a task and collect a paycheck. Lean manufacturing puts the frontline workers, or the "value adders" as Floyd describes them, in a position where they can affect company performance. They have a say in how things go.

That’s a powerful tool. Larger organizations looking to capture that same spirit when it doesn’t exist may struggle to change the culture. Smaller companies with fewer people can start to get the basics down and grow leaders that can help to maintain a winning culture.
