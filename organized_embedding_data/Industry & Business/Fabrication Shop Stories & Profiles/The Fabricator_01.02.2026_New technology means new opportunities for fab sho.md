P

lenty of metal fabricators get their start learning to fix things on the family farm. When the farm has a full-fledged machine shop on the property, it becomes kind of hard to avoid a future in metalworking.

Allen Mills grew up around manufacturing most of his life. His grandfather owned the farm, but also on that property in east Indiana was a company called HyFlex, which his grandfather started in 1964 after he came up with an idea for a scissor lift that could completely flatten when not in use—something competing products at the time couldn’t do.

Over time, HyFlex started manufacturing other items, such as pumps and mixers for cement equipment. While the business remained viable over the decades, it never grew to a largescale operation, always calling the farm home.

By the time Mills was a teen, he had a lot of experience cutting, forming, and welding metal and learning about things like hydraulics and electric motors from his dad. "I could probably do more than a lot of grown men at that time," Mills said.

After graduating from Purdue University with an agricultural engineering degree, Mills felt like he wanted to follow in his grandfather’s and dad’s footsteps, so he joined the family business. He also knew he eventually wanted to do his own thing.

For the first year, he worked on building pumps and mixers for the company. During this time, he was trying to introduce new technologies, such as inverters and variable speed drives, to product designs.

He also wanted more modern tools to help him with the job. That’s when he convinced his dad to purchase a shear, a press brake, and a single-turret machine from a bank auction. Someone else’s misfortune was going to be the foundation for an exciting new endeavor.

"I had never done anything with sheet metal at that time, but it was really well-suited for me," Mills said. "As we started to get into that, I started to run into people that had little parts and things they wanted made. So we did that, and it became part of my plan to diversify the company’s income stream."

In 2004, Mills had the opportunity to purchase a used laser cutting machine. That was shortly followed by the purchase of another laser. Mills had the two machine tools producing laser-cut blanks in a newly constructed, 10,000-sq.-ft. shop behind his dad’s pole barn. The small family operation was growing at a rapid pace.

A BUST CAN’T STOP THE GROWTH

Over the next four years, the metal fabrication business picked up. HyFlex had about 30 people showing up to work at the farm, which sat just a few miles off Interstate 70, but Mills knew they needed more space. Meanwhile, he knew he still wanted to keep up with new fabricating technology, which he saw as a way to grow without necessarily adding more labor.

By 2008, he had rented a building in a nearby industrial park. He also purchased his first laser cutting machine with automated load/unload capabilities.

What arguably ended up being the most important investment for the company at the time was the implementation of enterprise resource planning software from Global Shop Solutions. The software made it possible for the shop to put in orders and have information populate other programs without manual intervention. The ERP system provided more accurate costing information. It also connected with the new laser cutting machine.

When the Great Recession hit the U.S. economy toward the end of 2008, business ground to a halt. Mills said he feared going bankrupt. But as the days crept by, he soon realized that he was in an enviable position compared to his competitors.

To ensure that its robotic weld cell would be embraced by everyone, NexGen Manufacturing offered training to all applications engineers, floor supervisors, and technicians.

"We got started with the ERP, and it really turned out to be a growth thing for us at that time. We were able to have really low overhead, and I was able to compete with people at a time when there wasn’t a whole lot of work. Pretty quickly, we became maybe the busiest laser shop on the east side of Indiana."

A MODERN MANUFACTURER

Mills said that by the end of 2009, the company was able to double its output without adding any additional front office staff. The ERP system had proven to be just the tool to help with business expansion.

Today, the metal fabricating business exists on its own, spun off from HyFlex. NexGen Manufacturing inhabits an 85,000-sq.-ft. facility in Knightstown, Ind., with 85 employees. Some of the beliefs that put the fab business on this road to success still act as guideposts as the company seeks to grow as efficiently as it can.

Making the Most of the ERP System.

Running a job shop is a challenging way to make a living. Every day is a new adventure, but it also requires a lot of effort because each job is unique.

Mills said he wanted to get away from making everything for everybody and become more of a contract manufacturer, having more repeat business. That way the shop could leverage its ERP system, making it easier for quoting, building bills of materials, and creating job routers because the work has either been done before or it’s similar to what was done before.

Another thing happened when Mills and his team started working with these repeat customers. The customers saw the value of a shop having advanced controls over its processes and accompanying documentation for most activities, and they wanted to expand their working relationships with this up-and-coming metal fabricator.

The purchase of a powder coating business in nearby Anderson, Ind., allowed NexGen Manufacturing to establish more control over its lead times.

Embracing the ERP System.

Mills always was interested in new technology, so the ERP system was his playground. He and another engineer dove into the software package when it was first implemented and never really stopped exploring.

"Back then, especially with smaller companies, you didn’t have a lot of connectivity in shops, but I knew that the possibility was there," Mills said.

Global Shop officials worked closely with Mills and his team to develop ways to connect to various machine tools added to the shop floor. For instance, NexGen now has 6-kW and 10-kW Bystronic fiber laser cutting machines and five press brakes.

The parties also worked together to build a quoting process that streamlines a lot of other efforts, Mills said. Quoting is not a stand-alone activity. As that quote is being built out, bills of materials and routing are being created as well. When the quote is accepted, only NexGen’s engineers go through the validation process to check the numbers and add proper documentation where necessary. The ERP software sends the appropriate information to the machine tools, and the parts are ready for production.

Continuing to Add New Technology.

NexGen added a Salvagnini P4 panel bender to its shop floor in recent years, and it’s boosted throughput in a major way. The company calls it the "NexGen X-Factor" on its website.

As an example of the panel bender’s impact, Mills recounted the time NexGen had to quote a complex shelving part for a warehousing application. With 16 bends, the part was arguably too complex for a press brake and would have required the use of transfer presses if it were to be done efficiently and consistently. Instead, the panel bender was able to take a part that was going to need at least 12 minutes of production time and produce it in about 70 seconds.

"It’s reduced our work in process, and that’s something that we’re always trying to get down," Mills said.

Learning From Mistakes.

Although NexGen’s employee roster has grown in the past several years, its number of welders has decreased a bit. The implementation of cobots and a robotic welding cell have helped to absorb some of the additional welding activity.

Mills said this is the second attempt to implement automated welding at NexGen. The first effort sputtered because the management team didn’t do enough to make the transition away from manual welding easier.

"The second time through, we had some really good application engineers on staff and got them trained on the robot. Then they went out and transferred that knowledge to the shop floor supervisors, who then trained the operators," Mills said.

Growing With Customers.

Another factor that has helped maintain welding productivity is having engineers produce fixture designs while creating 3D CAD files. When the jobs are won, the welders can get right to work, not having to worry about figuring out how to build the fixture because the instructions are already available. The welders get the tools out of the rack, build the fixture on the Siegmund modular welding tables, weld the parts, take apart the fixture, and put it back in the rack.

"We probably tripled the output when we’re doing some of these prototypes," Mills said.

With quick turnaround now possible during the early stages of a product development lifecycle, NexGen can grow with its customers as some product lines grow. The company can assist with prototype development, move production to cobots as product sales climb, and eventually use a robotic weld cell once the product has reached a mature level in the market.

Removing Bottlenecks.

At the end of 2022, NexGen purchased a powder coating company about 30 minutes from its current facility. It was the logical step for removing a painful bottleneck.

Increasingly, customers didn’t just want metal parts; they wanted them finished. Mills said that at one time, his company had four trucks traveling all over to drop off and pick up powder-coated parts. When an opportunity arose to purchase one of those contract powder coaters, he jumped at it.

"It’s brought a lot of big companies to us that we might not have been able to supply before," he added.

REPLICATING THE FORMULA ELSEWHERE

The acquisition of the powder coating company might be just the first of other potential deals, Mills said, particularly as people look to retire from a manufacturing career. He thinks that NexGen’s business model can help to transform other organizations that might be otherwise considered stagnant.

"We’re looking forward to strategic acquisitions of companies that have good people and resources, and maybe they just need to be revived with technology," he said. "We’d like to layer that approach across another business."
