oug Layman, 53, is in the midst of a transition at Layman Fabrication Inc. in North Vernon, Ind. Whereas he once was content to run his small fabrication shop alone out of the family turkey barn to pay the bills and earn a modest living, his son Kyle has now joined the business. Growth is the name of the game.

Kyle has helped to lead the five-man shop to annual sales increases of at least 20 percent over the last two years. (You can read more about the shop in "Talkin’ ’bout the next generation, p. 86.) Further expansion is going to require more workers, and Doug isn’t so sure that the pool is deep with talent.

"It’s a dying profession, and it’s only going to continue," he said. "It’s going to be a problem because there are a lot of things that robots just can’t build."

No matter what you hear about next-generation dual-arm robots that can work side by side with a human co-worker and have "situational awareness" that prevents the robot from wildly swinging around and taking the head off that co-worker, you can bet we are several decades away from robots replacing real metal fabrication talent. Fabricators can respond quickly and creatively to production challenges in a high-volume, low-mix environment; robots require some kind of programming to be helpful in the smallest sense.

When it comes to industrial fabrication, which is Layman Fabrication’s specialty, robots simply aren’t going to be able to walk into a facility, listen to the customer describe the need for a chute to carry hot billet to a forge press, draw up a quick design that gets customer approval, and then rework that drawing into a print from which fabricators can work. Actually, you can’t find a lot of humans that can do all that either.

As companies cry out for a new generation of manufacturing talent, many are looking for basic skills. They want a welder who can focus on a task and produce a consistent weld over an entire shift with limited breaks. The money might be good, but the work hardly could be called challenging.

Educators rarely use the word "gifted" to describe those who gravitate to industrial technology classes. However, that’s shortchanging the students that have a real interest in the subject matter.

Actually, some smaller fabricators, Layman Fabrication included, have benefited from such scenarios. Metal fabricators leave those environments looking for varied projects and a more relaxed working environment. The money may not be as good, but they have the chance to hone their talents and grow as craftsmen.

Doug isn’t boastful about his own skills or those who work in his shop. He’s matter-of-fact as he describes that same hot billet chute that has to be designed, fabricated off-site, and delivered in sections to be installed and function as specified.

"Engineers can’t even engineer that," he said.

It takes a special talent, but one that has a hard time standing out in a culture that excessively pushes a four-year college degree as the only path to financial success. In fact, Kyle had teachers tell him that he needed college to make it in the world. Six years after high school graduation, Kyle is running a successful business, and some of his classmates who went the traditional college route are working on the shop floor of nearby manufacturing facilities.

Other classmates did get degrees and now have good jobs, but that wasn’t the case for all. That’s the lesson that needs to be learned.

Fabricating skills should be celebrated as much as the ability to hit a baseball or carry a tune. Fabricating something may not lend itself to entertaining the masses, but the talent and practices required to be good at the craft are certainly traits that can be admired by the community at large.

Like the effort to boost the image of manufacturing in today’s society, the process of recognizing and nurturing manufacturing talent in the schools is still in its infancy. But steps are being taken, and that’s reason for excitement. The SkillsUSA

®

National Leadership and Skills Conference, held June 22-26 in Louisville, Ky., this year, will host hundreds of students that have won state-based skills competitions. Robotics teams are being formed on high school campuses, and those efforts are resulting in nationwide robotic championship competitions. Manufacturing talent is making its way into the spotlight.

Still, work remains. In the report "Overwhelming support: U.S. public opinions on the manufacturing industry" from Deloitte and The Manufacturing Institute, only 37 percent of survey respondents indicated they would encourage their children to pursue a manufacturing career.

Companies and industry associations need to keep working and ensure that manufacturing talent doesn’t go undiscovered. The future of the industry depends on those efforts.
