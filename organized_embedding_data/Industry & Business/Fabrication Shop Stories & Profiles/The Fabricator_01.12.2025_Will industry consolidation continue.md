# Will industry consolidation continue?

[TARİH: 01.12.2025 The Fabricator]

Biz Talk

Maybe, but not in every niche of metal fabrication

TIM HESTON

D

uring Q1 2026, Ryerson Holding Corp. will have completed its merger with Olympic Steel. According to an announcement from Ryerson made in early November, the combined company will create the nation’s second largest service center, "value-added processor, and distributor … The transaction is expected to generate approximately $120 million in annual synergies" within two years.

The announcement has raised some eyebrows, especially for the smalland medium-sized players in the service center space. From the fabricator’s perspective, the term "value-added processor" caught my attention. Besides purchasing fabricators outright, service centers have performed first-step processing for years, and that fact still ruffles a few feathers among custom and contract metal fabricators. In many cases—especially for high-volume, blanking-only work—service centers bid against their own customers. That’s nothing new, just a market reality.

What will the most recent consolidation mean for fabricators? What are the long-term implications, especially when it comes to those value-adding services? Just imagine if a large service center with a national footprint had instant quote capabilities, not just for cutting but for a full range of value-adding processes, including bending and welding. Connect job information with real-time data from literally hundreds of sheet and plate cutting machines, as well as downstream forming and welding, and you attain the "mass customization" ideal that’s been dreamt about for years.

How realistic is this? To be honest, I really don’t know. What I do know is that to scale up and communicate effectively across dozens of plants, a company’s processes and machines would need to be dialed in to an extraordinary degree—no warped material crashing into laser heads, no denesting bottlenecks, no forming or welding issues. Still, the technological building blocks are there.

How do fabricators compete? Many go vertical — deep into complementary niches that require specific services a mammoth metal supplier really couldn’t provide. This might involve more welding and assembly, but it also might involve logistics, supply chain management, and design assistance.

Yes, the metal service center landscape is more consolidated than ever. The Nos. 1 and 2 players—Reliance Inc. and now a combined Ryerson and Olympic—will serve a sizable portion of the metal market. And the sheer number of deals in recent months and years is notable (something The Fabricator will start digging into over the coming months.)

When it comes to value-added services, the consolidated metal suppliers might win on volume, but they simply can’t have the focus of a contract metal fabricator that serves a collection of disparate and yet still complementary market niches.

And the market niches are extremely wide-ranging. In fact, just a week before the Ryerson-Olympic merger was announced, I visited a welding job fair at the Georgia Trade School in Acworth, Ga. During my visit, I stopped by the hiring companies exhibiting at the fair—and immediately was taken aback by the sheer variety of jobs available. I talked with recruiters about pirate ships, Pearl Harbor, deciduous trees, and the U.S. embassy in the Czech Republic—all within a span of about 30 minutes.

Pirate ships and trees came up in my conversation with Verlicia Carroll, director of human resources for Atlanta-based Exploring Inc. She described the varied fabrications ID3 Group (subsidiary of Exploring) has made for Atlanta’s burgeoning film industry and beyond. Animated, her hands outlining the shape of a hull from bow to stern, she painted a verbal picture of a recent project involving a welded subframe of aluminum frames clad with plywood and shot with a hard coat. It was a pirate ship destined for a dinner show venue (think Medieval Times, but with pirates) in Panama City, Fla.

"Within our custom fabrications group, anything you think of, these guys can design it, build it, and bring it to life," Carroll said. "Movie studios come to us for anything from basic movie sets to a highly complex custom fabrication needed for a specific scene."

Minutes later, I spoke with Jonny Herrera. He took pipe welding at the Kentucky Welding Institute. After spending 16-hour days learning the craft, he launched into a pipe welding career—not at the local factory, but overseas at the U.S. Embassy in the Czech Republic, welding bulletproof safe-haven structures. After that, he returned to Kentucky and tackled another golden opportunity: working with a contractor to weld critical pipe structures and other elements at Naval Station Pearl Harbor in Hawaii.

The visit tempered my view on industry consolidation. Sure, when it comes to first-stage fabrication, we might see more, but when you get further down the value chain, the niches are just too various. The trick is finding the underserved ones, then alleviating as many pain points as possible.
