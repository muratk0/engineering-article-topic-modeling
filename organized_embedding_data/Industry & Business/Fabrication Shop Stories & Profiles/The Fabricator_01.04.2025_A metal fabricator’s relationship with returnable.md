# A metal fabricator’s relationship with returnable packaging

[TARİH: 01.04.2025 The Fabricator]

Material Handling

It makes sense when it’s good for business

By

Dan Davis

I

n the world of metal fabricating, the focus of returnable packaging isn’t so much on saving the world but instead on saving parts and assemblies from damage.

Certainly, companies look to avoid sending excessive amounts of material to the landfill. That’s why they actively engage in selling scrap and reusing pallets and totes when they can.

But when it comes to customer relationships where returnable packaging is part of the mix, it just makes good business sense, according to Atlas Manufacturing, a metal fabricator with locations in Minneapolis and Chippewa Falls, Wis.

"The sheet metal industry is seeing a shift from simple parts to more complex assemblies," said Nawal Whig, manager of sales engineering/technical programs, Atlas Manufacturing. "Because of this, our customers are seeing a bigger need for going beyond the off-the-shelf options for packaging to looking at other options to safely transit their parts."

Getting Into Returnable Packaging

What really got Atlas to take a closer look at its packaging was the first couple of times it had to make international shipments. The length of the journey and the multiple transfers, from truck to container to truck again, provided ample opportunity for the assemblies to be damaged.

Packaging’s first role is protecting its contents. In some settings, the packaging also is designed to be rolled right up to an assembly area and provide easy access to the parts.

The first project called for shipments to Singapore. The long-distance delivery pretty much guaranteed that the packaging would be nonreturnable.

However, another project, where assemblies were sent to Puerto Rico, provided another opportunity to develop specialty packaging that was returnable. Atlas sent the shipments to its Puerto Rican client, who then used the packaging to send assemblies to another value-added supply chain partner in the U.S. From there, the returnable packaging made its way back to Atlas. This circular loop of usage has been going on for a couple of years.

"I would say that the international kickoff of those projects really got us to focus on what is needed to avoid damage to products and get the right packaging made," said Mark Engel, Altas’ executive vice president.

Over the past 10 years, Whig said that Atlas probably has worked with roughly 15 customers where returnable packaging was part of the business relationship. These relationships are not only characterized by repeat work and larger-than-normal volumes for a job shop, but also by familiarity that is achieved over several months. Engel said these types of customers typically start out asking Atlas for help on prototype development, which then evolves into prepping for production, small-lot fabricating, and then larger and more consistent orders. By that time, the customer relationship has advanced to the point where Atlas knows the work and feels confident in offering suggestions on how to improve part delivery for the betterment of both parties.

Returnable packaging is not a part of every metal fabricator-customer relationship, but with the right volume and circumstances, such packaging might make economic sense.

Motivations to Consider Returnable Packaging

Part safety is an obvious reason for most packaging decisions, but especially where more complex and valuable parts and assemblies are involved. Returnable packaging comes into play when the engineered shipping solution, likely comprising wood, foam, and casters, is durable enough to be used repeatedly.

But those aren’t the only motivations. There are other aspects to consider when talking about customer relationships.

Ease of Integrating Into a Customer’s Assembly Operations.

In some instances, returnable packaging is designed not only for shipping but also for lean manufacturing reasons. Engel recalled one instance where a fabricated door, completed with hinges and latches, was supplied to a customer in a custom-designed wooden cart that contained the 6-ft. doors in an upright position.

After delivery, the cart was wheeled right to the customer’s assembly area. Assembly technicians removed the door directly from the cart and slid it over to the cabinet, where it could be attached. The technician didn’t have to lift it out of a container that was below waist height.

In another example, a customer wanted parts delivered in plastic collapsible totes that could be brought to the assembly area. When emptied, the totes could be flattened when they were unfolded. Whereas a semitrailer could be filled with plastic totes on a delivery to the customer, Atlas could get all of the collapsible totes back on three pallets, stacked together and banded up.

Quick Identification of Part Quantity.

So much of lean manufacturing is based on visual clues. It’s a simplistic solution to a lot of production challenges that can grow very complex if they go unanswered.

Take

kanban

, for example.

Kanban

is a visual project management system that originated in post-World War II Japan. Shop floor decisions can be made quickly when visual reminders alert employees that something needs to be addressed.

In the world of metal fabricating,

kanban

is often associated with replenishing inventory, whether parts for a hardware insertion process or components needed for end product assembly. Someone sees an empty or near-empty bin and knows that replenishment is required.

The same visibility is often desired with custom-designed packaging, particularly if it’s a part of assembly operations. Technicians can look at the cart and instantly recognize when a new cart needs to be replaced.

"Our program manager is working with a customer now where we have designed packaging that takes into account their daily intake of parts," Whig said. "We came up with a cart where we have 32 blades [for a server system] stacked on shelves. It’s visible. You can easily see how many are in there."

That wooden container on wheels gets rolled directly to assembly operations, Whig continued. By the end of the shift, the container should be empty and returned to shipping, where it can be sent back to Atlas.

More Efficient Delivery Method.

The focus on the customer makes a lot of sense when discussing returnable packaging, but sometimes the spotlight should be on the metal fabricator.

"We have operations huddles every morning, and typically what will happen in those operations meetings is we’ll talk about pain points. For example, we might have some issues getting product ready for development," Whig said. "When these problems repeat itself over and over again with these OEM customers with repeatable volume, then we’ll evaluate labor and the materials savings, possibly leading to an investment in returnable packaging."

Watching a couple of people in shipping spending hours wrapping individual weldments for loading onto a pallet can lead a team to look for a way to place those same pieces in some sort of structured container with separate compartments. The time and expense tied to a new approach to packaging can pay off with new efficiencies in shipping.

Overcoming Obstacles

Cost also is a concern for a customer. If they are being sold on the benefits of using returnable packaging, they need to know that the cost associated with the program won’t outweigh any potential cost savings.

Engel recalled one of the Atlas programs that involved a packaging container to be rolled directly to the customer’s assembly area. To help achieve the customer’s buy-in, Atlas took on the packaging container design cost, even to the point of having two separate companies design wood transport carriers.

These were no small containers, measuring 6 ft. wide by 6 ft. tall by 3 ft. deep. The final design also had to take into account how these carriers were organized in semitrailers and moved on and off the truck.

"You almost have to go through a few of these cycles to get internal buyin at the customer," Engel said.

A successful project opens the doors to more opportunities, even perhaps having the customer share costs on developing the next returnable package design. That collaboration strengthens the metal fabricator-customer relationship to the point where each party becomes invaluable to each other’s operations.

Guidance for Other Metal Fabricators

Any discussion about returnable packaging has the potential to quickly expand beyond the need for returnable totes. That’s why it’s good to have access to expertise. For many shops, this will call for contacts outside their own four walls.

Atlas recognized that early on as it got more involved in these types of packaging discussions. It doesn’t make foam inserts or specialize in building wooden transport carts.

"We’ve been really successful because we have really good relationships with packaging suppliers, with each one having their own special niche," Engel said.

He added that these companies approach prototype designs from very unique viewpoints. When they are asked to compete on the same projects, they rarely deliver similar ideas. Just as there is more than one way to fabricate a metal part, packaging experts have their own thoughts on how those parts should be delivered.

Whig said that these returnable packaging discussions should be looked at as more than an opportunity to address a pain point in shipping. It can be the first step in developing more collaboration with the customer.
