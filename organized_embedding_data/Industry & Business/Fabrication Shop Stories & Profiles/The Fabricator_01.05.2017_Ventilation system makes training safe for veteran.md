# Ventilation system makes training safe for veterans

[TARİH: 01.05.2017 The Fabricator]

Applications

SITUATION

For Navy veteran Hernán Luis y Prado, the idea behind Workshops for Warriors dawned on him in the garage of his home outside Washington, D.C., where wounded war veterans came to tinker in his workshop to pass the time.

When the number of "tinkerers" grew from two to 10 and they started requesting drill presses and welders to beef up his workbench, Luis y Prado and his girlfriend (now wife) liquidated their belongings and poured the proceeds into founding Workshops for Warriors, a 501(c)3 nonprofit school that enables veterans to qualify quickly for jobs in advanced manufacturing while addressing a critical skills gap in the workforce.

Today, after a move across the country to San Diego and three expansions, Workshops for Warriors is booming at capacity in a 13,000-squarefoot facility. The program trains an average of 50 students every semester, and there’s a waiting list of 500 applicants. The free, accredited welding and advanced machining courses range from 112 to 160 hours, with classes running 12 hours a day, six days a week. The accelerated schedule enables graduates to earn nationally recognized credentials in just four months.

For now, Workshops for Warriors relies completely on donations to keep its school running. With the program bursting at the seams, its building has needed improvements, and for many years, it functioned without basics—such as a ventilation system.

"We had a big fan that blew air. That was it," said Luis y Prado. "We could only teach beginning-level welding, and not very often because the place would get smoked out. We couldn’t teach any fluxed-cored or tungsten inert gas welding because the shielding gas would go away. So we weren’t able to give the students the experience required for jobs that are industry-standard."

RESOLUTION

What happened next represents the growing corporate support from manufacturing companies that Workshops for Warriors has come to rely on. While representatives from Amada Corp. were contributing sheet metal, they recognized the fumes problem and asked their own venting supplier, Donaldson Co., to recommend a solution.

Scott Thomas, Donaldson’s regional sales director in California, remembers the day he and Donaldson Territory Manager Bob Walters took a tour through the facility.

"It was not a healthy work environment," said Thomas. "Hexavalent chrome is a known carcinogen produced when stainless steel is welded. And there are OSHA standards for other respirable fine particles. The school clearly needed fume control to be recognized by welding associations."

At the recommendation of Thomas and Walters, Donaldson executives agreed to donate a Torit

®

Downflo™ dust collector to Workshops for Warriors. The high-efficiency model was chosen with total operating costs in mind. With its 48 cartridges and fine fiber filter media, the Downflo is engineered to produce cleaner air with up to twice the filter life—a key advantage for a nonprofit trying to keep a lid on maintenance expenses.

Over the next six months, Walters, an Army veteran himself, made many trips to Workshops for Warriors, drawing plans for a ventilation system that would not only solve the immediate hazard, but grow with the program.

"We needed to factor in the number of welding stations they would eventually have, what kind of ducting that required, the ducting layout, and a place outside to locate the collector," explained Walters. "Our motivation was seeing what these veterans—some with lost legs or arms—were dealing with as they tried to recoup a life that was functional."

But the system called for other components beyond the dust collector, so Walters worked his connections in the industry. One Donaldson vendor supplied the ductwork and control panel. Blender Products Inc. provided a Spark Cooler

®

for spark abatement. And New York Blower supplied the fan free of charge. Reliant Steel and Aluminum agreed to contribute steel.

There was an immediate payoff in air quality and compliance with regulations.

"We’ve had no problems with inspections, and now our students don’t have to worry about safety as they’re training for a career," said Luis y Prado. "In fact, the Donaldson ventilation system has an incredible bandwidth, so we’re drawing over twice the fumes that are required at the welding surface. There’s no way we could have afforded to buy that level of equipment."

Installed in 2014, the equipment has enabled Workshops for Warriors to triple its welder training from six to 18 booths, and it will accommodate a planned expansion to 60 booths in the years ahead. Today welding courses include shielded metal arc (SMAW), gas metal arc (GMAW), gas tungsten arc (GTAW), and flux cored arc (FCAW). Machining courses include CAD and operation, plasma and laser cutting, and 3-, 4-, and 5-axis milling and lathing.

"We now have more installed welding booths on the West Coast than the U.S. Navy, the Marine Corps, British Aerospace and Engineering Systems, and General Dynamics combined—and that’s only possible because of Donaldson," said Luis y Prado.

"We have so many vets going through our program who say they’re tired of people trying to give them something," he continued. "They don’t want handouts. They want a way to transition. And that’s what we’re able to offer them. Not give them. Offer them."

Participating in that mission is gratifying for Donaldson and the other companies that have become donation partners of Workshops for Warriors.

"It’s a special feeling to know you played a small part in helping others," Thomas remarked. "The process opened my own eyes to the sheer number of veterans who have contributed to our country and come back without the necessary skills to move into the workforce. Playing a small role in correcting that has been rewarding."

Racing pit box manufacturer switches gears with waterjet cutting machine

SITUATION

For years Franklin, Tenn.-based Murrco Fabrication’s competition pit boxes have been the go-to tool cart for Saturday night racers.

Mike Murray, Murrco’s president and owner, decided to add waterjet cutting services to his one-man fab shop after noticing a growing demand for the service from his customer base.

Waterjet’s ability to cut a variety of materials and its reach into multiple industries were attractive to Murray, whose business weathered the Great Recession and now is benefiting from the rebounding economy.

"The waterjet service market is booming, and I wanted to get involved and establish myself and my company as a waterjet service provider," he said.

RESOLUTION

Murrco equipped its waterjet shop with a new industrial CNC waterjet cutting system built by Jet Edge. The machine can cut precision parts from almost any material in sheet sizes up to 5 by 13 feet. It cuts parts with a 55,000-PSI supersonic jet of water mixed with garnet abrasive, leaving a sanded, smooth edge with no burrs, slag, or heat-affected zone.

The company powers the waterjet with a hydraulic intensifier pump and fitted the system with IGEMS waterjet soft ware for CAD/CAM and nesting. The firm uses dtiCORE™ diamond orifices and Diamond Guard filters from Diamond Technology Innovations (DTI).

Since opening the new waterjet cutting service in August 2016, the metal fabricator has extended its business beyond the motorsports industry, gaining business from Nashville-area home builders trying to keep pace with the city’s roaring housing boom. Murrco has been running its waterjet at a breakneck pace, cutting primarily ornamental iron products, including handrails, custom mailboxes, and street signs. The company also is cutting a variety of race car parts for local teams, as well as parts for its signature pit boxes.

"As time goes on, I’m sure some new products will be added to Murrco’s racing product line," Murray said. "Production-type work in the motorsports industry is the goal.

"Having waterjet cutting services in-house opens up a wide array of possibilities and new markets for Murrco," he said. "I have cut parts from aluminum, mild steel, graphite, and wood. The thickest I have cut has been 2-inch-thick mild steel, and it was amazing how well the finished product turned out."
