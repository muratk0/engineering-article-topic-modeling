# Are we close to having humanoid robots in the shop?

Automation

Louisiana structural steel shop is interested in finding out

By

Dan Davis

rial Designer Render, Persona AI

I

n summer 2025, you read a news item about plans for humanoid robots to be deployed in South Korean shipyards to tackle welding tasks. You don’t think much about it because the commercial deployment is planned for the beginning of 2027 and, well, it’s going to take place thousands of miles away in an industry that is pretty different from the typical job shop environments that The Fabricator covers.

In January, you get an email pitching a new pilot program with the goal of advancing the use of humanoid robots in heavy-duty fabrication environments. More specifically, the email highlights plans to use SSE Steel Fabrication in St. Bernard Parish, La., as the real-world laboratory for the pilot.

A follow-up phone call several weeks later reveals that the small structural steel shop in South Louisiana is working with the same humanoid hardware developer planning to provide robots to the South Korean shipyards. A story that once seemed a world away now has domestic roots—in a small fab shop with all the hallmarks of metal fabricators typically appearing in The Fabricator.

Could humanoid robots, which once seemed only possible in works of science fiction, find a home in a real-world fab shop?

LOOKING FOR A FABRICATING EDGE

Justin Airhart, SSE Steel Fabrication’s COO, believes they can. In fact, he’s wondering why it took so long.

He said attending trade shows and seeing expensive equipment that hasn’t changed too much from the same technology being offered 10 years ago makes him wonder, particularly in the age of self-driving cars and AI. Meanwhile, the tape measure is still a must-have for the fabricating work being done in his 20,000-sq.-ft. shop.

"I can’t go and buy a $1.5 million piece of equipment because the world’s going to change. What’s the standard going to be? Will that be the standard for today or yesterday? Tomorrow’s standard is going to be even greater," Airhart said. From his vantage point, humanoid robots will be the standard in the not-so-distant future.

SSE Steel is a 70-person shop doing all sorts of heavy fabrication work—from major infrastructure projects to handrails for a local school—moving between 300 to 800 tons of steel fabrications per month. Even with the variety in work, the company still has invested in advanced fabricating technology, such as a PythonX beam processor roughly 10 years ago, and recently a 6-kW Piranha fiber laser cutting machine.

With that said, SSE Steel isn’t in a position to be investing in additional beam processing lines and robotic assembly systems. It’s already operating in tight confines, where large jobs call for creative spacing on the shop floor, so it really doesn’t have the room to drop in equipment with a huge footprint. Also, Airhart said he’s looking to make a technological leap that is both affordable and impactful for a small shop.

Watching Fox News one day, he finally found what he was looking for. Persona AI CEO Nic Radford was chatting with the news personalities about his company’s robots destined for the South Korean shipyards. The conversation immediately prompted Airhart into action.

"I sent an email to them in May of last year and said, ‘Guess what? I’ve got the data you need for your robots,’" he said. "I’ve got an ant farm for them to study."

Airhart reached out to the right party. Radford has a long history with robotic technology: He led the development of the Valkyrie humanoid robot for NASA in the late 2000s and early 2010s, and in 2014 he founded Houston Mechatronics, now known as Nauticus Robotics. With fellow industry veteran Jerry Pratt, Radford started Persona AI in what one industry writer called "one of the highest-profile humanoid startups to date."

What’s unusual about the Persona AI startup, however, is that it’s not chasing the same industries, like automotive manufacturing and logistics, as other humanoid robotics companies. It’s targeting heavy industries, one of which is shipbuilding, going as far as partnering with the American Bureau of Shipping, the global authority on maritime standards.

As Airhart began his discussion with Persona AI, he looped in Louisiana Economic Development officials, who were helpful in putting the robotics company in touch with other nearby shipbuilders and manufacturers of heavy-duty fabrications.

"So what SSE and the state of Louisiana allow for us is something closer, that is not up to the scale of a 50,000-person shipyard. And they allow us much more opportunity to collect data to pretrain and then train the models," Radford said. "It’s an incredible proving ground and engineering test bed as we then also get into production-oriented activities."

Radford called the deployment of his robots at SSE Steel "a dress rehearsal" for the deployment in South Korea in early 2027. Even then, it’ll be a staged rollout, gradually introducing robots and tweaking their performances before more robots are installed.

WHAT CAN THE ROBOTS DO?

Radford added that the focus on the shipbuilding industry and heavy fabrication makes sense because they struggle to find enough workers. The work is challenging for any human, particularly for an eight-hour shift, day after day. Manufacturers have invested in CNC equipment for cutting blanks and in robotic welding systems where it makes sense, but that only addresses part of the work in these environments.

"There is already a lot of automation deployed in the yard, but by far the lion’s share of activities to do are those that really require mobility, the dexterity of people," Radford said. "So if you can’t hire people, you need the mobility and dexterity of people, and you’ve automated everything else you can, what’s your solution? Well, humanoids."

Airhart is interested in what the robots can do in terms of fitting and tacking up something like ⅜-in. connection plates to beams. He’s encouraged by what Persona AI officials have shown him might be possible.

"Finding a fitter is typically everybody’s golden child in this industry," Airhart said. "Somebody that can fit materials is harder to find than a welder."

That might not be possible tomorrow, but in 18 months, it might be closer to reality. Having a couple of robots fitting parts and working with two welders on the graveyard shift could be a tremendous productivity boost for a small shop, and Airhart believes the humanoid robots will play even bigger roles in the future. It’s just a matter of having them learn how to operate in a fabricating environment and do it safely with their human colleagues.

"This humanoid robot will have a hand-held plasma in its hand, and it will be walking down a layout of 10 beams. It will be able to do everything that a beamline or a table can do just by walking over to the raw piece of material—without having the whole setup of the machine," Airhart said.

That would do wonders for quality control as well, because the robot is able to lean on the original 3D files as a guide for cutting holes and other shapes. The robot’s vision system then ensures that those cuts are done according to the print specifications.

The tasks don’t end there. Perhaps the robot could be used to deburr parts, sort parts after they’re laser- or plasma-cut, or just move parts from one location in the facility to another.

Radford calls humanoid robots the best of both industrial robots, which require guarding according to safety regulations, and cobots, which are designed to work closely with humans.

"We want to combine the raw capability of the industrial manipulators with the assistive and safe-to-workaround features of cobots," he said.

INSPIRED TO DO MORE

The conversations about advanced robotics and the power of AI to help in training them has inspired Airhart to see what exactly he can do using his structural steel fabrication knowledge and online AI systems. He’s spent plenty of weekends and late nights working on different projects, and he said he likes some of the early returns.

For instance, Airhart said that quality control has been a bottleneck for SSE Steel, and he purchased a software package that used augmented reality with the hopes that it could speed up tasks like verifying measurements, identifying errors, and guiding fitters during fit-up. While it looked promising, Airhart said the shop floor found that it took too long to set up, requiring that tags be placed strategically on a workpiece and then logged by AR goggles before the software could deliver the virtual overlay.

Thinking that self-driving cars have the ability to see the world around them, Airhart wondered why a mobile phone couldn’t deliver the same performance. It comes with a camera system. Could the right software turn the mobile phone into a QC tool?

After several weeks of going back and forth with an AI engine, Airhart said he’s close to creating his own QC scanner that operates on the same phone that people use to order lunch and update social media platforms. It’ll be able to tell him if workpieces meet print specifications, and if not, what’s wrong.

"Now I don’t have a system that’s built for everybody. I just need to add some salt and pepper to make it taste better," he said.

That’s just the beginning. Using the same AI engine, he’s also worked on developing his own version of Tekla software that helps him keep track of QC documents and maintenance records. Future plans call for the creation of his own estimating software and a 3D tool to help the shipping department load deliveries into trucks more efficiently.

In the meantime, Persona AI will be testing out its robots on the SSE Steel shop floor. The results won’t be evident for a while, but the anticipation is building. The South Korean shipyards will get their robots first, but Airhart hopes to get his shortly thereafter, even if it might be two years down the road.

"Tomorrow is a lot faster coming than people think," Airhart said.

Radford echoed that sentiment. If you are talking about a technology adoption curve marked by a slow start, accelerated adoption, and a tipping point where adoption flattens out while working its way to saturation, Radford said that the U.S. manufacturing community isn’t even at the takeoff point yet when it comes to humanoid robots.

"You’re just starting to see that. But we’ve been working on them for a long time," he said. "What AI has allowed us to do is make them pretty useful."

Now it’s time to work on the infrastructure. These humanoid robots might not have spouses or drinking buddies, but they still need to be recharged.
