# American-made monozukuri

[TARİH: 01.08.2013 The Fabricator]

From the Editor-in-Chief

Oct. 4 is the perfect chance to show off domestic manufacturing done right

Most Americans don’t know much about Japan. They know the country exports automobiles, electronics, and Godzilla, but probably don’t know much more than that. Even something as pervasive as sushi in American culture is foreign to some. (My mom, born and bred in the Deep South, refers to a California roll as “SOO-chee.”)

Manufacturers, on the other hand, know a great deal about Japanese manufacturing methods. They have been trying to copy or adapt Japanese practices over the last four decades. Terms such as

kaizen

(Japanese for “improvement) and

poka-yoke

(“mistake-proofing”) are now part of the everyday lexicon of most U.S. manufacturers. Those unfamiliar with the terms likely have at least heard of them, if not practiced them.

Now imagine the other side of this coin. Although a larger percentage of the Japanese population has a working knowledge of the U.S. and its history, do they really know what makes this country unique? They might know about Elvis and Route 66, but do they really know how the American manufacturing worker goes about his job?

The Japanese have a much better idea today, but it’s been a long time coming. For instance, in the days after the North American Free Trade Agreement went into effect in the mid-1990s, Japanese vehicle manufacturers were faced with the pressures of including more North American content in the vehicles assembled on this continent. That led to trade junkets to the U.S. where Japanese OEMs were looking for suppliers to shore up their U.S. supply chains.

Upon walking into the U.S. injection molding and metal forming shops, they didn’t see the pristine white floors and white walls that they might have seen in similar shops in Japan. These U.S. manufacturing operations were not as well-lit and actually might have had dirt or oil on the floor. In fact, even the floors weren’t clearly marked to separate employee movement from manufacturing activity. They might have nodded their heads in thanking their U.S. guests for their hospitality, but these Japanese representatives were shaking their heads as they were departing. This was not the way manufacturing was done in Japan.

“They had difficulty finding companies that had the same methodology and vision that the Japanese company had,” said Ellis Oishi, general manager, JIT Automation, Scarborough, Ont., Canada.

Oishi’s company is a supplier of quick die change products to the automotive industry. He was an observer of the disconnect that existed between Japanese OEMs and potential U.S. suppliers. However, it wasn’t until he attended an industry gathering in 2009 that he learned the term that defines this cultural gap:

monozukuri

, the Japanese word that roughly translates to “the process of making things.” Not only did American companies not look like their Japanese counterparts, they didn’t operate like them either, according to Oishi.

So over the last 20 years, Japanese companies have tried to do just that: make U.S. companies more like them. For the most part, they have been very successful, even to the point where the influence of certain continuous improvement activities exist outside of supply chains that work with Japanese manufacturers. The spirit of

monozukuri

, which has been in the spotlight since Japan rebuilt itself following World War II but has existed as part of the country’s makeup for centuries, has now become ingrained in U.S. metal manufacturers.

“Some people can make a copy of a product, but they can’t copy the way that the product was made,” Mitsuo Oka-moto, president of machine tool manufacturer Amada Co. Ltd., told visiting trade journalists in May.

Okamoto was speaking in reference to Japanese metal fabricators’ ability to stand out in the face of new competition in places like China and Vietnam that are able to compete aggressively on price. But his point is relevant to American fabricators as well. They need to ensure manufacturing processes are bulletproof and flexible, making them more valuable supply chain partners and more difficult to replace by other competitors, no matter where they might be in the world. They are developing American-made

monozukuri

.

The FABRICATOR

highlights these shops on a regular basis. Not only is it good publicity for the shops that are gracious enough to show us what’s going on behind the door, but it’s good for manufacturing in this country. It’s proof positive that manufacturing today has evolved from its dark and dirty past.

It’s not too late to make plans to host a Manufacturing Day event on Oct. 4. In the words of one of the organizers, the Fabricators & Manufacturers Association, this day is an opportunity for manufacturers “to open their doors and show, in a coordinated effort, what manufacturing is—and what it isn’t.” (To learn more, visit

www.mfgday.com

.)

The vibrancy of American metal fabricating shouldn’t be lost in translation. Show your community how your company successfully competes against the world.
