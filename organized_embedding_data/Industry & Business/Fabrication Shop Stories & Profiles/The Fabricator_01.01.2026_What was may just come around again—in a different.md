# What was may just come around again—in a different way

[TARİH: 01.01.2026 The Fabricator]

Tube Time

Industry veteran Rick Preckel looks back on 40 years in the tube business

By

Lincoln Brunner

I

n the 40 years since Rick Preckel started in the tube and pipe business, the landscape has changed. Actually, let’s call a spade a spade: Much like the steel industry that undergirds it, the tube and pipe sector in this country (and worldwide) has seen more change than your grandma’s couch cushions.

Like his beloved Cardinals, Preckel is St. Louis through and through, having begun his career in the industry in 1985 as a controller at Maverick Tube in the suburb of Chesterfield. In that time, Preckel has watched as stalwart companies like Maverick—at one time a publicly traded company and the nation’s largest producer of welded oil country tubular goods—got subsumed in the slow but steady advance of consolidation. Tenaris bought Maverick in 2006, simultaneously turning Maverick into a privately held subsidiary and convincing Preckel to shift his career path toward Preston.

Preckel is a regular contributor to this magazine and owner of Preston Pipe, publishers of the monthly Preston Pipe and Tube Report, easily the most comprehensive digest of data for the U.S. tube and pipe industry. The folks at Preston mostly track production and shipments of welded mechanical tubing, found in everything from fenceposts to fire engines.

That line of sight gives Preckel a perspective that few people have—particularly on mergers and acquisitions, undoubtedly the key driver of both headlines and change in the industry the past four decades. Off the top of his head, Preckel recited a laundry list of companies that have disappeared but were once household names in the industry: Standard Tube of Canada, Dofasco, Copperweld, LTV, and on and on.

Preckel used LTV as a prime example. In April 2002, the nascent International Steel Group, formed by investor Wilbur Ross, purchased the steelmaking assets of the bankrupt LTV and made those assets the foundation of a conglomerate that would absorb—in part or in whole— several steel companies, including former industry giant Bethlehem, as well as Acme, Weirton, and Georgetown. Meanwhile, Maverick Tube purchased most of LTV’s tube making assets.

Then, just three years later, ISG was acquired by Mittal Steel, which just 14 months later became part of Arcelor-Mittal Steel in a $33 billion deal that then represented 10% of the world steel market.

More recently, Cleveland Cliffs, U. S. Steel (now part of Nippon Steel), and most notably Nucor all have purchased multiple steel companies (Nucor’s being downstream companies, Preckel said). Nucor’s acquisitions in recent years have included Independence Tube and Southland Tube, both of which it bought in 2016.

Many other mergers and acquisitions have involved tube producing subsidiaries. And while it’s difficult to trace the direct effect of that consolidation on production numbers, according to Preckel’s figures, the U.S. produces only about two-thirds the mechanical tubing that it did in 1985. Whether that drop is due to imports, changes in customer habits, or a combination of the two is hard to say.

"That’s probably the most significant change that’s occurred in the industry over the years that we’ve been covering it—that rapid, massive consolidation, all sort of driven by less manufacturing of the products that use tube directly in the U.S," Preckel said. "It’s not that much different from other manufacturing industries. I think that in a lot of ways, it was just an evolution that was driven by the same kind of globalization changes that we have seen in other industries."

Business analysts love the kind of data that Preckel’s team produces. However, that "use tube directly" caveat is what makes tracking the production, import, export, and use of mechanical tubing so difficult for Preckel and anyone else who follows the tube and pipe industry.

As major industries like automotive, appliances, and agricultural machinery have globalized their supply chains, they’ve also striven for more and more streamlined production—kitting various parts in complex assemblies that use tubing produced in the U.S. that is then welded and fastened to other components, sometimes in other countries. That trend is only increasing as OEMs, hungry for cost savings, continue to search out ways to cut as many steps as possible from their overall production processes. All the back-and-forth tends to muddy the waters.

"You’re not talking about that being caused by a shift in imports or anything like that," Preckel said. "It’s really just changes in the market structure. A lot of this is due to … imported assemblies as opposed to just mechanical tubing that’s either made domestically or imported for those assemblies being made here."

As China continues to vie—unfairly, much of the time—for more and more of the steel and steel products markets worldwide, countries like the U.S. will have to continue adapting to survive—much like they have been during Preckel’s whole career. But what does that mean exactly for domestic tube mills?

"I think it probably doesn’t mean taking us back to 40 years ago when we did more manufacturing in this country," Preckel said. "Back in the day, you would have a mechanical tubing producer that would be down the street from a lawnmower manufacturer, and the only thing they would make would be handles for those lawnmowers. There was a lot of that kind of stuff that got set up. And then of course you’d get some of those manufacturers … that would then bring that tubing manufacturing in-house. Or they would start in-house and then it would end up being a company all on its own."

Tube production is alive and well in the U.S., but it’s become a game of intense consolidation.

Those days of one supplier serving just one company are long gone. But as stark as some of the industry’s changes have been, Preckel actually sees a day coming soon where specialty tube and pipe suppliers could arise once again to serve various markets that develop in unexpected corners of the economy.

When it comes to acquisitions, "I have to believe that a lot of that low-hanging fruit has been sort of picked already," he said. "And I think that what we’ll see is the producers continue to evolve and consolidate where it’s appropriate. And you’ll see new ones pop up too. I think you’ll see some changes where there are some niche opportunities and you can have a manufacturing operation that’s lean enough.

"In some cases, when you think about some of these things that are more purpose built, there will be opportunities for things to pop up and be additive to the sector."
