The implications of vertical consolidation

A

re service centers friend or foe? It’s a straightforward question without a simple answer. Several months ago, we bid on a huge tube laser cutting job, millions of pounds of steel tube cut and delivered monthly. We collaborated with our vendors to negotiate a favorable material price, ran test articles, and optimized cutting programs to create the best possible bid. Regrettably, even with aggressive low margins and state-of-the-art equipment, we weren’t price-competitive in volume. Why? Our primary metal tube vendor also has a tube laser. We were bidding against our own supplier.

This isn’t news. Service centers can’t do every kind of work, but large jobs requiring relatively simple value-added services are an easy win. They command enough scale to earn direct-tomill buying power and can therefore drive prices down far enough to win the big jobs. This can be frustrating for shop owners like myself, but it’s actually an important, healthy part of our manufacturing economy. We need those kinds of efficiencies to help reduce manufacturing costs in the United States.

Meanwhile, shops that buy from service centers differentiate in other ways. Service centers tend to provide generic services like leveling, warehousing, and distribution, supporting hundreds of industries. There is an awful lot of specialization generally required to serve any particular industry. So while a service center might win large, easy jobs, other manufacturers can target particular industries and serve them better. That means making certain kinds of products better, of course, but it also means learning how to bid and sell into those markets. While service centers might dabble in some high-volume verticals, there’s a limit. They can’t be good at everything.

That’s good news for shops like mine. We are optimized for high-mix, low-volume manufacturing at scale. We prefer jobs containing many unique part numbers, at relatively low unit counts, for thousands of customers. While our material vendors do manage some manufacturing in-house, the kind of work we prefer is firmly outside their wheelhouse. For now.

Homogeneity and Power

In my last article, I talked about some of the factors that keep manufacturing fragmented. I posited that in order for a market to consolidate under a few large, scaled-up companies, the market must have both homogeneity and "power."

Homogeneity is relatively straightforward: It means that there is little enough diversity in the market for one company to serve it well, at scale. If markets differ too much, it’s hard to serve them all well. That creates an opportunity for competitors to step in and do better. Homogeneity is highly relevant in manufacturing, because as it has been said, manufacturing is a technology, not a market. There are a lot of ways to make money as a manufacturer, and nobody can tackle them all.

The second element, power, refers to Hamilton Helmer’s "7 Powers" model, from his book "7 Powers: The Foundations of Business Strategy." Helmer’s "power" refers to a company’s ability to earn persistent differential returns, such as more moneymaking capability than competitors. Power is generated by superior margin and/or market share combined with a barrier to entry for competitors.

Scale economies is a great example. My company went head to head with a service center in a volume bid, and we didn’t even come close to winning. The scale buying power of service centers makes it impossible for us to compete. That’s the barrier component of power. Anyone not similarly scaled can’t really play in that space.

The Quest to Add Value

I have to revise my assessment of power, though. I said that power is one of the preconditions for consolidation, but that’s not exactly true. After reflection, I’d now say it’s more accurate that consolidation requires a barrier, but not necessarily differential returns. This is an important distinction, because while service centers clearly have a barrier in the form of scale economies, they can’t be said to have favorable returns. Service centers provide a highly commoditized service and yield net margins in the low single digits. They might have power against a job shop, but not against each other.

In fact, this is an important reason why service centers want to do more value-added work. They want to grow, but in a mature market with a handful of mostly undifferentiated players, price is the biggest knob to turn. They can only push that so far. So how else can they capture market share and margin? By offering more value-added services. At a minimum, service centers can capture more margin dollars by working on metal before they sell it.

But there’s also a great margin opportunity there. If job shops like mine earn better margins by doing more complex work downstream, then imagine the value a service center might capture by doing the same. If they simply matched my prices, they’d walk away with significantly better margins by virtue of their scaled buying power.

As we’ve discussed, the challenge is in capability. If you’ve read this far, you might wonder what any of this discussion has to do with technology. Well, here’s where it gets relevant: There is a seismic shift taking place in manufacturing, and it’s enabled entirely by new technology.

Great Machines, Great Software

There are two factors at play. The first is that new tech makes high-end machines more accurate and easy to use. The first press brake I ever operated was an old hydraulic machine from the 80s, with manual depth stops and backgauges. Getting that thing to bend accurately required a special blend of expertise, mathematical calculation, and voodoo magic. In contrast, the brakes we use today are programmed offline using our automated software, and they incorporate measurement tools that mean we can get parts right the first time, nearly every time. Technology has made it possible to add bending services much more easily than in the past. The same trend will be true for other technologies as well. Manufacturing is now easier, and that means companies can do more before losing their edge.

The second factor is software automation. I said earlier that service centers aren’t interested in our business model, for now. Why is that? There are a host of reasons, but one primary factor is that processing thousands of part numbers for thousands of customers, across thousands of orders, creates huge overheads. Someone has to bid on all those parts. Someone has to make sure they are manufacturable. People have to schedule the work, create the production programs, and of course make the parts, with all the complexity that entails. When everything is finished, someone has to communicate with the customers. Orders have to be shipped or delivered. Customer complaints have to be handled. Customers have to be invoiced, and payments collected.

Service centers aren’t structured to handle all these processes for thousands of small custom jobs per week. So to date, bidding on extra value-added services only makes sense for a service center when overheads can be amortized across lots of parts—that is, big jobs. But software automation can change that. My company automates every one of those processes, making it possible for us to handle tens of thousands of bids per week with no full-time quoting or engineering staff. Scheduling, purchasing, customer communication, invoicing, and receivables are highly automated as well. If you walk into our front office during the day, you might think we are closed, because most of the office work is automated.

Software isn’t exactly new (although it has had its biggest impact during my lifetime). But deep, in-house integration of the entire manufacturing software stack is a new trend, enabling highmix production at scale with low overheads. If a scaled service center figures out how to build something similar, it could be game over for generic job shops that haven’t specialized in a particular industry vertical. Yes, that describes my company. I honestly lose sleep over it.

As challenging as it might be for my shop and others, it would probably be very good for manufacturing in the United States. Ordinarily, metal products in the U.S. move through four or five layers on their way to the customer: mills, service centers, shops, OEMs, distributors, and finally customers. The supply chain stack will be different depending on the product, sometimes more efficient, and sometimes less. But if there are four or five layers adding margin, the product is going to be fairly expensive. Vertical integration will create efficiencies that make U.S. manufacturing more competitive globally.

It bears repeating that this may or may not happen. Service centers aren’t structured to operate as high-mix, low-volume fabricators. Software may be able to alleviate the complexity, but it remains a different model from a logistics and warehousing business. Perhaps in the end, it’s too different, too uninteresting, or too complex after all. Vertical integration might also happen from the bottom up, instead of from the top down. If a high-mix fabricator manages to consolidate enough volume to benefit from economies of scale like service centers already do, then the outcome would be much the same.

Democratizing Metal Manufacturing

The possible end state is compelling. Today, new startups often struggle to manufacture their products. Either they need to in-source, buying expensive equipment and learning to operate and maintain it; or they need to find local vendors that can execute well at a favorable price. A new business might succeed or fail based on where they happen to open their doors.

But if service centers absorb more value-added services efficiently, access to metal manufacturing will be democratized. It will become economical, accessible anywhere in the U.S., with scalability baked in. If one service center figures it out, the others will have to follow suit.

In this new environment, generic job shops may cease to exist. If your company offers just laser cutting and bending, for example, you’d be forced to compete with your own supplier, even for small jobs. And you’d likely lose. It may make less and less sense to buy a laser and press brake at any scale, since you can get that basic bent part from a service center at an unbeatable price and lead time. Again, this is because service centers command economies of scale. If they marry scale with efficient high-mix production, enabled by software, it’ll necessarily change the market.

The companies most likely to survive downward pressure from service centers are those that optimize to serve particular industries. Service centers can expand their in-house capabilities to provide generic services used by hundreds of industries. But they can’t scale high-touch design and engineering support or learn how to sell in every vertical. Shops that survive will have to do more than cut and bend a blank.

So, are service centers friend or foe? The answer is yes. They might upheave the market and make things difficult for a while, but if they do, I think U.S. manufacturing is going to be better for it.
