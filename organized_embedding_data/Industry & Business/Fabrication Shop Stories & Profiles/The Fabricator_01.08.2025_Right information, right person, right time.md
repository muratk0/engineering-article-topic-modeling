Information automation abounds at FABTECH

By

Tim Heston

FABTECH

T

he late Dick Kallage, longtime industry consultant who spoke frequently at FABTECH and conducted numerous lean manufacturing seminars for the Fabricators and Manufacturers Association, had a mantra:

You’re not in the metal fabrication business. You’re in the changeover business.

That has stayed with me as I continue to cover a quickly evolving industry. Kallage usually said it at seminars dedicated to custom and contract metal fabricators, not OEMs or other manufacturing models that use metal fabrication technology. Today, though, I honestly think the mantra applies to anyone who visits FABTECH, from the largest OEMs to the smallest job shops.

If an operation doesn’t have change down to a science, it’s in for a world of trouble. In this context, "change" could mean changing machine setups, but it also could apply to changing customer demands, product mixes, machine technology, even global supply chains. These days, everything seems to be in flux, and everyone needs to be able to turn on a dime.

Perhaps the greatest change I’ve observed in recent years has to do with how the metal fabrication industry views efficiency. Speed still impresses people. The rate that modern machines move is truly extraordinary. But as metal fabrication undergoes a generational shift, buyers at FABTECH are just as impressed with new and better ways to process and use

information

. Hard automation (machines) matters, but so too does the soft variety, the "information automation" that leverages software and other digital technology.

Think of an ultrahigh-power, linear-drive fiber laser. As in past years, people at FABTECH will gaze through the green safety glass to see lasers slice plate at speeds once thought impossible. Then the cutting head will stop and perform a rapid traverse with eye-popping acceleration. Seemingly instantaneously, the gantry will move toward the glass. The crowd will gasp, jerk their necks back, then chuckle. The cutting impresses, but the rapid traverse—the

change

that happens in between—impresses even more.

Machines and Software in Demand

The latest "Forming and Fabricating Job Shop Consumption Report," published earlier this year by the Fabricators and Manufacturers Association, was a harbinger. Government and business leaders

want

manufacturers to accept new work coming from reshoring and other supply chain restructuring initiatives. The industry is on the hunt for capacity like never before—not just in terms of volume, but in terms of support (to keep those machines running) and—especially—software.

Yes, the survey showed the usual suspects of machinery in high demand. Nearly 72% cited press brakes as their greatest need. But more than ever before, software has taken center stage, with scheduling (34%) and quoting (42%) platforms topping the list.

Step back from the specific numbers, though, and you start to see the bigger trend. Those who worked in metal fabrication several generations ago saw their moneymakers in fast equipment. They probably punched, cut, formed, and welded large batches, and the more inches or strokes per minute they could achieve, the better.

Recent generations focused on automation—specifically, the physical automation that manipulated and moved workpieces down the value stream. The fewer times workers touched a part, the better. Their "ideally automated" value stream involved parts never touched by a human. They imagined material being loaded directly into a tower, shuttled to laser cutting, denested and sorted automatically onto an autonomous vehicle, brought to a robot bending cell, formed, then placed on an autonomous vehicle for transportation to welding and beyond. Machines would keep producing and jobs would never stop moving down the value stream.

Tortoise and the Hare

The general thinking has shifted in recent years. Automation has become ever more important; those who’ve invested heavily also know they need to invest in training and support. Today, good maintenance practices are more important than ever, since today a single machine or system can produce so much. When automation is maintained well, overall throughput goes through the roof; when maintenance falls short and a machine breaks unexpectedly, shops can sometimes grind to a halt.

The industry continues to invest in automation and modern equipment, of course, because even with all the reactive maintenance, overall throughput rises with the latest technology. It’s like the tortoise and hare—but viewed from a realistic perspective.

Think about it. The hare can run in short spurts at 45 MPH. The tortoise moves at 4 MPH tops. Even if the tortoise never stops to sleep or eat, and the hare runs sporadically in those 45-MPH spurts, let’s face it, the hare still wins. The same goes with modern machines and automation in metal fabrication. An automated machine can produce so much over a given period that, even if it’s down occasionally, it still wins the race.

Thing is, even the "smartest" equipment can sometimes run the wrong race when it’s fed bad information: a wrong part revision, quantity changes, a delivery schedule changes. Someone in the office or elsewhere keyed in the wrong data or attached the wrong file.

Both the tortoise (old equipment) and hare can get hurt, but when that incredibly productive hare breaks down, people notice. An "injured" piece of automation can be a problem, hence the value of predictive maintenance. Rework ensues, and everyone puts in a valiant effort to get the job back on track.

For these reasons and many more, there’s a renewed focus on having the

right

information delivered to the

right

person at the

right

time, always. Kallage referred to these issues as "information waste." This could entail any piece of information (or lack thereof) that impaired flow down the value stream, from quote to cash. This includes information about machines (think predictive maintenance), the jobs flowing through the shop, inventory, the status of outside suppliers, and more. Good information keeps that hare running, always.

Poor material caused part distortion, bowing, and a crashed laser cutting head? Perhaps certain material wasn’t delivered or ordered in time? Better information in purchasing (working with reliable material supply partners) and better inventory management might help. Lost money on a job? Better information in estimating might have helped. Did a brake operator form a part backward? Better information can help here too—training, job documentation, modern brake controllers with 3D simulation, and inspection procedures.

AndreyPopov/iStock/Getty Images Plus

Like in the other forms of waste, you’ll never eliminate all information waste. Things happen. Kallage’s point was for people to stop acting out the definition of insanity: Doing the same thing and expecting a different result.

Software Is Everywhere

Consider again the operator who formed the part backward. At this year’s FABTECH, you’ll see press brakes with touchscreen controllers, complete with bend simulations that color-code blanks to ensure operators orient the correct way. Put another way, the control software provides

better

information and

less

information waste.

The same applies to every other piece of software you’ll see at the show. People want equipment to produce more in less time. Today, productivity has less to do with how fast the machine moves. "Fast" is now the default. If you’re buying modern equipment, it’s not a tortoise; it’s a hare.

Productivity now has a lot to do with how a machine collects, processes, and communicates that information, both to in-house operators and to those supporting that equipment. Communication helps keep the hare running; good software, paired with knowledgeable people operating and managing the equipment, augments and streamlines that communication.

If you’re heading to FABTECH this year, you’ll see plate rolls that can adjust themselves in real time; press brake programming that can detect collisions or other forming issues before any work hits the floor; equipment with sensors that can detect the moment something doesn’t look right. Predictive maintenance isn’t a new concept, but it’s now more valuable than ever. And it’s all driven by sensors and software.

The same increasingly holds true for everything that happens in the office. Software-enabled office operations keep things transparent and jobs on the move. No longer does a question about a drawing sit unanswered in email inboxes or a potentially lucrative request for quote go unanswered. And they address manufacturability issues as early as possible.

In fact, "in the office" and "on the shop floor" might become outdated terms in the future. Long gone (or at least it

should

be long gone) are the days of "throwing work over the wall" to the floor, where any manufacturability issue is manufacturing’s problem. Separating everything into departments surrounded by walls, real or metaphorical, hinders smooth information flow and encourages wasteful fiefdoms, all of which focus on local efficiency without considering the big picture.

Cutting productivity is up 40% this month

. OK, did we ship more?

No

. So what’s the point of all that cutting productivity? Good software alone doesn’t break down the fiefdoms—people do that—but it does help guide people in the right direction.

Avoiding Analysis Paralysis

At FABTECH, you might see more than a few attendees gazing at big screens with loads of colorful charts and graphs. Be they at machine tool or third-party vendors, Industrial Internet of Things (IIoT) platforms exemplify the "digitalization" of metal fabrication.

Some fabricators aren’t enthusiastic, and understandably so. One source on background told me IIoT was "their best friend and worst enemy at the same time." It tells you which machines are running and which are down. It can slice and dice the data and track job velocity through every routing.

OK, great. Now, what do I do about it?

When you get the data, trying to do something with it can feel like you’re scaling that Mt. Everest you just measured. You see so much randomness, so much variation, that you throw up your arms and concede that the chaos in custom fab is just a fact of life.

Implementing some basic lean principles (5S, separating workflow into logical value streams) helps fab shops take that first level of improvement, reducing the chaos. Connected IIoT platforms, machine monitoring, quality management, and other "information automation" software platforms then help take things to the next level. They reduce or eliminate the need to key in data. (Keying in the wrong quality data is a classic "information waste" with major consequences, like rework or, even worse, customer returns.) And they can help connect the dots to optimize a business.

Nothing’s more frustrating than creating "islands of lean" or "islands of automation." A shop can build flexible value streams around specific product mixes that hit the sweet spot and leverage a fabricator’s strengths—supported with observation, IIoT tracking, and other data—which in turn feeds back into the business development and quoting process that funnels revenue into the shop.

The Paper Test, Digitized

A few FABTECHs ago, I recall talking with a (now retired) consultant who described a simple, old-school way to measure improvement. Specifically, it was an incredibly simple way to track part-flow velocity, from raw stock to the shipping dock.

Phuchit/iStock/Getty Images Plus

First, the consultant said, know that running machines

don’t

make money;

shipped products do

. Machine uptime is a good metric, especially at a constraint process, but

machine availability

matters more. An idle, nonconstraint work center isn’t really a problem, as long as it’s available to produce at a moment’s notice. The real problem is when the machine

needs

to be producing but for whatever reason isn’t. The next challenge is defining

when

that machine really needs to be producing to facilitate high-velocity part flow, so more jobs can ship out the door in less time.

Here’s where the old-school paper test comes into play. Place a brightly colored piece of paper on a shelf of frequently used raw stock. Write the date and time on it. When the stock is cut, move the paper to a kit of denested blanks, and keep it with them as they travel down the routing to final assembly and shipping. Note the date. That’s the flow time, from raw stock to the shipping dock. If the flow time varies, why? Was an outside supplier late? Was there internal rework? Recording the flow time doesn’t solve problems, but it at least brings them out into the open. (You could look at hidden problems as another form of information waste.)

At last year’s FABTECH, I visited several vendors selling ultrawide-band location tracking technology that can withstand the interference in typical metal fabrication environments. Attach a puck (or clip, fob, or other location-tracking device) to the job traveler or ticket, and you get what looks to be real-time traffic info on the screen. I saw it as a digitized version of the paper test, but on hyperdrive.

The right integration of location trackers could help track the location and movement of every part, kit, subassembly, and finished product. The more that ships out the door in less time (without quality problems, of course), the better. Such tracking could reveal valuable data as shops automate their information flow and implement various tools associated with artificial intelligence.

Infrastructure for Information Automation

At FABTECH, you likely will hear "AI" uttered more than once—and for good reason. Yes, it’s hyped, but it’s also likely to be tremendously impactful.

You also might hear people debate what’s "real" AI and what’s just marketing. In my mind, that’s for computer scientists to decide. For practical purposes, though, whether a digital tool is "AI enabled" or not really doesn’t matter. If it helps a fabricator better manage information, I’m all for it. That’s why I like the term "information automation," because at least in my mind, "information" seems to be the overarching theme.

FABTECH

At FABTECH, you likely will hear "AI" uttered more than once—and for good reason. Yes, it’s hyped, but it’s also likely to be tremendously impactful.

In a recent white paper, technology and privacy attorney Vivien Peaden of Baker Donelson described four related tools that can help. The first is robotic process automation. "RPA refers to software robots that automate repetitive tasks with precision. The ‘bots’ in RPA interact with existing business applications and systems to streamline production and business processes, such as data entry, inventory tracking, and invoicing."

The second is machine learning, "which enables better predictions and decision-making at every stage of manufacturing. [To enhance predictive maintenance], machine learning algorithms analyze maintenance records to predict how and when equipment failure may occur."

Related intelligence helps automation "learn" complex tasks, like picking a cut blank from a skeleton or manipulating a workpiece in front of the press brake or stacking pieces onto an autonomous mobile robot.

The third is generative AI, "a subset of AI that generates content, design, and text," Peaden wrote, adding that GenAI can help generate standard operating procedures and offer quoting and sales support.

The final area is the AI baked into robotics. Peaden described cobots enhanced with AI that helps them "to perform repetitive manual tasks, such as quality control inspections, and operate machinery while safely interacting with human workers."

Peaden also wrote about what she referred to as "AI architecture," explaining that "similar to the cloud computing movement of the last decade, these enterprise architectures host AI applications that run the business operations of the next generation."

This insight got me thinking about how the next generation of metal fabricators will manage their businesses—perhaps in supply chains that are far more dynamic and connected than they are today. Mills, service centers, contract fabricators, and end customers (OEMs, construction and industrial firms, even individuals) all add value to metal: cutting, bending, welding, coating, assembly, logistics, and so forth.

Who should add value where, and for which jobs? How should my business grow and evolve to meet demand? How do I foster the best environment for my employees?

Progressive metal fabricators who make full use of information automation—including many attendees at this year’s FABTECH, no doubt—will know the best path forward.
