How one Texas high school built a mini-monster truck

RAFAEL GUERRERO

The team spent almost the entire 2024-25 school year working on this vehicle, which is one-third the size of a typical monster truck.

Students at Bryan High School in Texas unveiled their mini-monster truck at a Monster Jam event during the summer.

any high schoolers learn how to work on cars. Some even get the chance to build one from scratch. But to build a mini-monster truck from the ground up?

From the fall 2024 to spring 2025 semesters, 14 students at Bryan High School, Bryan, Texas, had the rare opportunity to do just that: build a one-third scale monster truck. The team unveiled the finished vehicle at a Monster Jam event in Austin last June. Thousands admired the small (by monster truck standards) vehicle, with the students and their welding instructor, Chris Lehde, on hand to hear the accolades.

"It was the coolest experience seeing how happy they were and with the product that they built and then seeing how many people were proud of them," said Lehde. "It’s something that’s a once-in-a-lifetime experience that I hope they hold on to forever—I can’t wait to see the doors that open up for these kids."

Want to Build a Monster Truck?

A chance phone call to Northern Tool + Equipment jumpstarted this unique opportunity for the Bryan High School team. Lehde had been making calls asking if anyone wanted to donate professional-grade tools to his welding program. When he reached out to Northern Tool, a representative for the manufacturer/retailer pitched him Tools for the Trades, its program that provides tools, guidance, and instruction to schools, students, and instructors in career and technical education programs.

It was then that Lehde learned of a unique project—a rat rod go-kart build—some Minnesota high schoolers participated in during the 2023-24 school year as part of the initiative.

It just so happened that Tools for the Trades was looking for a high school for the 2024-25 school year to take on a similar project—only this time it involved monster trucks.

"At first I was, ‘Oh wow!’ It’s a lot to take on your second year teaching," said Lehde, who, prior to teaching, had welding experience working in refineries, fab shops, pipelines, and oil fields. "You’re fixin to take on a big old challenge partnered with two major companies in Monster Jam and Northern Tool.

"But who am I to stand in the kids’ way? Let them go do their greatness," he added. "I can teach them and do everything they need to learn from this. … I sat and thought about it, and I said, ‘I’m down for it—let’s do this.’"

He had to keep the project under wraps ahead of a September 2024 public announcement of the project. Lehde told his students that a handful of them would be working on a big project that school year and that anyone interested should apply. From these applications, a small group of students were selected ahead of the project announcement.

"I didn’t ever tell them it was going to be a one-third scale mini-monster truck for Monster Jam," Lehde said. On the day of the announcement, "We went to the Northern Tool [store] here in Bryan and they had the monster trucks all out at the front of the store—and the kids’ faces just lit up."

‘They Absolutely Knocked It Out of the Park’

Building a monster truck—even a small one—is no easy undertaking. The design process took a couple of months, according to Lehde. The actual fabrication of the truck began shortly after Thanksgiving 2024, and the students performed final touches well into May 2025, just a few weeks before the unveiling in Austin.

Despite it being much smaller, Lehde said it is a fully operational truck. While students weren’t able to drive it, Lehde did.

"Yeah, that thing moves. It’s got some power," he said.

Some of the highlights of the truck include:

It is about 4½ ft. tall and roughly 7½ ft. long.

It has a 740-cm

3

electronic fuel-injected engine.

It is mostly made out of 1- by 1-in. and 1-by 1¼-in. square tubing that is about ⅛ in. thick.

The axles are made from 2- by 2-in. square tubing that is 3/16 in. thick.

It has 24-in.-tall wheels with 12-in. rims.

Students improved upon several skills over the months of work, including welding, grinding, and cleaning and soft skills like teamwork and meeting deadlines. They even learned how to paint a vehicle from Bryan, Texas-based Brazos Valley Auto Restorations. Two of the students even ended up interning with the shop because of this experience, Lehde explained.

Students were willing to work as much as possible on this project—they came in early, worked late, and even worked during weekends and holiday breaks.

"This project is not meant to be an easy sort of step-by-step [build]," added Frank Crowson, senior vice president and chief marketing officer for Northern Tool. "There is a lot of problem-solving, a lot of ups and downs, a lot of stressful moments throughout the school year in trying to make sure that this thing was fully functional by the end of the school year and ready to be unveiled.

"They absolutely knocked it out of the park."

As part of the year-long build, Northern Tool provided the engine, tools—from sponsors DeWalt, Ingersoll Rand, and Milwaukee—and in-person mentorship that included some Monster Jam drivers.

"I think everybody who hears about this project has their jaws kind of drop about the experience that these kids got to have," said Crowson. "This is not your typical high school curriculum, not your typical experience for a high schooler to be mentored by these drivers and these icons in monster truck driving and the Monster Jam world."
