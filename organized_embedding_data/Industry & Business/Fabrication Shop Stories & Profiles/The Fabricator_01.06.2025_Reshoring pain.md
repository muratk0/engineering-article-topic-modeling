# Reshoring pain

Why the U.S. struggles to manufacture at home, and how it can improve

CALEB CHAMBERLAIN

C

ompanies are on the hunt for domestic manufacturers. They are failing. President Donald Trump’s tariffs create incentives to reshore, but the U.S. manufacturing ecosystem isn’t prepared to meet the demand. When measured against overseas competition, the U.S. is often embarrassingly far behind. It’s not just about price, although that’s a big factor: domestic suppliers are often busy, disinterested, or otherwise hard to work with.

In a viral social media post, one frustrated buyer summed up his experience by proclaiming that "U.S. manufacturers are babies." His company had been sourcing custom metal kiosk enclosures from China for years (since I don’t know his name, I’m going to call him "kiosk-guy"). Chinese suppliers were accommodating. They took rough models, figured out how to fabricate them, designed custom-fit packaging, and were willing to ship them in any quantity at a great price. It was a painless, high-quality, turnkey custom manufacturing experience.

U.S. suppliers were rather the opposite. The general mood was that the job was too low volume, too much of a pain, and not designed for manufacturability (DFM). Shops dragged their feet to bid, and after weeks of back and forth, prices landed five to 10 times higher than overseas. Not only was the experience worse, it was way more expensive. Kiosk-guy concluded, "Of course we are going overseas. We’d do that even if the U.S. was cost-competitive, and it isn’t."

That’s hard to hear, but manufacturers have their reasons. For example, I own an on-demand metal fabrication shop, and I wouldn’t have been able to help this guy. My shop is all about exposing DFM issues to the customer and then turning manufacturable components around as fast as same day. But we don’t do pro-bono design or assembly, and we certainly don’t design and manufacture custom molded foam for shipping. We’d have invited him to make his own design manufacturable and upload his parts on our website.

The Worst Kind of Customer?

This example highlights one simplistic explanation for poor service: "That’s not what we do." I don’t think anyone expects to walk into GM headquarters with a napkin drawing and have a custom car made. "You make cars, don’t you? Why can’t you make this one?" That’s an extreme example, but the principle holds true for simpler products. A shop that fabricates electrical enclosures for airports and data centers isn’t optimized to make custom kiosks in quantities of five at vanishingly small margins. It’s a completely different kind of business.

There are other explanations, even from high-mix job shops. This kiosk example represents the absolute worst kind of customer. He doesn’t have a manufacturable design; he expects free design and engineering support; he isn’t a high-volume opportunity; and he is cost sensitive. This customer represents high needs, high risk, and low revenue potential. It makes perfect sense that he’d have a rough time.

This really begs the question, though: If this is such a bad customer, why can he get great service in China? How does China do it when U.S. companies can’t? And what created this massive disparity in customer experience? We might easily say, "Well, U.S. manufacturers just aren’t built to serve this kind of customer," and that’s very true. But how did we get here?

As manufacturers, we’ll all have insights here, ranging from the pithy (but materially true) to the nuanced: It’s hard to compete with low-cost Chinese labor. Raw materials are way cheaper over there.

They manufacture for cheap and make it worthwhile by stealing IP. China heavily subsidizes manufacturing, while the U.S. happily sends work overseas in the name of globalization. Chinese businesses aren’t regulated like U.S. companies

These explanations and others are probably all true in varying degrees, depending on the industry. The most alarming perspective is that these factors aren’t coincidental, but deliberate and fundamentally unfriendly.

Perspectives on Globalization

While I was in college a couple decades ago, there was widespread hype and optimism around globalization. In an ethics class, we read Thomas Friedman’s "The World Is Flat." Friedman argued that our increasingly connected world would produce better outcomes for everyone. We’d all specialize and cooperate, becoming so economically intertwined that wars would cease. Globalization, spurred by free trade, would change everything. I wish that it worked out to be true.

It has occurred to me that globalization only works if everyone plays by the same rules. How do IP theft and dumping fit into a globalized utopia? The simple answer is that they don’t. It has been argued that we are experiencing the aftermath of decades of weaponized industrial policy by China.

The narrative goes like this: China massively subsidized key sectors for decades, creating a glut of low-cost products for export. The U.S. not only allowed that to happen but actively encouraged it. An influx of cheap products was considered a boon of globalism, a sign that it was working. Meanwhile, U.S. companies competed with each other mercilessly. Any business that opted to buy more expensive (nonsubsidized) domestic products was punished in the marketplace. The long-term impact? An absolute gutting of the U.S. industrial base. Some domestic industries disappeared altogether.

It’s probably unfair to write off China’s capability as solely a product of economic warfare. I suspect that there are as many or more cases where motivated, smart people in China figured out how to solve challenging problems. Even absent government interference, decades of innovation would still have created a manufacturing mecca. If you do something a lot, you get good at it.

Whatever the case, the U.S. industries that didn’t migrate overseas tended to be highly regulated, constrained by logistics (moving metal around is expensive), or were able to optimize through scale. Jobs requiring ITAR, DFARS, or other government compliance were safe from competitive overseas pressure. Many surviving businesses pivoted to serve industries like defense that are immune to globalization basically by definition. Other industries survived by scaling on the backs of high-volume customers, where automation, economies of scale, and the realities of global logistics allowed U.S. manufacturers to remain competitive.

If all this is true, then it’s no surprise that some industries are underdeveloped in the U.S. Whatever happened to get us here, it’s still true that it’s often hard to manufacture domestically. The kiosk example is just one of many, and it’s a problem worth solving.

Enter Software Automation

To be honest, I’m highly optimistic about our collective ability to make things better, even for industries currently captured by overseas competitors. Seven years ago, my brother and I struggled to buy custom fabricated metal parts. I didn’t know it at the time, but I was "the worst kind of customer." I wasn’t a business. I wasn’t ordering something that would turn into volume production. I suppose I didn’t have any loyalty (I’d go wherever I had the best experience). And my jobs were extremely high mix and complicated. So, predictably, it was hard to find shops that would make our parts.

Because of that poor experience, my brother and I founded OSH Cut. We built the company from first principles to enable exactly the kind of service we wanted: instant prices through a web browser, automated self-serve manufacturability checks, fair prices, and rapid turnaround. We weren’t the only ones. Other competitors opened their doors at about the same time to offer similar services.

Fast-forward to today, I can comfortably say that between my company and our competitors, precision sheet metal has now become the most cost-effective, accessible, rapid custom manufacturing technology in the country. We handily beat slower, less tech-optimized overseas competitors.

In the big scheme, OSH Cut’s niche and impact is relatively small, but it highlights a couple important things. First, it’s possible to take "bad customers" (like me and kiosk-guy), understand their pain, and create great businesses to meet their needs. Pain is a clarion call for innovation. Do you think there’s a business opportunity for anyone who can scale highmix, turnkey, custom enclosures in any quantity? You bet there is, and I’m 100% confident that it’s a solvable problem.

Second, the United States’ solution to improve manufacturing customer experience isn’t going to look like China’s. They’ve ostensibly benefitted from decades of sustained subsidies, favorable government policy, and willing international buyers. They have a glut of skilled labor and networks of manufacturers interoperating in close proximity. We aren’t building anything like that in the immediate future.

Instead, we can leverage technology to marginalize their advantage. If they can scale high-touch bidding and engineering because they have cheap skilled labor, then we have to scale it with software automation. If they benefit from huge networks of proximal value-added service providers, we must integrate vertically and build best-in-class, software-driven processes to improve logistics. If they benefit from weaponized subsidies, we have to encourage our leaders to develop sane, sustainable trade policies that help level the playing field, even if they are uncomfortable in the short term.

Why I’m Optimistic

Last month, I wrote that I’m optimistic about the future of U.S. manufacturing, and this is why. Digitally enabled manufacturing is still in its infancy, both domestically and globally. Everywhere I look, there’s a product category that desperately needs to be overhauled or replaced by modern software infrastructure.

The advantage of this approach is twofold. First, absolutely nailing customer experience means you will tend to aggregate volume across normal regional boundaries. Second, most of the advantages enjoyed by our overseas competitors are marginalized by software and process automation paired with good execution.

These innovations can’t come fast enough. We need the ability to make more things at home. We need solid manufacturing jobs and supply chain resiliency. And we need to enable innovation by making more manufacturing technologies fast and accessible to everyone, including kiosk-guy. This is a once-in-a-generation opportunity. Let’s get started.
