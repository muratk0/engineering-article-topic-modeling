# The perfect pitch for manufacturing

From the Editor-in-Chief

It’s more than making things; it’s making the next big thing

“Manufacturing is not dark and dirty anymore.”

“Manufacturing creates real value for an economy.”

“Manufacturing represents a new challenge each and every day.”

These are some of the pitches I’ve heard those involved in the metal fabrication industry throw out over the years as they try to convey that a career in manufacturing is a fulfilling one. They are all true. They also are kind of uninspiring when the target audience is young people. (“Yea! My workstation has an illuminance of 150 lumens/m

2

!)

Now try this one: “Creating the next big thing starts in manufacturing.”

A metal fabricator offered up that statement on the first day of a Lincoln Electric-sponsored, two-day seminar, Strengthening Manufacturing Education: An Experience Summit, in late June. Everyone was sharing thoughts on how to improve upon the same boring pitches for manufacturing that are presented to students.

The statement is simple in its presentation, but incredibly deep with its implied scope. If young people want to change their world, they probably need to know how to make things in general.

Creating the next big thing starts in manufacturing.

Just think about some of the work taking place around us. SpaceX, Hawthorne, Calif., is rethinking the way that space travel is accomplished, even to the point where it is doing most of its own metal fabricating. In 2014 Ford, Dearborn, Mich., will produce a largely aluminum-bodied F-150® truck, the top-selling vehicle in the U.S., in hopes of shaving about 700 lbs. of weight, and veteran metal formers have figured out how to produce these body panels in a consistent and quality manner. Even on a smaller level, a company like Carmi, Ill.-based Elastec/American Marine (“Connecting 3-D modeling to nesting,”

The FABRICATOR

, September 2012, p. 62), a manufacturer of pollution control equipment, is working on next-generation oil recovery equipment that is used to minimize the impact of oil spills on the environment. These manufacturers are working on projects that have huge ramifications for everyone’s future. They are making a better future.

That message should resonate with a majority of millennials, the 80 million people who are now aged 11 to 31. They outnumber the baby boomers that they’ll ultimately replace in the workplace, but they have slightly different outlooks on work. (Those of us who count ourselves among Generation X will have to play peacemaker between the two dramatically different demographics.)

PricewaterhouseCoopers recently conducted an in-depth study of the work beliefs of millennials because the global accounting and consulting firm’s own workforce will be transformed in the next several years. The company says that by 2016, 80 percent of its workforce will be millennials, who are committed to work but on much more flexible terms. They realize they need to work to live, but they don’t live to work.

Additionally, this group wants to make an impact on the world they live in. A survey—Talent Report: What Workers Want in 2012—from the nonprofit group Net Impact revealed that 72 percent of students consider “a job where I can make an impact” as very important to their overall happiness. Other surveys suggest that these young workers will hit the streets in a hurry if they feel they aren’t getting that sense of personal fulfillment.

What better environment to foster these needs than the metal fabricating industry where small businesses dominate?

Many fab shops have experience with extremely flexible schedules as they had to balance workload and financial realities during the Great Recession; to avoid blanket layoffs, these companies asked the workforce to work fewer hours and different shifts. Anecdotal evidence suggests that many shops kept these flexible schedules because some employees liked them and grew used to them.

Also, metal fabricating organizations tend to be flat in nature. The door to the president’s office is often a 5- by 10-ft. sheet away from the door to the shop floor. A young worker who has the ability to interact with important decision-makers and possibly glean knowledge from them is a much more satisfied employee.

I’m not sure if this manufacturing pitch has legs, but it did fire me up. I talked my 15-year-old son into signing up for a drafting class as part of his sophomore schedule, even when he initially responded, “I’m not going to be an engineer” at the beginning of the conversation. I told him that making things is a great way to make a life for himself in the future. He bought it. I think other young people will as well.
