# Changing of the guard at Goodhart Sons

[TARİH: 01.01.2025 The Fabricator]

Sons replace their fathers at Pennsylvania job shop

By

Dan Davis

Goodhart Sons’ Wyatt Imhoff (far left) and Cameron Leakey (far right) have stepped into superintendent roles once held by their fathers, Scott Imhoff (second from the left) and Jody Leakey (third from the left). Scott and Jody worked for three of the four generations that have led Goodhart Sons over the years. Scott retired last year, and Jody retired in 2022.

W

hen people describe a metal fabricating firm as a "family company," they imply that the company is owned by family. That’s accurate, but often doesn’t tell the whole story.

Take Goodhart Sons in Lancaster, Pa., as an example. Frank G. Goodhart purchased the sheet metal fab shop where he began his career as an apprentice in 1935 and established the company that now bears the family name. Today, the fourth generation of Goodharts are involved in the day-to-day management of the business. (Marc Goodhart, president, fabrication operations; Chad Goodhart, president, installation operations; Matt Goodhart, installation project manager, are brothers, and their cousin, Mike Goodhart, is director of project operations.) The family company has survived over the years when so many other similar companies have not.

But that’s not the only family involved here. When a company, particularly one involved in manufacturing, has been open for 90 years, it likely has employed multiple members of other families as well. Well-managed firms tend to employ folks that see their employer as a desirable place to work and recommend it to family members.

That was the case for Scott Imhoff and Jody Leakey, two former superintendents at Goodhart Sons. Imhoff, a 44-year veteran of the metal fabricating and installation company, and Leakey, who worked for the company from 1978 to 1994 and then 2015 to 2022, are both retired, but before that happened, they had the chance to work with their sons. Since their retirements, they also have had the chance to see their sons be promoted to the same supervisory roles they once had.

As part of their duties, Wyatt Imhoff oversees the company’s yard, and Cameron Leakey is in charge of blasting and painting activities. Both are responsible for specific projects and oversee fabricating activities in the company’s eight bays. The Goodhart Sons campus comprises five different buildings, four for manufacturing and one office that houses support staff and the installation crew, which sit on 16 acres.

The Fabricator chatted with the Imhoffs and Leakeys to find out more about this family dynamic within the traditional family-owned fabricating company. A lightly edited version of the conversation follows.

The Fabricator:

Wyatt and Cameron began their metal fabricating careers as welders. Scott and Jody, how did you get involved in metal fabricating?

Scott Imhoff:

When I was a kid, I actually worked on a farm for a while, so I kind of learned to weld a little bit there. That sparked my interest. I worked with a friend of mine, who ended up becoming my brother-in-law, on this farm, and he was a welder at the time. That’s what got me going there in that direction. Plus, my father was also in the steel fabrication and welding trade. [Editor’s Note: Scott also went on to graduate from the vocational school.]

Jody Leakey:

I basically went to vo-tech [now Lancaster County Career & Technology Center]. Back then it was 2 ½ years of welding. I went to Brownstown vo-tech. I would cross by Goodhart every single day, go on to vo-tech, and then come back by Goodhart again. When I graduated, I interviewed here at Goodhart, and that’s where I started at back in 1978.

The Fabricator:

With both of you starting out as welders, did you think you would work for Goodhart Sons as long as you did?

Scott Imhoff:

No. I didn’t have any clue. But it all worked out that way.

Jody Leakey:

No. I started right out of high school and then I left in 1994. I was a leader at that time. So, 20-some years roaming around different industries of the same nature, welding and fabrication. Towards the end of my career, I got a phone call from Goodhart, and I went back here for the last eight years of my working career.

The Fabricator:

What did it mean to you when your second generation got involved in this multigenerational metal fabricating business?

Scott Imhoff:

I have to be honest, I had mixed feelings about it. I knew my son was a hard worker, but I also knew what kind of situations he would have to get geared up for. But it’s great that he is here and that he’s doing a good job. I’m proud of him.

Jody Leakey:

I would say the same thing. I think Cameron started around the time that I came back.

I knew that I could be honest with him. But at the time, he didn’t know really what he wanted to do for a while. Then all of a sudden, he started taking classes outside of high school.

I’d always told him if you want to really learn a trade and learn how to fabricate, Goodhart is where you go to learn. And if you stay there, it’s a good place to stay. I never regretted working there.

So when he chose to come here, I had mixed emotions. I was hands-off right when he started. He’s his own man. I tried to teach him.

Scotty was his boss at the time, and he was either going to make it or break it with Scotty. He wasn’t going to get any help from me.

The Fabricator:

Cameron and Wyatt, what led you to this path of working in the metal fabricating field?

Wyatt Imhoff:

I actually started going to school for auto mechanics. I went two years to the CTC and graduated. I spent maybe a year or two working on cars in a local shop. I took my hobby, tried to make it a career, and ended up hating it.

I then got into commercial construction type of work, running jobsites all over the state and in New York, Ohio, and Maryland. I was working a lot out of town, and at the time, my wife and I were wanting to start a family. I wanted to land somewhere where it was a little more steady.

If you would have asked me in school if I wanted to be a welder, I would’ve said no. I’d always been around it. There was always a welder in the shop, but I never had that much interest in it. One day, I just wanted to do it. And that led me to getting a job here.

I knew the very basics of welding and fabrication, but I had zero experience. But the guys here on the floor kind of took me under their wing. I worked for Jody at the time. He taught me a lot.

Jody Leakey

: Tell him that you didn’t like me after your interview.

Wyatt Imhoff

: Well, I came in late, and I think years later I learned that Jody did not like late interviews. But I was working at the time. The only time I could come in was after hours. So I came in after a long day. It was a late interview, and he grilled me good. At the end of it, I took a weld test. I had no idea what I was doing. At the end of it, I think I called the old man, and I was like, "Man, I don’t know if I want to work for that guy."

Jody Leakey

: You know, I compare him to Scotty. You have to measure up. You can’t be Scotty’s boy and be a slouch.

Wyatt Imhoff

: But I got the job, and he taught me a lot. Even Cameron taught me a lot because he had a lot more experience when I started. Yeah. Everybody has really helped me learn and grow over the last seven years.

Cameron Leakey

: I really didn’t know what I really wanted to do as a career. I knew it wasn’t something like college or a degree. In high school, I took metal shop and wood shop, and I really like the hands-on work. Around that time, I worked at a car wash close to here, and I helped rebuild stuff at the car wash and helped on the maintenance side of it. I really liked all of that stuff.

That’s what I thought I wanted to do—that type of maintenance work—but I wasn’t really sure. So I went to vo-tech at night, did some welding, and got a certificate in welding. After school, I left the car wash and worked for a trailer company. That didn’t really work out.

I applied here actually before my dad came back. I applied and had an interview, but there weren’t any positions available at the time. Eventually, however, I got a call about a job opening. I ended up interviewing with Scott. I joined his crew up in Building Five, which is kind of removed from all the other buildings, and I just started learning from the guys on the floor.

Some days were difficult, and some days were good. But I learned a lot of fabrication skills and a lot about welding. I just kept growing. As I progressed here, I wanted more and more as far as different jobs and other opportunities. Ultimately, I was picked for this job.

The Fabricator:

Jody and Scott, can you comment on how training goes at Goodhart Sons? How do new people learn the ropes?

Jody Leakey

: Goodhart has always been pretty liberal as far as giving people the opportunity once they prove themselves. I’m from the older generation that when you got a new guy in, you gave him the grunt work to do. You would see if he measured up and if he wanted to learn. Then you just kept giving him more and more.

I remember my first week here—I was trying to lift something that was heavy. I didn’t know what I was doing, but I thought I did. I ended up smashing a welding machine. I mean, it was junk.

I thought my butt was fired, but they saw something in me—that I wanted to learn. So they kept me on, and we kept going.

Scott Imhoff

: Just like the older guys who taught me, I always looked for the hard worker, the one who was always here on time, the one who was here when we needed him to be here, and the one that really put out a lot of effort. But you need to remember that not everybody learns at the same rate as everybody else. Some people pick it up quicker.

But if you’re willing to learn and you are willing to work hard, Goodhart will see you through. If you work here, that means they saw something in you.

When I came out of vo-tech, I mean, what I had learned was somewhat beneficial. But when you come into a place like this, it’s a different world. I remember coming here for the first time to take my weld test, and I was scared. But I was willing to work hard, and they put me with some really good people back then. I worked with them every day.

Jody Leakey

: When I first started, I was 19. When you’re young and dumb, you don’t think much about the future.

But one day, Frank Goodhart Jr. pulled me in his office, and he just sat me down. I thought that was my career. He just basically had a nice chat with me. I remember him saying, "If you want to make something of yourself, you have the opportunity here to do that." That was really an eye opener for me. I owe a lot to Goodhart for doing that.

And they still do it today. Marc Goodhart [who is president of fabrication operations] does it with the younger generation. If they see something in somebody, they’ll spend some time trying to get their butts straight.

Cameron Leakey

: With Wyatt and I doing the interviewing and a lot of the hiring now, we still look for the same things—the good work ethic, the attitude, the attendance, all of that stuff. Even at job fairs that we go to, that’s one thing that we preach about our culture. We can train the other skills. Just like when they started and just like when I started, we’re still doing that stuff. Maybe not the best welder, but the best attitude.

Wyatt Imhoff

: We’ve turned people away that have been some pretty good welders and fabricators because they wouldn’t be a cultural fit.

The culture is huge to us here because as long as we’re all getting along, we have the same goal. We don’t need a bunch of superstars. We need people that want to work hard and want to work together as a team. That’s more important to us than anything. Teaching somebody to weld and fabricate is easy. We can’t teach you how to be a team player.

The Fabricator:

What was it like for you coming up? Did you feel like your experience at Goodhart Sons prepped you for your current superintendent roles?

Wyatt Imhoff:

I would say so. Coming up through the floor, starting at the bottom and working the way up gives us a little bit of perspective into what we’re looking for in hiring people for the floor. It keeps us a little humble. It’s not that long ago that we were right there doing the same thing as everyone else.

I also think that it really helps us in our roles because we know what needs to be done. We’ve done it, and we also can do it.

Cameron Leakey

: I think that has helped us. Obviously, we have the same last name as what the bosses did. But we went through it and are still going through it. People can say that we got the jobs because of our dads. But because we started on the floor, we had to work hard and prove to the people that we know what we’re doing. We just didn’t get this job, this promotion, or this leadership position because our dads were there.

We didn’t get any handouts because our dads were the superintendents. My dad would’ve been the first person to tell me that I messed up, and I guarantee you, Scott was the same way. But people don’t know that. People don’t see that. That’s why it was important for us to work hard and work our way up. That helps with our positions now. I feel like we have the respect on the floor that we need.

The Fabricator:

What was it like for both of you to step into the superintendent roles?

Wyatt Imhoff

: I would say a little bit intimidating at first—only because we’re both under 10 years of experience and some of the guys have 40-some years’ experience. Obviously, there are things that they know that we just don’t know yet because we haven’t had quite the same amount of exposure to things. But I would say overall, our promotions just made sense. That’s the way it was progressing.

I’m glad that we both got to take these positions because we work great together. We’ve always been great partners the whole way through, from being on the floor and to being lead men and now superintendents. We’ve been able to lean on each other currently and in the past when maybe if we didn’t know exactly what to do, we could kind of talk amongst ourselves and work it out. So it’s been a great experience. I enjoy my job. I enjoy coming here every single day. I enjoy the people I work with.

Cameron Leakey

: I would say similar things. We had different positions here, leaders and foremen, before we were superintendents. We were slowly assuming more responsibility, and then it just progressed. I think we were the two clear candidates for the two superintendent roles.

Having said that, these are really big shoes to fill. You have to live up to the expectation that was left and continue that. You have to somehow improve upon what was already good. So that’s something that we have to work toward every day—upholding the standard, and then how do we make it a little better?

It was a little daunting at first. I don’t think we fully knew what we were getting into. We had enough tools in our toolbox to know what to do, but you never know until you’re right there. But it’s been a good experience.

I’m happy with the position, and this is something that I wanted to strive for. It’s very rewarding. Now I can understand why my dad didn’t like the late interviews.

Jody Leakey

: I can say that I wouldn’t want to be either of these two guys because it’s probably been a little rough for them being in the position they are with the last names that they have. But at the time, when I was retiring, there were other leaders, people who were responsible for crews. So there were other leaders to choose from at Goodhart.

I also can tell you, and Cameron can attest to this, that I would’ve never recommended Wyatt for my job if he wasn’t qualified as an individual, regardless of his last name.

Scott Imhoff

: They didn’t ask me if I felt Wyatt could take over. They asked if I thought Cameron could take over. It’s like Jody said. If I didn’t think Cameron could do it, I would never have agreed to it.

The Fabricator:

Did either of you have any words of advice once they accepted the positions?

Scott Imhoff

: Oh, sure. I said, "Run."

But I was working with them on a daily basis when they stepped into the positions. It was pretty fun.

I retired in June, but I pretty much handed everything over to them in January [2024]. I was just kind of here to help. So it was good to do it that way. I was still here to help when they ran into any issues.

Jody Leakey

: All I told Cameron is, "Don’t forget where you came from. You better be willing to do the work that you’re going to assign to somebody."

The Fabricator:

Cameron and Wyatt, do you still go back to your dads for advice?

Wyatt Imhoff

: Yeah. The old man has a shop at his house, and there are times we go over there and try to solve problems over a few beers.

Cameron Leakey

: It’s good to have the ability to still have contact with both of them and have them as a resource is still huge.

As much as I don’t want to talk about work when I see him at home, there’s always some part that comes up or something I need advice on. So as much as I don’t want to do it, I do it.

Jody Leakey

: It’s a little funny when you retire because nobody ever tells you what to expect in retirement. For me personally, being involved in this career and involved with Goodhart, I’ve always kind of knew what was going on at the company. Now I find myself curious about the company. I want to know what’s going on.

So when Cameron comes over, I kind of drill him a little bit because I’m curious. I kind of miss it a little bit.

The Fabricator:

Scott, do you kind of miss the shop as well?

Scott Imhoff

: I miss seeing the people.

I also miss the challenge. Give me something that makes me ask, "How are we going to build this?" I do miss that.

I have some equipment at home, so I do some work for some other people. It’s not quite as challenging as the stuff we did here, but I keep doing it anyway.

The Fabricator:

Cameron and Wyatt, what do you like about the new roles, and what has been the biggest challenge?

Wyatt Imhoff

: Some of the most rewarding stuff that I’ve experienced is seeing some of the young guys that we brought from the floor with us. These are guys that really wanted it. They might not have been 100% ready, but they were eager. They had drive.

I would say that our leadership crew now are probably some of the best leaders that we’ve ever had. They’re good guys. They work together as a team. We have a lot of fun working with them.

Cameron Leakey

: I agree that the core of our leadership crew is probably the best it’s been. Everybody gets along, which is great.

I think one thing I see as a challenge, as Scott said, is the daily question of, "How are we going to build it?" Every day, it’s something new. How do we get through it?

But it keeps me engaged with the guys on the floor. Helping them really keeps me going.

And it seems recently that the projects are getting bigger. So that’s also a challenge.

The Fabricator:

Jody and Scott, what’s it like to see this new generation take over on the shop floor?

Scott Imhoff

: It’s good to see the younger guys willing to step up and take on leadership roles. This company has been in business for 90 years, so it’s good to have a young crew lead things over the next 30 or 40 years.

Jody and I, we taught them a lot. We tried to teach them everything we could, but there were certain things that you couldn’t teach, certain things they just had to learn on their own—especially when it came to prioritizing work for people. We always had a lot of customers wanting their stuff at the same time. How do you prioritize all of those delivery dates and keep everybody happy? That’s something you really can’t teach. It’s something you have to learn as you go.

Jody Leakey

: I’m extremely proud of Cameron and Wyatt for doing the job that they’ve done so far.
