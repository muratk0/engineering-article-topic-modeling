Biz Talk

Systems thinking guides future investment

TIM HESTON

W

hen you talk to most custom and contract metal fabricators, they discuss building customer relationships into true partnerships. But what is a "partnership," exactly? In most cases, volume enters the picture. Those "whale" accounts provide consistent cash that funds the business and facilitates reinvestment. Meanwhile, that "long tail" of lower-quantity work represents potential for growth.

That mix of work, though, can create some challenges. A large customer requests a last-minute change, so managers bend over backward to fix an issue. That reaction ripples through the operation and pushes other orders behind and ontime delivery southward. Fabricators still crave that production work, though, despite the periodic disruptions. A small order might be incredibly profitable, but it alone won’t make payroll.

This environment creates a dichotomy in the custom metal fabrication world. Recent KPI surveys from the Fabricators and Manufacturers Association show an industry that takes liquidity seriously. In recent years, average debt-to-equity ratios among survey respondents hovered between 0.5 and 0.9—very low for a capital-intensive business like contract metal fabrication. (The ratio climbs in cycles as fabricators reinvest in the business, of course. Back between 2013 and 2015, the average reported ratios in FMA’s survey were between 1.7 and 2.3.)

At the same time, however, reported on-time delivery rates stay between 80% and 90%, and new customer dollars remain a very small part of the overall revenue pie. The peak in new customer dollars in the fabricator KPI survey occurred in 2021, when FMA surveys showed new customer dollars making up 7% of total shop revenue. Typical averages hovered between 3% and 6%. Note that 2023 represents the latest data available; future data likely will reveal higher new customer dollars, considering the boom around AI, data centers, and power generation.

Boom periods aside, the fact remains that the new customer experience involves a lot of friction. Existing customers can consume a lot of capacity, and absorbing new ones isn’t easy, especially if those new accounts demand a lot of capacity in a short period. From all this stems the worry that domestic manufacturers simply won’t have the capacity to absorb additional work, a serious concern especially now as OEMs weigh their supply chain options.

When I walked the halls of FABTECH in early September, though, I saw a new emphasis on systems thinking. Yes, the laser power race is still on, but talk now centers less on inches per minute and more on how new technology will reshape the market and eliminate secondary processes—like the machining of laser-cut plate (up to

4 in. thick

) that was previously cut with a plasma or even an oxyfuel torch.

Even more talk centered around

connected processes

, and how operations can get the right information to the right machines at the right time. This is spurring new thinking around automation. Yes, it makes sense that more fiber lasers now are automated, with towers and even parts stacking. The laser cuts so fast that everything around it is a constraint, hence the need for that automation.

Now, however, fabricators are zooming out even farther. "If you’ve got 10 operations on your line or job routing, you can make operation five the best it’s ever been, but if operations six, seven, and eight can’t handle it, your net gain is zero, so what’s the point? Automation for the sake of automation is almost always a mistake."

That was Graham Wloch, Carmel, Ind.-based director of business development for Visual Components North America, a company that specializes in process simulation up and down the value stream. The technology aims to show the big picture, like how parts move between operations, but also granular detail, like how robots access welds and how other factors affect process quality and flow. "Manufacturers need to understand the dominoes, the unintended consequences."

Simulation technology is already being used for production planning, with manufacturers performing "virtual trials" of equipment installed into a virtual production line, complete with integrated off line machine programming, before the actual cell is installed in the factory. Wloch added that the technology could also apply in sales and quoting. "In the sales process, you use simulation as a process validation," he said. "If the order is won or the purchase happens, then you can do all the granular programming and simulation upfront and have that as a deliverable well before the job hits the shop floor."

Imagine a day when a customer uploads a request for quote package, and within days or hours—or perhaps even immediately, for certain types of work—that customer is sent a price

and

a high-level process simulation through bending, cutting, welding, even powder coating, perhaps with some baked-in design for manufacturability suggestions? Within the company, engineers see a more detailed simulation before the job is won.

Such front-end automaton might make that "long tail" of lower-quantity orders less arduous to handle. Once the customer pulls the trigger and finalizes the design, the granular programming, process planning, and integration starts. When the order hits the floor, everyone knows how that job will flow with the dozens or hundreds of other jobs on the floor at any given time. That information helps unleash fabrication capacity and, not least, enable smarter capital investments that consider the big picture—no more "islands" of efficiency and automation.

Will this imagined scenario ever spread throughout metal fabrication? Probably not for a while, but the potential is there. Regardless, the past 20 years have seen some incredible evolution in machinery. Nearly everything today cuts, bends, and welds faster and better than it did before. Over the next 20 years, I feel we’ll see a similar evolution in software. Judging by the talk and technology at FABTECH, the industry is just getting started.
