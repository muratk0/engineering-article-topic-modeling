# Defining a metal fabricator’s mission

[TARİH: 01.05.2026 The Fabricator]

Biz Talk

Insightful questions from the MODEX show

Richard McPhail, executive vice president and CFO of Home Depot, speaks at the MODEX show in Atlanta.

W

hen the Orion capsule of Artemis II splashed down off the coast of San Diego last month, I felt a burst of nostalgic pride—and not just because it signaled a return to humanity’s exploratory nature, of people on the move toward (literally) uncharted territory. I also saw the formed metal enveloping the capsule and thought back to my visit more than a dozen years ago to Hardwick Co., a fabricator in Birmingham, Ala.

Gene Wilson—who by 2011 had worked at Hardwick for more than 50 years—recalled his time working as a teenager on a special cone-rolling project, one using proprietary, top-secret material. Turns out, he was cone-rolling some of the shells for the capsules used in the Mercury space program.

My mind drifted to Artemis the following week while visiting the MODEX show in Atlanta. The moon mission involved the coordination of countless parties, all working toward a singular goal. That in essence is supply chain management: information and technology that fulfill promises and keep products on the move.

"This event is full of supply chain folks who I bet share a common frustration. I know our team has it all the time. As a company, you need to define what it is you’re trying to sell, how you’re going to sell it, and who you’re going to sell it to. Great supply chain managers need to be very specific about who the customers are and what the customer experience looks like. We need to make sure everyone is aligned. Clarity of language is probably one of the most important things.

"And I’ll admit, we’ve caught ourselves forgetting about the customer and skipping to, ‘Wouldn’t it be cool if we could build this?’ We started asking this before asking, ‘Who’s the customer, and how are we going to sell to that customer?’"

So said Richard McPhail, executive vice president and CFO of Home Depot, during MODEX’s first keynote address. These questions help define the business’s mission. Not every mission is an Artemis moonshot, but that doesn’t mean the mission should remain vague or undefined altogether.

The MODEX show floor was full of new material handling technologies, including AGVs, AMRs, even magnetic levitation technology that uses electromagnetic fields to propel carts without physical contact. Carts float along on a magnetic floor, carrying payloads up to several thousand pounds.

Exhibitors showed everything from racks and wheeled carts to quadruped and bipedal robots, with motion driven by advanced software accelerated and enhanced by artificial intelligence. (I lost count the number of times I saw "AI" among all the trade booth signage.)

When I heard that insight from the Home Depot executive, I thought about all the different fabricators I’ve visited. At most places, demand evolves from word-of-mouth marketing. Friends and acquaintances have needs, and the founder bends over backward to try and meet those needs, first in a garage, then in a rented facility, and eventually in an expansive plant with room to grow. Relationships can take custom fabricators a long, long way.

Eventually, though, growth hits a ceiling. At that point, managers take a step back.

Who are our customers, really?

They might come from different industries and have different requirements. But the fabricator won their trust for a reason. What’s that reason? That answer helps define the business.

One reason might be ease of ordering and quick delivery. Shops that allow a broad swath of customers to upload files and reply with an instant quote help fill this need. At the other end of the spectrum are contract metal fabricators that shape their businesses around a diverse set of customers that all share certain qualities. These could be types of jobs, material types (like a stainless fabrication specialty), customers that follow certain demand patterns (lawn care and ag, for instance), certain quality or regulatory requirements (think medical, aerospace, and defense), or project timelines (like industrial fab and construction). The customer types go on, and the optimal combinations are endless. It’s why no one fabricator operates like another.

Following the Home Depot executive’s line of thinking,

carefully defining

the customer mix is key. Most fab shop managers I talk with rely heavily on the 80/20 rule, or Pareto Principle, stating that 80% of the results come from 20% of the effort (inputs). Focusing on the vital few helps move a business forward, while focusing on the exceptions can lead to analysis paralysis and overwhelming inertia. No operational methodology, continuous improvement approach, software, or manufacturing technology can be everything to everybody. They can’t handle all fringe cases, but they might be able to really make a difference in a fabricator’s core area of business. That puts ROI analyses in the right context and pushes reinvestment forward.

This is especially important now, considering how one key attribute seems to permeate up and down the supply chain: quick response. "Back in 2015, we felt if we could deliver to 90% of the United States within two days, we thought that was the gold standard. Now, no one is satisfied even with one-day delivery," Home Depot’s McPhail said at MODEX. "In the world we live in now, supply chains are never stagnant. You always need to think about the next stage of improvement. That requires agility and really knowing what your supply bases can do … [including] during periods of volatility."

He added that more than 50% of Home Depot’s products now are sourced within the U.S. The goal has always been to build a diversified, agile supply base that can help the company serve a tremendous variety of customer needs, from the DIYer needing a certain pipe for fixing a leak

right now

to the construction pros that need material and tools delivered in specific quantities and sequences over periods of weeks or months. I’m sure custom fabricators can relate.
