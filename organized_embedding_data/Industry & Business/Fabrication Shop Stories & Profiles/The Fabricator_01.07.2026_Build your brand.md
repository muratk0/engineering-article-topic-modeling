Editor’s Corner

3 steps to cut through the digital clutter to stand out

By

Dan Davis

T

wenty years ago, if someone had approached me with advice for building my brand, I would have laughed. Do I look like a running shoe?

But you might have noticed some changes in the world over the past couple of decades. Legacy consumer media—newspapers, television, and radio—has lost major ground to internet-based enterprises, and people now gather that information on the internet and social media. That’s about 200 million active websites. YouTube alone has more than 5 billion videos. That’s a whole lot of noise.

The internet is a logical place to connect, but it can be hard. Company websites are overseen by IT departments that don’t prioritize engaging with visitors, and social media feeds typically are maintained by people with little knowledge about the organization for which they are working. Reaching out often can be a dead end.

Perhaps nothing exemplifies the coldness of the digital world more than the process of looking for a job. This process can frustrate even the most positive person. Most people don’t believe their resumes even reach humans as AI-enhanced applicant tracking systems pick out key words in documents to justify whether they end up in an inbox or the trash can. Standing out is tough.

That’s why branding makes sense, and why I don’t scoff at the idea any longer. To stand out, you need to believe you are worthy of special attention.

So in the spirit of self-help experts everywhere, this is my three-step program for helping an individual or organization to stand out from the crowd.

1. What’s your plan?

Every project starts off with a goal, and this is no different. What is to be accomplished? What’s the timetable? What kind of resources can be dedicated to make the goal achievable?

One of the challenges here is reigning in the goal’s scope. Too large of an objective makes preparations overwhelming and possible results underwhelming. A more specific target makes it easier to prepare specific steps that can lead to success.

Think about a metal fabricator that wants to increase sales—a worthwhile goal, but also kind of vague. Targeting a hot sector, like data centers, may be a better idea.

The internet is a cost-effective way to reach potential buyers in this sector, and videos on the website and social media deliver the most information in the shortest amount of time. Videos that explain what the fab shop can do can be hosted on the website and shared on social media.

Frequency in social media communications matters. Without a regular schedule, a brand is easily forgotten.

2. What’s your brand?

How do you want to be seen in the marketplace? What can you offer that makes you memorable to your target audience?

This is when individuals or organizations can tell their story, share their knowledge, and demonstrate their skills. Don’t be shy. Remember this is an exercise in trying to be recognized.

Again, social media can help here, and content for those channels helps to craft the story. The right commentary, photos, and videos can build trust with followers.

Facebook and Instagram are good platforms for building local connections. In fact, a lot of small businesses lean on Facebook as a substitute for their own company website. Regular posting can make you known to people, and then the message can be refined to a specific audience.

YouTube and LinkedIn are probably best for a broader reach. Videos allow the outside world to see a company’s capabilities and learn from its experts. This builds familiarity with viewers and answers questions that they might have. It’s not a replacement for face-to-face communication, but it hopefully leads to more of that type of conversation.

If you are looking for a helpful tool to assist with the video creation, consider using Opus-Clip, an AI-powered video editing tool that can transform long-form videos into short clips optimized for platforms that allow video. If you want to target an audience in another country, HeyGen is an AI-powered video generation platform that can take video and automatically alter the speaker’s language.

3. Where do you stand?

This is where you get to let the industry know about your belief system. It’s a peek behind the curtain as to what makes a person or organization tick.

It can be support for a cause, industry-related or not. Maybe it’s alignment with a group or trade association. It could be as simple as being a sponsor of a local youth league team.

For example, several metal fabricating companies are contributors to SparkForce, the Fabricators and Manufacturers Association’s foundation. That link shows that those companies are helping to grow the next generation of metal fabricating talent through support of scholarships and manufacturing-related summer camps.

This three-step plan might help someone find the connection that they are seeking. And you don’t even have to worry about buying a DVD set or signing up for a bothersome e-newsletter!
