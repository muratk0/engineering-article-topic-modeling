# Increasing sales by telling your company’s stories

Shop Management

"Build on your own real estate" to connect with customers

By

Steve Rainwater

To bolster a reputation in the metal fabricating industry and to attract the attention of potential customers, companies need to share their news and case studies via channels that make the most sense for them.

J

ust 25 years ago, many B2B manufacturers did not have websites. At the time, websites were novelties, and while their future usefulness was obvious, infrastructure was lacking. Manufacturers stuck with their tried and true business models that produced acceptable results.

Websites got more notice after Larry Light came on the scene in 2004. The head of marketing for McDonald’s, he coined the term

brand journalism.

Light said it took more than one ad or one message to tell the story of a brand, because brands have multiple dimensions and a variety of stories to tell.

Within a few years, big and small brands were building big and small websites. Joe Pulizzi, one of B2B media’s own, had been using the term content marketing since 2001 and cranking out volumes of brand journalism via custom publishing. Seeing the future in multiple strategies and platforms, he founded the Content Marketing Institute in 2011.

Pulizzi believed content marketing would expand the craft of storytelling exponentially. The audience would no longer be told what to do and when to do it, like in an ad. Buyers would become more independent, and sellers could compete fiercely, giving both a win on a more level playing field.

The Growth of Content Marketing

By 2014, Facebook and LinkedIn were already a decade old, each having undergone numerous iterations but still hardly used in B2B marketing. And today, the layers of digital content in cyberland are almost infinite, and yet another tech—artificial intelligence—is all the rage.

The world is obsessed with content. From TV streaming to TikTok youth creators, myriad individuals spend all day attached to their phones, consuming content for most of their waking hours. It’s way beyond critical mass.

But back to your website. Pulizzi is also known for another mantra, shared by in-the-know marketers. He said the future for B2B content will be "built on your own real estate." The home for your company’s content should be its website. For B2B manufacturers, it’s the only way to maintain a semblance of order. Social media algorithms are designed to help the social media companies, not you. And legacy media can only feature so much at once.

So it’s up to you. You are the publisher.

What’s Changed in Marketing—and What Hasn’t

Reliability, caution, and practical innovation are among the admirable traits of fabricators and metals manufacturers, but this calculated approach to business doesn’t translate to online marketing. Some companies fumble awkwardly with content. A few companies are filming high-quality videos on iPhone XV while others struggle to keep a static webpage up to date. Fabricators are fragmented in this sense because it’s not one size fits all. A tiny shop may be achieving great results from marketing with content, while a much larger company might get excellent business outcomes using old-school strategies. But for everything that has changed in marketing and sales, a couple of things haven’t.

First, since your revenue comes from those who can do business with you, one of the most important marketing goals is to create opportunities to sell to companies that are a fit for your services. If you think this sounds like Sales 101, you’re right.

Second, and right out of Sales 102, nearly everyone agrees that business, sales, results, and revenue are still the products of relationships. A handshake is a handshake (even if some are now virtual), and trust is trust. Trust is built through both personal experience and reputation—and reputation is bolstered in the media.

Company news is a highly effective way to build trust and strengthen reputation using media. When you highlight multiple aspects in the life of your business by sharing milestones and showcasing customer engagement, you make it easy for your audience to keep you top of mind.

Reporting company news is much more than adding a paragraph in your newsletter about your newest employee or announcing your new machine purchase. Weave together stories so potential customers will understand why they should do business with you. Educating them while cultivating your ongoing relationship leads to sales.

The Case for Company News

Self-sufficiency, entrepreneurship, and innovation are bedrocks of the metal fabricating industry, and so is selling. Manufacturers love to sell—it’s in their DNA. Many started small and sold their way to prosperity or acquisition.

Buyers also love to buy, based on understanding and perceived value. But perception is no longer developed or persuaded through personal conversations alone. In today’s content-rich environment, you need to deliver your sales experience differently. Digital content must be ready on demand to address buyer concerns, questions, and interests, guiding them to a foundation for their decisions. Remember, new prospects are already qualifying who they’ll buy from before ever speaking with anyone, and your existing customers could still be lured away by your competitors.

Your customers and prospects need to know what sets you apart. Using company news as a basis for your content positions you as active and authoritative in your field. And it’s now technically possible to surgically pare down your audience so your company news reaches just the right people to open doors and create relationships. When you share company news strategically, you help the audience learn what it’s like to work with you, without sitting down for a meeting. You build confidence and trust.

But industrial buyers still need to understand features and benefits. One of the best ways to accomplish both is through story-driven reporting. Well-crafted stories are at once an opportunity to share highly relevant technical information and to explain it in the context of working with your company. Telling stories as part of a strategic communications program allows you to communicate your unique selling propositions in detail, side by side with your company culture, in formats potential customers prefer. Stories are interesting and helpful.

A case study, for example, is much more than a list of all the reasons your products and services are superior. Of course, you should communicate your competency. But in a more compelling way, you need to convince buyers you can solve their dilemmas and that working with you will be a valuable, seamless process. It’s Sales 101 dressed like Media 101. Story-driven reporting drives qualified attention.

You Need a Strategy

In the sales-driven culture of small to medium-size manufacturing, it’s easy for marketing with content to become a random affair. Stories are inserted into the sales process to augment and support the persuasion and inform the relationship-building. But a well-designed strategic content program should also persuade while you sleep and while you’re running parts.

Your best stories become your best content, so always be identifying your best stories. And remember that story-driven reporting doesn’t just inform your customers and prospects, it informs your own internal teams and efforts. These stories are windows into what your company does well and provide insight for how to position your company in the markets you serve and aim to reach.

Resources Dictate Tactics

Manufacturers live in reality every day, measuring production capacity and allocating resources. Telling stories with company news is no different. You need a system to bring them to light and put them into production. Perhaps you already have a well-developed marketing department that’s producing and distributing your company news, or maybe you use an agency. But many small to medium-size fabricators don’t have the budget for dedicated marketing and are augmenting sales with content developed by whoever has time to do it.

Remember that you can do plenty without a full-blown agency relationship. If you make a plan and work the plan, you can accomplish a lot with the resources you already have.

The Website Hub and the Killer App

Your website should be the hub of your marketing. It’s real estate you control. Even if you don’t have thousands of visitors, developing this tool is critical to your operation everywhere else. There are no algorithms or rules controlling messaging on your website, and you can make your own editorial schedule.

You’ve been told the killer app is social media, with its millions of potential followers. But in fact, the quickest way to get your customers’ and prospects’ attention is (still) through their inbox. Notice how legacy media and social media send you emails constantly? This is why.

Send emails to your lists smartly, strategically, and discreetly, and use them to stay top of mind. Your best stories, newsletters, and podcasts reporting your company news are on your website; emails simply bring your audience over for a visit. It makes the most sense to build on your own real estate and lead potential customers there.

BUILDING RELATIONSHIPS THROUGH MEDIA

Relationship-building in media is traditionally divided into four categories:

Owned media

is your website, newsletter, copyrighted manuals, and podcast. You own them and control the content, use, budget, rules, and schedule. This is information that you publish for distribution; it’s video that lives on your system, produced by you.

Earned media

is nonpaid exposure through mention or placement on someone else’s owned media, such as magazines, books, TV, radio, and podcasts. These media sources credibly recognize you, your business, products, or stakeholders. Of course, earned media can sometimes be negative and disparaging. Is it really true there’s no such thing as bad PR? Well, that depends.

Shared media

is owned by a third party and used according to certain terms. Social media sites such as LinkedIn, Facebook, Instagram, and Substack are all forms of shared media.

Paid media

is an advertisement or published content that you have paid for. This could be a billboard, print ad, pay-per-click ad, event sponsorship, wrapped vehicle, or apparel, to name a few. The lines in paid media are getting blurred now, as ads are often small packages of content.

All four of these media types can be useful to your business. Small and medium manufacturers should develop priorities to determine resource allocation to support revenue opportunities.
