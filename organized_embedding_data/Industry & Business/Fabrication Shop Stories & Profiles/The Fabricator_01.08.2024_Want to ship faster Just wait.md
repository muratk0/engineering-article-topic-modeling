# Want to ship faster? Just wait

Biz Talk

The counterintuitive nature of job shop production

Tim Heston

W

hen I learn anything new, I tend to simplify, perhaps oversimplify, just so I can wrap my head around the concept or technology. That happened when I dove into the lean manufacturing literature. I learned about the classic wastes like overproduction, waiting, defects, and inventory.

The classic wastes of lean manufacturing certainly apply to custom fabricators, but adapting them isn’t always straightforward. Inventory of all types are considered "waste," but removing too much inventory can starve a shop floor of work in a hurry. Manufacturing involves some unpredictability—machines go down; material or workers aren’t available; someone just screwed up. Stuff happens, and inventory helps keep jobs moving forward when that stuff happens.

Does this task keep all jobs moving forward as quickly as possible?

That question is my gross oversimplification of lean manufacturing and, really, any improvement effort a custom metal fabricator takes on. "As quickly as possible" keeps part-flow time top of mind (either faster flow down the routing and/or a shorter routing by eliminating secondary operations). The "all jobs" part of the statement keeps overall throughput in the equation. If one expedited job pushes other jobs behind, overall throughput falls or, at best, doesn’t budge.

At the core of the issue is a custom fabricator’s customer mix. According to recent financial surveys from the Fabricators and Manufacturers Association, fabricators might have hundreds of customers, but fewer than a dozen of them make up more than half of a company’s revenue. Those big accounts likely have thinner margins, but the orders keep the jobs shipping and the cash coming in the door. One quick-turn, low-quantity job might have a really healthy margin, but it alone probably won’t cover payroll. And so, fab shops do all they can to make those whale accounts happy. They run ahead and keep jobs at various stages of completion so they can respond as needed. And when an order comes in the door, everyone aims to get that order to the floor as quickly as possible. Still, does that strategy keep all jobs moving forward as quickly as possible? Or do all those jobs hitting the floor as soon as possible send detrimental ripple effects, leading to turbulent production waters?

Prasad Velaga, founder of Optisol LLC, a Texas-based scheduling software company, thought it was the latter. When I spoke with Velaga in 2022, he described capacity-planning scenarios in which

delaying

order release actually shortened lead times, often by a dramatic amount. (For more on this, search for "Perfecting the metal fabrication job shop production schedule" on

thefabricator.com

.) Releasing jobs early means that they spend most of their time as work-in-process (WIP). Even worse, the inventory can become lost, damaged, or obsolete. It’s metal fabrication’s version of "hurry up and wait."

Fabricators aim to reduce batch sizes—not necessarily to single-piece or single-sheet flow, but at least to a point where changeover time doesn’t hinder part flow. Jobs might not be flowing in kits or "batches of one," but they aren’t lumbering down the routing in huge batches either.

Still, what about the primary cutting process? Jobs might require different gauges and material grades, so grouping kits on a single sheet isn’t always practical. Fabricators also need to optimize material yield, which often encourages programmers to reach ahead in the schedule to fill a sheet.

I’ve seen a few operations really dial in their remnant organization (automatically printed labels, clearly marked inventory locations, etc.), but most simply run entire sheets. Dealing with remnants simply costs more than it’s worth, and holding blank inventory on repeat orders is relatively low risk, as long as you’re not stealing cutting capacity from where it’s needed most.

The trick is making best use of immense cutting capacity to closely control that postblanking WIP buffer. The more productive and predictable cutting is, the less WIP you need, and, hence, the shorter your overall lead times can be. Forming and other workstations pull from blank WIP, after which the flow accelerates. WIP doesn’t wait long before more value is added to it.

Real life’s messy, of course. Component suppliers could be late. Customers request late changes. This is one reason waiting to release orders really makes sense. If purchased components aren’t ready or customers change an order, those on the shop floor haven’t seen the work anyway. No one’s scrambling to process rework because, well, the work itself hasn’t been released yet. It sounds counterintuitive, but perhaps the fastest way to make something is to wait until the last possible moment.
