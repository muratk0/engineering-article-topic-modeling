# Embracing AI in metal fabricating engineering

[TARİH: 01.08.2025 The Fabricator]

Tech Spotlight

Kinney Mfg., accustomed to adopting advanced technology, creates a tool that optimizes key front office activities

By

Dan Davis

A Nebraska metal fabricator is using AI to help streamline tasks like part design, and it thinks it can do the same for other fab shops looking to optimize front office activities.

fter almost 20 years of working for large companies and in the private equity space, Randy Kinney decided to make the transition to owning a small business, purchasing IntoMetal of Lincoln, Neb., in September 2011. Staying small wasn’t part of the plan, however.

The first step in trying to grow a larger presence in the metal fabricating field was updating the machine technology at the 30-person shop. One of the most important investments was a 6-kW fiber laser, one of the first in the state.

In 2018, Kinney Mfg., the new name given the metal fabricating company, purchased another fab shop, Precise Fabrication in Beatrice, Neb. The move provided more fabricating capacity, only about an hour’s drive from Lincoln.

More equipment investment followed, including three 10-kW fiber laser cutting machines for the two sites. Then came tube laser cutting, robotic welding, and laser welding.

During this growth phase, Kinney Mfg. also launched three brands, which its metal fabricating shops support with fabricating capabilities. M Grills is a series of charcoal grills and woodburning smokers, and Blazn Grill Works is a line of pellet grills. Industrial Maid is a line of commercial and industrial air filtration equipment.

The latest news from Kinney Mfg. is an 85,000-sq.-ft. addition to its 40,000-sq.-ft. building in Beatrice. Kinney said that the entire facility is air conditioned and has windows throughout the addition to provide outside light to the shop floor. It also is home to more advanced technology, such as automated guided vehicles, which will be used for transporting materials between the lasers and the press brakes and other downstream departments.

"If you talk to anybody that works with us, we’ve always just had the mindset for growth. Since Day 1, it’s like, ‘How do we grow? How do we support our customers? How do we grow with new technology? How do we allow our people room to grow so that they’re not trapped in a small company where they don’t have options or opportunities?’" Kinney said.

The keys to allow that growth, according to Kinney, are having the right people, equipment and systems, and real estate. With more than 200,000 sq. ft. dedicated to manufacturing, Kinney Mfg. has the room to take on more fabricating work. It also is committed to updating its fabricating technology and systems, which is a constant battle. The people part of this equation, however, is a greater challenge.

Kinney Mfg. is competing for quality workers in an ever-shrinking labor pool, and it’s not getting any easier. The pressure to address this situation is even greater as this company and others prepare for a wave of reshoring activity expected to hit the U.S. in the coming years.

"Everybody’s going to be trying to hire people right now to support this growth back in the U.S.," Kinney said. "And on the engineering side, you’re going to find folks that maybe are good at SolidWorks or good at some CAD system, but how do they learn about your company? How do we help them get the 15 years of knowledge that some of these experienced people have?"

The Answer? AI

Knowledge transfer is difficult. A fab shop might find success in having an experienced worker mentor a less-experienced co-worker or having veterans jot down work instructions about jobs. But even with that, the full transfer of tips, tricks, and guidance is not guaranteed.

With the emergence of artificial intelligence (AI) in almost all parts of life, Kinney decided to put it to use at his company. A new worker might not know as much as a lead supervisor with 20 years of experience, but AI might be able to assist in decision-making that could match that of the metal fabricating veteran and still reach the same end point.

Kinney Mfg. has developed its own software, powered by an AI engine, that helps streamline part design. It’s currently in use at the shop, and Kinney said that he’d like to commercialize it in the coming months.

"We can put in a PDF, part number, or drawing into our software, and the system’s going to look for everything in our database that we currently do today that is similar to that part," Kinney said. "In the past, when you wanted to search for something, you had to know kind of what you were searching for. With AI, that isn’t the case anymore."

Depending on the directions given to the AI, the software delivers results along with a confidence score, indicating how close the delivered files match the original request. A new engineer now can find an original design or even something that is very close to it and get right to work, instead of spending hours trying to find a file or beginning design work anew even though an existing file somewhere in the database might provide time savings.

"I don’t need to have a 15-year engineer know how to integrate a new part. I can take a guy that knows SolidWorks really well and give him my AI tool. It’s going to pull up all the quality issues, efficiency notes out of our ERP system, and every other part that’s similar to it that we’ve already done, where you can see other job, equipment, or setup notes for those similar parts," Kinney said.

The AI assistance doesn’t end there. It’s going to be able to help speed up production on the shop floor as well by keeping tabs on just who is doing the work. It all starts when an operator begins a new job.

"Our AI system is going to look at the individual, the piece of equipment, and the part number and then go through all of our historic production involving that operator and the parts made on that machine, similar parts made on similar machines, and parts made by other operators on that machine," Kinney said. "We then generate what we call a Success Score for this operator.

"By looking backwards, we’re able to then use AI to predict this person’s success rate based on quality and efficiency for the job that he’s getting ready to clock in," he added.

If his Success Score is high enough, the operator clocks right through and starts the job. If the Success Score is in a band that’s not quite high enough, but it’s acceptable, the operator has to meet with a lead supervisor and have a quick conversation to make sure he really knows what to do to deliver a quality part. If the Success Score is too low, the operator is not assigned to produce that part.

AI isn’t governed by naming conventions either, which can sometimes be an impediment to simply finding a file or, on a grander scale, migrating files to a new software environment. "AI isn’t just looking at the drawing itself. It’s looking at the material type, material thickness, the size of the part, the bend features of it, and the notes," Kinney said. "It’s ripping all of this content, and it does it so fast and so inexpensively."

The Impact at Kinney Mfg.

When asked what the biggest impact the AI has made for his company, Kinney said that it probably is related to peace of mind. When something needs to be addressed, it’s now done completely, not limited by what engineers and machine operators can remember to address.

For example, Kinney Mfg. recently had a quality issue with a customer’s parts, and corrective action was required. The corrections were made, and the actions documented in files related to those three parts. However, three sister parts, very similar in nature, didn’t get the same correction actions documented in their files, leaving open the possibility of a similar mistake occurring down the road to similar parts that weren’t included in the original quality issue.

The AI engine is able to search the company’s database for parts that share similarities with the part that was the subject of the corrective action. Once located, an engineer can decide if the resolutions need to be added to the files of those parts.

"It’s such a black eye with the customer because if I screw up part No. 1 with them, fix it, but then two weeks later send them part No. 2 and it has the same error on it, they’re not going to be happy," Kinney said.

Kinney is anticipating that others might be interested in enjoying the same peace of mind and newfound efficiencies. That’s why he’s hoping to roll out the AI-powered software in 2026 and offer licenses to other metal fabricators.

The company behind the software is called MFG AI. It’s designed with metal fabricators in mind, but that’s not going to prevent it from being useful for others inside a metal fabricating company.

For instance, the software plans to have a module for purchasers. When an engineer proposes buying a part from a certain vendor, the purchaser will be able to drop the part design into the software, and the AI will search the company’s transaction history with vendors who have provided that part or one similar. The AI also will review on-time delivery statistics and pricing structures to deliver a list of vendors, ranking them according to a success score, determined by the historical information from the shop’s own database.

"AI gives you that value of doing that and more," Kinney said.
