ezco Fabrication answers every request for quote (RFQ) that comes in the door, and no job is too small. That strategy helped this Lafayette, La., shop—launched in 2006 in the heart of oil and gas country—grow into a 30-employee, diversified custom fabricator.

"We have major customers in oil and gas, but as everyone knows, serving the oil and gas business is a roller coaster. We recognized early on that we needed to diversify our customer base. That’s why we don’t turn anything down. We could run a quantity of one, two, or 2,000."

That was Bryan Lagrange, CAD manager, adding that in recent years, he’s noticed similar attributes among the many customers that come to Mezco with low-quantity work. Many have an idea, but they don’t have easy access to CAD. Lagrange pointed to a model on screen showing an outdoor fire pit with a distinctive design. Its individual parts, however, were extraordinarily simple: formed brackets or various blank shapes like circles, rectangles, and triangles.

Would there be a way to automate the quoting of these individual, completely configurable components? As it turns out, there is. Today, Mezco customers can enter a web portal, configure common shapes, receive real-time pricing, and submit an order—all within minutes. And according to Lagrange, this is just the beginning.

Scaling the Long Tail

Most job shops share a similar customer mix, with a handful of large "whale" accounts followed by a long tail of low-quantity orders. When they attempt to evolve into a contract fabricator, they aim to increase the number of those whale accounts.

But what about that long tail of small accounts? Automated quoting software has helped. A few operations allow engineers (and really anyone) to upload CAD drawings and receive an instant quote for cut and bent parts.

If you can draw something in CAD, you can upload and get the ball rolling. But what if you just have an idea—a napkin sketch? Budding entrepreneurs might dream big at first, but they don’t have access to CAD. The barriers seem too great, so they abandon the project, and their ideas never come to fruition. Considering all this, would there be a way to streamline the entire "napkin sketch" process? The leaders at Mezco are hoping they’ve found a way, and a unique approach to product configuration software is playing a key role.

Product Configuration in Custom Fab

Log on to the Mezco website today, and you’ll see a list of capabilities—cutting, machining, powder coating, and more—but you’ll also see an option for online ordering. Clicking that brings you a link managed by Spok-bee, a company that offers a cloud-based product configuration platform.

Spokbee launched a little more than three years ago with a vision of "closing the loop" between various relationships in the manufacturing supply chain. Visit

marketplace.spokbee.com

, and you can see various configurable products offered by different manufacturers.

Mezco is the first job shop on the platform. How does a job shop use a product configurator, when one order can be completely different from the next? This is where that closing-the-loop concept comes into play. Custom fab shops make variations of certain basic shapes for a variety of purposes. They could be components for other product line manufacturers; small-quantity orders for hobbyists and consumers; or a variety of other businesses that need certain parts in a hurry. Someone might come with a napkin sketch, and the fab shop’s designers use basic shapes to put together an initial design. As sources explained, Spokbee’s online product configuration marketplace aims to connect and streamline all these customer interactions.

For instance, someone might choose a custom door from one manufacturer but find they also need a few custom brackets to mount that door. Through Spokbee, they can order the bracket—fabricated by Mezco—as well as the custom door, sold by another manufacturer. Alternatively, an engineer at that door manufacturer (or any other product manufacturer, for that matter) might order a custom bracket from Mezco through its Spokbee storefront.

"We shared our vision with a few manufacturers of big assemblies, and it turns out that they have issues customizing and visualizing various products." That was Mac Cameron, who founded Spokbee with the goal of eliminating the barriers of custom product development.

Imagine a large industrial manufacturer that, on occasion, deals with a design that requires a few miscellaneous brackets or plates. Creating prints and sending out RFQs take time. Moreover, the fabricator might need to adjust those prints and redraw them in CAD. That’s a lot of time spent for a small order. Mezco’s online Spokbee storefront could streamline the process.

The Product Configuration Storefront

Visit the Mezco storefront today, and you can order a few direct-to-consumer products like lanterns and other decorative items. But you also can order and configure certain bracket shapes, metal rings, circles, base plates, gussets, and more. Customers can choose material type, grades, thicknesses, and the type of machine the product will be manufactured on. The type of cutting machine dictates the tolerance, and bend radii are limited to those that can be achieved with Mezco’s shop tooling. Users can change the length and width dimensions along with other factors. With every change, the price updates automatically.

A Mezco employee views an order for a custom bracket. There’s no bidding or design process. The configurator designs the product so it can be manufactured by Mezco’s equipment. The first time Mezco sees it, the order has been priced and accepted.

As Tim Olivier, Mezco operations manager, described, "We chose these shapes because we’ve seen demand for them. People come in the door and say, ‘I need a stainless backsplash.’ That’s why we offer a configurable rectangle shape."

"We’re offering those base plates [on the Spokbee product configuration platform] because of the demand we get from construction," Lagrange added. "People arrive on the jobsite, and they find that someone forgot to order enough base plates, gussets, or other parts for the job at hand. So, they can order a few from us, and very quickly."

"This really gets us to the people who don’t have CAD, those who want something instantaneously," Olivier said. "The younger generation interacts through technology. Many don’t want to pick up a phone and call a job shop."

"This is just a start," Lagrange continued, adding that in the coming months, the shop plans to add a broader range of products and higher levels of configurability. The entire process will include deep integration with Mezco’s 3D CAD platform that, not incidentally, is also based in the cloud.

Engineering in the Cloud

Outside the Spokbee marketplace, when Mezco wins a bid, customers send in drawings and Lagrange recreates or imports them into Onshape, a CAD system that users access through a web browser. The Onshape platform "gives everyone a single source of the truth," Lagrange said.

The shop doesn’t attach drawing files to email or have multiple design files organized on its servers for every job. The only file Mezco employees manage for every part is the one in Onshape, which also keeps a detailed history of every change made—when and by whom—since the design was created. Mezco customers can securely log on to Onshape as well, so they can see their jobs, the latest designs Mezco has, and all the iterations their jobs have gone through.

Olivier added that the Onshape interface is simple and accessible, which is key to ensuring everyone can access and understand that one-version-of-the-truth approach. After all, accessing and viewing the design file is only half the battle.

Understanding it

is just as important. "My children use Onshape to design parts for our 3D printer at home," he said.

Onshape even offers a free version to hobbyists and others who don’t mind having their designs available to the public. Once they have an idea for a product to sell, they can pay for an Onshape license (which makes files private) and get started on their design.

Even so, simply designing in CAD doesn’t guarantee a product can be made, and here is where Mezco sees the value of product configuration software in custom fabrication. When customers order from the Spokbee marketplace, the configuration engine uses data from the Onshape platform. When customers configure a bracket on the website, all the design attributes are tailored to match the capabilities of Mezco’s equipment, including specific edge tolerances, bend radii, and certain dimensional constraints. (Cameron said that, eventually, the platform will allow users to upload a CAD file and retrieve an instant quote. "This functionality will first work with simple parts, then expand to any file that is designed for sheet metal.")

If a product makes it through the configuration engine, Mezco can make it. In this sense, manufacturability is baked into the design process. It’s that integration that will help bring about the next phase in Mezco’s product configuration plan: on-the-cloud product design. "We have the [manufacturability] rules to fabricate products at Mezco," Cameron said. "In the near future, we’ll have an Onshape app launch in which we provide design guidelines, and we’ll allow makers to design in Onshape and launch new sheet metal products that they can sell and Mezco manufactures on demand." He added that the app will start with just one-piece parts incorporating cutting and bending, "but we plan to expand to multiple pieces, which could include welding."

The idea would help extend the "long tail" of low-quantity opportunities even further. People could create a design and make a prototype quickly, even if they don’t have access to CAD. And even if they do have CAD access, they might choose to use the Onshape platform that’s connected to Mezco, with immediate pricing and manufacturability included with the design. Why? It’s simply more efficient: no more back-and-forth emails asking for clarifications, no more tedious "design cleanups" in the fabricator’s front office. Once a design is complete, it’s good to go.

Opportunities in Product Development

For custom fabrication in general, such an integrated approach to product design could open the door to more opportunities in prototyping and product development.

"It’s very difficult to turn away anyone, because you never know what’s walking through the door every day," Olivier said. "We’d love to be part of projects that could become something larger. In my mind, this kind of automated product configuration gives us a better chance of that happening."

"For us, it’s about diversification," Lagrange said. "We’re in Louisiana, so oil and gas is king, but we know it’s a roller coaster. If you don’t diversify your customer base, that’s when your shop closes."

"The vision for product configurators is incredibly exciting," Cameron said. "When you can reduce the time it takes to launch a new product, you can create better products quickly, and you can start solving problems faster. I think we’re going to have an explosion of unique designs that don’t just remain an idea in someone’s head. They will actually be manufactured."
