Why is

The FABRICATOR

so large? Are all your publications that size?

Laura Knoll Knoll Steel Inc. Spring Grove, Ill.

Editor’s Note: The size of the magazine dates back to the early 1970s when it was first launched as a newspaper-type publication. As the years progressed, the publication transitioned to a magazine format, with a glossy cover and magazine-quality paper. The size, however, remained the same because advertisers enjoyed having their ad appear in such a large format. We get a handful of complaints each year, but we keep it in the larger format because people have come to expect it that way.

What About Fabricating Skills Development?

Editor’s Note: In the October 2019 "Welding Wire" e-newsletter, Digital Editor Gareth Sleger highlighted the cover story from the September/October 2019 issue of

The WELDER, The FABRICATOR’

s sister magazine. Editor Amanda Carlson spotlighted a group of women making their way through a Chicago-area community college welding program. That editorial preview prompted this response.

I have been working in fabrication more than 40 years as a welder, certified welding inspector, machinist, CAD engineer, and welding and machining instructor. I’ve worked in every aspect of sheet metal fabrication, including forming, laser cutting, and punching. I am currently a fabrication and engineering manager.

What I have noticed in those years is a lack of training for press brake, punch press, and laser cutting machine technicians. Yes, technicians. I’m not talking about operators/button pushers. All of the technicians we have either received their training at shops previously or we have trained in-house.

As an example, we searched for a competent brake technician for five years, and he was a fellow I had worked with previously at another shop. The second qualified person came 14 years later.

Also, on average we interview and test 40 to 50 welders to get one with the skill set and work ethic we look for. We have had success training some of our younger welders—typically 19 and 20 years old—that came to us with basic welding skills, good mechanical aptitude, and good work ethic.

Fabrication should be a more attractive career path as I have earned a good living in this field.

Edward W. Lawther Cygnus Manufacturing Corp. Saxonburg, Pa.

Editor’s Note: This Oct. 22 comment was a response to "Where lean manufacturing and Six Sigma meet" by

The FABRICATOR’

s Continuous Improvement columnist Jeff Sipes. The column first appeared in the October 2019 issue.

I found this article very interesting to read. When most people think of lean manufacturing and Six Sigma, they think of the two going hand-in-hand together as process improvement. It was a surprise to read that in the ’80s and ’90s the two were viewed as having counterproductive consequences as different improvement initiatives within companies competing for resources.

This leads me to believe that people today may have the ideas of the two terms intertwined. A lot of people will tell you that Six Sigma means process improvements.

As time has gone on, I believe the two terms have meshed together, now having similarities and working well when used together, because they are all under the umbrella of continuous improvement.

Michelle Caputo South Kingston, R.I.
