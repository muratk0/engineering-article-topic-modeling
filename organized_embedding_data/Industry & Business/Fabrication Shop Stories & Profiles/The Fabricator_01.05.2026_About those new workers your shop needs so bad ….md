# About those new workers your shop needs so bad …

[TARİH: 01.05.2026 The Fabricator]

Editor’s Corner

Young people entering the metal fabricating field aren’t likely to have the same skills as their predecessors

By

Dan Davis

E

very metal fabricating company is looking for that same unicorn: the young person that grew up on the farm and has great mechanical and welding knowledge because when things break on the farm, they need to be fixed.

The problem is that those young people were much more prevalent when more people actually lived on farms. In fact, many metal fabricating companies can trace their starts to skilled craftsmen who repaired not only their own farm implements but did the same work for others. Those types of skills are always in demand—no matter the time period.

But farm living is rarer nowadays. Families and individuals have moved closer to urban areas over the years for job opportunities and access to amenities not available in the country. Simultaneously, schools cut back on their vocational education opportunities for cost savings and lack of interest. That creates a scenario where most kids are graduating from high school with a diploma in hand, but no experience working with their hands.

Some metal fabricating companies haven’t come to grips with that reality. They are still scouring their surrounding communities for that young man or woman who can walk in, pick up a welding torch, take a look at a blueprint, and get to work.

Others, however, are accepting the reality of who’s available in the labor pool: people with no real manufacturing skills or background who are interested in learning something new.

Jim Belosic, CEO of SendCutSend, in Reno, Nev., pointed this out in a LinkedIn post back in March. His company, which started in 2018 as a quick-turnaround shop with a laser cutting machine and its own instant quoting system, now occupies three buildings in Reno, has two more locations in Kentucky and Texas, and offers capabilities such as bending and forming, CNC machining, laser tube cutting, hardware insertion, and finishing. With the growth comes a need for more employees, and Belosic said it’s easier to find people with the right attitude and focus on skills development later.

At the time of that post, his newest 12 manufacturing hires were a doughnut shop worker, accounting assistant, elderly caregiver, janitor, bartender, AI data analyst, call center representative, nurse, dollar store associate, hospitality industry veteran, school custodian, and graphic designer. People with backgrounds like these don’t sound like candidates for manufacturing careers, but they all have one thing in common: They’re curious about what metal fabricating offers. When the fish are on the line, the fisherman has to set that hook and reel in the prize.

"I’m asked all the time about ‘how will manufacturers find skilled labor as we scale and reshore’ and the answer is we have to create it ourselves," Belosic wrote in his social media post. "Relying on someone else (a school, the government) to create skilled labor for you means you’re gonna be waiting a while. Just do it yourself."

That’s a lot of pressure, but companies that have invested in machine tools and software that help new hires quickly get up to speed can handle it. Learning curves are still a part of life at a metal fabricating company, but for modern shops, they are now measured in days, not weeks.

In this month’s story highlighting EXP Fabrication’s transition into laser cutting (p. 48), Ethan Blankenship, a project manager, explains how inexperienced operators of a new laser cutting machine can get to the point of producing quality parts pretty quickly.

"It might take somebody a week and a half to get a new operator on a laser to be somewhat proficient with it," he said.

Of course, more experience leads to better throughput. When a poor cut is recognized by the laser cutting machine operator, does that person have enough experience to determine the cause? Was it related to gas behavior? Was the right cutting speed used? Could the optics be dirty?

Guiding that operator on a journey from a machine operator to an expert who understands why something happens and what can be done to solve shop floor problems is now the challenge for metal fabricators looking for new talent. The labor pool is full of candidates who are curious and have work experience; they just don’t have a manufacturing background. If metal fabricating companies want to be ready for the opportunities that await a re-industrialized nation, they’d better figure out a way to make use of these types of workers.
