# Franke Foodservice Systems

[TARİH: 01.12.2018 The Fabricator]

A-frames move kits of parts from laser cutting to deburring and bending.

A key visual indicator of the work at Franke Foodservice Systems, part of the Franke Group, is the blue A-frame carts. Every one of them has kits of cut parts, and when they’re on the move, that’s a good sign.

The Fayetteville, Tenn., plant produces food and beverage equipment for various restaurants and other commercial facilities. Some products are made to order, then shipped immediately; some are made to stock; and other parts are sent to the company’s spare parts warehouse.

Stainless steel is its bread and butter, with an average thickness of 16 gauge. About half of the parts range from 12 to 48 in., about 20 percent are larger than 48 in., and the remaining pieces in the mix are less than 12 in.

Everyone here works with a proprietary ERP system that the company developed in-house. Typical for OEMs, the engineering department is separate from programming: Engineering performs design work. Once work orders are released (and about 20 to 60 are released every day), the scheduler requests job information, including the DXF file, the model, and a PDF of the print. Jobs are pulled based on the due date. The programmer then nests the job, fitting different work orders together, sometimes looking out a week ahead for maximum material utilization. These nests are delivered to the floor in a packet that will entail about eight hours of laser cutting time. With this packet come the job travelers, which are assembled with color-coded cover sheets that tell the factory floor what orders to work on next.

The packet first arrives at the raw material area, at which point the material handler pulls sheets from the rack system and organizes them by grade and thickness. The material handler stacks sheets in the order they are to be processed, as reflected by the job travelers in the packet. This could entail, say, two sheets of 20-gauge 304 stainless followed by one sheet of 16 gauge, followed by four sheets of 14 gauge, and so on. He also prints labels—which play a critical role in de-nesting and beyond. The material handler then delivers the material and work orders to the laser machine operator, who builds up the production plan at the control.

It takes 24 hours from order release to the start of nesting. Hot jobs take between five minutes and four hours, depending on the urgency.

The company uses high-performance automatic nesting. If grain direction does not matter, programmers will nest parts within parts.

Large jobs take five minutes to nest; a single-part job takes one minute.

20 percent of parts are smaller than 12 in.; 60 percent are 12 to 48 in.; 20 percent are larger than 48 in.

The scrap rate averages 17 percent, including cutouts. About four remnant sheets are generated each day.

For hot jobs, the line leader approaches the operator and hands him a traveler with a red cover sheet.

Assist gas: bulk nitrogen with filter and dryer.

The machine operator is responsible for loading and unloading sheets using roller tables.

Hot job travelers are given a red cover sheet.

Next comes de-nesting and deburring. The color-coded travelers show which parts in a nest get sorted separately. Each job traveler also has those labels, which operators affix to every part that they remove from the nest. The travelers have a map of the nest that shows which label goes with what part. This applies even to the smallest of parts. Each bracket, for instance, is placed in a bag and labeled. Each label has the part number, work order number, date it was nested, and other critical information.

Why affix labels to every part? It has obvious benefits when it comes to parts tracking, a huge plus considering the operation’s part diversity. With all of the company’s custom products, Franke’s laser operation often cuts a part once and never again. And many parts look similar, like the left- and right-hand piece of an enclosure assembly. At any point in time, employees can turn to their ERP system and see exactly where every part stands in fabrication and assembly.

But does this operation starve downstream operations? Not really, and it goes back to identifying the constraint process, which at Franke is in deburring and bending. Besides, a missing part that needs to be recut wastes far more time than an operator spending a few seconds affixing a label.

Also, "deburring" isn’t an apt term, because pieces rarely emerge from the laser with a burr. But because of design requirements, parts need a rounded edge, something even a perfectly tuned laser cannot produce. The company has attempted to deburr (or "edge round") in the assembly operation. That is, assemblers would deburr only the exposed edges that needed it, not everything else. But deburring cosmetically critical formed parts can be a complicated affair and lead to more parts being scratched and sent back for rework, adding more costs and complications.

Theoretically, hemming would hide some edges, but not all, and identifying and separating parts that would not need deburring takes more time than simply sending all parts through the deburring machine.

Programming also sometimes causes a constraint in laser cutting. If a kit of parts has pieces on different sheets, it can take time for an entire kit to be ready for downstream operations, especially when the packet size is too large (that is, a batch of jobs takes too many hours to complete). Still, this doesn’t happen often, and when it does, programming makes a note to avoid the cut sequencing problem in the future.

Regardless, thanks to Franke’s investment in solid-state lasers, the cutting operation, even without automation and with manual parts labeling and a secondary deburring process, part flow remains consistent. According to the team at Franke, the laser cutting process never starves the shop’s constraint processes of deburring and bending, which governs the operation’s overall throughput.
