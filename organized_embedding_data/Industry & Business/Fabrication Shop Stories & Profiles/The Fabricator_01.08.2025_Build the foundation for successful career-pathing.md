hen I joined Nix Industrial in 2013, we were a tightknit team of fewer than 15 people operating out of one shop in Poseyville, Ind. It was a fifth-generation family business with a rich history dating back to 1902. Over the years I’ve worked in and led various departments, including sales, marketing, human resources, IT, and operations. These experiences shaped my understanding of what it takes to build and support a high-performing team.

Today, we are a 200-plus-employee company with operations in Indiana and Ohio. From 2003 to 2023, we experienced 100-times revenue growth—a journey fueled by grit, innovation, and a strong culture.

That growth didn’t happen without some hard lessons learned. Early on, one thing became increasingly clear: We lacked role clarity and structured career paths. Starting a formal career-pathing program sooner could have accelerated our momentum and eased organizational friction.

To that end, I’ll share the steps we implemented to build a career-pathing program that aligned our values, culture, and long-term goals. Whether you’re a fabricator, machinist, or business leader looking to invest in your workforce, you can use these steps to create a foundation for sustained growth.

Define the Career Paths

Clear career pathways provide structure, set expectations, and align individual ambitions with a company’s strategic goals. Without defined pathways, team members can feel lost or undervalued, leading to disengagement and high turnover.

At Nix, we recognized that a one-size-fits-all approach wouldn’t work for our diverse team. Instead, we chose to define four distinct pathways, each tailored to the specific needs and career aspirations within our organization. These make up our career-pathing program known as PATH:

professional advancement targeting happiness.

(As you’ll soon notice, we love acronyms.)

Production.

This includes welders, fabricators, machinists, painters, field service technicians, and similar roles. Typical progression starts at apprentice and advances through journeyman, team lead, foreman, operations manager, general manager, and ultimately vice president.

Production Support.

This includes estimators, engineers, detailers, project managers, purchasers, maintenance, safety, quality, and continuous improvement professionals. Typical progression moves from coordinator to manager, director, and finally vice president.

Administrative Support.

This includes roles like accounting and finance, human resources, IT, training, and general administration. Like production support, this path also follows the coordinator-to-vice president trajectory.

Business Development.

This focuses on client-facing and growth roles like outside sales, inside sales, digital sales, marketing, R&D, and PR. It follows the coordinator-to-vice president progression.

Traditional vs. Nontraditional Career Paths

Some team members follow a traditional path, advancing within one track (from apprentice to vice president in production, for instance), while others take a nontraditional route, moving across pathways—like a production journeyman to inside sales, then into project management in production support. This flexibility lets employees explore different aspects of the business, build diverse skills, and find roles that match their strengths.

The Nix LEAD Level 1 class focuses on foundational communication and operational excellence skills.

By offering both paths, Nix has been able to promote from within and build a resilient, high-performing team. One of our tenured team members, Dakota, began his career as a paid high school intern, and upon graduation he accepted a full-time apprenticeship position. Throughout his tenure, he transitioned to become a journeyman and eventually a team lead within the production pathway.

As our organization grew, we needed additional quality control oversight within our custom fabrication business unit. Dakota saw this opportunity, obtained the necessary position requirements (like becoming a certified welding inspector), and moved into the production support pathway as our quality and logistics coordinator. Dakota is now the operations manager of one of our largest business units.

Clarify Roles and Expectations

Creating roles, responsibilities, and expectations (RR&E) is a crucial step in any career-pathing program. RR&E documents serve as a strategic tool that clearly outlines an individual’s position within the organization. They serve as a foundation for performance management, providing a consistent benchmark for reviews, feedback, and professional development. They help companies onboard new hires effectively and prepare future leaders. And they help prevent knowledge silos, miscommunication, and informational bottlenecks, protecting daily operations from disruption.

Strong RR&E documents provide an overview of the role: What is the role? Where does it fit within the organization? Perhaps most important, why does it matter?

They also define key

responsibilities

, describing what the role is accountable for, concrete actions, and desired outcomes. If a team member goes on vacation, these duties need to transfer to another person.

Finally, RR&E documents define

key expectations

that set the standard for performance, behavio-r, and communication, aligning individual actions with company goals. These should include quantitative metrics (KPIs) and qualitative standards (leadership behaviors) that define what success looks like.

Every role should align with the company’s strategic priorities. Specific responsibilities and measurable expectations should be both meaningful and impactful. It’s also essential to update these documents regularly to reflect the needs of the business.

Skills for the Climb

To achieve their career aspirations, team members need the right tools, training, and support. Leaders might struggle to delegate tasks or meet team goals if they constantly address performance issues. At Nix, we recognize that effective training programs for both technical and leadership skills are essential for closing skills gaps, improving safety, and boosting morale.

Proper training makes everyone more productive. Well-trained employees are less likely to have accidents or mishandle equipment, reducing downtime and costly repairs. Good training also makes employees feel supported and equipped to succeed. They become more engaged and are less likely to leave.

Creating a career-pathing program is only part of the equation. You must engage your team in the journey. How do you foster engagement? First, make it personal.

Everyone who joins Nix knows about their potential career path—or paths—within the organization.

Technical Skills

Nix has a technical skills training program called CRAFT, which stands for

cultivate real advancement for tradespeople.

(See? I told you we liked acronyms.) The program helps cross-train employees in every area of the shop. It categorizes team members into four levels.

1.

No previous training

, which means they come to us with no industry experience.

2.

Basic

, which means they’re able to perform tasks with supervision and understand safety protocols and general operations.

3.

Advanced

, meaning they can operate with minimal oversight, and they’re able to troubleshoot and solve problems.

4.

Mastered

, which signifies they’ve mastered a skill and are capable of training and leading others.

We customize each level for each area of the shop, whether it be a process like aluminum TIG welding or running automated equipment like robotic plasma cutting. Standard curriculum and guidelines are available for each area to support their advancement.

Employees also are referred to external vendors to advance their skills. Resources like the American Welding Society certification programs and courses, along with training programs provided by vendors and machine manufacturers, help to build well-rounded individuals with the technical expertise we need to keep the work flowing smoothly.

Leadership Skills

Time for more acronyms.

Leadership exploration and development

, or LEAD, is a leadership development program that blends handson learning, mentorship, and practical application. Like its CRAFT cousin, LEAD has several levels, each designed to help every team member climb the career ladder.

LEAD Level 1

is a 16-week program for anyone with at least one year of employment. It focuses on foundational leadership skills, communication, and operational excellence. It includes virtual sessions, in-person meetings, book reviews, and personal presentations.

LEAD Level 2

is a two-part program for those invited to participate by a sponsor from the senior leadership team. This program consists of a year-long leadership section that meets quarterly to review topics such as taking, understanding, and leveraging a DISC profile (which reveals different behavioral styles); conducting crucial conversations; delegation and decision-making; and project ownership.

The second part of the program reviews management tasks and philosophies. Participants use the Nix Industrial Online Training Platform to progress through modules covering human resources principles and laws, operational excellence, sales and marketing, finance and accounting, and strategic planning.

Once participants graduate from the LEAD Level 2 program, they join the rest of the leadership team in

LEAD Level 3

. In this cohort-style program, participants select topics specific to our needs. They meet off-site biannually and virtually during the remaining quarters.

Making Sparks

Creating a career-pathing program is only part of the equation. You must engage your team in the journey. How do you foster engagement? First, make it personal. Share success stories, like the story that I shared about Dakota earlier. Ensure that leaders promote the program and set an example by participating in their own career development. When you stop learning, you stop growing.

Next, communicate clearly and continually. Make use of newsletters, evaluations, and digital communication boards throughout your operations. At Nix, all employees gather each week in-person and virtually to discuss new initiatives, safety, quality, and culture. Also be sure to celebrate milestones. Recognize and reward employees who actively engage in their career growth, both publicly and in one-on-one settings.

Finally, offer mentorship and peer support. Establish a culture in which everyone supports each other’s growth. This in turn creates a network of mentors. We refer to this as our FAM, or

friend and mentor

, program. (Yes, more acronyms.)

Any successful career-pathing program must be dynamic and responsive. We at Nix ensure this by gathering regular feedback. To do this, we rely on various tools, the first being the pulse survey—short, regular surveys that gather insights into employee satisfaction and program effectiveness. We conduct at least two companywide surveys yearly. The surveys do mention PATH and continuing education, but they’re not centered solely on them. We also host small focus groups to dive into specific issues and improvement opportunities.

Encourage leaders to hold regular career development conversations—and don’t forget the value of one-on-one meetings. If you are not conducting regular

stay interviews

, you are missing out on valuable feedback. Companies routinely conduct exit interviews. Why not gather insights before an employee leaves, when you can make a difference?

As Nix continues to grow, we remain committed to supporting our team’s ambitions, creating opportunities for personal and professional development, and building a strong organization. For those looking to follow a similar path, remember that investing in your people is the foundation for success. It’s the spark that lights the way to 100-times growth—and beyond.
