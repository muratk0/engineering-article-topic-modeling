Without employee buy-in, a metal fabricating company can’t truly be as safe as it could be

Jeremy Miller of BTD Manufacturing explains how his working group resolved a safety issue at a hypothetical company during an exercise at the 2026 FMA Safety Conference on May 12.

Dan Davis

Bill Toms is an effective speaker at a safety conference. It’s not just what he says; his physical being is a testament to respecting safety risks and doing what’s necessary to stay safe.

Toms has a prosthetic arm and a foot. He lost both in an accident that occurred when he was working as a lineman in North Carolina in 1997. The five-man crew he was a part of was replacing older electrical lines with newer ones capable of delivering more power. Toms grabbed a part of the line mechanism that he thought was depowered; it was not. When he closed his grip, 7,200 volts shot through him, entering his right hand and exiting out his right foot. He couldn’t let go as his hand muscles contracted tightly.

"It was like it all happened in slow motion," he told the crowd gathered for the FMA Safety Conference, hosted by Marsh McLennan Agency in Schaumburg, Ill., on May 12.

Toms eventually got to a hospital in Winston-Salem, N.C. He had fifth-degree burns and needed five surgeries to stabilize his limbs and ensure there was no infection. (Toms said that the doctors estimated the heat from the shock to be around 35,000 degrees F.)

"I was thankful that I was alive, but I also knew my life wouldn’t be the same again," he said.

He was discharged from the hospital after 10 days. He spent the next several months learning to live with his prosthetic limbs and figuring out what he could do for a living. He certainly wasn’t capable of climbing utility poles anymore.

Seven months after the injury, his former employer, MB Haynes Corp., reached out to him to see if he wanted to join the company as an assistant safety director. He agreed because he saw himself as a "poster child for safety."

Now, in addition to being MB Haynes’ vice president for safety risk management, he’s also an in-demand speaker on industrial safety. His message to the FMA Safety Conference attendees was to be vigilant against complacency. For instance, on the day Toms was electrocuted, he was not wearing his rubber gloves, designed to protect a person from electrocution, because it was a warm day, and he also just assumed that electrical flow had been shut down before he made his fateful grab.

"Most workplace accidents don’t happen because people don’t know the rules; they don’t respect the safety risks," he said.

Hearing such a message from a speaker like Toms is memorable, but it’s still not enough to change behaviors. People need to be coaxed and reminded that workplace safety is good for all.

Avoiding the Blame Game

The manufacturing environment can go a long way in encouraging people either to embrace or to ignore efforts aimed at reducing injury risk in the workplace, according to Danny Smith, a senior safety consultant with SafeStart. An organization that focuses on blame—pointing out individuals involved in accidents or even near-misses and following that up with disciplinary measures—will have a hard time getting employees to be honest and involved in improving safety performance.

This "blame, shame, and retrain" approach, as Smith described it, discourages people from reporting unsafe behaviors and problem areas in a facility. Some employees are cast in a negative light if their mistakes are shared without context. Some of those same employees might be slighted, perhaps by being micromanaged or given undesirable schedules or tasks.

"Pointing fingers does not make things better. It makes you bitter," said Smith, sharing a favorite quote of his. "We need to be coaching folks. We need to be communicating with them in a positive way."

The key is for organizations to focus on safety goals. With the focus on the outcomes and steps taken to achieve them, employees feel like it’s a team effort rather than a witch hunt for unsafe workers. This establishes an environment of accountability without fear of repercussion, Smith said.

Goals are established. Best practices are shared. Unsafe activities are pointed out, and steps are taken to avoid them. When a nearmiss or an accident occurs, the team can reorient, working to ensure it doesn’t happen in the future, Smith added.

"Instead of asking ‘Who’s at fault?’ ask ‘Who’s accountable?’ It pulls everybody into the conversation and keeps it positive," he said.

Safety Strategies for the Shop

Dan Shaevel, a safety consultant with Marsh McLennan Agency, said companies also need to realize that safety compliance is not the same thing as being committed to safety. Compliance is merely a signal to the outside world that an organization has met the legally minimum safety requirements.

"Just because you can pass an audit doesn’t mean that you are a safe company," he said.

A great example of a company making the minimum safety effort is one that holds safety meetings where only accident reports are shared. Follow-up to ensure that similar accidents are avoided in the future rarely happens.

"Functional safety is empowering employees, managers included, to make safety decisions by engaging with them directly," he added.

Shaevel offered some strategies that metal fabricators can use to inspire more employee involvement in safety matters:

• Appoint safety liaisons

. A lot of shops can’t afford to have a full safety team, but what if motivated individuals were given a few hours a week and a slight pay increase to assist with safety efforts? They can act as informal safety monitors, keeping tabs on the shop floor and mentoring new hires. They also create a talent pipeline in case the company needs to replace a safety director or expand the staff.

• Create a safety reporting system.

Employees should be able to report unsafe practices or suggest areas where investments can be made to prevent injuries. They also should be assured that management will take every submission under advisement. To encourage buy-in from long-term employees, safety liaisons should ask them to report safety concerns from the past that have not been acknowledged. Solving some of these issues early on will help safety liaisons build trust with their co-workers.

• Change the safety language.

It’s not "safety first"; it’s "people first, safety always." Stories about co-workers have a greater impact than statistics. Repeating safety messages in different ways, such as with signage, in enewsletters, and in safety talks before shifts, can help ensure everyone is informed.

• Hit the shop floor

. A safety director, manager, or anyone interested in reducing the risk of injury in the workplace should actually walk the shop floor and watch people work. They can compliment good work practices, address opportunities for improvement right away, and ask employees for their input. That type of engagement lets workers know that company leaders are serious about safety, and they are more likely to take initiatives more seriously.

Awarding Excellence

The award recognizes one company each year that demonstrates exceptional safety performance, exceeding OSHA recordable incidence rates while fostering a deeply rooted culture of safety.

This year’s winner is CDH Custom Roll Form, Warren, Ohio. The company recently celebrated five years without an accident.

Marion Body Works adds automated wall and floor manufacturing systems

Marion Body Works, a national manufacturer of commercial truck bodies, has implemented two major automation systems at its Shawano, Wis., facility: a fully automated bonded sheet and post wall assembly system and an automated floor assembly gantry system.

Together, these systems have transformed previously manual, labor-intensive processes into streamlined, controlled operations that have improved quality and consistency, increased throughput, and created a safer work environment.

Installed in November 2025, the automated floor assembly gantry system replaced manual drilling and fastening with a servo-controlled, automated process. The router capabilities of the floor system further improve accuracy, fitment, and appearance of pipe pockets and D-rings.

The new wall automation system, which became operational in April 2026, consolidates multiple manual steps, including material preparation, fastening, bonding, and sealing into a single, integrated process using robotics. The system also introduces plasma treatment technology for improved adhesion and durability of the walls and truck bodies.
