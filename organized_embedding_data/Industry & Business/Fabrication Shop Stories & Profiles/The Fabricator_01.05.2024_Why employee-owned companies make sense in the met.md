# Why employee-owned companies make sense in the metal fabricating industry

[TARİH: 01.05.2024 The Fabricator]

Shop Management

Financial and cultural benefits stand out

By

Dan Davis

These metal fabricators working on a large project at Robinson Inc., De Pere, Wis., also happen to be owners of the company. As of mid-October 2023, the company became 100% employee-owned after transitioning to an employee stock ownership plan (ESOP) framework.

n Oct. 19, Robinson Inc., a De Pere, Wis.-based metal fabricator with nearly 600 employees and more than $200 million in annual revenues, announced that it had become 100% employee-owned. For those involved in the transition, it made total sense.

As the company’s shareholders were looking to make the transition away from ownership, they had several options, including selling to a private equity group or even a competitor. But they recognized that the Robinson employees had played an important part in helping to build the organization, and they didn’t want them to feel like their futures were in doubt after any type of ownership transition.

"The employee stock ownership plan [ESOP] fit best with the Robinson culture. It also was important to take care of the people," said Sam Thomas, Robinson’s vice president and chief operating officer. "It was really attractive to be able to add another benefit to the team and really reward them for their hard work. And that’s something that can go on for decades."

Robinson is not alone in its interest in the ESOP framework. Manufacturing is the largest industry sector for ESOPs, with 20.9% of employee-owned firms being manufacturers, according to the 2024 National ESOP Database from the National Center for Employee Ownership (NCEO). Other sectors that have a large number of ESOPs include professional, scientific, and technical service (19.2%) and construction (16.9%). (According to the latest data available from the U.S. Department of Labor and other sources, as of 2021, the NCEO reports that 6,322 companies have an ESOP. That covers 36,355 participants.)

Why does the manufacturing sector lead the way in the adoption of employee ownership of companies? When you look at the benefits, it’s easy to understand.

A Natural Fit

An ESOP actually is owned by a trust, but the employees have a beneficial interest in the trust. The employees are more shareholders than actual business owners.

Having said that, in many instances, they can influence company direction, even if they don’t have a seat behind a big desk. For employees to increase the value of their company shares, the company needs to be successful enough that its fair market value increases. As anyone in a metal fabricating company can attest, the business is complex enough and gets more difficult when people aren’t working toward the same goals. Employees can contribute to profitability by ensuring that quality parts are delivered on time to customers.

"The key to [ESOPs] is that if people own a piece of the place that they work, they are just going to care more. You’re going to try harder. You’re going to serve customers better. You’re going to be more engaged. You’re going to be more passionate about the business than if you’re just an employee showing up every week to get a paycheck," said Brian Baker, president/CEO of Sentry Equipment in Oconomowoc, Wis.

Sentry Equipment, a manufacturer of equipment that supports production efforts in industries such as power generation, petrochemicals, and wastewater treatment, became an ESOP in 1986, when younger members of the company’s founding family, the Henszeys, showed no interest in pursuing ownership of the business. Baker joined the company in late 1995 as vice president and CFO before assuming his current position in 2013.

Even when the ESOP announcement was first made, Sentry Equipment would need several years to pass before it really hit its stride under its new ownership framework. Dick Henszey, who had led the company for numerous years before stepping aside in 1986, was a strong leader and involved in most decision-making, creating an environment where people waited for direction. Baker said that delayed the creation of a "culture of employee ownership."

Maintaining the employee-focused culture was the driving force in Robinson Inc. moving from private ownership to an ESOP. The annual fish fry is one of many ways the company tries to thank the employees for their efforts.

Given that ESOPs are often exempt from federal income tax requirements, they are often in a good position to consistently make key investments, such as in new capital equipment.

"It’s good to have people that are willing to take orders, but you want to have people that understand that they need to make decisions, take action, and make things happen, rather than sitting around and waiting to be told what to do," Baker added. "If you really want to have a great employee-owned company, you have to make that transition."

Baker said that five pillars need to exist to support the creation of an "ownership" culture:

Transparency. Employees need to see the financial information and understand their role in profitability and the strategic vision.

Engagement. Employees need to feel that they can contribute and are expected to do just that. They need to feel like they are a vital part of the organization’s success.

Trust. Everyone needs to trust others in the organization. They need to know that company leaders are making the right strategic decisions and that co-workers are doing what they can to accomplish goals that support the company’s strategic direction.

Responsibility. Employees should feel emboldened to make things happen, but also understand that accountability comes with that decision-making.

Growth. An environment that supports professional growth, skills development, and continuous improvement is one that paves the way for business expansion.

"The transition to an employee-owned culture really had the business take off," Baker said.

Sentry Equipment has made several acquisitions in recent years, and it’s added locations as well. It now employs 200 people with smaller satellite locations in Houston and Salt Lake City.

This type of business success doesn’t surprise Adria Scharf, associate director for the Institute for the Study of Employee Ownership and Profit Sharing, Rutgers University. She sees the culture of manufacturing companies as being a natural fit for ESOPs.

"The incentive alignment benefits of the ESOP can often meld really well with the sort of process improvement approaches already at work within manufacturing companies," she said.

"When I interview people in ESOP manufacturing companies, you learn that when you give employees stock, it enables the employee to connect the company-level financial information with their own work-related choices.

"So, if you are trying to encourage shop floor workers to focus on expense control or improve productivity, they can connect the dots because the employees as stock owners now have a direct and personal link to the overall company success."

Scharf added that manufacturing companies, because of their established natures, tend to avoid the churn in employees that restaurants and retailers experience, which really helps the ESOP framework shine. Because workers tend to have extended stays at manufacturing companies, they can end up accumulating a lot of shares in the company, whether they are cognizant of it or not.

The Financial Reward for the Employees

"ESOPs are really good at rewarding that longterm tenure," Scharf said.

Baker said that Sentry Equipment has seen about 15 "ESOP millionaires," executives, engineers, salespeople, and shop floor workers that received at least $1 million when their shares were sold back to the company upon retirement. Of course, that doesn’t count the others that spent 20 to 25 years with the company and left with $750,000 or $900,000. This payout would be on top of any other retirement account they might have set up.

Thomas said that he sees the ESOP framework as being the perfect complement to offering profit-sharing and 401(k) programs. While other companies might pull back on such programs in favor of propping up the ESOP, he doesn’t think that’s the best strategy, particularly when younger workers really aren’t focused on a possible retirement payment that’s 25 or more years away.

"We have five generations in the workforce. The value sets of each of those generations are a little different. The ability to balance the short-term gratification with the quarterly profit-sharing with the long term in the ESOP keeps people engaged. It’s a really good balance," Thomas said.

ESOPs tend to be better for workers in the long run than non-ESOP organizations. A 2010 NCEO analysis of ESOP company government filings in 2008 found that the average employee in an employee-owned company sees about $4,443 per year in company contributions to the ownership plan and has an account balance of $55,836. Additionally, that same analysis revealed that 56% of the ESOP companies offered at least one additional retirement plan.

Meanwhile, the Bureau of Labor Statistics reports that only 52% of private industry workers chose to participate in a retirement plan. In an ESOP, the employee’s company shares can be sold to provide some sort of financial cushion once the worker leaves, whether they chose to participate in another retirement plan or not.

The Financial Reward for the Owners

When a manufacturing company is being sold, it has a few options. It can be sold to a competitor, which breeds some insecurity for employees as they wonder if the new owners will consolidate operations and close the new acquisition. It can be sold to private equity, which definitely causes worry as these types of investors have a reputation of cutting expenses to the bone in hopes of flipping the business down the road for a nice reward. Newcomers to the industry also are an alternative, but they need to learn the business, which can lead to rough sailing given the new leadership’s lack of experience.

In the best-case scenario, selling a company to those who know the business totally makes sense. They know what works, and they know the market. ESOPs allow that to happen.

Thomas said that Robinson spent more than two years investigating the ESOP process and structure. They needed to learn not just what it was, but also how to set it up.

"When you think about benefits and getting people to buy into what you’re trying to accomplish, which benefits our team members and their families, the ESOP is probably the only way to really accomplish that," he said.

The ESOP arrangement allows the seller to enjoy several benefits: a fair market value sale price, continuation of the business in familiar hands, and even active participation in the business after being sold. It’s not usual to see a former owner stay onboard in some sort of leadership capacity, particularly if no one is ready to step forward and assume the role.

The seller also might stay involved because they might not get their money right away. Scharf said that sometimes a full payout at the sale is not possible, so the seller has to wait to be repaid over several years. She added that this sometimes is a stumbling block in the creation of an ESOP because some owners want to cash out right away and start drinking tropical cocktails on a beach. That’s why private equity groups are always attractive buyers because they often can deliver the full asking price right away.

But there’s another financial reward that comes after the final sale takes place. This one applies to the new owners—the employees. The tax benefits associated with being an ESOP company are great.

An ESOP that is 100% employee-owned and is an S Corp., an Internal Revenue Service designation for a corporation that chooses to pass corporate income, losses, deductions, and credits through to the shareholders, usually is exempt from paying federal income tax. Also, in many states, according to Scharf, those same companies are exempt from state income taxes.

"Saving on federal income means you are cash rich in terms of your position of being able to pay down debt, acquire new technology, or acquire competitors," Scharf said. "It’s really quite a powerful tax incentive."

Baker agreed with that assessment, but he bristles when someone calls it a "tax loophole." He contended that Congress knew what it was doing in creating this tax arrangement for ESOPs.

"They knew that this was something that would give employee-owned companies a leg up. They thought it was a good idea because they thought employee ownership was such a good idea," Baker said.

Obviously, such a tax arrangement helps with cash flow. That not only helps to fund expansion plans and capital equipment investments, but it also gives the company the funds to buy shares back from employees who leave or retire, something that it is obligated to do as part of the ESOP rules.

The Right Choice

Sentry Equipment has grown from 65 employees to 200 and expanded its presence to the west and south since becoming an ESOP. Baker gives a lot of credit for this growth to the culture that’s given employees a reason to be engaged in every aspect of the organization’s operations.

"Being an employee-owned company for almost 40 years, there’s really this unbelievable sense of pride I have when I look at all the great things that we do for the world with our products, equipment, and services that help our customers manage their processes," Baker said.

Robinson projects its 2024 annual revenue to be $238 million. The transition to an ESOP has gone smoothly, but more importantly, it’s helped the organization maintain the same culture that made it possible to grow to the size it is. Employee ownership also bodes well for the future.

For both new and old customers finding out about Robinson’s new ownership arrangement, "I think they find it satisfying, or even a relief, that there is a long-term plan for us to continue to do what we do," Thomas said.

So that leaves a major question: If ESOPs work so well for manufacturing companies, why aren’t more ESOPs formed? Scharf said that it might be because the arrangement works too well.

About 250 ESOPs are formed every year, but at the same time, because of their successful operations, they are often the targets for acquisition by other companies, Scharf said. ESOPs are victims of their own success.
