Manufacturing Day 2014 smashes all participation goals

Manufacturing Day 2014 exceeded all expectations, with events extending far beyond Oct. 3.

A total of 1,679 events were held across North America, in all 50 states, three Canadian provinces, and Puerto Rico. For the first time, more than a dozen virtual events were held, allowing participants to join in from anywhere. Discovery Education and Alcoa hosted the largest virtual event, with more than 100,000 students participating. Based on data collected from host company evaluations, more than 260,000 people participated in live events. This brings the total number of individuals involved with Manufacturing Day activities to nearly 400,000.

President Barack Obama attended his first Manufacturing Day event, an open house at Millennium Steel Service in Princeton, Ind., and issued a proclamation declaring Oct. 3, 2014, as Manufacturing Day in the U.S. The focus of this proclamation is summed up in these words: "With ingenuity and a determined spirit, hardworking Americans are creating products and unlocking new technologies that will shape our nation and grow our economy. On National Manufacturing Day we celebrate all those who proudly stand behind our goods and services made in America, and we renew our commitment to winning the race for the jobs of tomorrow."

ThyssenKrupp Steel Europe, Fraunhofer IWS, LIMO partner for laser research

ThyssenKrupp Steel Europe, the Fraunhofer Institute for Materials and Beam Technology (IWS), and LIMO (Lissotschenko Mikrooptik GmbH) have initiated development work on a new laser technology that the partners will use to improve the properties of steel strip surfaces. The collaboration, part of the OSLO research project by the German Federal Ministry of Education and Research (BMBF), is scheduled to be completed by 2017.

Together, these organizations are researching how the properties of ferrous steel strip materials can be influenced in a targeted manner by laser surface treatment in a continuous inline, wide-strip process. To provide steel with better corrosion protection, for example, the laser will uniformly heat the steel’s surface layers over the entire strip width. Since the laser heats only the surface rather than the entire volume of the wide strip, the process also is energy-efficient and environmentally friendly.

"To enable these types of shorttime surface treatments, a laser system never before available anywhere in the world has to create a lengthscalable, uniform, and extremely thin line of light," said Dr. Jens Meinschien, vice president innovations management at LIMO. "To meet this challenge, we’re engineering a powerful 35-kW semiconductor laser featuring sophisticated beam shaping and high beam quality."

The partners aim to develop a new type of laser surface treatment and possibly bring it to maturity for eventual series production.

People make the difference at 2015 FABRICATOR Leadership Summit

Keynote speaker Ken Schmidt, former director of communications strategy for Harley-Davidson, presents to several hundred attendees at the FMA Annual Meeting.

Machinery remains at the very core of metal fabrication. Cutting automation has become the new norm. Precision press brakes and bending automation are spreading as never before, and driving it all is advanced software, both at the machine and enterprise level. The fab shop has become a technological cornucopia.

Technology’s pervasiveness, however, brings its own challenges. A shop can make great investments in technology and, in the short run at least, underbid the competition and win more business—but for how long? Years ago just having a laser set a fabricator apart. Now, in at least some North American markets, it’s difficult to imagine a precision sheet metal operation without one.

Although a shop can’t compete without advanced machinery, equipment alone eventually won’t be a differentiator—and neither will stellar quality, on-time delivery, and low prices. What, then, is left? It boils down to people and, more specifically, the connections between them: the salesperson and the customer, the manager and the new hire, the press brake operator and the welder.

That message pervaded

The FABRICATOR

’s Leadership Summit, held Feb. 25-27 at the Gaylord Palms Resort in Kissimmee, Fla. The summit was held in conjunction with the Toll Processing Conference, both of which were part of

The FABRICATOR

s & Manufacturers Association’s Annual Meeting.

"If we stand only for selling a product, that’s all people will know us for, and we can be replaced by someone else at a cheaper price."

So said keynote speaker Ken Schmidt, former director of communications strategy for Harley-Davidson. Schmidt spoke of the struggles the iconic motorcycle brand had in the 1980s, when the company was known for making mediocre bikes. So Harley did what it had to do to improve product quality, and then marketed that quality to customers. Problem was, customers still weren’t buying because other companies offered quality bikes with similar specs, but at a lower price.

The motorcycle had turned into a commodity, and Schmidt and his team realized that they had approached sales and marketing ineffectively—by selling products, and their specs, to customers. "They’re not customers," he said. "They’re

people

."

He added that people don’t buy on specifications, but on emotion. At Harley-Davidson, the market was controlling the narrative, a rational one based on numbers: price and specifications. "Part of how we killed ourselves, when people began copying us, was using rational language to differentiate ourselves. We pushed them to others because we said the same things they did, but we charged more money." Today people don’t buy Harleys; they buy the Harley experience.

(Editor’s Note: For more on Kevin Schmidt’s presentation, see this month’s Biz Talk column.)

The metal fabrication industry usually doesn’t delve too far into consumer markets, especially ones as emotional as the Harley experience, but the people in this business—suppliers, employees, and customers alike—aren’t emotionless automatons either.

In one strategy session, Dick Kallage, principal at KDC & Associates, Barrington, Ill., talked about how in some areas price pressures tend to be increasing in a major way, thanks in part to excess capacity. Modern machines produce more in less time with fewer resources, which in turn puts the pressure on pricing. "Where do you think the profits we’re seeing in the stock market are coming from?" Kallage pointed to his audience. "They’re coming from you."

Attendees network during roundtable discussions.

Still, the one area that can’t be copied, at least exactly, is the human aspect of the fab shop: the processing decisions front-line personnel and managers make. "A machine is a pretty bad project manager," Kallage said.

How those project managers choose to implement technology can set a fabricator apart, a point that hit home at the event’s technology roundtable, which featured representatives from Amada, Bystronic, MC Machinery Systems/Mitsubishi Laser, Mazak Optonics Corp., and TRUMPF Inc.

Each presented case histories about how technology is changing the business. Some showed how the solidstate fiber and disk lasers can cut even thick materials aggressively. Some machines do this by offering higher powers and focusing optics designed so that a beam can slice effectively through plate. Other systems cut thin and thick materials by altering the beam characteristics in real time.

All this cutting productivity has forced people to ask new questions and consider new processing alternatives.

With so much cutting throughput, how do we avoid a bending bottleneck?

Today’s bending centers with automated tool changes, some robotized and some not, help fabricators handle all the work flowing from fast lasers.

Should we bend this part or weld it?

This becomes a viable question with extremely fast solid-state laser welding robots. Instead of laser cutting one blank that requires multiple bends on a press brake, a fabricator can laser-cut separate blanks, place them in a fixture, and laser-weld them to form the final shape. Sometimes the laser welding cell can share the same laser source with a cutting system.

Machines and automation do the work, but to make the best use of all this technological wizardry, people need to ask the right questions and be curious and open to new approaches. This was keynote speaker Todd Grimm’s message. President of Edgewood, Ky.-based T.A. Grimm and Associates, Grimm spoke on emerging 3-D printing technologies, especially ones involving metal deposition, either via a laser or an electron beam. Such technologies need the right application to make sense. The most notable, General Electric’s fuel nozzle made with additive manufacturing, made sense not because of lower piece-part costs or shorter cycle times. "It was about, ‘We can save you $1.5 billion in fuel costs,’" Grimm said.

He added that additive manufacturing won’t replace the laser, press brake, or even machining center anytime soon. But it may help a fabricator design a quality fixture, welding fixture, or robot end effector.

It goes back again to asking the right questions, and to do that, fabricators need the right information. This is where the use of shop management software, including enterprise resource planning (ERP), comes into play. This year’s software panel discussion included representatives from Epicor, Exact Online, KeyedIn Solutions, and ECi M1. Panelists answered questions about data migration, going paperless, using cloud-based ERPs, and how the most effective software presents the right information to those who need it.

Software can’t run a shop on its own, of course. People need to act on the information software provides, and they also need to act on data threats. During a session headed by Steve Douglas, director of technology for CNA Risk Control, attendees discussed the myriad threats the digital world of business now faces. Some threats are small, isolated events, whereas others are catastrophic. Hackers can gain unauthorized access; viruses can infect servers; people can release information accidentally; someone can even just physically steal a device, like a laptop.

Douglas described how the easiest, not-very-technical ways to breach a system are the easiest to combat. It boils down to teaching employees about the threat and using a common-sense defense strategy.

The annual FMA Golf Outing took place during the final afternoon of the conference. The winning team was, from left to right, James Mauro, MicroFab LLC; Andrew Mackessy, Ometek Inc.; Paul Siders, Ometek Inc.; and Bill Isaac, Mitsubishi Laser.

The strategy doesn’t necessarily hinge solely on sophisticated technology, but also on well-trained people making smart decisions. A hacker may gain access to a system, call an employee and claim he’s from an Internet service provider, and ask that person to change their password. As soon as that person changes the password, the hacker gains access and wreaks havoc. If people have the right knowledge, a company can avoid the havoc.

Since the first Leadership Summit a decade ago, informal roundtable discussions—fabricators talking to other fabricators—have remained at the heart of the event, and this year was no different. And perhaps no other issue spurs passionate discussion quite like workforce development. Participants discussed various ways companies are tackling the problem.

Discussions ranged from where skill development begins (parenting) to a new way to look at company management. Shop owners and managers discussed their partnerships with local community colleges, some of which have become quite progressive in developing technical talent.

Several roundtable participants discussed how their company structures have changed in recent years. As one attendee, the president of a 200-person shop, said, "We changed the culture of our organization five years ago to become an employer of choice." The shop first benchmarked all its wages to ensure they met or exceeded industry averages for those employees who were meeting or exceeding performance expectations.

Second, the entire management structure changed, and they pushed decision-making authority closer to the front lines. If a front-line worker has an idea to improve things, he may well have the authority to communicate and make that change. Such an engaged, well-paying, progressive work environment has helped attract some of the best technical talent in the area.

The participant conceded that change wasn’t easy. People—especially managers—pushed back, and many left. But over 18 months of "denial and pushback," the company slowly changed to an entirely new culture.

After they listened to the attendee’s story, other roundtable participants sat silent, just for an instant. Then one attendee spoke up. "Wow. Can we have your card?" Everyone laughed; cards were exchanged. As in previous years, that interaction was what the 2015 summit was all about.

North American robotics market has strongest year ever in 2014

Robot orders and shipments in North America set new records in 2014, according to the Robotic Industries Association (RIA), Ann Arbor, Mich.

A total of 27,685 robots valued at $1.6 billion were ordered from North American companies during 2014, an increase of 28 percent in units and 19 percent in dollars over 2013. Robot shipments also set new records, with 25,425 robots valued at $1.5 billion being shipped to North American customers in 2014. Shipments grew 13 percent in units and 6 percent in dollars over the previous records set in 2013.

The automotive industry was the primary driver of growth in 2014, with robot orders increasing 45 percent year over year. Nonautomotive industries also performed well throughout the year, growing 7 percent in total over 2013. The standout nonautomotive industries in 2014 in terms of order growth were plastics and rubber (25 percent), semiconductor and electronics (21 percent), and metals (16 percent).

The fastest-growing applications for robot orders in North America in 2014 were arc welding (58 percent), spot welding (57 percent), assembly (16 percent), and material handling (11 percent). RIA estimates that some 230,000 robots are now in use in U.S. factories, placing the U.S. second only to Japan in robot use.

CB Fabricating expands facilities, production capacity

Anderson, Ind.-based sheet metal fabricator CB Fabricating Inc. has announced that in less than a year it has expanded its production capacity by 75 percent, added a second shift, and hired three full-time employees to meet growing demand from clients in multiple industries.

The firm added an Amada FO MII 3015 NT laser flex cell, with automated load/unload and remote monitoring, in October 2014, along with an Amada press brake. The company plans additional hires in 2015.

CB Fabricating has been selected as one of three finalists for the Small Business of the Year Award from the Madison County Chamber in Indiana. The award honors businesses of fewer than 100 employees, in existence for five years or more, that have excelled in visible growth and achievements, adhered to their brand promises, and invested in the community both through business and community support.

Goodfellow introduces comprehensive custom manufacturing services

Coraopolis, Pa.-based Goodfellow, a supplier of small to medium quantities of high-quality metals and other materials for research and product design, has expanded its offering to include custom manufacturing and finishing of components to customer specifications.

The company can help customers select, if desired, the most suitable materials for the application, then manufacture the desired component to customer drawings and finish with custom coatings and surface treatments.

Services include precision machining, cutting, turning, rolling, drilling, grinding, and milling; forming, bending, and shaping; coating with almost any metal or polymer to a defined thickness; and surface treatment, mechanical polishing, and chemical polishing.

Rafter delivers three weld squeeze boxes

Strongsville, Ohio-based Rafter Equipment Corp. has delivered three RT-2500S HFI weld squeeze boxes for a North American mechanical tubing producer. The boxes are used for producing heavy wall thicknesses and high-yieldstrength materials.

The design follows the company’s push-on-center weld box philosophy that eliminates the cantilever forces on the roll tooling clevises (or yokes). The heavy-duty Timken tapered roller bearings within the roll tools provide increased bearing life, says the company. The boxes can handle up to 3.00-in.-OD by 0.188-in.-wall tube running HSLA material.

The worm-screw head roll adjusters are in a sealed box to reduce maintenance. The head roll adjustments are run through the back of the unit’s sealed frame and brought to the operator’s side for simplified access.
