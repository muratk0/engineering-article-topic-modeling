M

etal fabrication abounds with new technology that can run circles around older machines. Thing is, when you visit shops, you’ll find that new technology isn’t pervasive. Even at the most progressive fabricators out there, decades-old machines tend to run alongside new systems with 3D touch controls and all the bells and whistles. Some shops survive and even thrive for decades relying mainly on very old machines and paper-based information systems. Why is this, exactly?

The answer might at first seem obvious: No one can afford to replace every machine all at once, and revamping front-office technology and procedures can’t happen overnight.

That’s true, but if the issue were solely about machinery cost, new equipment would be a lot more pervasive thanks to the free market’s creative destruction. Those who buy the latest laser, brake, or welding systems would eventually edge out competitors with less aggressive capex strategies.

Internal forces would seem to push companies toward newer machines as well. As Chris Kuehl, economic analyst for the Fabricators and Manufacturers Association explained in this month’s Fabrinomics, the mix of old and new technology has created the "lost skills" conundrum. Older workers running older machines never get a chance to spread their knowledge before they retire. When they do retire, workers who had operated those newer machines have trouble transitioning to the old ones.

Old iron dies hard, but you’d think the pressures to invest—workers retiring, immense productivity of newer machines—would push old iron’s demise. Yet it hasn’t, at least not everywhere.

I attribute this to two realities pervading the industry. The first is that metal fabrication serves an incredible diversity of end markets with equally diverse manufacturing requirements. Some require parts measured in thousandths of an inch and fractions of a degree. In others, a tape measure will do the trick. Some markets require individual parts delivered quickly. Others involve subassemblies, field work, myriad certifications, quality systems, process audits, and some serious project management on timelines that can last months or even years. Some sectors are driven by relationships; others are more transactional. Some markets change continually; others stay relatively static for years—just ask anyone processing part prints that haven’t changed in decades.

These diverse demands drive even more diverse technology investment strategies. Some operations need to buy a new laser or brake every few years just to keep up. Others keep certain pieces of equipment for decades. The demands of the end market ultimately determine what manufacturing technologies fabricators choose—and often, it’s a mix of the old and new. One company I visited recently had a 1950s-era stamping press operating at one plant, producing high-quantity pieces that haven’t changed in years. Another facility at the same company housed the latest in laser cutting and precision bending. Every machine, old and new, met a market need.

The second reality is the incredibly diverse throughput rates of different work centers along the metal fabrication value stream. You have fully automated cutting and punching centers feeding into manual denesting, deburring, and grinding areas. You’ve got robotic welding next to manual coating booths or semiautomated inline powder coating systems that require people to mask holes and hang each part individually. You’ve got outside service provider delays. A part can take seconds or minutes to cut, bend, and weld, but it still can take weeks to deliver.

Customers don’t buy something because it was made on a specific machine. They buy something because they believe it’ll be delivered on time, at the right level of quality, and for a competitive price. What matters is the

delivered result

.

"For all the headline-grabbing buzzwords, the core of our industry hasn’t changed all that much in two decades." That comment came, interestingly enough, from someone who sells metal fabrication machinery. Adrian Wright is a fiber laser specialist at Daltons Wadkin, which offers (among other machines) Kimla fiber lasers in the U.K. He argued that, although individual machines have become incredibly efficient, when you zoom out and look at the productivity of the entire metal fabrication value stream, from quote to cash, "the needle has barely moved."

This isn’t to say modern equipment isn’t a critical part of the growth equation. It’s just not the whole equation. At the FMA Annual Meeting in late February, I spoke on a panel with five shop managers who invested heavily in blanking and bending technology (watch for detailed coverage soon). They touted impressive growth and some eye-opening throughput numbers. New machines were part of that, but culture and continuous improvement played significant roles too—as did software.

"In theory, the path forward is clear: interoperability, data-driven manufacturing, and smarter scheduling powered by AI," Wright said. "Imagine simply inputting what you want to make—dimensions, material—and the system generating the program automatically, allocating it right to the machine, and managing flow through to finishing."

That last part of the sentence is key. We’re there at certain stages of the value stream—especially in cutting and bending—but not all the way to the shipping dock. This is why, in FMA’s latest Forming & Fabricating Job Shop Consumption Report, the demand for scheduling and quoting technology has remained so high. More than 46% of respondents said areas in quoting and estimating represented one of their "greatest technology needs," while 36% said the same about scheduling.

AI is already having an impact on software in metal fabrication, but I think we haven’t seen anything yet. Indeed, AI itself could create a virtuous circle. Some shops are seeing unprecedented levels of demand from AI and the wide-ranging infrastructure needs supporting it. That demand spurs metal fabrication technology investment and new ways of thinking that take advantage of all AI has to offer.

AI could lower the cost of customized technology development. Imagine widespread use of autonomous mobile robots between operations, of smart job sequencing for minimal setup and maximum part-flow velocity. The revolution won’t happen everywhere. Such is the nature of an industry serving widely disparate end markets. But in some areas, AI could well move the needle—perhaps farther than anyone ever thought possible.
