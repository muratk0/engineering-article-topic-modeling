E

veryone can agree on one thing: Manufacturing ain’t what it used to be.

Industry associations and manufacturers themselves have been trying to drive that message home over the past several years. They see baby boomers retiring at a pace of 10,000 per day in the U.S., and they are worried about who will replace them in positions of importance on the shop floor and in the front office. Manufacturing company owners and managers have real reason to be worried if they look out and see only gray-haired employees.

They are also battling the negative perception that a lot of people—old and young alike—have about manufacturing. Manufacturers have tried to take the offensive over the last decade. (If you’d like to help, visit

www.mfgday.com

and learn how you can put a positive spin on a career in manufacturing.) The efforts have been noble, but it’s an uphill battle.

A recent survey by the manufacturing industry association SME bears this out. If companies want young people to consider a manufacturing career as a possibility, they need to work on the parents.

The nationwide survey of parents revealed two noteworthy findings:

1. More than a fifth of those surveyed still view manufacturing as "outdated" or being associated with a "dirty work environment."

2. Nearly 25 percent of those surveyed do not think manufacturing is a well-paying profession.

Frankly, those numbers actually seem pretty promising, but they still hold room for improvement. But how much improvement can be expected?

Have you ever been in a fabrication shop where a lot of grinding or waterjet cutting is taking place? There’s plenty of dust, and industrial vacuums running all day long won’t pick it all up. Now a modern fab shop with a dusty floor is probably not the same thing as a 1930s manufacturing plant, but it might be construed as dirty nonetheless. Simply put, not all manufacturing environments can be compared to a high-end machine shop where parts are made for the medical industry. Sometimes you have to get your hands dirty.

A job in manufacturing may not deliver a guaranteed ticket to a comfortable middle-class lifestyle anymore, but not many jobs in today’s economy do. Manufacturing, however, can deliver an opportunity to attain what may become a challenging, exciting, and perhaps prosperous career.

Also, not all jobs in manufacturing are well-paying. SME notes that the average U.S. manufacturing worker makes $77,506, but that is an average of a very wide expanse of skill sets, from laborers to engineers. Researchers at the University of California at Berkeley released a study in May that found federal and state government spent about $10.2 billion from 2009 to 2013 on social programs, such as food stamps and subsidized health care, for families headed by individuals that worked factory jobs. Those families needed public assistance to get by.

These stubborn realities don’t disqualify manufacturing from being a great career choice. Parents and young people alike just need to shift their expectations. Manufacturing offers people a freedom that plenty of other career paths don’t:

• People without college educations run some of the most successful fabrication operations in the U.S. They went to the school of hard knocks, whether in the armed forces, in a garage, on a farm, or in some other environment where hands-on experience was stressed. They learned how to run a business and how to work with people as they continued in their careers. It was on-the-job training that meant something: These individuals often had to live with their correct and incorrect decisions. It’s an education that’s hard to replicate in a four-year college curriculum or a short-term MBA course.

• People without a manufacturing background can find a home in today’s manufacturing world. There’s the accountant who wanted to own a business and knew that a well-run fabrication shop could be a money-maker. There’s the English major who couldn’t find a teaching job and now runs a quality department. There’s the architecture major who ran into the Great Recession and now has mastered 3-D CAD. If people can commit to the job and show some initiative, they can find a nice career path.

• A manufacturer rarely has to worry about monotony. Fabricators in particular know this to be true. They are working on different parts and are called upon to solve production puzzles every day. Shop floor technicians and engineers get to work with the latest manufacturing technologies, like lasers, servo-electric equipment, and additive manufacturing.

A job in manufacturing may not deliver a guaranteed ticket to a comfortable middle-class lifestyle anymore, but not many jobs in today’s economy do. Manufacturing, however, can deliver an opportunity to attain what may become a challenging, exciting, and perhaps prosperous career. That’s the reality that needs to be embraced by the manufacturing community and those that they are trying to reach.
