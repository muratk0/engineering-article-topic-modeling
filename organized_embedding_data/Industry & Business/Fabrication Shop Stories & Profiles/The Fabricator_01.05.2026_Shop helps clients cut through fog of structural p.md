or David Letterman fans, the Top 10 Lists from his late-night show’s fictitious home office in Wahoo, Neb., were a nightly must.

Alas, those legendary lists—and the downtown sign paying tribute to Letterman’s greatness—have ceased to be. And while there are no stupid pet tricks or Alka Seltzer suits to be seen in Wahoo, this town of 5,040 people just west of Omaha still boasts at least one organization willing to do something daring and different: Rebellion Fabrication.

In fact, that’s all the owners of the 7-year-old fab shop want to do.

JUMPING OFF THE CLIFF

It all started during lunch at a Chinese restaurant a few years ago, when what became Rebellion was just a garage shop run by co-owner Ross Gredys, before he and his friend and future business partner, Matt Cade, had any clue what was coming. But let’s back up.

Gredys had resigned as a project manager for a large structural fabricator in the area in 2017 when he decided make the shop he had started three years prior his full-time gig. He worked that business for a few months and then, with the backing of an investor, offered to buy a bigger fab shop in Wahoo that specialized in miscellaneous metals fabrication—stairways and railings and other similar pieces.

The owner turned him down flat.

Rewind a bit more: News that the owner was looking to sell had traveled all the way to Gredys’ former employer, which had offered to buy the shop before Gredys even knew it was available. That deal fell through, but Gredys’ old bosses did him a solid and told him that the shop was indeed for sale.

It was still early in 2018 when Gredys called the owner back. The man confirmed it—his wife had retired, and if he couldn’t find a buyer he liked by the fall, he was calling it quits and selling off the assets. Gredys made a note to call him in May.

But, perhaps reading Gredys’ mind, the man called him in late April. The rest is Chinese restaurant history.

"I thought I was going to have to negotiate with him because we were pretty far off the first time," Gredys, 41, said with a grin. "His first question was, ‘Does your offer still stand?’ And I said, ‘Yeah.’ And I wrote him a check, as a deposit, for money I didn’t have in my bank account. And then I had to sit there and eat lunch with him for an hour. But it was just one of those situations where I was like, ’Yeah, this is what I want to do.’"

But then a twist right after lunch: Gredys called his financial partner, who did a 180 and backed out—leaving Gredys with a handshake deal to buy a fab shop for which he now had no money.

In a mild panic, Gredys called his friend Cade, an insurance and financial services professional who had no fabrication experience but did have a long list of clients in the manufacturing sector. Cade agreed and came through with the funds—at what both partners acknowledged was a fire-sale price.

"We figured … worst case scenario, he wanted to get rid of it so bad that if it didn’t work out, we could probably sell everything and break even," Gredys said.

But the economy was strong. There was more work than fabricators to do it. The company had backlog to clear out. And Gredys had all those prior relationships to tap.

No need for contingencies, though. From the day Rebellion opened its doors in April 2019, things moved fast. The business started with 11 employees. By October, it had 32. What Cade thought was going to be a short-term silent partnership quickly turned into his full-time job once he caught the spirit of what was going on and what kind of partner he had in Gredys.

"He’s going to jump off the cliff, and he’s going to figure out how to build a plane," Cade, 42, said of his partner. "And he’s going to trust the people around him to build it."

PLAYING THE HERO

That’s where Gredys’ love for the impossible proved valuable. That passion was nourished while he worked as a project manager (PM) with his former employer, where he spent years listening to clients’ likes and gripes and formulating ways he could address both.

Communicating.

In those conversations, he identified a common thread that ended up being the mainstay of how the company approaches all of its projects: communication.

"When I was on the PM side, I hated sitting behind my computer," said Gredys, whose mischievous grin and full-sleeve tattoos bear witness to his imaginative mind. "I was very proactive in the sense that I spent a lot of time on the jobsites talking to the general contractors, talking to the directors, what do they like, what they don’t like. You would always hear more about the things that they didn’t like. I [thought], ‘We could solve this if we just started having conversations sooner.’"

When COVID-19 hit in March 2020, the shop kept its doors open by working more directly with general contractors early in projects, filling what Gredys had come to see as a gaping hole in how large-scale projects got done.

Embracing Ambiguity.

For example, as a project manager, he saw that designers might provide 80% to 90% of the structural drawings to a fabricator, but the architectural information many times isn’t as clear cut as it once was. For miscellaneous metals projects (of which Rebellion still does many), project leads were telling fabricators, "Here are the concepts, shapes, sizes, specifications, everything. You figure out how to build it."

At that point, two kinds of shops emerge: one that wants a designer to tell it what to do so it can do it, and the other that revels in the latitude that ambiguity provides. Thus, the seed of what later sprouted as Rebellion’s M.O. was sown.

"All of a sudden, I saw freedom," Gredys said. "I’m like, ‘We can fine-tune this to the way we do stuff. The architect doesn’t really care about that crap. It’s just not getting incorporated into the set of drawings to be built a specific way out in the shop.

"That was a big motivation, because this shop was previously [focused on] miscellaneous metals, and that’s where I had seen most of the complaints. It was in the coordination—not enough of it on the front end of projects. I feel, as a fabricator, I can help a design team. I’m not going to try to be an architect or an engineer, but I’m really good at putting stuff together."

HARD PROJECT SOLIDIFIES DIRECTION

Then, about three years ago, Rebellion got a chance to put that mindset to the ultimate test with the Adventure Path Playground at Lewis & Clark Landing in downtown Omaha. The project was so mired in delays that work on its complex design hadn’t progressed in more than a year when Rebellion caught wind of it. And it wasn’t just one thing bogging it down but several—foremost being that the original fabricator was in over its head and struggling to find "up."

The playground is a series of massive carbon steel hollow structural section components. The main columns (too tall to stand up in Rebellion’s shop) and the main side rails that form the walking path are 85/8-in.-OD tubes with 3/8-in. walls. The components also include 5/16-in.-wall helix tubing with a 3.5-in. OD and handrails made of 1.5-in.-OD tubing. And there’s a lot of everything: The final product—176 ft. long and up to 25 ft. tall—was fully assembled at Rebellion, filling 12,000 sq. ft. of Rebellion’s floor space for six months before it was disassembled and shipped in pieces down the highway to Omaha.

The last in a three-part series of downtown projects, the playground was being overseen by construction and engineering megafirm Kiewit. It was scheduled to open in a year, but the former fabricator was reporting that it would take it a year and a half to finish what hadn’t even found its way into real drawings.

At that point, Kiewit was more than a little desperate for a shop competent enough to do the work and crazy enough to tackle it on such short notice. In this case, that meant contracting to build something that no one was truly sure could be—a process that began with Gredys meeting with Kiewit for about four months to hash out every last one of the details that had bedeviled Rebellion’s predecessor.

Within Rebellion, the job required reverse-engineering what only existed in concept into CAD drawings of all those giant components. To be honest, Rebellion ran before it could walk—the essence of a ready-fire-aim approach that the team can laugh about today because it turned out so well.

"Aim? Aim? It was just fire," Cade joked at Rebellion’s facility recently about taking the job. "There was no ‘what if?’ scenario. It was, ‘This is going. How do we actually make it happen?’"

HOPPING ON THE TRAIN EARLY

The Rebellion team made it work so well, in fact, that not only did it complete the playground on time, but it also took so many lessons from the process that the job became the template for how the shop does business.

It all has to do with Rebellion as the fabricator being the sounding board for everyone at the party—architects, designers, carpenters, electricians, and whoever else has skin in the game—to determine how the project literally will come together, all the way down (with the playground, at least) to what kind of fasteners are being used to screw in decking.

"It was like proof of concept—we can do this," Gredys said of the playground. "[It was a] confidence boost, not only for us, but for everyone involved, actually seeing this thing go together. We were able to incorporate and coordinate with all these other trades and bring them to the place that we were building it. All of a sudden, we were not just fabricators; we were kind of like construction coordinators of this specialty element.

"Not only the relationships that we had with the GC, but some of our best design relationships now have come from that. There was this tailwind that just started pulling us into all of these other specialty projects because they got a different level of service from us, not just from the quality of the fabrication, but like, ‘What are you trying to accomplish with your project?’"

THE ROAD AHEAD

In doing that, Rebellion is now going to its customers with services that stretch far beyond mere fabrication and welding. The shop’s value proposition is to be what might be called a value-added problem-solver.

"Our goal is to take care of a headache that they didn’t realize they had," said Greg Moreland, 44, Rebellion’s director of strategic growth. "Sometimes it feels like these initial engagements are like going to someone—it could be a design team that came up with a really cool concept—and calling their baby ugly right out of the gate. In reality, it’s more like acknowledging that the baby’s incomplete.

"Our job in this stage … is to say, A, can it be built, and B, can it be erected? And we can bring our knowledge from the fabricator perspective to that conversation."

One person who knows just what kind of growth spurt Rebellion’s value proposition has undergone is Ryan Thompson, who started at Rebellion six years ago as a welder and is now the shop’s director of operations. One of Thompson’s favorite tools these days is the Microsoft HoloLens augmented reality headset that he and other employees use with FabStation software to visualize project components virtually and then measure the accuracy of the real things to within 1/16 in. against the part drawings that have been uploaded into the system. Recently, the shop used its AR capabilities and the software on a complicated louvered-roof project that was composed of 37 individual frames made of rectangular tubing that were all bolted on with no welding. The units were galvanized and had a high-performance coating, which meant no slipups allowed. Using the software and AR capabilities cut two weeks of erection time because of the accuracy the shop could maintain with its new tools.

"You get one shot for those," Thompson, 30, said of the galvanized frames. "Every single one just went together flawlessly. Every single frame we built, we verified with that [software]."

As further evidence of the software’s impact, Thompson referred to a stairway project that incorporated a curved 14-in.-OD pipe—a $60,000 piece of material. Again, the raw material cost alone made it a do-or-die cutting and placement task that the software helped the team pull off to perfection.

"If we just set the thing the hard way, where you take dimensions and roll them and find out where you need to go, it would take a day or two," Thompson said. "Set it in an hour. Since so much of what we do is out of the model space, to be able to put that [headset] on and just verify and check, it’s been a game changer."

Gredys said now that Rebellion has the experience it does, he and his team aren’t afraid to take on work that other shops might shy away from.

"It’s not the lowest-hanging fruit in the sense of the general industry," Gredys said. "Because of our systems and our processes, it might take some more coordination, but … it fits how we’ve built our shop. It’s not like everything we do is the cool, crazy stuff, but it’s a value that we bring as an organization.

"We’re not just looking at a set of drawings and building what’s on the drawings. We’re trying to understand who’s involved, who the players are. There’s a lot of equipment involved, so there’s a lot of coordination and things like that, but it allows us to sell our services and our value in a specific way."
