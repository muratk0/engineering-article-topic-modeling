Tenere’s John Wikre is happy how part flow has progressed, but he plans for it to be refined even more. After all, the company invested in the technology to make it happen.

Tenere’s punching and laser cutting handle the organization’s prototype and low-volume production. The manufacturer, which has a large plastics division in Colorado and additional sheet metal fabrication in Monterrey, Mexico, covers the gamut of large-scale sheet metal manufacturing, from prototyping for new product introductions to full production runs.

The organization’s prototype and low-volume sheet metal work for core customers is key to its growth. The faster and better it can turn those jobs, the better those core customer relationships become. After all, a portion of those prototypes turn into full production jobs—and the cycle of growth continues.

In the 1990s and early 2000s the company mainly used standalone lasers and punch presses. Part profiles weren’t as complex, runs were longer, and the company found it had greater throughput using separate punching and laser cutting machines.

Over the past few years the part mix changed. Fewer jobs are welded, more are riveted or otherwise mechanically assembled. This has meant more countersinks, extruded taps, and other forms, which Tenere now performs on its punch/laser combo. Many parts also have odd shapes that, if processed exclusively on a punch, would require special tooling. With the combo, of course, the laser can cut those shapes efficiently. The punch/laser combo also has an extensive tool storage rack, which increases flexibility and utilization even further.

Wikre and his team are now re-evaluating their nesting practices. Tenere’s nests consist of groups of parts from the same part family. He called this "family nesting." It’s a step beyond batch nesting, in which each sheet is full of identical parts, but it’s not quite dynamic nesting, in which each sheet has different parts from different jobs.

Wikre is happy with the current material utilization. But he knows that the modern cutting machines Tenere has can work even closer to actual demand.

"True dynamic nesting will happen when we’re programming almost as it’s going into the machine, within an hour of receiving the orders. Every sheet could be different. We’re not there yet. But we will be."

The company’s part mix offers such potential. The cutting machines etch part labels as necessary, so sorting a true dynamic nest shouldn’t be a problem. And besides, the company has part-picking automation. Some parts go down a chute, but others are picked and neatly stacked, ready for the operator to sort and transport them downstream.

From there it’s right to bending, with virtually no waiting. The company doesn’t manage mountains of work-in-process—arduous in any situation, but extremely challenging when dynamic nesting, considering the WIP can come from dozens of different jobs. As soon as a kit of parts is complete, it is carried a few yards away to the brakes, including multiple automatic-tool-change systems. Before all the pieces arrive, the operator scans a barcode from the job jacket, and the brake starts to set itself up. Forming commences immediately thereafter.

This kind of flow is already happening, even with Tenere’s family nesting approach, but Wikre said true dynamic nesting will push efficiency even further.

The company may need to change how it processes and releases orders on the front end. But with these and other procedural changes, Wikre’s vision is to be turning around jobs within hours after they’re released.
