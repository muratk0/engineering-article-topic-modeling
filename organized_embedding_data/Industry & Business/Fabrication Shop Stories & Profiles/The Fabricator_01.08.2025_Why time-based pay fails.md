ompanies are evaluated on measures like sales growth, on-time delivery, cost reductions, and profits. And they want to motivate their employees to drive performance. But there’s a problem. Companies want better performance, but their employees’ pay isn’t tied to or determined by that performance.

Most employees get paid for their time. The more hours they work, the more they’re paid. So, they focus on the time they’ve put in because this determines how much their paycheck will be. Even salaried employees are expected to put in their time.

This creates a mismatch between what companies want from employees and the way employees are rewarded. Companies want one set of outcomes, but they reward employees for, and measure, something different. And because employees get paid for their time, they have no motivation, self-interest, or accountability to drive the results managers want.

Gainsharing aims to correct the mismatch. The concept is simple: When employees help the company earn more, they themselves earn more.

Rising Costs, Rising Challenges

Paying for time becomes even more detrimental as input costs rise. When input costs increase, companies need to get more output per dollar spent. They need increased production, reduced waste, and improved performance—all of which are exactly the kinds of outcomes (sales, speed, cost savings) that time-based pay does not incentivize.

Overtime makes this misalignment even worse. With overtime, employees effectively get a 50% pay increase. They’re paid for time, not results, and have little reason to work faster or smarter.

Gabe Widmer, director of operations at Dayton-Phoenix Group, a West Lafayette, Ind., manufacturer that makes products for locomotives and heavy industries, recalled how workers used to respond when management demanded more efficiency: "Are we going to get a raise? If we do better, what are we going to get out of it?"

The absence of a clear answer fostered resistance. "We redesigned the line that should have resulted in a 30% improvement in productivity," Widmer said, "but we were seeing 15%. People were asking, ‘Why should we make 20 more a day if we don’t get anything out of that?’"

Productivity was tied directly to the clock. "If they had 20 minutes left in a shift, they wouldn’t start a new job," Widmer explained. "There was no reason for them to do any more." With nothing to gain, time itself became the reward.

With gainsharing, that dynamic flipped. Now, employees at the company don’t just accept improvements; they drive them. "They see [20 minutes left] as time for another half of an assembly they could finish," he said. "That goes toward our greater good—our gainsharing dollars."

This alignment of objectives and rewards is especially important when a company needs to offset rising costs. Mounting cost pressures make it even more important to hold employees accountable for delivering results. Yet when pay remains tied solely to time, not results, holding employees accountable remains difficult. Employees simply clock in and out, meet the letter of their jobs, but fail to do what it takes to achieve the needed results.

How Gainsharing Builds Alignment

Gainsharing isn’t profit-sharing. Profit-sharing pays out if the company beats certain profit goals, yet it doesn’t tell people what exactly they need to do to make those profits happen.

Gainsharing, on the other hand, tells employees what they need to do to earn their bonus throughout the year and gives feedback, so they can adjust in real time. Gainsharing follows the 80/20 rule, which says that 20% of effort drives 80% of the results. The rule helps identify the short list of factors that really drive profits. This way, everything that’s done to drive gainsharing results drives the company’s bottom line. Everyone is working toward the same targets. The better the business does, the better employees do. This converts the workforce from passive participants into "co-owners of the outcomes."

Companies might start gainsharing programs by offering a bonus of 3% to 5% of profits, then grow the percentage from there. The greater the gains, the more profit a company can afford to contribute. When gainsharing goals are achieved, bonuses are paid, so people get rewarded for driving measurable results on the most important performance issues that influence business success.

What Does "Good" Look Like?

Before anything else, a company needs to define what "good" performance looks like over a certain period, such as a week or a month. What sales or production do we need to achieve? What cost goals (labor, material, etc.) must be beaten? What delivery dates need to be hit? This "big-picture" plan for the week or month also has the company beating its profit goal. Achieving this plan becomes the centerpiece, the goal everyone works toward. Everyone wins financially (employees and the company) when the goal is beaten.

To adjust effort, tactics, and strategies in real time, employees need feedback on goal performance. They must know not only how well they and their department are performing but also how the big-picture performance plan is coming together. This feedback shifts the focus from

effort to achieved performance

. In the end, it’s not about how hard everyone works or how much time they put in—it’s about whether everyone achieves the business goals.

Boots-on-the-Ground Connection

Employees need that through-line connection from their work to the overall result. This is critical to motivation, but it’s also something many companies struggle with. Companies may know what jobs need to ship this week, but they struggle to specify what a given department needs to do by when, and what an individual worker needs to do on a given day.

When workers see the through-line connection between their work and the overall gainsharing result, they see themselves as part of something bigger. They’re no longer just a working individual. They’re part of a group effort, a team. They belong, and they’re important.

The Fixes: Incremental Improvement

It’s a cause-and-effect world. So, if there is a plan and it isn’t achieved, there’s a reason why. And if changes aren’t made, the problem will occur again.

The lasting, incremental gains from gainsharing largely come from these "fixes" that are put in place. It’s like sanding off a bump on a rough surface. When you remove the bump, you don’t hit that bump again in the future. It becomes easier to plan the work and work the plan. This is where the lasting gains come from. Employees who spot problems and put fixes in place become heroes. They’re making it easier for everyone to achieve their gainsharing bonuses.

A shift occurs where employees see company processes as a system and, ultimately, get actively involved in fine-tuning that system. Everyone is on the same team, pulling in the same direction.

Unlocking Capacity to Seize Opportunity

As global supply chains continue to shift (due to reshoring, geopolitical changes, or emerging markets), new customer needs and market gaps are appearing. But only companies with capacity available can jump in and secure new contracts, customers, or partnerships. Without open capacity, you’re stuck watching those opportunities go to competitors.

When employees are motivated and paid bonuses to improve throughput, quality, or costs (via gainsharing), they actively help unlock hidden capacity within existing systems. It’s in employees’ self-interest to do so. This freed-up capacity then can be used to pursue new business without major new investments. You’re giving employees skin in the game to make the system more productive, not just busier.

Opportunities to fill gaps left by disrupted suppliers can come up suddenly. Resistance here is understandable because having to change existing plans and work setups can be annoying. Being flexible isn’t easy. But when you show employees that fitting in urgent orders or new, ultraprofitable work (to be done in the hours they’ll be working anyway) will increase the bonus they’ll get this month, it adds a different yet positive perspective. Having open capacity and positive, flexible employees makes your operation agile and far more competitive.

Consider recent changes at Dayton-Phoenix Group. As Widmer described, the company combined three different work centers into one "hybrid" work center that could produce three different product lines. The arrangement helped manage highly variable demand. "We’ve been able to do that because employees are open to doing whatever we need to do to get product out the door."

Once everyone is pulling toward the same goal and sharing the benefits of

succeeding together

, everything changes, and the momentum builds. But it starts with getting rewards aligned properly, so employees are focused on maximizing performance instead of the hours they put in. Gainsharing doesn’t eliminate the possibility of overtime, but it doesn’t incentivize it either.

Alignment Is Essential

Time-based pay is simple, common, and fundamentally flawed. It rewards time clocked into the job, not performance. As companies struggle to deal with rising input costs, labor shortages, and dynamic market opportunities, the cost of misaligned incentives becomes too great to ignore.

By tying employee compensation to actual outcomes, gainsharing converts workers from clock punchers into active problem-solvers. When incentives are aligned and focused on the most important issues, dramatic performance improvements occur. In the current, increasingly competitive business environment, such alignment isn’t optional—it’s essential.

A fabricator’s guide to gainsharing in 4 steps

Step 1: Are we busy enough?

If the shop doesn’t have enough work, it has no gains to share. Managers determine a certain amount of work that needs to be shipped over a certain period—like a week or a month—for employees to have a shot at that gainsharing bonus.

Step 2: What does my group need to do to achieve the bonus?

This usually includes a specific dollar amount that ships (or moves downstream) in a given period. From that, the group works together to increase throughput to meet or exceed the metric.

Step 3: What do I need to do to achieve the bonus?

With the group’s plan in place, important tasks on an individual level emerge. This can involve specific tasks or goals to achieve every day to keep a job on track.

Step 4: If we didn’t achieve the plan, why not?

Gainsharing itself doesn’t eliminate problems, but it does incentivize people to eliminate them. Again, they have skin in the game—a gainsharing bonus.
