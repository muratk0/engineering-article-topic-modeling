T

he largest cost center at a typical metal fabricator is raw material. You and your purchasing personnel continually manage the intricate mechanics of setting price, weight, and other transactional details like payment terms, all while considering the state of financial markets and mill operating capacity utilization rates.

So many hidden costs come from a simple misunderstanding of purchasing terminology. For metal fabricators purchasing sheet metal, plate, and tube, the confusion stems from three principal areas: first, how the weight of metal is calculated; second, the often-overlooked verbal nuances of sourcing from overseas; and third, misunderstandings about a key concept in the purchasing profession: economic order quantity.

1. Consider Actual Versus Theoretical Weight

How a steel’s weight is calculated differs based on how you as a buyer choose to transact business with a vendor. You can use terms such as

actual weight, theoretical minimum weight

, and

theoretical nominal weight

. Knowing what each means and how they apply to your business opens a pathway toward significant savings— and that’s before you ever delve into the actual per-pound commodity price discussions.

Say you’re buying 7-ga. hot-rolled material from a supplier that offers you a theoretical nominal weight of 0.01983 in., yet in reality the thickness ranges from 0.1713 up to 0.1872 in. The differences between the minimum and nominal thickness can represent a significant price difference.

The lessons extend far beyond the world of metals transactional standards. Many of the things you buy and sell are large, bulky, and freight-intensive. Selecting the appropriate transactional standard often means the difference between profitability and breaking even on a job.

2. Know the True Cost of Sourcing Overseas

Through my years in consulting, I’ve witnessed numerous buyers lured by low-cost products from overseas. Certainly, such items have a place when logistics, exchange rates, and total landed cost analyses all validate the decision. However, understanding prevailing economic cycles and their intrinsic risks might alter your decisions in seeking offshore supply.

These goods often hold a unique set of intrinsic financial risks. The most prevalent is shipment delay, which can add costs via fuel charges for extended travel or demurrage charges for items stuck in port during extended outages like strikes and pandemic work stoppages.

The years since 2020 bore witness to numerous black swan events such as chip shortages and low water levels in the Panama Canal limiting the number of daily crossings, all while a ship stuck across the main channel of the Suez blocked traffic for multiple weeks. Today, numerous warring factions are driving alternative routes into the mainstream as vessels incur additional costs associated with the route around Africa’s Cape of Good Hope.

As we delve further into 2024, we see the potential for strike actions along the U.S. East and Gulf Coast ports as of Oct. 1, which could create more delays (and potential demurrage charges) into existing offshore supply chains. We can always hope that those employing overseas supply already built sufficient safety stocks to cover any unanticipated delay.

Still, such actions eat up available liquid cash reserves rapidly. Calculations examining buy quantities must account for ongoing consumption rates while adding for delays and the extended lead times associated with ocean crossing— even in a calm geopolitical environment.

What’s more, election years also represent a potential source of upheaval for global supply chains. An incoming administration might hold different views on dumping and tariff assignments than the outgoing administration. Ordering overseas prior to an election means ensuring your supply chain is poised to absorb any unforeseen fees and taxes not presently on the books.

Once you prepare for the cost of inbound goods, you then need to select the best terms of shipment. Shipping terms define who’s responsible for the transportation costs and where the transfer of title for the goods will occur. The "transfer of title" piece is important with respect to items damaged in transit.

Precise definitions matter here, and for international transactions, those definitions are governed by Incoterms, or International Commercial Terms. Incoterms comprise a set of 11 rules from the International Chamber of Commerce that outline roles of buyers and sellers in the transfer of goods on international markets. A small change can mean so much. A difference of three little letters defines who owns inventory, who holds risks, who pays tariffs and duties, and where goods will be delivered.

EXW:

"Ex Works" is employed where a seller offers goods for sale from a specific location and the buyer is responsible for transit costs while assuming risk of loss liability during transport.

FCA:

"Free Carrier" is where a seller pays shipment to a given delivery point selected by a buyer. The destination may be a terminal, a port, or a place of business. The title transfers to a buyer at the named point, and the buyer is responsible to offload the goods.

CPT:

"Carriage Paid To" places more risk on buyers. However, the seller clears goods for export. The title to the goods transfers when they arrive at the agreed-upon carrier for shipment. The selected carrier (or carriers) brings the goods to a buyer’s destination at the buyer’s risk.

CIP:

"Carriage and Insurance Paid" is used when a seller pays freight and insurance (up to 110% of value) to a buyer’s representative at an agreed-upon point. Goods are not covered to their final destination. However, they do get to a buyer’s country to arrange final transit.

DAP:

"Delivered at Place" is used when a seller agrees to pay all costs of transit and cover losses until goods reach a specific, defined location. However, buyers are responsible for covering duties, clearances, and taxes associated with the import.

DPU:

"Delivered at Place Unloaded" (formerly known as DAT: Delivered at Terminal) describes a transaction where the seller is responsible for all fees up until the unload point. In this instance, a seller is covering transit, risk, and import fees.

DDP:

"Delivered Duty Paid" holds most of the risks for a seller associated with transport, taxes/tariffs, fees, and clearances until the goods reach an agreed destination port.

FAS:

"Free Alongside Ship" places substantial transactional burden on a buyer. Goods are placed alongside a vessel in port with proper customs clearances arranged. However, buyers take possession and hold all risks once the goods are loaded onto a vessel for transit to the destination. This often requires the purchase to be covered by insurance.

FOB:

"Free on Board" is used in both domestic and internal markets. However, these terms are typically reserved for maritime travel. You may see "FOB Origin" (point of shipment) or "FOB Destination" (your location) defining where risks pass to the buyer. These terms define which party is financially responsible for damages or product replacement. They can be particularly consequential as they can directly affect your company’s inventory valuations and losses.

CFR:

"Cost and Freight" is used where a seller arranges water transportation to an agreed destination only. Buyers carry all risk for damage and loss while goods are in transit, and the buyer must secure insurance coverage for the goods.

CIF:

"Cost, Insurance, and Freight" features a seller paying to get goods cleared to be exported, loaded, and shipped to a buyer’s port of destination. Once loaded, however, a buyer must cover insurance costs and the cost of movement to the final destination.

3. Know Where EOQ Falls Short

Cost-of-cash calculations are essential. They help determine appropriate inventory levels and how long is too long to hold such inventory. Each company deals with slightly different bank rates and thresholds for performing these calculations, so you need to check with your accounting department about what numbers to plug into an equation.

Time on the shelf for goods in a plant equates to increased costs for those goods. This has to do with the time value of money. The longer you hold something, the more expensive it becomes, regardless of its unrealized market value.

Here’s where economic order quantity (EOQ) comes into play. The EOQ formula determines the optimum inventory level for a given item. You calculate it by taking the square root of the following: [2 ×

(Annual Demand × Ordering Cost)]/Holding Costs

.

The EOQ formula falls short, however, because it assumes that demand volume as well as holding and ordering costs remain fixed, which (of course) often isn’t the case. You might need to recalculate EOQ numbers quarterly or more frequently to keep pace with changes in the market due to inflation or other stimuli. Also, be sure to balance EOQ with how suppliers conduct business, including their minimum order quantity, or MOQ.

Both buyers and suppliers need to realize a profit for business to work and continue. Unfortunately, this means many of us may be subjected to MOQ. When examining these quantities, consider your cost of cash and how it relates to your EOQ. Remember your negotiation skills here since simple changes to payment terms, quantity, or price may afford you the opportunity to make a deal bear fruit for all parties involved.

A supplier should be your partner, not an adversary. Ask questions. Play through scenarios and see if your supplier’s quantity requirements are set in stone.

Beyond Price

Your purchasing strategy goes far beyond price-perpound negotiations. You need to select the right quantity of goods to purchase, the minimum quantity available to purchase at a given price, where to purchase (global versus local market)—and know what the real cash flow impact will be.

Hidden costs abound in metal fabrication, and many of them come from purchasing decisions that don’t consider the big picture: the agreed-upon terms, the required order quantities, and all the complexities that arise when sourcing internationally. But opportunities for savings abound as well. Consider the freight terms, inventory levels, banking, and supplier order quantities. As you dig into details, you could find that lower prices are available at any time during any economic cycle—with the right purchasing agreements.
