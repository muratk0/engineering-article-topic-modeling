# Well begun is half done

Expertise » Continuous Improvement

Design principles build guardrails for the road ahead

By

Jeff Sipes

Y

ou are considering some big changes to your operations. Could be a new line layout or overhaul of the flow. Might be the need to challenge how all your resources are used in anticipation of a new contract that could double your business. Or perhaps you are looking at a new facility to expand the business.

You could jump right in and begin to arrange the details. Maybe you presuppose a solution as a result of what you saw at another company. Or you might have a "just do it" attitude and forge ahead.

As appealing as that might be, particularly if you’re an action-oriented person, you could be making a big mistake. Before diving in to make big operational changes, consider the use of design principles.

What Is a Design Principle?

A design principle provides guidance and stability to what you have defined to be important in the design process. If you’re developing a new layout for a product line or a plant, a design principle is a high-level statement that identifies a characteristic, outcome, or description to enable and sustain alignment among stakeholders.

Think of a design principle as a high-level "what" rather than a "how." Since it is more conceptual than tactical, you might think the design principle is too fuzzy. Not to worry at this stage of your project, because the conceptual ideas will frame in the detailed work you will do later.

Before starting any project that would bring about significant change, you should create a set of balanced, holistic design principles. Eight is a good number to aim for, give or take. If you have too few, they won’t be sufficiently holistic or thorough. If you have too many, you can lose focus and find it difficult to discern what is really important.

A good design principle might be, "We will run to a rhythm or takt time." At face value it’s a simple statement. But dig deeper and you’ll see the meat and potatoes in this design principle. Are you running in a rhythm today? Or do you think you are running in a rhythm today, but upon honest and objective assessment, you conclude you are not? Are you running in a rhythm, but only a few people really understand how and why? If the answer to any of these questions is yes, then this design principle is a big deal.

How Do Design Principles Help?

Design principles help align everyone—leadership, designers, and the people who will work in the new process—around a few important ideas. This alignment is critical because its absence results in confusion and the wasting of scarce resources. Consider again the design principle, "We will run to a rhythm or

takt

time." How do design principles align the different people at your company?

Leaders

. Intentionally or unintentionally, leaders influence the design process. If they do not understand or see the value of running to a rhythm, then their actions may simply encourage the status quo or even work against the lean ideas that enable rhythm. If they poo-poo the design principle, then you can believe that nearly everyone deeper in the organization will follow their lead. In contrast, if leaders rally around this design principle, you can bet the odds for success are far greater.

Process Designers

. These people might come from different areas and represent multiple levels in the organization: perhaps an engineer, a production manager, someone from supply chain management, a quality technician, and a couple of hourly employees from production. These people are tasked with the process layout. Just imagine their performance if they are completely aligned around the set of design principles. The well-performing group will be more productive, mature, objective, and enlightened in their thinking. Without alignment, they might lack common understandings and focus only on their own function’s success.

Purpose of Design Principles

Provide a higher-level understanding of concepts that help define the details of layout and execution.

Get everyone onboard so that company resources do not work at cross purposes.

Create alignment among all personnel.

Those Who Work in the Process

. Finally, what about the people who will work in this process once it is implemented? This could include welders, machinists, production supervisors, material handlers, as well as procurement professionals. They might not be directly involved in designing the new process, but they can still be aligned around the set of design principles. The design principles can help them understand what is important and why it is necessary to challenge the status quo. They also help people to be patient and open to whatever the project outcomes are. The design principles become a significant part of the message about how the business is changing and improving.

Design Principles at Work

As you read the example that follows, put yourself in the shoes of one of the team members. Imagine how you would hold yourself and the rest of the project team accountable in a constructive, objective, nonemotional way.

Let’s say you have a project team to lay out and define flow for a new plant. Who creates the design principles? The effort will be led by the project team with input from leadership, management, and your lean

sensei

. Your company may have some design principles from previous initiatives that can serve as a starting point. You also check your company’s operating principles, as covered in previous columns. (Editor’s note: To access previous columns, visit the

fabricator.com/author/jeff-sipes

.)

You brainstorm, think creatively and aggressively, and lean on the lean body of knowledge. You take your time to thoroughly contemplate the design principles. And you ask other stakeholders to challenge what you developed.

You also present the design principles to a panel of employees to get feedback. This is important because the design principles will provide the foundation for the whole layout and flow effort. Plus, they will be the "guardrails on the highway" to keep you moving toward a desired destination.

A set of design principles might look like the following (this is for illustration purposes and not intended to be prescriptive for your situation):

Run with rhythm and at

takt

time

Process will continuously flow with no backtracking

Parts will be scheduled and delivered according to how they’re used

Information and parts will arrive just in time—not too early and not too late

No unsafe intersections and no crisscrossing

Effectively utilize unique resources (cranes, power/utilities, high-bay space, etc.)

Flexibility to handle surprises

Some projects are tactical with limited risk, while others are strategic and loaded with risk. High-energy, take-action manufacturing professionals have a bias toward jumping in and getting going, but there may be risks associated with executing without adequate planning.

As team members define and refine the principles, they develop a deeper understanding of what is important and how lean concepts drive toward a desired state—one that may stretch previous boundaries and enhance performance. As they share design principles with other stakeholders, they listen, learn, and refine the principles in a way that moves toward organizational consensus.

During the design of new layouts and processes (or whatever is in your project scope), the team uses the principles in midpoint checks to make sure they are being true to what is important. Finally, they expect company leaders to hold the team accountable for having fulfilled the spirit of the design principles.

Imagine the project team has defined a portion of the product flow. As they develop the flow and layout documents, they see that they have not provided the space and travel path for the material handlers to deliver parts. Although they have optimized space for the assemblers, the assemblers will constantly be disrupted because of either having too many or too few parts.

As team members check their design against the design principles, it becomes apparent they have violated at least one: "Parts will be scheduled and delivered according to how they’re used." They talk about accountability, then refine the process while keeping true to the design principles.

Good Execution Needs Framing and Planning

Projects come in all sizes and shapes. Some will be tactical with limited risk, while others will be strategic and loaded with risk. As high-energy, take-action manufacturing professionals, you have a bias toward jumping in and getting going, but there may be risks associated with executing without adequate planning.

The design principles address an important component of those upfront framing and planning needs. With all the benefits the design principles provide, you will be off to a great start and will rightfully expect to have a successful outcome. As a wise plant manager once said, "Well begun is half done."
