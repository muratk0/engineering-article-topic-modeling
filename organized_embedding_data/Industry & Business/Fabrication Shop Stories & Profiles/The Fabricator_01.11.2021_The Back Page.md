The Back Page

Netflix show brings metal artists (and FABTECH attendees) to the small screen

By

Eric Lundin

Read more from Eric Lundin at

www.thefabricator.com/author/eric-lundin

W

hether featuring motorcycles built from scratch, old cars turned into spiffy hot rods, or miscellaneous scrap metal used to build who-knows-what, cable television has been bringing metal fabrication into our living rooms in grand style for more than 20 years. "American Chopper," "Monster Garage," and "Junkyard Wars" had many loyal fans and even turned a few fabricators into television stars.

Shortly before FABTECH 2021 got underway, streaming service Netflix released a new metalworking series that got one step closer to the reading audience of

The FABRICATOR.

A contest sort of show that ran six episodes, "Metal Shop Masters" featured seven metal artists as contestants, two of whom attended the 2021 FABTECH expo in Chicago. One of the contestants, Rae Ripple, was featured on the cover of the March/April 2020 issue of The WELDER, a sister publication to

The FABRICATOR

. We asked Ripple a few questions about her experience as part of a television show cast.

Lights, Camera, Arc!

The FABRICATOR:

How did you find out about the show? How were the contestants selected?

One of the challenges was to make a sculpture with a very different shadow. Ripple sculpted a chaotic jumble, representing the era before creation; the shadow is an homage to Michelangelo’s most famous work, depicting the creation of Adam. Ripple was the only contestant to use the shadow to represent the background and light for the subject.

Hyperkinetic and chatty, Ripple shares a quiet moment with her teammate, Tom Patsis, as they await the judging of a grill they designed and built.

Ripple:

I got a casting email from Netflix. I had gotten casting emails before, but I always thought they were bogus and never replied to any of them. By about the sixth email from Netflix, I finally decided to respond. After that, I sent them some videos and we had a ton of Zoom calls.

FAB:

Despite the contestants having varied backgrounds and working styles, did a camaraderie develop?

Ripple:

On some reality shows, the environment is really catty and the contestants trash each other. Welders are blue-collar workers, and blue-collar workers stick together. We took this show as an opportunity to support each other and take care of each other.

FAB:

Just one of the six builds did not involve parts in motion. Do you see yourself incorporating moving parts in your work in the future?

Ripple:

I never dealt with parts in motion before the show, and this really sparked an interest in getting things to move. I’m super dyslexic, and even something like gear rotation is difficult for me, but the show got me to shift my thinking. I already have a lot of ideas about incorporating motion into my work.

FAB:

Your style relies on intuition, spontaneity, and some guesstimating. In Episode 3, you were teamed up with a meticulous and careful contestant, Thomas Patsis. Did you enjoy working with each other? Do you think you might dedicate more time to the planning, measuring, and marking aspects from having worked with Tom?

Ripple:

I think Tom was nervous at first, but I think he enjoyed working with me. I learned the value in using posterboard to create templates to get the sizing right, which I had never done before. Now I use templates on every sculpture that I do. I used to just start with an idea in my head and work my way through the project, but I always had to do some recutting. Using templates for everything saves a lot of time.

FAB:

What was the best part of being on the show?

Ripple:

Other than being around all of these cool, badass artists, I saw my work in a new light. I used to think that anyone could do this sort of thing and that my work wasn’t anything special. I definitely had gotten comfortable in my work, and I wasn’t growing as an artist. On the show, I really had to dig deep and really push myself. I didn’t think I’d make it past the first round, but I did, and went all the way to the semifinal. Most of us are our own worst critics, me included, and the show taught me to ignore little imperfections.

When the show came out, I binge-watched all of the episodes, and I thought, "‘Maybe I am a badass artist too.’" Artists, welders, everyone—we really shouldn’t be so hard on ourselves.
