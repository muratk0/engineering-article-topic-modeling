# When plasma makes the most sense

[TARİH: 01.02.2025 The Fabricator]

Plasma Cutting

Cutting tables give Michigan fabricator tools it needs to fly

By

Lincoln Brunner

The need for thousands of this metal part, with opposing bevels on each end, motivated Jorgensen Steel Machining & Fabrication to purchase three plasma cutting systems for the job.

Images: Jorgensen Steel Machining & Fabrication

N

ecessity is the mother of investment.

Jorgensen Steel Machining & Fabrication in Tekonsha, Mich., discovered that truth under the best of trying circumstances a couple of years ago when a new job popped up for a customer looking for tens of thousands of parts. The job would have buried the company in work and left no room to serve other customers with the equipment it had at the time.

The 23-year-old company serves a broad swath of customers in the automotive, aerospace, defense, and emergency response industries, among others. But this particular job involved cutting fence pickets—about 32,000 of them—from 3/8-in. mild steel plate for a large bridge being built in the area. The pickets had to be profiled and have opposing bevels cut on them to diminish wind resistance. Jorgensen had an older, low-power laser at the time, but cutting the pickets with that machine would have cost the company time it simply didn’t have under the job’s deadline.

"It was a daunting task," Jorgensen COO Daryl Blashfield said. "We were either going to have to laser-cut all these things and then run them through machining centers and machine all the bevels on them or find a way to cut the bevels on them."

Jorgensen opted for the latter, but Blashfield still wasn’t sure if he wanted a laser or a plasma cutting machine to do it. On the advice of a third-party salesperson for whom Blashfield is grateful, he ended up choosing the latter.

"He said, ‘You know, I’d like to sell you this laser, but this isn’t a laser job—this is a plasma job,’" Blashfield recalled. After one search for a suitable plasma table proved futile, Blashfield happened upon Cleveland-based AKS Cutting Systems, which offered him a plasma table that includes the company’s robo-kut 5-axis beveling system—just the thing he needed.

AKS had a machine in stock. Jorgensen jumped on it and was cutting pickets about a month later. Such was the job’s urgency, though, that Blashfield and his team grabbed the initial round of training from AKS and then ran ahead as fast as it could.

Risky? Maybe, but the new machine performed so well that within a year, Jorgensen bought two more plasma tables from AKS to keep up with the work.

"We did the training just for profile cutting, and then they wanted to come back a couple of weeks later and do the bevel cut training, and we didn’t have time for it," Blashfield said. "We were under the gun, and the project was ready to start.

"AKS bent over backwards," he added. "They couldn’t have a guy on-site, but they made a guy available by phone and were able to remote into our computers and help us with the programming. They actually talked us through all the bevel cutting over the phone, and by the time they actually showed up in person to do the bevel cutting, we were already successfully doing it."

Capabilities and Cost

In the shadow of permanent "Now Hiring" signs at shops nationwide, that quick learning curve might be the key value proposition for plasma systems these days for shops weighing the choice between plasma and laser cutting.

A plasma table capable of cutting a typical range of plate and sheet will likely cost less than $200,000, plus accessories. A laser with comparable thickness capabilities is going to run multiple times that amount, albeit with significant advantages in edge quality and, for thinner material, sheer speed.

However, for the ⅜-in. fence picket material, Jorgensen saw its profit margin more than double over the fiber laser it was going to use because of the speed and consistency that its new plasma tables have offered.

The ability to bevel-cut on the robo-kut 5-axis plasma table from AKS Cutting Systems enabled the shop to avoid a secondary machining operation on the fence pickets it cut for a nearby bridge project.

"It was noticeable—I mean, a huge difference," Blashfield said. "We quoted this job to laser-cut the parts and machine the bevels, so being able to plasma-cut them, have them coming off the machine ready to just deburr and do a little edge rolling, and then sell parts—that was a huge swing for us."

Chris Phillip, marketing manager at AKS, acknowledges the many advantages that lasers have over plasma but encourages potential customers to look at not only the cost differential but also the training and maintenance requirements for each.

"You can buy five lasers—wonderful," Phillip said. "You might even have the business for them. Do you have the people to operate them? I have never had a business owner that I’ve talked to that doesn’t say, ‘Tell me about it.’ That’s their biggest thing, finding people to do the work, which is why people lean into automation. Well, you still need people to fix it. You still need people to program it. That’s ultimately one of the biggest driving factors in that ROI conversation. Sure, the system can process 50,000 tons of steel a year. Can your people process that?

Is there a guy standing there who’s capable of keeping that machine up and running? "I can train you how to operate the plasma table in about two hours, and then I can train you how to maintain it and keep it going in less than three days."

Jorgensen, for its part, has been able to run three plasma tables with two operators—one skilled operator and one of its temporary employees, a pool from which the 40-person company has had to draw extensively during the bridge job.

"I would say the ease of use is much simpler than a laser," Blashfield said. "We added a lot of temp workers for this project because it was a start-and-finish kind of project. We didn’t just hire people to let them go later. They knew all along it was a temporary job, so we had fairly unskilled labor that was helping load and unload these machines, and even get to the point where they could help change out the consumables. So, I would say ease of use versus a laser or most types of machinery was pretty easy."

But really, if anything about the new plasma cutters impressed Blashfield, it was how they allowed the company to set it and forget it. In fact, the bevels the tables are cutting have stayed within 1 mm of tolerance, well within specifications.

That consistency is a product of a sensor that tracks the arc length of the plasma emitted by the torch and adjusts itself accordingly. That reality was a far cry from the performance of a plasma table that Jorgensen had previously used, which Blashfield tactfully described as "less than repeatable."

"I really felt like, moving into this, it was going to be a nightmare," Blashfield confided. "I thought, ‘We’re going to be upsetting this thing nonstop.’

"It took a few weeks to get up and running on this, and when we finally got it dialed in, I went about my merry way running things around here. Three or four months later, I went to the guys and I said, ‘How often are you having to adjust the bevel heights?’ My operator said, ‘We haven’t adjusted that since we got it dialed in.’ We just run sheet after sheet, and … anytime it starts to wander off at all, it’s usually because the consumables are starting to get bad. We change out the consumables, and the accuracy is right back up, and we go right back in."

Good Enough Is Great

And though that 1-mm tolerance might be anathema to a company fabricating parts for automotive or aerospace applications, it works well for shops like Jorgensen that are cutting high volumes of parts on thick material that don’t have tight tolerances. For those jobs, and for shops cutting unknown varieties and thicknesses on those kinds of parts, the ROI on plasma tables is worth considering, Phillip said.

"What does the work really require?" Phillip said. "When you’ve got a 1-in.-thick weldment base plate to hold up a telephone pole, it doesn’t need to be ±0.005 in. It can be ±⅛ in., well within the spec of a plasma table."

In addition, a company should look at its book of business, its business model, and the talent it has on hand to perform the work—and then make its capital expense decisions.

"If you’re a private equity company, look at laser; if you’re an owner-operator, look at plasma," Phillip said. "Those different business models have two completely different sales processes. A laser works really well for a lot of consistent work. If you are going to cut any thin metal and at a high volume and it’s consistent, then it’s laser 100%, all day. If you don’t know what you’re going to be cutting next week—it might be steel, it might be wood, it might be ¼ in., it might be 3 in.—that’s where plasma excels."

Not that Jorgensen has given up anything in terms of edge quality on the pickets, at least as far as its customer is concerned, according to Blashfield.

"They’ve been very happy," Blashfield said. "The only complaint we’ve had was basically on the workmanship that we had with some of our temp workers in the deburring process—they would get a little aggressive with things. It turned out that our customer really liked the look of the plasma-cut bevel edge. It’s a very smooth cut."
