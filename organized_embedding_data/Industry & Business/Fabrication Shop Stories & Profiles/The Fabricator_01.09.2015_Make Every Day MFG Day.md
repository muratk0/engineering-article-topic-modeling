I

t was a moment when work life collided with private life. I was sitting at a picnic table waiting for the second heat of the day for the America’s Cardboard Cup Regatta in Crystal Lake, Ill., on June 27. My son and some of his friends from the percussion section of the Crystal Lake Central High School band had constructed a Harry Potter-themed boat, which actually had a dragon gracing the front of the cardboard contraption that was supposed to stay afloat for the length of the 200-yard course.

Near the picnic table was another team who initially drew my attention because of their large batch of cookies on a nearby table. (I’m a poor picnic preparer. Water, stale tortilla chips, and lukewarm salsa don’t do much to foster a festive atmosphere.) As my eyes pulled away from the sweets for a brief moment, I noticed the team’s entry: a 250-lb. cardboard catamaran called Pressing Matters. A flag with the sponsoring company, Kenmode Precision Metal Stamping, hung from the boat’s center.

This Kenmode craft also happened to be destined for the heat in which my son’s team was participating. As everyone walked to the starting line, we could see the other team’s pressing matter: Could their ships stay afloat? But Kenmode’s Pressing Matters wasn’t going to have to worry about that.

In fact, the catamaran won the heat, and if not for a collision with the cardboard entry of the CLCHS percussion section in the first turn, it might have been fast enough to qualify for the finals, which was filled with much sleeker, skiff-type cardboard boats. (For more on the entry and some of the design and manufacturing effort behind it, see "Metal stamper takes to the lake" on p. 114). All in all, it was an impressive debut for the Algonquin, Ill.-based metal stamper.

In a follow-up interview several weeks later with the key participants responsible for constructing Pressing Matters, I asked them if the cardboard creation with the company name emblazoned on it prompted any discussion with nearby festival-goers about what exactly Kenmode does. While no indepth conversations of that kind really developed, regatta participants did exchange some tales about how they approached making their entries.

"This really opens up people’s eyes as to how things are made," said Tom Anderson, a longtime Kenmode tooling specialist.

He couldn’t be more correct. You only wish that others had the same chance I did to chat with these stamping and tool and die experts about their cardboard boat that probably could have weathered a trip down some river rapids. They smiled and laughed, recounting the multiple people looking over a person’s shoulder as a cut was made in the cardboard or the multiple coats needed to create the final finish.

These conversations don’t have to be relegated to the hallways of manufacturing facilities and, hopefully, in the pages of trade journals like

The FABRICATOR

. They can happen as part of everyday meetings. Metal fabricators and formers just need to put them in a position to let them happen.

For those interested in engaging in those types of conversations with people that may know little or nothing at all about manufacturing, mark Oct. 2 down on your calendars. That day is the fourth annual Manufacturing Day (MFG Day), a nationwide effort that aims to improve the perception of manufacturing in the eyes of average U.S. citizens.

In 2014 more than 400,000 people attended 1,679 registered events in the U.S. These included open houses at fabricating facilities, machine tool showrooms, and educational institutions. MFG Day sponsors, one of which is the Fabricators & Manufacturers Association, believe that participation could reach 2,000 events this year. Visit

www.mfgday.com

for more information

These events are where the hearts and minds of parents and young students can be won. They can learn what the life of a maker is—not a paper pusher or a computer jockey. They can learn how each day is filled with different challenges, not the same mundane activities that fill the desk-bound lives of most people. They can see metal fabricating as a possible career choice, not a dead-end job that helped to drive several generations away from the vocational trades.

For those interested in engaging in … conversations with people that may know little or nothing at all about manufacturing, mark Oct. 2 down on your calendars. That day is the fourth annual Manufacturing Day (MFG Day).

The community outreach efforts don’t have to end after one day in October. They can continue by simple involvement with local Chamber of Commerce events and career days at local schools. A metal fabricator also might consider building a cardboard boat.

Promoting metal fabricating doesn’t have to be an arduous task. Make it an enjoyable one, and let others know about the fulfillment that comes with the job.
