T

he Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA) is undergoing a great amount of change at the moment. The agency’s 2 024 fiscal year budget is $740 million, a $107 million increase over 2023. This boost will help cover the cost of 432 new full-time employees, which is desperately needed because many compliance officers are retiring. (In 2021, for the first time in OSHA’s history, there were fewer compliance officers throughout the agency than there were when the agency was created in 1973.)

These new compliance officers will have the benefit of having senior executive staffers around to help train and guide them. This is the first time in years that 100% of senior executive staff positions within OSHA have been filled. That’s in all regions and all directorates. Having said that, industry is still looking at having a lot of compliance officers in the field with little industry experience, which likely means longer inspections as these OSHA representatives learn on the job.

With the increase in compliance officers, inspections are trending higher, but not as high as one might think. Inspections obviously decreased during the pandemic (21,680 in 2020; 24,358 in 2021; and 31,280 in 2022), and they still have a ways to go before reaching levels in the 2010s (40,993 in 2010; 40,614 in 2011; and 40,961 in 2012).

Penalties keep rising as well. This is actually Congress’s fault. Back in 2016, Congress implemented a 78% increase in OSHA penalties because the agency had been stuck with a fine structure that hadn’t been changed since 1970. Simultaneously, Congress tied future penalty increases to the Consumer Price Index, which gauges inflation. With that in mind, fines for most offenses are now over $16,000.

The number of significant offenses is increasing too. (At one time, significant events were described as those that resulted in at least $100,000 in fines and additional penalty enforcement actions. Today, that figure’s been increased to $130,000, as a reference.) During the first two years of the current administration, industry has seen 538 significant penalties levied, with 343 of them coming in 2022. That’s more than the final three years of the previous administration, which issued 477 significant penalties from 2018 to 2020.

Stepping Up Enforcement

Any discussion of noteworthy OSHA action really needs to begin with the agency’s Severe Violator Enforcement Program (SVEP), which was started in 2010 to go after the worst companies when it comes to safety. Current OSHA leaders felt that existing SVEP-qualifying criteria might have been too soft, so they changed it. In addition to being targeted for SVEP when a willful or repeat citation results in a fatality or more than three hospitalizations, a manufacturer might be drafted into the program after two or more high-gravity willful or repeat citations (or failure to abate notices) in the same inspection alleging violations of any standard.

Once a part of SVEP, the subject is under the microscope. And it’s not just one location that is scrutinized for three years (or two years with enhanced settlement terms). Any of the facilities connected to the one plant in question also are watched closely. OSHA basically has the right to enter any of the company’s locations to see if it is doing what is necessary to address safety issues of concern.

The SVEP penalty period lasts much longer than it did in the past. It used to be three years from the initial inspection or citation. Today, it’s three years from the final decision on the order, which only comes after the time taken to draw up the original citation, which could take months, and the weeks involved in settlement discussions and the contesting of any penalty.

The stakes are big for those in SVEP. These offenders have to pay all the penalties associated with the original citations, which is no surprise, and they have to deal with all of the abatement of their establishments. If these companies receive another serious citation during this threeyear time frame, the clock begins to run again. For some companies, it could be a real struggle to exit the program.

Also available in the OSHA enforcement toolbox are instance-by-instance citations. For those who are old enough, they were once referred to as egregious cases.

Under the previous policy, the alleged violations were characterized as willful and included aggravating criteria, such as a fatality or catastrophe or persistently high injury rates. In January 2023, this policy was significantly expanded. Now any high-gravity, serious violations related to falls, machine guarding, respiratory protection, lockout/tagout, and "other-than-serious recordkeeping violations" could result in a company being issued this instance-by-instance citation. Also, this would apply to multiple machines and employees. For example, if a lockout/tagout violation is found, the citation could be issued for every one of the authorized employees that were deemed not having the proper training. A serious citation can become that much more serious given the scope in which OSHA wants to apply it.

Employer has received a willful, repeat, or failure-to-abate violation within the past five years.

Employer allegedly failed to report a reportable injury or illness.

Also in January 2023, OSHA issued an enforcement memorandum to discourage grouping of citations. The reminder directed offices not to group similar violations in cases where the goal was to achieve deterrent effect. For instance, if OSHA visited a manufacturing facility and found enough shortcomings related to hazardous communications, such as a failure to have safety data sheets for various chemicals or a general chemical inventory, that inspector could write the manufacturer up for every instance. However, if the OSHA inspector found that the manufacturer was operating with good intent, it could group all of the violations into one citation and include the violations as subitems. OSHA leadership deemed that area inspectors were using the grouping strategy too much.

National and Special Emphasis Programs

In recent years, Special Emphasis Programs have allowed OSHA to announce major initiatives even in the face of shrinking budgets. These programs can be national, regional, or local, and they’re designed to target either hazards that exist in a bunch of industries or certain industries that have a big and recurring safety problems.

In 2024, OSHA announced a new national program targeting the warehousing industry. That joins other national emphasis programs like the one OSHA has for amputations.

In reality, these types of emphasis programs are policies that are designed to allow OSHA to inspect certain employers more often because of the industry those employers are in or the hazards that are in their workplaces. But companies typically have to fall within certain North American Industry Classification System codes or have a certain number of employees to qualify.

In February 2023, OSHA reissued the Site-Specific Targeting (SST) program. OSHA originally adopted it back in the 1990s, but it ditched it after 20 years because it was deemed inefficient. Under the program, the agency used objective data, such as workers’ compensation information, to identify companies that had higher-than-normal averages of injuries or illnesses when compared to industry segment averages and put those companies on a list for possible inspection. Now, OSHA is going to try it again.

The big difference now is that manufacturers must submit OSHA 300 logs electronically, providing OSHA a convenient reference for its new SST program. Those companies with a DART (days away, restricted, or transferred) rate above the industry average are more likely to be candidates for inspections than their counterparts with lower rates.

Other national emphasis programs include combustible dust, falls, and warehouse and distribution centers, the latter of which has seen increased injury rates in the face of a warehousing boom following the major supply chain troubles during the pandemic. Old emphasis programs still in place have a focus on amputations, powered industrial trucks, lead, and silica.

Electronic Records Submission

Back in 2016, President Barack Obama’s OSHA adopted a standard that required all employers of a certain size and in certain industries to report their 300 log data electronically. However, when President Donald Trump assumed office, he decided that companies only need to submit 300A forms, the summary that has to be posted from February to April every year. Because it’s a summary of the data on the 300 log, it doesn’t give any detail, so the data is essentially useless.

As of January 2024, there are two submission requirements: The 300A forms for companies in the three categories (200 or more employees, 20 to 249 employees in industries listed in Appendix A, and 100 or more employees in industries linked in Appendix B) need to be submitted, and OSHA 300 logs and 301 forms for establishments with 100 or more employees (in industries listed in Appendix B) need to be sent in.

OSHA still has a national emphasis program focused on powered industrial truck safety. Even if the agency hasn’t announced any new initiatives around its older emphasis programs, OSHA can still use those focus areas as a reason to ask for an inspection.

What’s OSHA going to do with that data? It might use it to fuel the SST and similar emphasis programs. It more than likely is going to post the raw data on its website, which makes it open to interpretation. Union officials and plaintiff attorneys might find the raw data of particular interest as they try to push their agendas.

Overview of Rulemaking

In August 2023, OSHA issued a proposed rule that cleared the way for third-party participation during OSHA inspections. The proposed rule stated: "This rulemaking will clarify the right of workers and certified bargaining units to specify a worker or union representative to accompany an OSHA inspector during the inspection process/facility walkaround, regardless of whether the representative is an employee of the employer, if in the judgment of the [certified safety and health officer] such person is reasonably necessary to an effective and thorough physical inspection."

This effort to include third-party participation in inspections dates back to failed rulemaking during the Obama administration. The courts rejected that original premise because it was a pretty clear attempt by the administration to allow OSHA to invite in union and/or plaintiff-related third parties with or without expertise to participate in those walkarounds.

In 2021, President Joe Biden’s administration reintroduced that rule with some twists and turns. The final form of the rulemaking now defines the third parties as having "a variety of skills, knowledge, or experience that could aid in the CSHO’s inspection." According to OSHA, these third parties should bring some additional value to the inspection.

OSHA also continues to try and fashion a heat illness standard. Agency leaders had hoped to get a standard, for both indoor and outdoor environments, published before the end of the Biden administration, but that’s not likely to happen now.

Simply put, it’s hard to find a lowest common denominator that is applicable to all ages and body types. A 23-year-old working on a roof in the Texas sun is going to react differently to a 53-year-old.

Recent talk in the OSHA community suggested that 80 degrees F might be the target temperature. If so, that temperature would trigger 15-minute rest breaks every two hours. (Indoor workplaces with temperatures under 80 degrees F would be exempt from any such requirements.) Of course, that leaves a lot of questions, such as "What is the official source of the temperature reading?" and "Would a site require multiple temperature readings to account for different shade and airflow?" The conversation gets pretty complex pretty quickly.

Where does this heat illness rulemaking now stand? The National Advisory Committee on Occupational Safety and Health Heat Group met in April 2023 to discuss recommendations for a final standard. OSHA then convened a small business panel, per the Small Business Regulatory Enforcement Fairness Act of 1996, to look at the issue in August 2023. In November 2023, that panel issued its report, and it was hypercritical of the rulemaking effort.

Recognizing the stumbling block, OSHA might do something like copying some state OSHA plan requirements related to heat illness, but the federal agency really doesn’t care for the flexibility that some of the state-level standards offer. California, Colorado, Minnesota, Oregon, and Washington generally have specific requirements for heat acclimatization, quantities of potable water, rest and shade requirements, and a trigger temperature of around 80 degrees F. In February 2022, Nevada OSHA, on the other hand, published a draft of its proposed heat illness regulation that requires employer adoption of a heat illness program and action where the heat index reaches 90 degrees F, based on the web bulb globe temperature, which takes into account air temperature, humidity, radiant heat, and air movement. (The web bulb globe temperature was developed by the U.S. military in the 1950s to prevent heat-related deaths in training camps.)

As of now, OSHA is limited to focusing on outdoor and indoor heat-related hazards as part of a national emphasis program launched in April 2022. The initial focus areas were any outdoor activities in communities experiencing a heat wave, per National Weather Service declaration, and indoor activities near radiant heat sources, such as foundries.

Given the difficulty that current OSHA personnel are having in trying to find common ground to pursue proposed rulemaking and the certainty of legal appeals if such a rule should ever see the light of day, it is likely going to be a while before any national heat illness regulations are finalized and have an impact on manufacturers.
