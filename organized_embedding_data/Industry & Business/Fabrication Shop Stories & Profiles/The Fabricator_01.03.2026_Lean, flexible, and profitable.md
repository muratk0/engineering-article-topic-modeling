Shop Management

How a pipe fabricator doubled monthly revenue

By

Tim Heston

Epstudio20/iStock/Getty Images Plus

"I

t is very difficult for people to buy into new ideas when they’ve been doing something for ages, and somebody comes in and tries to change things. Now, I can confidently say that, even though this was a problem, we overcame it. We made sure every single person in the shop was involved."

That was Sultan Ahmed Mohammed, manufacturing engineer at McWane Plant & Industrial, reflecting on the lean transformation of the company’s Houston plant. The high-productmix operation produces a wide variety of pipes and fittings for the water treatment sector—and it’s just one of numerous plants MPI operates across the country.

A year and a half ago, the 15 shop floor employees at MPI-Houston produced about $600,000 worth of pipe a month. Now, they’re producing $1.2 million. "Those guys on the floor completely own that success," Mohammed said. "They know we’re not here to saw-cut and thread pipes and weld flanges. We’re here to ship pipe, and the more pipe we ship, the more money we make."

WINNING HEARTS AND MINDS

The top of Mohammed’s LinkedIn profile reads "Lean Fanatic." At the University of Houston, when attaining his master’s degree in industrial engineering, he took classes from Shahrukh Irani, PhD, who introduced him to Job Shop Lean—a methodology that adapts lean manufacturing to the high-product-mix, low-quantity operation. Irani also runs a consultancy called Lean & Flexible LLC, with services focused on the job shop environment.

Mohammed could have first described how he delved into a product-quantity analysis that also incorporates revenue, profits, demand trends, and job routings; how he put together a value network map; and how this led to splitting the welding and machining departments in counterintuitive ways. He didn’t harp on the details, though, at least not at first. Instead, he went straight to the most challenging aspect of any lean transformation:

winning hearts and minds

.

"I made a habit of discussing every change with all 15 people on the floor," Mohammed said. "We have morning huddles. We get everyone involved early on, and we ask for feedback. With every idea, I’d say, ‘Do we have any pushback? What do you think?’"

Initial conversations involved building perspective. Sure, learning to "see" waste is critical. Workstations need to be clean and organized. Setups need to be measured, work instructions documented. "But for me, I had to introduce a goal employees could relate to, to believe in," he said. "That was the financial goal."

The financial perspective helps make some of lean’s core attributes intuitive: The more dollars you ship in less time, the more money the operation makes. Machine uptime measurements might give you an idea of asset utilization, but if more work isn’t shipping out the door in less time, revenue doesn’t grow.

This can help sell the idea of quick changeover, low batch sizes, and minimal work-in-process (WIP). The faster work flows through the operation, the better. WIP inventory represents money sitting on the floor, and it’s not worth anything until it ships.

"Lean is so much more than learning to identify waste," Mohammed said, pausing briefly for emphasis. "It’s so much more."

THE VALUE NETWORK MAP

When bare ductile iron pipe arrives at MPI-Houston, it’s put on a lathe, which threads the ends. Pipe flanges also are faced to ensure a good seal between the flange and the pipe. If the pipe requires a hole, it moves to the drilling department. If it requires a collar, it moves to the welding cells. It then moves on to paint, then shipping.

The flow seems straightforward until you delve into the "ifs." Pipe diameters vary tremendously, as do job requirements. In welding, some pipes require a circumferential seam, others a stitch weld to better manage the heat. The variability is immense.

"In our case, value stream mapping just wouldn’t make sense," he said. "This is where the value network map helped us."

The value network map (VNM) essentially identifies product families using a product-process matrix. Discovering jobs that have similar routings and machine setups helps uncover potential material flow improvements.

Even before the VNM, Mohammed already could see that routings had a lot of backtracking. "Anything and everything was placed where they could find space." As just one example, welding was placed right after sawing. "Pipes would be cut, bypass the weld department, undergo various processes, come back to the weld department, then go all the way to the end of the plant again to the paint department. Based on the process flow chart we created, we changed the layout entirely."

Today, pipes arriving on-site move directly to sawing, which feeds the entire downstream flow. That saw department sends material directly to machining, with all the lathes and threaders, then to a bucking machine that twists on and tightens a flange, before another lathe faces the end of that flange.

"Machining is a critical step. We have seven lathes, two threaders, and one bucking machine," Mohammad said. "We needed to place each of these machines to ensure the flow was continuous, and we didn’t lose efficiencies with excessive material handling. So here, we divided the shop. One half was devoted to big pipes, another for small pipes."

Specifically, any product 3 to 12 in. dia. flowed through one side of the plant; anything from 12 to 54 in. was on the other side.

Volume is the deciding factor here. The 3- to 12-in. pipes are the shop’s "runners," with consistently high demand and relatively high quantity. Pipes from 14 to 30 in. are "repeaters," products run somewhat frequently at lower volume; those above 30 in. are "strangers," jobs run infrequently with relatively unpredictable demand.

A saw cuts pipe at the initial workstation in MPI-Houston’s plant. The cutting operation feeds to distinct value streams.

In this instance, it made sense to separate out those repeaters from the other products. To make this happen, the plant effectively was split down the middle, with each machine positioned as close as possible to sawing as well as the central aisle.

FEED THE CONSTRAINT

Paint is the plant’s most dynamic process, and the principal constraint. Pipe is painted red for above-ground use and black for underground, and the ratio between the two varies significantly. Sometimes the plant processes 30% red pipe, 70% black; other times it’s the reverse, and sometimes it’s 50-50.

Initially, red and black painting bays were on different sides of the building. The bays were placed in space that happened to be available—certainly not for optimal workflow. "We’d have our material handler move pipes from both bays and stage them for palletizing and shipping, since these pipes often had to be shipped to the same address," Mohammed said.

To resolve this, the team consolidated both red and black painting bays near the end of the product flow, adjacent to palletizing and shipping. Most important, they ensured the size of the bays could be adjusted as demand changed.

HOW GOOD DATA DRIVES PROFITS

As momentum for improvement increased, Sultan Ahmed Mohammed at McWane Plant & Industrial’s Houston operation reached out to some data science students at the University of Houston. Their goal: To identify the kinds of products that would maximize operational profitability.

To do this, they analyzed product families—specifically, different ranges of pipe diameters: 3 to 12 in., 14 to 30 in., and 36 to 54. in. What combination of these product families, and in what quantities, could yield maximum revenue and profits over a single month?

"That simulation model helped change what drives sales," he said, adding that this works best when "the market becomes the constraint." That is, shop floor capacity is such that the company can comfortably underbid competitors and still remain significantly profitable. When the market is the constraint, good data helps sales make the most out of the company’s competitive advantage.

Details will differ depending on a job shop’s product and customer mix, of course, but as Mohammed described, because MPI-Houston knew what kind of work drove the most profits, it could then communicate that with sales, who could drive their efforts to maximize the plant’s greatest strengths.

"This is what really helped us get where we are today," he said, "with 15 people shipping $1.2 million worth of product every month."

Based on this, three attributes decided how the bays were arranged: pipe length, pipe diameter, and end configuration. Some pipes had one flange on one end; others had flanges on both ends. Sometimes painters worked on bare pipe with no flanges. "Based on this, we had two cells with three different bays, which we could resize based on demand," Mohammed said.

He added that painters worked by a schedule driven by promise date, not FIFO, or first-in, first-out. Before the lean transformation, in fact, the entire operation followed an informal form of FIFO. Sales orders were pushed to the floor, and operators tackled whatever pipe happened to arrive first at a work center first, regardless of due date.

Today, all work orders are released based on the promise date. This way, for most work, the shop’s water spider (material handler) stages work so operators know exactly what to work on next.

ABOUT JOB CHANGEOVERS

Due dates drive the schedule, but they don’t necessarily drive the sequence at specific work centers. "After promise dates, the most important element in the schedule was to minimize changeovers between jobs," Mohommed said.

MPI-Houston’s pipe fabrication plant looks empty, but it’s anything but. The shop simply lacks noticeable piles of work-in-process. With increased part-flow velocity through the plant, the operation today ships more products (and earns more profits) than ever.

Sultan Ahmed Mohammed

The part flow in machining is a prime example here. Pipe diameters could be 6, 8, 10, or 12 in. With the promise date established, the schedule then depends on optimizing the sequence of work to minimize changeovers in that machining cell. In this case, this means machining 6-in. pipe followed by 8 in.

"Moving from 6 in. to 8 in, the changeover is so minimal, we didn’t even put it on a process flow chart," Mohammed said. The optimal job sequence progressed up the pipe diameters, then reversed back down. This effectively minimized the changeover.

He emphasized that this approach works

only

because the operation already had perfected the order-release schedule from the office. The order due date, not order receipt, triggered its release to the floor. Only with this established could the machining department (or any other work center) optimize the sequence to minimize changeover.

OPTIMIZE WELDING

MPI-Houston’s welding process doesn’t change with the pipe length, diameter, or (of course) color. It instead changes with the coating that’s on the pipe’s inside diameter. Most have either cement or a special kind of epoxy that’s used for drainage.

Cement-lined pipes can handle the welder putting a circumferential fillet weld around the entire OD. The epoxy-lined pipes, however, require torch preheating, stitch-welding, and another application of epoxy before the piece is sent to the painting cell. And so for welding, products are separated based on these job requirements.

WORKCELLS AND SHOP CULTURE

Today, MPI-Houston’s saw department feeds two distinct value streams. Everything moves forward; no backtracking required. Flow is rules-based: Machines are set to work on a

specific range

of pipe diameters. Welders handle

specific groups

of pipe (complete fillets on concrete-lined pipe versus stitch welds on epoxy-lined pipe). This feeds into a dynamic paint area that adjusts depending on demand: red versus black pipe, the number of end flanges (or lack thereof).

When you look at the operation, you might think lean concepts are intuitive: A clean shop floor with established part flow just makes life easier for everyone. What’s so hard about winning hearts and minds?

The core of the matter has to do with capacity, both in terms of machinery and a person’s skills and abilities. "It was always believed that if a machine has the capacity, it should do everything in our product mix," Mohommed said. After all, an idle machine "loses" money while a producing machine "makes" money, right?

This perspective leads to the classic "push" mentality that builds WIP. Why should a machine run if downstream processes don’t have the capacity to handle the additional work, meaning the job couldn’t ship any sooner? Meanwhile, machines being used outside the rules set for them (that is, a specific product family) effectively "steal" from highly profitable capacity that could be sold.

"I had some pushback, but I said, ‘Give me three months and I will show you results.’ And sure enough, within several months, the number of pipes we shipped jumped—from 600 to 650, then 700, then beyond." The financial results helped overcome that resistance.

"The greatest challenge, culturally speaking, was on the shop floor," Mohammed said, "and I could understand why."

For years, the shop’s most skilled technicians could prove themselves by working on any job put in front of them, from the smallest pipe to the largest. In fact, fabricating large pipe was reserved for the company’s most experienced machinists and welders.

"Prof. Irani used to say, ‘You could have an amazing shop layout, you have good ideas, and you even have money to implement projects. But if you don’t involve people early on, you will never achieve your results.’

A year and a half ago, the 15 shop floor employees at MPI-Houston produced about $600,000 worth of pipe a month. Now, they’re producing $1.2 million.

I’m glad I knew it before I started," Mohammed said. "That’s why I never stopped our morning huddles with all 15 people on the floor. I always said, ‘This is the idea. What do you think?’

"At first, no one would respond. Eventually, people started talking. They realized they were being respected. They knew that an idea that, say, cost $100,000 but resulted in an improvement of $200,000—which actually happened, by the way—was being given with consideration, their way of thinking and seeing their own value. Each one of them knew the process more than anyone else. They knew their ideas were vital for the company’s success. Because of this, everyone bought into nearly every improvement idea."

Every idea Mohammed brought to the table, at least one shop employee would ask, "Why? We’ve been doing this for 30 years. Why are we changing?"

Here, the "why" was about being part of a profitable, sustainable organization that, at this writing, is gradually becoming a model for other MPI plants across the country.

"Everyone at the plant completely owns the improvements we’ve made. Every single person knew our goal was to ship $1.2 million a month. That’s how all 15 of us came together. They might work in the machining department. They might operate our saw to cut pipe. They might be expert welders. Regardless of where they worked, they decided to come together and say, ‘We’re not here to cut, machine, weld, or paint pipe. We’re here to ship pipe. So, what can we do to ensure the shipping department can ship more pipe than ever before?"
