T

he Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA) can start issuing much higher penalties starting Aug. 1 because of new upper limits that Congress granted the agency in last year’s budget bill. The $7,000 cap on serious violations goes to $12,600 and the $70,000 limit on willful and repeat violations increases to $126,000.

The new authority coincides with aggressive action OSHA has been taking in recent months. For example, the agency just issued a final rule in March that increased the permissible exposure limit (PEL) for breathable silica, a substance often present in metalworking facilities, such as those that use sandblasters to finish parts. All sorts of industry sectors have complained about the difficulty of meeting the new, lower 50 micrograms per cubic meter (μg/m

3

) of air averaged over an 8-hour shift, down from 100 μg/m

3

. About 30 percent of general industry isn’t meeting the current 100 μg/m

3

limit.

There is no question the OSHA is chomping at the bit to issue higher penalties. At least that’s the perspective of Valerie N. Butera, a lawyer at Epstein Becker Green in Washington, D.C., who focuses on OSHA issues.

"At a recent American Bar Association OSH law meeting, OSHA’s head, David Michaels, indicated that OSHA is likely to take advantage of the entire increase," Butera said.

The agency already had started getting tough on companies that violate the Severe Injury and Illness Reporting Rule that went into effect Jan. 1, 2015. OSHA kept fines to $1,000 in the first year but recently started handing out higher fines, including one to an employer for $70,000 for a willful violation.

"I read this as another indication that the penalty increase will be severe enough to have an enormous impact on employers and that it will likely be implemented as soon as possible," Butera added. "With the uncertainty that comes with the election, OSHA has been working feverishly to finalize many of its most important endeavors, and I anticipate that the increase will be treated in the same manner."

Obama Administration Considering New Action on Steel Imports

It may have nothing to do with the high visibility of foreign imports in the U.S. presidential campaign, but the Obama administration held a hearing in April on foreign steel imports and their impact on both U.S. steel producers and U.S. steel consumers.

It was no surprise that China was the main villain at the hearing held by the U.S. Trade Representative (USTR) and Department of Commerce (DOC). The problem is that steelmaking capacity worldwide is growing, led by China, and demand is weakening, leading to overcapacity.

In the U.S., crude steel production decreased by 10 percent in 2015 to 78.9 million metric tons. In 2014, steel imports increased 37.9 percent by volume over 2013, though imports dropped in 2015. Meanwhile, U.S. steel exports have been decreasing steadily over the last four years, showing a 17 percent decrease between 2014 and 2015.

In the lead-up to the hearing, the USTR and DOC issued statements that the Obama administration was looking at all angles to address this "challenge."

A few days after that hearing took place, Secretary of Commerce Penny Pritzker and Trade Representative Michael Froman went to Brussels for a meeting with other Western country trade officials. Those parties agreed on a joint statement that presents a shared view on the challenges associated with the excess capacity facing the steel industry and workers in their respective countries. The joint statement proposed a series of steps to address the challenges, but no actual action was taken there.

At the hearing, the Metals Service Center Institute (MSCI), which is concerned about a loss of domestic steelmaking capacity and the impact of increased import tariffs on U.S. manufacturers of goods using steel, suggested that if the U.S. government increases tariffs on imported steel, it should also impose a corresponding and offsetting tariff on steel-containing products identified by USTR in consultation with domestic steel-consuming companies.

Spokesmen for neither the MSCI nor the USTR responded to a query asking how likely it is the U.S. government would go that double-duty route.
