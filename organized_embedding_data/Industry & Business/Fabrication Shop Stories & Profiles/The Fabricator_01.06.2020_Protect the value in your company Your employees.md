# Protect the value in your company: Your employees

From The Editor-In-Chief

Disregard for their safety is bad for business and bad for manufacturing’s image

Read more from Dan Davis at

www.thefabricator.com/author/dan-davis

A

s a metal fabricator, you probably haven’t given much thought to the outbreaks of COVID-19 cases in meat-processing facilities in the U.S. That’s too bad, because with each new publicized case of another coronavirus hot spot, someone reading a newspaper or watching the television news is thinking how horrible life in a manufacturing facility must be.

You can laugh at the supposed linkage between a fab shop and a poultry-processing facility, but in the minds of most people, it’s hard work for meager wages. Despite all the public relations promoting modern manufacturing as being clean, safe, and advanced, people tend to focus on a death count.

Much has been made of workers themselves being to blame for the spread of the coronavirus. (Nebraska Gov. Pete Ricketts defined it as a "community issue.") Many live in multigenerational housing arrangements and carpool to work. The theory goes that the coronavirus could have spread easily among those who live together in close quarters. Meanwhile, they go to work and are very close to each other cutting and packaging meat.

Even with knowledge that these outbreaks were occurring, other U.S. facilities didn’t make the major adjustments to avoid what was happening at these meat-processing plants. In fact, some remained open even as the number of diagnosed COVID-19 cases grew. The United Food and Commercial Workers International Union told CNN that nearly 8,000 workers had tested positive for the coronavirus and at least 27 had died as of mid-May. Did those deaths have to occur?

The battle to change the image of manufacturing is an ongoing one. This is a setback, but that’s to be expected. Some people just don’t get it and never will.

What all metal fabricators can do, however, is act as responsible employers and do what is best to protect their employees. For all the times that I’ve heard fab shop owners and managers talk about how important their employees are to their success, now is the time for them to put their money where their mouth is. Protect those who deliver the most value and show those in your community that a job in a manufacturing facility doesn’t have to be a dead-end gig.

Probably the most important thing they can do is require sick employees to stay home. That sounds easy enough, but as the meat-processing facilities learned, if people make more money by showing up to work than they do sitting at home sick, they likely will come in to work with that fever. That puts the onus on the employer to look at if or how workers are compensated for sick time and ensure that the sick do not infect the healthy.

When employees are at work, they need to act as if they are still abiding by rules recommended by medical professionals when shelter-in-place laws were in force. Practice social distancing and wear a mask. The social distancing part of this equation is a bit easier for metal fabricators than it might be for employees who are gathered around a conveyor belt of some kind. The processing of sheets and coils of metal requires a lot of room, and that’s the kind of open spaces that doctors want to see if people are going to show up for work. As for masks, even homemade cloth ones dramatically reduce the chance that you can infect someone else with your infectious droplets as you exhale. Masks are especially handy for containing droplets when you cough or sneeze.

Also, if you have staff members who are at greater risk of contracting the coronavirus—those 65 and older, the obese, and those with severe underlying medical conditions like heart or lung disease or diabetes—they will require greater attention. Can they do their jobs at home? Can contact with them be minimized?

For those that believe this coronavirus crisis is a manufactured conspiracy or simply overblown, be aware that taking prevention measures also makes good business sense. Guidance from the Occupational Safety and Health Administration recommends a review of the facility and work tasks to determine risks and then implementation of processes and safeguards to protect employees. These efforts will go a long way in protecting the company if an employee contracts COVID-19 and later claims that the company didn’t take the necessary steps to minimize the risk associated with being exposed to the virus.

A president of a fabricating company told me that he wanted to make coming to work safer than going to the grocery store during this pandemic. He said he believes he can do it with the right processes in place and having everyone commit to following them.

That’s the message people need to be hearing about working in manufacturing. Everyone matters, from the front door to the office, through the shop floor, and to the shipping area. United we stand. Divided we fail.

FMA Certified Education Centers (CEC) are community and technical colleges, trade schools, and universities that specialize in training adults for careers in the metal forming, fabricating, processing, and machining sectors. They offer coursework for local students year-round and serve as host locations for many types of FMA professional development programs as requested. A council of members convene six times a year to plan and execute special programs on worker training for educators and human resource managers from companies of all sizes.
