OSHA proposal would lower beryllium levels, increase workplace protections

The federal government is proposing a new standard that would dramatically lower workplace exposure to beryllium, a widely used material that can cause lung diseases. The proposal would apply to an estimated 35,000 workers covered by OSHA.

Currently OSHA’s eight-hour permissible exposure limit (PEL) for beryllium is 2.0 micrograms per cubic meter of air. Above that level, employers must take steps to reduce the airborne concentration of beryllium. That standard was originally established in 1948 by the Atomic Energy Commission and adopted by OSHA in 1971. OSHA’s proposed standard would reduce the eight-hour PEL to 0.2 microgram per cubic meter. The proposed rule also would require additional protections, including PPE, medical exams and other medical surveillance, and training.

The 1971 limit significantly reduced fatalities due to acute beryllium disease but, over time, it became clear that exposure below that limit also had damaging long-term health effects. OSHA initially proposed to lower the PEL for beryllium in 1975.

The need for a new standard was recognized by the nation’s primary beryllium product manufacturer, Materion, and the United Steelworkers, the union representing many of those who work with beryllium. Together, they approached OSHA in 2012 to suggest a stronger standard.

OSHA estimates that the rule could prevent almost 100 deaths and 50 serious illnesses each year. Workers who inhale beryllium particles can develop a debilitating, incurable illness known as chronic beryllium disease and are also at increased risk of lung cancer. Dangers arise when beryllium-containing materials are processed in a way that releases airborne beryllium dust, fume, mist, or other forms.

The majority of current worker exposures to beryllium occur in operations such as foundry and smelting operations, machining, beryllium oxide ceramics and composites manufacturing, and dental lab work.

Tour report: Moving metal in Cincinnati

Before the beginning of

The FABRICATOR

’s 2015 Technology Summit in Cincinnati Sept. 29, Dick Kallage, president of operations consultancy KDC & Associates and author of this publication’s Improvement Insights column, asked the attendees to focus not just on the technology, but on the product flow. A fabricator can spend a lot of money on equipment to make parts fast, but if the parts are just sitting in the middle of the aisle waiting for the next downstream process, that fabricator is asking for quality and shipping problems.

So what were attendees—and others if they have the chance to tour another metal fabricating facility—supposed to look for? Is the shop floor filled with work-in-process? Is the production area clean and organized? Is the shop running full production? How is material movement determined? Do the employees looked engaged? Are there visual reminders in the shop to update employees on production and quality metrics?

Although job shops and OEMs with metal fabricating operations fabricate different things for different customers, they all share a common challenge: how to move metal through the shop efficiently.

That was the focus for the attendees as they visited eight Cincinnati-area fabrication facilities over two days. Just how are these fabricators getting metal out the door as efficiently as possible?

The first visit to

Tri-State Fabricators Inc.

, Amelia, Ohio, set the tone for the rest of the tours. A large operation with fabricating on one end and powder coating and painting on the other end of the 125,000-sq.-ft. facility, the plant has a 49-shelf River automated material processing system from Mitsubishi Laser/MC Machinery Systems that keeps its 4,500-W CO

2

ML3015eX laser fed during the two shifts during the week and unattended on weekends.

Until it had the material shelving system installed, Tri-State’s Bob Smith said the company struggled with raw material management.

"Before we had the River system, we had material piled up all over," he said, "and the one you needed was always at the bottom of the pile."

Now the raw material is delivered and staged for the laser cutting machine when it needs it. Laser-cut sheets are removed and set aside for parts removal.

Kallage later commented on the shop’s clean sight lines. Aisles were open, making it easier to move jobs downstream in the manufacturing process.

Gaining an edge in deburring

Figure 1

ARKU, a company known for its coil and part leveling equipment, has launched a new series of deburring and edge-rounding machines, which are now available in North America.

You don’t normally associate business improvement with an Oktoberfest celebration, but fabricators got that opportunity in the Cincinnati area on Oct. 8.

German machine tool manufacturer ARKU Coil Systems Inc., known primarily for its coil and part leveling equipment, hosted fabricators and business partners for its annual celebration. This one, however, was unique because the company also was unveiling its new EdgeBreaker

®

automated deburring and edge-rounding machine. That made this celebration an "Okto-Burr-fest," according to company promotional literature.

Any fabricator that has to wrestle with deburring or edge rounding on metal parts for safety reasons or customer requirements knows that it can be a dirty and difficult job.

But it also might be a necessity to stay competitive in the future.

Dick Kallage, principal of management consulting firm KDC & Associates Ltd. and the author of

The FABRICATOR

’s Improvement Insights column, opened the day with a reminder that the industry faces a real challenge in trying to replace the 750 to 1,000 workers who are retiring from manufacturing companies each day.

"Those people tend to be skilled workers. They aren’t rookies," he said.

Industry estimates suggest that 750,000 to 800,000 U.S. manufacturing jobs remain open because employers can’t find the right people to fill them. That’s up from 600,000 in 2013. The numbers are likely to grow even more as governmental and educational institutions struggle to train enough workers the manufacturing community desires. Kallage said that a supply of young skilled workers simply isn’t there because young people still are wary of manufacturing jobs.

Where does that leave companies looking to replace the retiring skilled workers on their shop floors? Kallage said the business solution is to reduce the number of qualified workers required to produce the required output. That means technology.

Kallage called them "lean machines." These are machine tools that support waste elimination and minimize the need for skilled labor for some manufacturing activities.

That’s what ARKU officials hope the EdgeBreaker machines (see

Figure 1

) can do for deburring tasks. In the larger machine model, oscillating rollers grind down the unwanted burrs, and transverse-running rounding abrasive blocks round the edges on both the top and bottom of parts before they exit the machine.

The machine is designed to deburr parts from 0.13 to 3.15 in. thick and round the edges of parts 0.03 to 3.15 in. The minimum part dimension that the unit can handle is 6 by 1.5 in. (held by a magnetic belt), and the maximum part weight is 550 lbs. Feed rates are from 0.66 to 19 FPM.

A touchscreen control allows the operator to dial in just how much grinding and edge-rounding action are required for the part. When the right combination of grinding drum and abrasive block height and feed speed has been found, the operator can save the recipe in the control software. Up to 1,000 different jobs can be saved.

Figure 2

One person typically needs two minutes to deburr this 50-mm-thick part on the right. The EdgeBreaker machine can deburr five of those parts in 70 seconds, according to ARKU officials.

Of course, as the machine is used, the consumables wear. The operator can push a calibration button after a job or a series of jobs, and the machine will readjust to take into account the consumable wear from the previous work. The machine also alerts the operator when the consumables are nearing the time when they should be replaced.

Thomas Weinfurtner, ARKU sales manager, said the machine is designed to be simple to maintain. The grinding drum slides in and out for quick replacement of the belt. Weinfurtner said the changeover typically takes about two minutes.

The machine has a patent-pending abrasive block-replacement system. Instead of an operator having to wrestle with a belt with blocks attached, the EdgeBreaker has a carousel to which any combination of abrasive blocks (for grinding) or rounding blocks (for edge rounding) can be attached, either on top or the bottom. The operator can remove and replace the blocks with one click. Weinfurtner said that it might take a person about 30 minutes to replace all of the blocks manually.

Later in the day a speaker addressed the state of laser technology and just why automated deburring might be growing in importance. When solid-state lasers first appeared on the scene as a sheet metal processing tool around 2010, the technology was going up against CO

2

lasers, which had matured over the past 30 years. The solid-state cutting technology reduced costs for both running and maintaining the laser, and today sales of these fiber and disk lasers outpace those of traditional CO

2

lasers by a great margin. Even so, the technology is rather new, so laser cutting machine manufacturers continue to fine-tune the machine tools for all of the applications that a metal fabricator might encounter.

For instance, while great strides have been made to boost solid-state lasers’ cutting speed and resulting edge quality on thick materials up to 1 in., the industry has some work to do with the medium gauges from 0.1875 to 0.250 in., according to the speaker. As a solid-state laser pushes through the medium gauges, it leaves dross. Advancements in beam control have allowed the machine toolmakers to address this shortcoming in the thicker materials, but work is still needed elsewhere. That’s where the automated deburring machines can help fabricators keep up with these solid-state lasers and not overburden a shop floor with tons of manual deburring.
