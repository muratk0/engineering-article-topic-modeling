One concept, however, isn’t so common: single-piece, or kit-based, part flow. The concept has worked wonders for high-volume, low-mix manufacturing. The idea is to decrease work-in-process (WIP) and, hence, shorten lead times. But as the product mix grows more diverse, the kit-based flow concept becomes more difficult to apply. Setups simply take too long. Machines spend so much time being set up, overall throughput sinks, and machine utilization plummets.

Today, however, the situation is starting to change. Batches are shrinking, and some operations are implementing true kit-based part flow, with different blanks dedicated to a single assembly flowing sequentially through forming and downstream to assembly—no waiting for massive batches of parts to be produced, stored, retrieved, and staged for final assembly.

Making this possible is the reality of zero changeover time offered by a technology that isn’t new, but its impact on lean part flow is now starting to be realized: the panel bender.

The Panel Bender Stands Apart

For years, lean manufacturing touted the benefit of single-minute exchange of die (SMED), a concept that originated at the stamping press. Carefully staged with the right tooling close at hand, a press brake could achieve a single-minute (or perhaps "several-minute") changeover, especially if it formed a limited range of products, material grades, and thicknesses. Automatic tool change technologies at the brake, including a V die that automatically adjusts its width for the job at hand, shortens changeover time even more.

That said, changeover times still range from one to several minutes. Look at the panel bender in action, and you can see how changeover happens nearly instantaneously. You’ll also notice that, when an operator pulls up a program and loads the first blank, the first part produced is a good part.

First part, good part

is a critical aspect of quick changeover and overall quality. After all, a changeover really can’t be "quick" if an operator needs to run a multitude of tryout parts. And if bad parts make it to downstream processes, the inefficiency can be even more profound. Rework can be one of the greatest wastes in high-product-mix manufacturing, especially if the error isn’t caught until late in the value stream. The nature of the panel bender, combined with good programming and simulation (more on this later), helps make

first part, good part

a reality.

Panel benders differ from press brakes in several fundamental ways. When you air bend on a press brake, the die width governs the radius and, hence, the amount of elongation you get and the final part dimensions you achieve. A thicker material generally requires a toolset with a larger die width—necessitating a tool change.

A forming department might design a staged setup, with punch and die toolsets placed across the brake bed. Together, these tools might process a wide range of parts within one or more part families—but the range isn’t infinite. In most high-product-mix situations, operators must change over tools for the next job. This encourages larger batches, so those setups can be amortized over larger runs.

Here’s where the panel bender can have a real impact. The machine has segmented blankholder tools that hold the material in place as upper and lower bending blades fold the sheet metal to the desired angle (see

Figure 1

). The blades themselves don’t move in a straight line but instead in interpolated upward and downward movements around a pivot point (or bend point) to bend the flange. The blade’s relationship to the bend point determines the radius. Blade position and motion can change to suit the radius you need to achieve in a specific material thickness. In other words, one toolset can bend material grades and thicknesses ranging from the thinnest to the thickest stock, within the machine’s bending capacity.

FIGURE 1 Segmented blankholder tools hold a workpiece while upper and lower bending blades bend a flange in the positive and negative direction. The throat depth governs the flange length that can be achieved.

The only tool changes involve the rearrangement of blankholder tools to accommodate different bending lengths without colliding with previously formed flanges. This change occurs in a matter of seconds. When possible, the panel bender arranges its segmented blankholder tools in a way that can accommodate every bend line on a part. Still, if the blankholder tools do need to change over in the middle of a part’s bending sequence, the rearrangement usually doesn’t prolong the bending cycle significantly. Again, the clamping tool rearrangement occurs in seconds, not minutes.

To form a new part, an operator simply pulls up a part program, loads a blank against a locating device, and commences the bend sequence. From that point, the machine knows the part location. In automated loading scenarios, be it via a robot or in a blanking and bending flexible manufacturing system (FMS), job changeovers can be even more seamless, with the line producing one different part after another.

Note that whether kit-based part flow makes sense depends on myriad factors throughout the plant floor. In some cases, a fabricator might be able to separate a product family into its own value stream and—again, thanks in part to the near-zero changeover time at the panel bender—achieve (or at least get close to) single-piece or kit-based part flow. Other operations might take a parts-supermarket approach, with the sheet metal fabrication department replenishing an inventory buffer before final assembly.

That said, forming remains central in the precision sheet metal value stream, and near-zero-changeover times in forming open up various part flow possibilities. There’s no need to amortize a setup with large batches. Products can flow in whatever lot size that best optimizes throughput for the entire operation.

Capabilities of the Panel

Bender Why not have a bending department full of panel benders, able to produce any lot size, from one (single-piece flow) to dozens or hundreds? This might work for certain product mixes, but panel benders do have certain limitations, which is why nearly all fabricators use panel benders to complement their press brakes, not replace them entirely.

So, what are the panel bender’s limitations? First is the flange length. The upper and lower bending blades are built in a "C" configuration with a designated throat depth. This usually limits flanges to 10 in. or less, though some panel benders can bend deeper enclosures.

Second is part size. Panel benders require parts to be at least a certain dimension so that the part manipulator has something to grasp to manipulate parts between bends (see

Figure 2

). The blankholder also needs enough surface to clamp and secure the workpiece.

Some panel bending systems have cutoff options that allow them to form extremely narrow and small parts. A blank is positioned for bending between the clamps. The blades make the bend before the sheet is positioned for a shearing operation. If the blank has a series of cuts perpendicular to the bend line, the shear can cut off numerous small brackets that can be carried away by a conveyor. Alternatively, those perpendicular cuts can have microtabs that hold small pieces together before an operator breaks them apart.

FIGURE 3 Precision cutting on the laser and forming on the panel bender created the part (right) with a half-open corner joint. However, subsequent welding (center) required time-consuming grinding and polishing (left), creating a manufacturing constraint.

FIGURE 4 This part redesign moved the cut line into the flange. The result: Arc welding, grinding, and polishing were replaced by a simple spot welding operation.

Again, a panel bender’s part manipulator that moves pieces between bends needs a flat surface to grasp. A complex form with multiple positive and negative bends in different areas, including deep down-flanges protruding from interior windows, can present challenges.

The same holds true for parts with large interior windows or cutouts. Yet again, the part manipulator needs something to grasp, and a large empty space in the middle of a blank creates issues. Some operations get around this by leaving the window’s slug microtabbed in place during bending, then removing the slug after forming.

Weighing the Tradeoffs

All these strategies have tradeoffs, and this is where some holistic thinking can help. For instance, some might argue that forming a part with microtabs on the panel bender is inefficient, considering those tabbed edges need to be deburred.

Still, the productivity improvements might be so great that even with that added deburring operation, panel bending still might make sense. Also, the microtab itself might not need to be deburred if located on, say, an unexposed edge or right on a bend line. Just as on the press brake, panel benders elongate the material along the radius, and that "stretching" can help "shrink" a microtab to a point where it’s not an issue for downstream processing.

Of course, all these options need to be put in a broader context—especially when it comes to downstream operations. No matter how fast a particular machine runs, constraint processes govern throughput. A panel bender might achieve some great "local efficiency," churning out numerous parts per hour, but what if it’s just making a downstream bottleneck worse?

Design for Manufacturability

What’s causing that downstream bottleneck? Sometimes, a minor part redesign can free the constraint and unleash additional throughput. For instance, consider a part with a bump-formed flange and an adjacent edge that’s welded onto the bumped contour (see

Figure 3

). The resulting edge creates a half-open corner joint that requires some serious forming and cutting precision, which today’s machinery can achieve. Even so, the arc weld along that joint requires manual grinding and polishing—a serious constraint. It’s also a difficult procedure, and cycle times vary greatly depending on who’s welding and grinding.

That variability increases the need for WIP buffers. The more WIP you have, the longer your overall manufacturing time, and the more cash you have sitting in inventory. Unpredictable cycle times in grinding and polishing also make smooth, kit-based part flow very difficult to achieve.

In this case, moving the cut line slightly up the flange changes everything. The panel bender’s tools can form that narrow flange, followed by the bump form, after which the piece can be brought to a quick resistance spot welding operation to secure the joint (see

Figure 4

). That frees the constraint and makes manufacturing more predictable, which in turn spurs more options for part flow.

The Software Trifecta

Optimizing part flow for just one product or product family can be somewhat straightforward, especially if the product has a dedicated value stream. Imagine a scenario in which parts are redesigned to optimize the capabilities of the panel bender. Every piece is cut and bent in sequence, then sent down the value stream to assembly as needed. WIP is minimal and partflow velocity, from the first cutting operation to the final shipment, is sky high.

Of course, few operations are this simple. Some pieces just need the flexibility that press brakes offer. Assemblies can have multiple material grades and thicknesses, so blanks need to be nested on separate sheets on the laser. Beyond this, most fabricators process hundreds or even thousands of different part numbers. Optimizing everything at once just isn’t practical.

This is where software automation fills a need. Manufacturing software in precision sheet metal fabrication falls into three broad categories: programming and process simulation, production control, and the Industrial Internet of Things (IIoT)—a software trifecta, so to speak. Programming and process simulation catches manufacturability issues before jobs hit the floor. Production control connects the shop floor to ERP and helps schedule and manage production. Finally, IIoT tracks performance and provides data feedback for process improvement.

The first element,

programming and process simulation

, ensures process stability, looking at everything from the correct bend programming to optimal placement on a laser cutting bed, not just for material yield but also process stability and efficient material handling. Every detail enters the picture—ease of part removal from the skeleton, material handling time, error-proofing in part sorting, and (of course) efficiency in bending. Perhaps most important, programming and simulation software helps ensure quality, error-free processing—making the concept of

first part, good part

a reality.

The second element,

production control software

, looks at hundreds of factors at once, then gives planners the information they need to sequence orders in ways that provide the most value. This might include kit-based or small-lot-size part flow for certain orders, but other orders might benefit from certain levels of batching.

Finally,

IIoT

provides a holistic look at machine performance, sending notifications in real time, measuring overall equipment effectiveness (OEE) and revealing true machine uptimes—when the bending blades are actually bending flanges on the panel bender; when the ram is actually moving on the press brake; when the tools are stroking on the punch press; and when the cutting head is moving on the laser.

This in turn spurs different strategies to take within the other two software platforms in the trifecta: production control and programming. For instance, does it make sense to nest multiple jobs together or fill a sheet with just a few parts? The thinking goes that batching would increase WIP and, therefore, slow production. But by how much? Compare that with nesting multiple jobs in one sheet. This might increase material yield, but it also could increase part sorting complexity and lead to lost or damaged parts.

IIoT technology also can reveal similar jobs that could be pulled into specific value streams or share common job routings. For instance, a laser could supply a carefully managed parts supermarket that supplies downstream operations, some of which are segregated into their own value streams that—again, thanks to the panel bender’s zero changeover capability—process a variety of kits, with nearly zero WIP between work centers.

Forming at the Heart of it All

When you analyze value streams and job routings in this industry, you soon realize that, for any precision sheet metal part, planning starts in bending. The method and tooling used—be it a panel bender or press brake—in many ways governs what happens up and downstream.

The bend process determines the blank size in laser cutting, but the forming process’s precision and flexibility also affect what can be achieved downstream. As described previously, just moving a bend line slightly can change a welding process and greatly simplify the entire manufacturing process.

What nesting strategy at the laser is best for process stability? When should orders be released? What is the optimal part flow—large-batch, small-batch, or kit-based (single-piece) flow? A high-product-mix sheet metal operation can be extraordinarily complex. But software, combined with some creative thinking, can manage the minutiae and, at the same time, reveal new ways to optimize the whole.
