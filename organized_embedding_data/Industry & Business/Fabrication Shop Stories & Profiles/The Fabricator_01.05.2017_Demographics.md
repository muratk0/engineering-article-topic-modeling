Quick turns, positive cash flow, and a sustainable business

Bill Hewgley President, Metalworking Solutions

Chattanooga, Tenn

.

Bill Hewgley

had recently turned 55 in 2006 when he launched his custom fabricator, Metalworking Solutions, after a career in retail display manufacturing. He worked among the office fiefdoms. He also endured private equity buyouts, economic recessions, layoffs, and rehiring. He spent decades riding the unpredictable roller coaster ride of corporate America.

It’s little wonder why Hewgley now likes to keep things simple, and it’s apparent immediately after you walk through Metalworking Solutions’ door. The few people in the front office wear multiple hats. And walk out to the manufacturing floor, you’ll see a shop full of modern machinery—including a punch system that retrieves and stacks parts automatically, and an automatic tool change press brake—but not many people and not much inventory.

Most raw stock inventory is bound in metal straps, held on consignment. Twice a month the material supplier visits the shop to bill for what the shop has used. Yet on average, jobs require only 18 days to make it from one end of the shop to the other, and many require even less time. Sometimes the company ships so quickly that it receives payment for the job

before

it has to pay for its material.

It’s not unusual for a custom fabricator to turn its inventory about 12 times year, maybe more or less depending on the nature of the work. Metalworking Solutions turns its inventory more than 70 times a year.

In his initial business plan, Hewgley described how his company would be the FedEx of custom precision metal fabrication. "We basically use technology and lean processes to offer rapid response," Hewgley said. "And I’ve invested in equipment ahead of when we’ll need it."

This quick-turnaround ability has helped Hewgley and his team sell the concept to various companies, which in turn led to a well-diversified customer base. No one customer makes up more than 10 percent of the shop’s business, which mitigates the feast-or-famine nature of custom fabrication.

Hewgley and his team aren’t afraid to turn down business, either. He recalled one large customer that visited his plant and was so impressed by the company’s automated operations and quick-turn capabilities, it offered the shop a large contract. Hewgley said the contract really wasn’t a good fit.

It was a truly massive amount of work, one that would have required investing in more people and equipment. But as the shop size grows, so does its complexity. In fact, Hewgley doesn’t dream of scaling up to become a massive operation in a single facility. His current operation employs about 60 people.

Instead, he envisions opening other regional custom fabrication centers, each operating independently yet also adhering to the Metalworking Solutions way of doing business. Keep each small, and turn inventory like there’s no tomorrow, and you have a business model with stellar cash flow, ready to absorb the ups and downs.

Indeed, some fabricators fall on hard times not in the depths of a recession but during the upturn, as they hire, invest, and try to deliver without running out of cash. The people at Metalworking Solutions don’t worry about that, considering the stellar cash flow the company generates turning inventory more than 70 times a year.
