# Good data needs a two-way street

[TARİH: 01.07.2026 The Fabricator]

Biz Talk

And why a one-way street leads to apathy

A

ttending a software event in May, a video animation struck me. I was in Nashville, Tenn., at Epicor Insights, the ERP company’s annual customer meeting. The video, broadcast on an expansive screen behind the main stage, showed Earth with glowing lines crisscrossing the planet, depicting the digital threads connecting global commerce.

From there, the video zoomed in fast—to continents, countries, communities, a manufacturing plant, and finally an individual worker operating a machine. The message: Every move a business makes—a single sales call, a shift in the production schedule—can be put in a global context. Optimal agility helps win the game.

That video animation stuck with me when I was talking with Jim Belosic at Reno, Nev.-based SendCutSend, a custom fabricator that allows customers to upload files on its website and receive an instant quote. The company plays in the same sandbox as Utah-based OSH Cut and California-based FabWorks. OSH Cut’s Caleb Chamberlain and FabWorks’ Jonathan Sessa are both featured in June’s Next-Gen Metal Fab podcast.

At SendCutSend, Belosic said the company would be

tripling

the size of its data team soon. Yes, the fabricator has a "data team," which works with its proprietary software that, in essence, handles everything from quote to cash. The more I talk with people like Belosic, Chamberlain, and Sessa, the more I think about metal fabrication not as a

manufacturing

business, but as an

information

business.

The act of "making things" still is at the heart of it all. "I just love making parts," Belosic told me, and I could tell he was earnest. Manufacturing isn’t the easiest way to make money, after all. Having a passion for the act of cutting, bending, and welding metal makes it all worthwhile.

But these days, it’s not the speed of machinery that’s the manufacturing constraint; it’s the

information

fabricators have and how they use it. The information could be from preventive or predictive maintenance systems that keep machines running reliably. Or it could be a dynamic scheduling system that keeps constraint processes maxed out (maximizing throughput and dollars shipped out the door) but

doesn’t

throw work at every machine that happens to have capacity (leading to excess work-in-process).

In essence, the right information allows everyone to know exactly what to work on next: the

next

job on the shop floor, the

next

customer communication, the

next

cost estimate, the

next

sales call. The decision might be pushed to the front lines, not dictated by some overarching system. Still, the people making those decisions aren’t acting blindly—at least if they have the right information.

That information comes from both internal customers (upstream and downstream operations in the plant) and external customers (in lean manufacturing, "external customer" refers to any external entity that touches a business: suppliers, vendors, and customers).

Finally, as you zoom in—like that video at Epicor Insights—you get to the skilled worker, the machine, and the unlocking of tribal knowledge.

"I recall one plant manager we worked with who held so much tribal knowledge. When we went home at night, he’d get calls about fixing a problem. When he went home at night, he took his knowledge with him."

That was Andrew Robling, principal product marketing manager for manufacturing at Epicor. He made that comment during an interview at the Insights event, explaining that in this case, documenting tribal knowledge— through guided instructions on a phone, tablet, or other device—made a world of difference. Over time, as more instructions were documented, the system intelligence grew, and the guided instructions became more customized.

stevanovicigor/iStock/Getty Images Plus

Such instruction isn’t limited to words and pictures either. Robling added that video animation could help, along with all sorts of visual cues to help an operator through a problem.

Even so, the flow of information—including tribal knowledge—should never be a one-way street. Belosic’s approach at SendCutSend is a case in point. The "digital first" fabricator has mountains of data. Customers upload files, orders are processed automatically, schedules are automatic and dynamic. In some sense, data really does run everything. But in another sense, it really doesn’t.

Belosic described the company’s "train driver," the plant water spider (to use a lean manufacturing term) who delivers products with a vehicle that looks like a luggage train used on airport tarmacs. The driver stops, converses with operators, asks about their day, about any challenges they might be having, and what ideas they have. The driver (and everyone else throughout the day) shares that knowledge with others, including the data team, which in turn helps incorporate that intelligence into the organization’s proprietary, overarching software systems. That virtuous cycle of feedback shapes the company’s core strengths.

It also feeds the company’s in-house training, both for new and existing employees. The last thing Belosic wants is a bunch of disengaged button-pushers. Learning should never stop. When everyone goes on autopilot, a manufacturer (or any company, for that matter) falls behind.

Now, marry that virtuous cycle that with broader "external" intelligence from customers, suppliers, machine tool vendors, and other industry partners. Next, zoom out farther to broader market intelligence, to unemployment figures, interest rate expectations, weather conditions, food harvests, trends in RV travel, demand for off-road recreational trucking, new plant openings, old plant closings, population concentrations, where people with the right skills live—the list is endless. This is where I see AI really taking root, connecting the dots, and revealing patterns humans couldn’t decipher on their own.

Still, in the center of this AI- and data-driven whirlwind sit us humans: how we relate and work together and, most important of all, how we learn from one another. The long-term outlook won’t be pretty if the flow of information from AI becomes a one-way street. The same applies to businesses led by those who ignore the data revolution, reinvest as little as possible, keep knowledge tribal, don’t rock the boat, and do their best to keep things just as they are.

Companies that use fabricated metal now have valuations far exceeding $1 trillion. Let that sink in for a bit. The world’s changing. Those who successfully change with it could be in for quite a ride.
