Why you should care about economic data

Dr. Chris Kuehl

… but don’t overreact

So what?

I get this question often. I suppose it is the curse of the economist.

This prompts me to relate a story of a client. There was a senior VP who asked that very question:

So what?

What did the material mean and what was he supposed to do with this knowledge? What impact would this have on his assignments, his division, and the company as a whole?

It gets exponentially more challenging when looking at a membership as diverse as that within the Fabricators and Manufacturers Association. I dump a great deal of information on you in these pages. What exactly are you supposed to do with all this?

The answer varies according to how relevant the given information is to your industry. If you feed into the aerospace sector, you have little interest in the automotive sector. I offer these three quick guides to using the mounds of info we throw at you.

1. Separate immediate impact from the long-term impact

. Things that are happening right now leave you with few options. All you can do is react to them. Some are positive (like a new order) and others are negative (natural disaster or sudden economic crisis). You want to know about these so you can decide whether to implement your contingency plan. If you don’t have some of these plans ready, get them.

2. The long-term impact should interest you the most

. This means you have time to set strategy. Most of the data we provide is aimed at these strategic decisions. What will an interest rate change mean to you? What trends are worth watching—supply chain, workforce, inflation, consumer behavior? Some material I cover focuses on what happens as far out as a year.

3. Think of the connections

. You know your own company and industry like the back of your hand. What is less familiar are those outside events that could affect your company. If the supply chain crisis jacks prices up, how will your sector react and what will that mean to you and your customers?

If politics affects trade with China or Europe or any other nation, what does that mean to your suppliers and your consumers?

What is the difference between noise and reality? The media tends to over-dramatize, and that is especially the case when the subject is economics. It seems that all that data strikes people as boring, and it is the mission of the media commentators to make it more exciting. This can very often be misleading.

The Mid-Summer Sell-Off

A case in point was the mid-summer frenzy in the markets that had people asserting a recession was certain and imminent. What was really going on? There were three triggers, and all tended to be somewhat misread.

The first issue was a less-than-robust jobs report from the U.S. Unemployment rose slightly, from 3.9% to 4.3%. That said, many failed to put the employment data in context. The Bureau of Labor Statistics has six cuts of the data, and U-3 is the simplest as it just counts the people on unemployment or officially in the system. The U-6 reading captures the "discouraged worker" and the "involuntary part-time" worker. As these people decide to reenter the formal system to seek a job, they shift from U-6 to U-3. Workforce participation levels actually went up.

The second trigger was a bit more obscure. The yen carry-trade process has altered significantly, and that has triggered some investor panic. The process is complex, but essentially it means that investors borrow money in a country with very low interest rates so that they can invest in another nation where rates are higher. The Japanese have had extremely low rates even as other nations have been hiking them. They were still at zero. Then the Bank of Japan decided that inflation was an issue and started to hike rates. They also wanted to bolster the weak yen. That put all those investment schemes in jeopardy, and many were forced to start selling assets. It will take a while for that dust to settle, but the impact has been minimal.

The last trigger was the big tech sell off. This has been expected for months as most agreed that these stocks had been overvalued. Some asserted this was AI’s "Segway moment." Remember when the Segway was going to change the world? Now it belongs to the mall cops. Has AI been overhyped? Investors seemed to think so, at least for the moment, and they elected to bail out of these stocks for a while. By the end of that crazy week, most of the investors had started to come back.

Industrial Capacity Shows Some Slack

The industrial capacity utilization numbers have been very steady—or so it would appear. There is actually more volatility than one would expect given the data. Manufacturers (or any business) have slack capacity for two fundamental reasons.

First, demand has fallen off and production has slowed in response. That is usually the motivation for lower capacity utilization numbers, but there is another motivation. As companies move to adopt new technology, add machines, or invest in robotics, there will be a break-in period as the company determines how to integrate these acquisitions. That can take as long as 12 months for maximum productivity to occur. There has been a great deal of investment in these new technologies in response to the worker shortage, and that means that capacity numbers are perhaps lower than they will be once these machines are maximized.

The PMI Shows Contraction

The Purchasing Managers’ Index (PMI) data can be a bit confusing at times. To begin with, there are several versions of this survey out there. The best known are the ones from the Institute for Supply Management and from the S&P 500. The one from ISM tends to get more data from smaller companies while the S&P version focuses on larger operations. We look at the S&P version. The other distinction is between the overall index and the new orders index. Now the overall index is showing the U.S. entering contraction territory again with numbers in the 40s. Unfortunately, the new orders numbers are even less robust, and the U.S. fell into contraction again in late summer.

This is the more future-oriented aspect of the study and suggests that many manufacturers are a bit more cautious about the future. New orders are an indication of expected business as the purchasing managers are asked to make inventory-build decisions. In many ways, this data is more important than the standard PMI data.

Capital Spending Trends

Capital spending has seen its share of ups and downs, but over the last year it has been sitting at record levels as business has reacted to various challenges. The most obvious has been the labor shortage. If a company can’t find the trained and educated personnel needed, it will have to invest more heavily in robotics and technology. Even the smaller companies are turning to this option.

A second motivation is also somewhat labor related. If a company in the U.S. wants to compete with companies that are in low-labor-cost environments, they have to seek a technical advantage. In the last few years there has been a great deal more investment in tech in the service sector as well. It doesn’t match the level in manufacturing or even construction, but it is growing quickly.

Threats to Watch

If we are to ignore the hysterics of recent months, what should we be paying attention to? There are three threats that bear watching. The first is the supply chain as there has been a major hike in container prices—over 300%. This is due to the continued threats to shipping in the Red Sea. This is not going away anytime soon.

Threat No. 2 is connected to inflation reaction. The rate of inflation has declined, but the prices are still high, and that has triggered consumers to start spending less, especially those in the lower- and middle-income categories.

The last threat comes as the U.S. gets more aggressive on trade. In the long term this may mean more domestic production, but in the short term it means import prices will be up.

The Danger in Overreacting

The bottom line for all this is that overreaction can present a major problem. This becomes even more of an issue in an election year as each candidate resorts to scare tactics to convince the voter. Every blip becomes a major deal, but many are simply a blip.

The underlying data in the economy remains positive—solid GDP growth expected in Q3 after a 2.8% pace in Q2, unemployment still at historic lows, retreating pace of inflation despite wage gains, solid retail numbers as the holiday season approaches. There are certainly still factors to create concern, at least at this writing (in early September), but an imminent recession appears very unlikely.
