# Making the most of hardware insertion activities

[TARİH: 01.04.2026 The Fabricator]

Like most shops, LJ Fabricators looks to optimize its fabricating activities, which also includes the act of installing hardware

By

Dan Davis

Greg Johnson, president of LJ Fabricators, shows how graphics guide an operator through the hardware insertion process.

After decades of consistent capital equipment investments, LJ Fabricators is focusing on the processes that can help the company get the most out of its metal fabricating technology.

"

W

hen we’re purchasing capital equipment, we’re doing it to get better—like with our first fiber laser. We didn’t have a capacity issue. We just wanted to be a part of the fiber world," said Greg Johnson, president of LJ Fabricators Inc. in Rockford, Ill.

"There’s a point where I think if you stop investing in new equipment, you get left behind. At some level, you don’t want to get to the point where you have to spend $8 million just to catch up. It’s almost impossible."

Johnson made this comment as he discussed some of the major capital equipment investments made over the last decade, including a 15-kW Bystronic fiber laser and a Schroeder metal folding machine from Metalforming USA. He estimated that the company has spent an average of $500,000 each year over that time trying to stay current and optimize flow on the shop floor.

It’s quite the evolution for the job shop, started by Johnson’s dad in 1983. He opened his doors with only a notcher, shear and a single-punch duplicator. Six years later, he purchased his first laser cutting machine, and LJ Fabricators soon learned what advanced metal fabricating technology can do for a shop.

"We’re now at the point in our company history where we have a nice book of technologies, but now it’s time to fine-tune what we do," Johnson added.

A LOOK AT HARDWARE INSERTION

Hardware insertion is an interesting place to start to see how a shop operates. Most shops do it. Some do it more, while some aren’t too involved with the process. A lot has to do with the current slate of customers and what they are looking for from their metal fabricating service suppliers.

LJ Fabricators began modernizing its hardware insertion activities in 2017. The company purchased a Haeger 824 OneTouch Lite hardware insertion machine to complement an older machine with a CNC.

Johnson explained that the machine has a turret design that accommodates four different tooling setups, rotating when needed. It features two automatic hardware bowl feeders and two manual-feed stations.

"A lot of times, you have hardware-intensive parts with three to four types of hardware pieces. Some of them might be heavy with one or two, but not all four," Johnson explained.

The real advantage is the four tooling stations in the lower tool changer, he added. Machine operators can handle parts that require up to four hardware pieces in just one machine setup. They avoid all the headaches that come with working with a single-station machine, such as keeping track of work-in-process and taking time to switch out tooling.

The machine also is quick, with one insertion taking place every 2.5 seconds and switchover from one tool to another in 3 seconds, the manufacturer estimates.

Johnson said that LJ Fabricators considered purchasing a machine that could be set up with four automatic bowl feeders, but the design had a smaller throat area. He estimated that the Haeger 824 has a 24-in. throat.

The company purchased a similar hardware insertion machine in 2019 because it was pleased with the first machine’s performance. The second unit also provided some redundancy in case the first machine happened to be down.

OPTIMIZED HARDWARE INSERTION

Just having the right equipment doesn’t make a manufacturing process efficient. People have to be trained and put in a position to make the most of the process. For LJ Fabricators, that starts with the job router.

The router provides the hardware insertion machine operator with the information needed for the job: tooling setup, material type, and hardware. Johnson said this is a long way from the early days of the shop, when the experienced craftsmen got a copy of the print that documented only a job number, quantity, and due date. The shear and punch operators could look at the print and know what needed to be done. The router now provides a lot of guidance, including where the hardware can be found.

At this point, it should be noted that both hardware insertion machines are placed strategically in the LJ Fabricators’ facility. All material moves north to south, with sheet metal being delivered right where the laser cutting machines and punching machine are and hardware insertion nearby. Once the hardware has been put in place, parts can flow to forming or welding.

Located near the hardware insertion machines is everything the operators need, which helps them avoid excessive time away from their tasks. Hardware inventory is located in a small turnstile storage system near the insertion machines. A machine operator with the appropriate part number for hardware can quickly find what’s needed and grab the right amount to complete the job. In a cabinet close to the turnstile is the tooling, including bowl feeding sets.

While the router provides an overview of the hardware insertion job, the machine control provides the step-by-step instructions. With the ability to watch the job sequencing on the color monitor attached to the machine, the operator can follow color-coded steps showing where each insertion should go on the workpiece. The color-coded steps also match up with the color of the tooling station that is needed to complete that hardware insertion.

The machine also provides a laser target to help the operator line up the workpiece so that upper tooling strikes where needed. This is a long way from the days of relying on the operator’s judgment in positioning a workpiece before inserting the hardware.

"This type of technology does allow a company to be more competitive and to use less-experienced operators in front of the machine," Johnson said.

Located a few steps away from the hardware insertion machines, a turnstile cabinet holds all of the company’s hardware inventory.

LJ Fabricators has cross-trained numerous employees to work the Haeger machines, giving the company flexibility when demand for hardware insertion services spikes. But even if a regular operator is not available to run the machines, Johnson said, a supervisor needs only about an hour to get someone new trained to complete a job.

KEEPING TRACK OF HARDWARE

Of course, hardware insertion only works when the hardware is available to be inserted. That’s why LJ Fabricators has taken steps to ensure that hardware bins in the turnstile cabinet always have appropriate inventory levels.

"We run a production planning report that shows demand for hardware. We can look at it and see demand, putting this particular hardware item in the red, or in the negative, so we need to order," Johnson said. If the process is working as intended, the hardware insertion machine operator shouldn’t have to worry about a lack of hardware before a job.

Inventory counts are maintained with the help of the actual operators, who have to enter the hardware amounts used to complete the job into the company’s ERP software. If the quantities are not provided, the company’s business activity software sends an email to a supervisor stating that no hardware was assigned to a job even though the job has been closed.

This notification software looks at any sort of database and can be programmed to send emails indicating when certain activities linked to the database do or do not take place.

"The one thing that we have really found is that to do something like this, you need someone who knows how to write SQL views," Johnson said. That skillset is useful in creating SQL views that can dynamically retrieve data from underlying base tables every time they are queried, acting as sort of windows into the data.

READY FOR WORK

Some of the more interesting hardware insertion inquiries LJ Fabricators receives come from companies that just don’t want to deal with it.

For example, the shop had a customer that was actually inserting hardware with a press brake. The customer developed a fixture to hold the hardware and the workpiece, found flat punching dies, and used the ram to push the hardware into place. The problem with that approach is that a brake operator really couldn’t be sure the force and time under pressure were correct for proper hardware clinching. With that knowledge, the customer approached LJ Fabricators to do the job instead.

Shops that have the right hardware insertion equipment, skilled operators, and processes are more than willing to help.
