# Getting paid

[TARİH: 01.01.2014 The Fabricator]

FROM THE EDITOR-IN-CHIEF

Don’t assume that a busy shop is flush with cash

Dan Davis, editor-in-chief of

The FABRICATOR

, can be reached at

dand@thefabricator.com

.

When should a metal fabricator consider a job complete?

a. When it is picked up for delivery to the customer.

b. When it arrives at the customer’s receiving dock.

c. When the customer’s payment is deposited into the fabricator’s account.

The only real answer is “c,” although many fabricating operations think otherwise. Of course, they think wrong.

Most involved in this industry know of a shop that appeared successful, but ultimately had to close the doors. The parking lot was full of workers’ vehicles. The trucks regularly dropped off raw materials. Delivery trucks left at the same time each day taking parts to customers. All looked right—right until the doors were locked one last time, never to open again.

Businesses may be busy, but they may not be profitable. That’s something every metal fabricating company should be cognizant of.

Metal fabricators typically can attack maximizing cash flow several ways, but the two that jump out are working with a bank to establish a line of credit and shortening job turnaround times.

I chatted with a fabricator in early December who stressed the fact that they remind employees that a job isn’t complete until they get paid. Quick turnarounds and on-time deliveries are stressed, but it all boils down to getting paid for the work that was done. Fortunately, the fabricator’s work and its response time are incredibly valued. In this case, customers are deeply satisfied and well-established, so prompt payment is not an issue. Cash rules—and keeps the doors open for another day.

Gerald Davis, the author of

The

FABRICATOR’s Precision Matters column, wrote about this very subject 10 years ago. In “Wise words from the mountaintop—Part 2” (February 2004, p. 42), his business guru atop the mountain had to stress to the inquisitive Davis that cash flow should be his business’s highest priority. Not customer satisfaction. Not growth. Not profit.

“Strategically, you must see your business as an economic entity, not simply as a manufacturing company. You have policies and procedures for every process in your manufacturing plant, but your policies and procedures for cash flow are informal at best and reactionary at worst. Your customers trust you and mightily rely on you. The greatest service you provide your customers is to manage your business expertly. Business failure because you cannot manage your cash flow does them no good at all,” the wise man said.

Metal fabricators typically can attack maximizing cash flow several ways, but the two that jump out are working with a bank to establish a line of credit and shortening job turnaround times. The line of credit is a great resource for those shops that can make that arrangement, but sometimes banks aren’t in a giving mood. That makes the latter scenario—getting jobs out as quickly as possible—that much more of a production requirement.

The fabricator mentioned earlier continually works on improving production efficiency. It is trying to get to the point where it can turn the work orders into shipped goods in about three days, down from seven days only a couple of years ago and from more than a week before that. That type of improvement means lead-times can be reduced, and the fabricator’s customers become used to service that they might not be able to find easily anywhere else.

The fabricator improves its order-to-shipping cycle. The customer issues a payment sooner than it might otherwise because the product was delivered in a matter of days, and it wants to reward the supplier for its positive behavior. The customer recognizes the fabricator’s efforts and increasingly relies on the company for more work. The fabricator has successfully closed its order-to-cash cycle while simultaneously earning more work from the existing customer base. That’s a position every shop wants to be in.

In the end, a fabricator doesn’t directly control when it gets paid, but it can put itself in a favorable light. It can respond to customers’ orders quickly with quality products and do so on a consistent basis. It eventually emerges as an indispensable link in its customers’ supply chains.

The Fabricators & Manufacturers Association’s

2013 Financials Ratios & Operational Benchmarking Survey

(“How top performers get it done,” Biz Talk,

The FABRICATOR

, October 2013, p. 58) reveals that fabricators are doing well in managing debt and generating the cash necessary to maintain day-to-day operations while also socking money away for future investments. They have learned a very important lesson: If the fabricator shows its best, it’ll be shown the money.
