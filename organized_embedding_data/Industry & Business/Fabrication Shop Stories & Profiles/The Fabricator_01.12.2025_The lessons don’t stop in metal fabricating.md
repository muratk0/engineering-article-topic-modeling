# The lessons don’t stop in metal fabricating

[TARİH: 01.12.2025 The Fabricator]

Shop Management

How one shop moved from making parts for one client to becoming a lean custom fabricating machine

By

Dan Davis

TMS has a ton of experience working with aluminum, a key part of many tradeshow projects it worked on over the years.

I

f the U.S. Navy prepared Duane Swift for anything, it was to know a little bit about a lot of things. After 30 years, that was a lot of knowledge.

Swift served from 1963 to 1993, first learning about diesel engine operation and maintenance and later gaining insight on welding, brazing, air-conditioning, and refrigeration, not to mention pretty much all aspects about life on board a submarine. In his later years, he was involved in project management, overseeing operations that served the needs of thousands of sailors. By the end of his 30-year career, he had gone from working in engine rooms to walking the halls of the Pentagon as an E9 Master Chief Petty Officer—the highest enlisted rank in the Navy.

So when he entered civilian life, it’s not surprising that he ended up finding a home and success in the chaotic life that is metal fabricating. He got connected with a Chicago-area company that designed tradeshow exhibits and ultimately became its one-man fab shop.

A huge contract to provide a majority of the components for bank kiosks inside of a large chain of Chicagoland grocery stores in the late 1990s changed the nature of his fabricating life. For instance, one of the components for the kiosks was a teller screen that required a lot of holes. That led to the purchase of a Strippit 3000, one of the first major machines Swift acquired. Also around this time, his son, Todd, joined him, assisting with shipping of these fabricated components to stores nationwide, the result of an ever-expanding project.

While Todd also did a stint in the U.S. Navy like his dad and brother, he said that he did not get the same "fab genes" that they got. But like his dad, he could tackle projects and had an interest in new technology, something that was evident during his short-lived career as a photographer in the late 1990s and early 2000s, when he was an early adopter of digital camera technology.

Todd’s photography career coincided with a winding down of the bank project. Meanwhile, Duane opened his own place, The Metal Shop (TMS) in 2002.

In the ensuing years, TMS added two machining centers, a press brake, and seven welding tables. Todd’s wife, Yi, who had an engineering degree, went to work for her father-in-law and became the go-to person for programming the machining centers and running the programs. Because TMS was pumping out a lot of 6- by 6-in. tubing for exhibit projects, it was using two Fadal 4020s for the machining work. It eventually bought an extra-long milling machine to process the tubing more efficiently.

As the exhibit work kept growing, TMS looked for a way to help itself with the "crazy" lead times, Todd said. That led to the purchase of a waterjet and a used laser cutting machine—an immediate improvement over the routing machine TMS had been using for the aluminum work. It also helped the company meet ever-shortening lead times while improving productivity and quality as well.

Two Noteworthy Observations

By 2007, when Todd joined his father again at TMS after a stint selling race car tires, the shop had evolved into an enterprise that made customers happy and the bank nervous. Its main customer, like others in the industry, treated TMS as an extension of its own company. When orders were made, turnaround was expected in a matter of days, typically three. Todd said that type of response time was baked into the crew that worked at TMS at that time, and they typically made things work.

Of course, that was great for customers, including the few nonexhibition-related companies that TMS did work for at the time, but it didn’t allow for flexibility in production scheduling. It also created stress for a team already tasked with taking customers’ designs and turning them into reality.

The bank was stressed as well.

"The bank would look at us and go, ‘You have too many eggs in one basket,’" recalled Todd, now a principal of TMS. "I would say, ‘I know, but this is how the business is right now.’"

That dynamic changed during the Great Recession. Orders still came for exhibition-related work, but payments didn’t. Those days motivated TMS to start planting the seeds for diversification efforts.

That push was exacerbated when the longtime customer got new leadership and decided to start quoting work instead of dealing directly with TMS. By 2014, Todd said TMS knew it had to assume the role of a real job shop, opening itself up to work from those looking for metal fabricating expertise.

Lessons Learned

This new approach was going to require a change in thinking for TMS. It meant a greater commitment to quality and having to learn the business side of job shop work.

"When we did the exhibition work, you could be less conscious of time spent on a job. You were able to charge for the time you worked," Todd said. "Once we went to standard quoting for jobs, then we had to learn to become more effective and hit the quoted times. This at times was difficult because the overriding business driver had always been ‘get the job done’ no matter the cost. Just don’t be late."

TMS quickly learned that a shop doesn’t stay in business with unnecessary overtime.

Today’s TMS—a job shop with fewer than 10 employees and annual sales that have reached as much as $4 million in recent years—is in a much better place for having learned that lesson and several others.

Knowing How to Build In Profit

Todd credited the Fabricators and Manufacturers Association’s Financial Ratios and Operational Benchmarking Survey, now known as the Metal Fab KPIs Survey, with giving him an idea of just how he should be adjusting his quotes to ensure TMS was making a profit. Because the industry’s players tend to keep to themselves, a metal fabricating company owner or manager might be leaning on instincts and tradition to figure out what to charge people.

The FMA survey that Todd referenced indicated that margins for fab shops the size of TMS fell within 5% to 7%. That, of course, can fluctuate depending on competition in the area or a company’s ability to do something that others simply can’t.

While some might think that markup and margin mean the same thing, they don’t. Markup is the amount added to the cost of a product to derive the sales price. The margin is the profit that remains from the sales price once the cost of goods is subtracted. Knowing the margin is consistently where it should be enables a shop to keep track of its financial health and profitability.

"We learned this from a very experienced businessperson from a very large company that we hired during Covid. He transformed the thinking and got TMS to change from ‘get the job done and hope we made money’ to ‘get the job done and make money!’" Todd said.

The other thing helping TMS to stay profitable is saying no to jobs where a profit simply isn’t feasible. Todd called it minimizing the 20% of the business that causes 80% of the headaches and the drain on everyone’s energy.

He admitted that it’s hard to do, especially when a fab shop is all about pleasing customers. But that can be a detriment if the shop gets caught up in small jobs that have no real chance of growing into something larger and no room for profit-making.

"We’re trying to transition out of the minimal stuff," he said.

Investing in Automation When Feasible

Coming out of the pandemic, TMS was down to five people. Fortunately, a major contract from a garage door manufacturer helped keep the shop going.

The part, still being fabricated today, has been joined using a robotic wedling cell since 2011. That helped to keep TMS afloat during the first year of the pandemic.

Though not a machine shop, TMS also invested in a turning center to produce the shafts to create a self-contained cell. Todd said that the gains in production efficiency with the new capabilities made sense.

"It’s still being used to do that same job today," Todd said. "In fact, they’ve became one of our biggest clients."

Don’t Stop the Automation Push

About two years ago, TMS purchased a welding cell with a cobot and a gas metal arc welding power source. The main reason was another high-volume part, an aluminum frame for the door of a dog kennel. TMS takes the aluminum extrusion, machines it, bends it into shape, welds it together, applies a logo plate, and sends it off to be painted.

TMS was doing thousands of these door frames, so some sort of automation made sense. However, it was a hard sell for the welders, who were convinced the wire-fed welds weren’t going to be as strong as the six gas tungsten arc stitch welds that were to be replaced.

"So we sent the part out to Milwaukee for nondestructive testing," Todd recalled. "What was the difference? Not a lick of difference. As long as the settings are right during the MIG welding, it’s going to be just as strong as the TIG weld."

When part production switched entirely to the cobot cell, it was hard not to see the productivity improvements. A recent team meeting revealed the fact behind the observation: The cobot was doing the welding job in almost half the time that a manual welder could do it.

"It’s continually getting better, and we’re chasing even more improvements," Todd said.

Look Beyond the Shop Floor

Getting metal fabricators to take on continuous improvement projects isn’t hard. If done right, they can make life easier for the people involved in a process and open the door to increases in productivity and quality performance.

Shops often fail to take that same critical eye to the front office, but that is changing as young people expect to have the same digital experience when shopping for metal fabricating services as they do when they order toilet paper from Amazon. They want it to be a convenient and reliable process.

That’s why Todd brought on a young person as head of IT. One of the first things he did was relaunch the website with plenty of pictures, providing a visual clue as to the shop’s many capabilities. Also on the website, located at the top and to the left, is a quote button. It’s a small but notable change, Todd said.

The TMS IT director was able to use an inline frame to take information from the Paperless Parts quoting platform and create an online form for the website. The contact information that the visitor plugs into the form goes directly into the quoting database so that it doesn’t have to be retyped. The form also asks for the delivery date and project details, while including a function for uploading part files. If the visitor has a question, the website has a chat bot function to offer quick answers.

Todd said the next step is to automate the quoting function for simple parts, say a laser-cut part with one or two bends. That gets the shop closer to recreating an Amazon-type experience but also lets window shoppers get an estimate for a part without tying up someone on the phone.

Smart Investments Come in All Sizes

This summer, TMS installed a Keyence laser marking machine and a Keyence 3D laser coordinate measuring machine. These small devices will be used to enlarge the company’s reputation.

Todd said the laser marking machine allows TMS to keep another secondary process in-house. For instance, the logo nameplate that goes on the dog kennel door would likely have been outsourced in the past, but now TMS can simply program the machine to mark several laser-cut blanks. By not outsourcing the task, the company has reduced turnaround time and the risk of quality problems. TMS also can use the laser marking machine to produce business cards and personalized giveaways such as pens and dog tags.

The CMM, purchased with a large table, is used for quality checks, even if the customer doesn’t specify that action. Todd said TMS uses it for first and finished part measuring to ensure parts match specifications on the print. In the case of higher volumes, every 20th or 50th part can be pulled for a quality check.

"I’d like to include a little card that says something like, ‘Thank you for your order. It’s been checked for quality by whomever,’" said Todd, comparing the action to notes craftsmen include with high-end handcrafted objects.

Looking to the Future

Metal fabricating, in general, is a complex craft. Making metal do what it doesn’t want to do takes skill and creativity. Proper schooling and training can provide that foundation from which a metal fabricator can launch a career.

The business of metal fabricating is another story. That’s something that’s hard to learn in a classroom. Typically, the best lessons are learned through experiences.

Learning is a lifelong process. TMS is on that journey, as so many other shops are. There’s no real map, but you have to keep moving forward. Even the small steps will get you to where you need to go.
