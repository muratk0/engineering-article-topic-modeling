# Robinson’s workforce, revenue, and fabrication space skyrocket

T

hat DePere, Wis.-based fabricator Robinson Inc. has mushroomed from 300 employees to more than 900 in the past seven years is remarkable in its own right. But when its leadership attributes that increase to the same COVID pandemic that decimated so many of its peers, it becomes something different entirely.

Call it good fortune. Call it serendipitous. What you can’t call it is accidental.

Robinson is primarily a supplier of enclosures, fuel tanks, and platforms for the energy sector, which represents 90% of Robinson’s business. If you’ve ever seen one of the giant diesel motors outside a hospital or school or stadium either generating electricity or standing ready to provide backup power, there’s a better-thaneven chance that Robinson fabricated the steel surrounding it.

PRE-PARING TO GROW DURING COVID

Today, Robinson operates as one contiguous unit, but as recently as 2019, the company operated as six divisions: custom fabrication (the real source of the company’s growth), pipe and vessel, custom enclosures, product handling concepts (which created automated material handling systems), heating and cooling, and gas trailers. Every division had its own sales team, estimators, and project managers—representing a lot of duplication of effort.

Robinson President Sam Thomas and his team recognized that the company really does one thing. Doing many different things only detracted from the main thing. So they pared down—way down.

"At the end of the day, what Robinson did and still does is bring in raw material—mostly steel—and we put value into it and we create something," Thomas said. "We don’t need to have all of these divisions. So we ripped that apart, and we became one Robinson and moved to one structure—one sales team, one project management group. And that really put us in a position where we could start to cross-train. I think it really helped change the culture, where we didn’t have these siloed divisions, and it really allowed us to come together as one team."

That new mindset came just in time to help the company cope with the havoc caused by COVID mandates and the shutdowns that hit other businesses. (Robinson, considered an essential business, was allowed to continue operating.)

In fact, the pandemic so injurious to other companies actually turned out to be a blessing to Robinson and its employees.

"If COVID hadn’t happened, Robinson wouldn’t be where it is," Thomas said bluntly. "The reason for that, at the time, with the industries we were in and how we were looking at the business, we needed capacity. We needed to hire a lot of people.

"We took an approach that we weren’t going to give COVID energy," he added. "We wanted to create kind of a safe space for people to come where they didn’t have to watch the news. We did all the things you had to as far as the masking and all the mandatory stuff, but we didn’t give it a ton of energy. I think that really helped us. We didn’t get distracted. We stayed focused."

People suddenly unemployed heard by word of mouth that Robinson was a good place to work—and was hiring. Many of them were in the service business: waiters, dishwashers, bartenders. Solid workers, but not much manufacturing background.

"We actually hired a lot of really, really good people, but they didn’t have any experience," Thomas said. "As we were putting all of these people on the floor week after week through orientations, it was loading up our coordinators and supervisors on the floor where they were spending a lot of time training. We needed to find a way to kind of fast-track that and have a more organized way of onboarding people."

TRAINING IS KEY

That push to streamline that onboarding process led to the creation of the Robinson Training Academy (RTA) in early 2024.

Walk into the RTA building at the company’s DePere 1 facility, and you’re suddenly inside something akin to a tech college lab. Here, five full-time instructors—two for welding, two for assembly, and one for electrical work—train the newbies for 12 weeks, attuning them to Robinson’s way of doing all three disciplines. All the instructors came from within Robinson, so trainees get onboarded by people familiar with the company’s M.O.

The atmosphere in the building during FMA’s tour was focused but not frenetic. But don’t let the laid-back feel fool you: The program is intense. For example, in the three weeks that trainees spend on welding, they gain proficiency in five different MIG methods. The program recently added a plasma cutting table, and the company is looking at ways to integrate some forming equipment to train new hires on bending processes, as well.

"We try to get people so they don’t feel so overwhelmed when they get to the shop floor," RTA Supervisor Josh Besson said.

So far, people coming out of RTA are saying it’s done just that, Thomas remarked. In fact, it’s done so well that the company plans to replicate it at the new 622,788-sq.-ft. fabrication facility it is currently building in Altoona, Iowa. The new facility, slated for completion in January 2027, will employ 100 people, the company predicts.

"As we’re bringing people on there, we really want to use that process," Thomas said of RTA’s current program. "The supervisors, the coordinators, these people coming out of the training academy and going out onto the floor—they’re more ready for work. We’re able to add them to capacity projections sooner.

"I also think from a retention standpoint, it’s helped us a ton," Thomas added. "Our facilities can probably be pretty intimidating. What we do is big, so there’s complexity to it. We’d bring [people] in, they’d go through orientation, and then the next day they’re out on the floor, and they’re working amongst the controlled chaos that we have, and I think we lost people that way. I think this process kind of slows it down for them, allows them to acclimate themselves a little bit to Robinson before they go out into the major manufacturing facilities and become part of that process."

That acclimation to company culture may once have mainly entailed learning the ways of the shop floor. But over the past two-and-ahalf years, it’s also meant bringing in new owners by way of Robinson’s Employee Stock Ownership Plan (ESOP).

EMPLOYEE OWNERSHIP CHANGES THE LANDSCAPE

By transitioning the company to the ESOP on May 1, 2023, Robinson in effect gave everyone on the payroll a personal stake in the company’s present and future welfare. In fact, Thomas remembers the move as a real flag-in-theground moment for its people-first ethos.

"We have two goals: No. 1 is, I want Robinson to be the best, most convenient place to work," Thomas said. "Goal No. 2 is, we’re going to create as much value for our team members and their families, using the ESOP as the vehicle, as we possibly can. I really don’t have any goals beyond that—that’s really where it starts and ends. I think everything beyond that lends itself to those two goals. If we’re doing those two things, it brings value to our customers.

"It makes us the employer of choice. And it allows team engagement and everyone to be in it for each other and for themselves. We’re not doing this for an owner."

With the ESOP, the company awards quarterly profit sharing bonuses to every eligible employee. And while the quarterly bonuses represent the shorter-term cash benefits some employees prefer, the company also offers a more typical 401(k) matching account for longterm retirement planning.

The ESOP has changed the way company executives have to monitor cash flow, as growth in both revenue and number of employees presents opportunities as well as balance sheet liabilities. The trick is to monitor closely how many shares are released annually while also keeping an eye on vested employee departures and retirement dates across the five different generations represented in the company’s workforce.

Fortunately for everyone involved, the overall financial picture has improved substantially: Three years ago, Robinson took in about $150 million in revenue; for the current fiscal year ending June 30, the company projects $350 million in revenue, with backlog of $378 million already on the books.

Thomas said that for the next fiscal year, ending in June 2027, Robinson aims to bring in $500 million—a haul that would, based on 2025 numbers, affix the company squarely in the No. 2 spot on The Fabricator’s FAB 40 list, right behind fellow Wisconsin fabrication powerhouse and perennial No. 1 Mayville Engineering Company Inc.

"I’ll just say this: a billion in revenue is on the radar for us," Thomas said.

In the meantime, the company will continue to manage continued expectations for growth both in revenue and employee count.

"The way that all ESOPs work is, there’s a determined amount of time that all of the shares are allocated and released to employees," Thomas explained. "Obviously, as you grow and you have more team members, only a certain amount of shares are released every year. So, as you have more employees, the number of shares going out becomes lower, but we’ve been able to benefit from some very substantial growth over the last two or three years since we’ve taken on the ESOP.

"So, you’re seeing account balances for these employees as they become vested become sizable very quickly. That’s been a huge benefit to us. And there’s a lot of excitement when those share value statements go out every January."
