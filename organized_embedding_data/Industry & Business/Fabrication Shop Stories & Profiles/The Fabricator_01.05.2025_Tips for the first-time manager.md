I

magine you’ve worked as an expert press brake operator for years. You can unfold a formed sheet metal part in your head, visualize the bend sequence, and perfect a press brake program in no time flat. For years, you were the department’s technical expert—an informal leader, but you had no official direct reports. Now you’re stepping into a supervisory role, and for the first time, you find yourself leading the next generation of operators. What now?

Plenty has been written about how technology can help make the next-generation fabricator more productive. Younger workers grew up with smartphones and touchscreens. Programming and simulating work offline seems utterly natural to them. Technology investment seems like a no-brainer: If your team has the right equipment, they can be more productive—right?

Not necessarily, and quite often, the issue resides on the people side of the equation—specifically, leadership. Stepping into a management role for the first time is both exhilarating and demanding, especially in the precision-driven world of metal fabrication. While technical expertise often paves the way for a promotion, leadership requires far more than mastering processes. It’s about inspiring people, cultivating trust, and making decisions that elevate both individuals and the team.

The Shift From Expert to Leader

Perfecting a craft demands discipline and adaptability—qualities that also define exceptional leadership. Yet, unlike machinery that follows exact specifications, people require motivation, guidance, and clear communication. Good leadership brings out the best in others while ensuring operational excellence.

Many new managers in metal fabrication have honed their expertise in welding, press brake operation, or laser cutting. However, true leadership requires shifting focus from executing the work personally to developing the talent around them. Being skilled at a trade doesn’t mean you can automatically teach it. While expertise is crucial, effective instruction requires breaking down concepts, clear communication, and adapting to different learning styles.

Establishing structured training programs that cater to hands-on learners, visual thinkers, and those who thrive with step-by-step instruction ensures that knowledge is successfully transferred and retained. A great leader not only masters the craft but also perfects the art of imparting it to others.

This transition isn’t effortless, but it is essential for growth—both for the leader and the team. The key is recognizing that leadership success is no longer measured by individual technical output but by the collective development and achievements of the team.

Elevating People Through Leadership

True leadership is rooted in influence, not authority. The most effective leaders coach, mentor, and empower their teams. They foster a culture of support, accountability, and continuous growth. And they acknowledge that each team member has unique strengths, learning preferences, and perspectives.

With a coaching mindset, effective leaders don’t simply issue directives; they guide their teams toward solutions. They encourage problem-solving, pose thoughtful questions, and provide constructive feedback. In short, they cultivate a learning environment. They invest in developing others, offering skill-building opportunities, and recognize both effort and progress.

High standards drive excellence, but they must be paired with the right level of mentorship. Good leaders establish clear expectations, provide guidance, and create space for employees to grow.

When employees talk, good leaders listen and strive to understand. At the same time, they articulate expectations clearly. Ambiguity leads to mistakes, just as unclear specifications in fabrication result in defective products. Good leaders also tailor their communication styles. Every team member is unique; some require direct guidance, while others thrive with autonomy.

Lead With Emotional Intelligence

Many first-time managers assume their primary challenge will be managing the workload: deadlines, quality control, and efficiency. However, the real challenge often isn’t the job itself; it’s managing the people behind the job.

Employees don’t leave personal struggles at the door. I experienced this firsthand in my first management role when I was leading employees twice my age. Their technical expertise wasn’t the issue—it was the personal struggles they brought into the workplace that made leadership complex. As a leader, developing emotional intelligence—the ability to recognize, understand, and manage both your own emotions and those of your team—is a skill that takes time to cultivate. Effective leaders don’t just oversee tasks; they navigate the complexities of human dynamics.

To that end, remain self-aware and composed. This alone will help you establish a strong leadership presence. At the same time, practice empathy. Acknowledge employees’ personal struggles but ensure they do not compromise accountability and performance. You do this by separating

empathy

from

over-involvement

. You need to care, but you cannot assume responsibility for everyone’s burdens. Setting professional boundaries while remaining supportive is essential.

All this requires practice and continual reflection. To help, seek out mentors. Experienced leaders can give you valuable insights into navigating difficult situations.

Effective leadership extends beyond technical expertise or authority; it hinges on understanding individuals, demonstrating emotional intelligence, and cultivating a workplace where employees feel both valued and accountable.

Replacing Yourself: Developing the Next Expert

First-time leaders must transition from being the expert to becoming a good coach. They will likely coach people who are much less proficient in the skills they have long mastered. The challenge is ensuring these employees not only perform well but also gain the confidence and skills to excel under your leadership.

This lesson became clear to me through the example of my father. He was the president of a national general contracting company, but to himself, at his core, he was a carpenter. He loved nothing more than being on-site with his toolbelt on, immersed in work. However, as the company began to grow and the number of individuals reporting to him did as well, he stood at a crossroad.

Rather than focusing on strategic growth, hiring, and scaling the business, my father was still tied closely to daily operations. It was his business partner who challenged him by saying, "If you really want to be a leader and grow this organization, you need to lose the toolbelt."

He could have remained in the familiar role of a hands-on owner-operator, continuing to work alongside his team day in and day out. While this would have ensured steady business, it would have also limited the company’s ability to expand. His presence on-site meant he was still tied to the daily operations rather than focusing on strategic growth, hiring, and scaling the business. It was his business partner who challenged him by saying, "If you really want to be a leader and grow this organization, you need to lose the toolbelt."

This was a defining moment. It wasn’t about abandoning his skills but rather recognizing that his role had evolved. His success no longer depended on his individual craftsmanship but on his ability to guide and elevate others. By stepping back from the work itself and empowering his team, he allowed the company to expand beyond what he could have accomplished alone.

Leaders must pass the torch. Instead of holding onto their own expertise, they must shift their focus to developing the skills of their team members, ensuring the organization continues to grow and thrive.

Practical Strategies for Developing New Experts

Break skills into milestones. Just as a fabrication project follows a step-by-step process, breaking complex skills into achievable learning stages helps new employees gain confidence and competence over time, all while managers track progress and provide support. Documenting this process and creating a standardized training framework ensures consistency, with future employees following the same structured approach.

Also, always be sure to provide hands-on training. Demonstrate best practices while allowing employees to try, make mistakes, and improve under guided supervision. Leading by example builds both skill and trust, and it remains one of the most powerful and effective ways to teach.

Instead of constantly correcting mistakes, ask questions that prompt problem-solving:

What do you think went wrong? How would you approach it differently next time?

Encouraging independent thinking accelerates learning.

Never micromanage, of course, but do establish checkpoints. Regular progress reviews keep employees accountable without overwhelming them. Offer constructive feedback while reinforcing improvements.

Finally, celebrate progress—not just perfection. Recognizing small wins helps build confidence. A first-time welder mastering a technique may not produce perfect welds immediately, but reinforcing their improvement fosters motivation and long-term growth.

Recognize Leadership Blind Spots

New leaders often face hidden challenges that can hinder growth. One of the most common blind spots is over-involvement—struggling to transition from doing the work themselves to trusting their team. This often manifests as micromanagement, an instinct to jump in at every sign of struggle, or difficulty delegating any meaningful responsibility.

New managers can mistake constant involvement for leadership, but in reality, it hinders team growth and independence. Employees who are not given space to make decisions, solve problems, and learn from mistakes often become dependent rather than confident contributors. A leader’s job is not to be the problem-solver—it’s to create problem-solvers.

To prevent this, be intentional and communicate how your role is changing. If employees don’t understand why you’re stepping back, they might assume you’re just avoiding responsibility. Instead, openly share the approach.

Explain that providing space is a way to foster growth and psychological safety. All this helps employees understand that the leader is still present, just in a supportive capacity.

Another blind spot is assuming leadership is about having all the answers rather than fostering a culture of continuous learning. The best leaders never just dictate solutions but instead guide their teams to develop their own. Encouraging critical thinking, seeking input, and admitting when you don’t know something strengthens team cohesion and trust.

If you as a new manager recognize these blind spots early, you can adjust your approach before they impact team effectiveness and morale. Developing self-awareness and regularly reflecting on leadership habits ensures that first-time managers continue growing into strong, adaptable leaders.

Leadership as an Evolving Craft

No two leadership journeys are the same. The best leaders continuously refine their approach, learning from experience, seeking feedback, and staying adaptable.

They understand that leadership is not about control but about unlocking the potential in others and creating a culture where individuals and teams perform at their best. They also know that embracing a coach mindset helps individuals grow and, in turn, strengthens the entire organization.

As you step into leadership, what’s one habit or mindset shift you can commit to today that will help not just you, but your team, thrive in the long run?
