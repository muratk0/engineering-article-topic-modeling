# Managing Seasonal Demand

[TARİH: 01.05.2017 The Fabricator]

The power of subsidiarity

Robert L. Du Fresne President/CEO, Du Fresne Manufacturing Co.

Vadnais Heights, Minn.

Bob Du Fresne

had another sleepless night in October 2010. The president of Du Fresne Manufacturing Co. knew his custom fabrication business had met some challenging times. He was about $20,000 short of making payroll—a first for him. A religious person, he lay awake that night and prayed for a sign, a signal,

something

that would show he was on the right path. Two days later, a check for $22,000 arrived in the mail. For some reason a customer paid in 10 days instead of the normal 45.

A fabricator can diversify its customer base, cross train personnel for better part flow, and use

all

sorts of other tactics to handle the economic highs and lows. But how does it get through something like the Great Recession, when all sectors are depressed?

After his sleepless night, Du Fresne had an epiphany: His custom fabrication company needed to make the competition irrelevant. That sounds good, but how does one do this exactly?

Du Fresne likes to discover new words, and he came across one that describes his management philosophy well:

subsidiarity

, which means that decisions should be made not by top managers but by the people who actually are affected the most by the decision. This works, Du Fresne said, when you have defined and documented a company’s key systems and processes. But again, those decisions should not be dictated from the top down, but agreed upon by people who are affected by the decision, including those who perform the work.

"You actually take away people’s dignity if you don’t allow them to make decisions that they’re capable of making, and it affects their life," Du Fresne said. "When they are given that dignity, it’s amazing what they can do."

Here’s a principal metric at Du Fresne Manufacturing: How many times does a new job make it through the system with no rework or other corrections, and customers get exactly what they want, ultimately making the competition irrelevant? Today, Du Fresne said this metric is at 98.4 percent.

So how do you define "making the competition irrelevant" exactly? Du Fresne Manufacturing asks its customers to rate it against its competition in eight categories, including competitive price, financial stability, product quality, delivery, industry expertise, flexibility, technology leadership, overall costs, and accountability. Customers rated Du Fresne Manufacturing higher, sometimes much higher, than the competition in all but one area: competitive price.

This customer survey is one of numerous other processes the fabricator measures, and results have been so positive that in 2014 the company won the Minnesota Excellence Award (judged under the National Baldrige criteria), a prestigious prize given to manufacturers who have significantly increased market share and profitability.

Du Fresne Manufacturing gets nearly 50 percent of all the metal fabrication work its customers outsource, and it’s gotten that market share without offering the lowest price in town.

This also softens the impact of an economic slowdown. Ideally, should the next Great Recession come, the fabricator will be the last supplier to lose orders. For Bob Du Fresne, this means no more sleepless nights.
