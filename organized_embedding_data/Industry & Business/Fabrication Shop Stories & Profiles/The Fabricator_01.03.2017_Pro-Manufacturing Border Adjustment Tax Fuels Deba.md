T

he pro-manufacturing aspects of the emerging Republican tax reform package are apt to be among the most controversial elements of the plan. That is because House Ways and Means Committee Chairman Kevin Brady’s border adjustment tax is seen by some as anti-consumer. That has led to opposition from some industry sectors, retailing most prominently.

Simultaneously, support from manufacturing groups has been muted. The National Association of Manufacturers has not taken a position.

Matt McMullan, a spokesman for the Alliance for American Manufacturing (AAM), said his group is taking a "wait-and-see" position until House Republicans actually publish legislation. However, he added that, in concept, a border adjustment tax would encourage U.S. manufacturing.

Brady, R-Texas, has been talking up the idea incessantly. It was included in the House GOP’s 2016 tax reform plan called "A Better Way."

Currently, the corporate income tax applies to businesses’ income from

production

in the U.S. Under a border adjustment, the income tax would apply to businesses’ income from sales in the U.S. The AAM said companies that use U.S. plants would benefit, while those that import their products would pay the adjustment tax. So companies such as GE and Boeing, both of which are large exporters, would benefit. On the other hand, retail giants such as Walmart might take it on the chin.

There is a strain of thought that any sort of border adjustment would strengthen the U.S. dollar, making imported goods less expensive than they would with a weak dollar, which helps to ease the financial impact for importers of goods and U.S. consumers. In theory, this also would encourage U.S. manufacturing.

President Donald Trump has not endorsed the border adjustment tax idea, previously staking his "Made in America" plan on taxing U.S.-based company products made abroad.

"AAM doesn’t think a blanket tarifton imports from Mexico, which the president has referred to at times, is a particularly good idea," McMullan said.

While the border adjustment tax has broad support in the House, Sen. Orrin Hatch, R-Utah, chairman of the Senate Finance Committee, has not weighed in.

OSHA Sets New Beryllium Exposure Standard

The Occupational Safety and Health Administration published new limits on worker exposure to beryllium, alloys of which are precision machined for military and aerospace applications. Some welding consumables also are manufactured using beryllium.

The final rule establishes new permissible exposure limits of 0.2 micrograms of beryllium per cubic meter of air (0.2 μg/m

3

) as an 8-hour time-weighted average and 2.0 g/m

3

as a short-term exposure limit determined over a sampling period of 15 minutes. It also includes other provisions to protect employees, such as requirements for exposure assessment, methods for controlling exposure, respiratory protection, personal protective clothing and equipment, housekeeping, medical surveillance, hazard communication, and recordkeeping.

Beryllium is known to cause cancer and other fatal diseases, such as chronic beryllium disease (CBD) of the lungs, when even very low levels are inhaled.

In-service Pipeline Welding Allowed

The federal agency that prescribes safety procedures for steel gas and petroleum pipelines will now allow welding on in-service metal pipelines.

The Pipeline and Hazardous Materials Safety Administration (PHMSA), a part of the Transportation Department, just published a wide-ranging final rule responding to a congressional bill passed in 2011. A metal welding provision was not included in that bill, but the PHMSA took the opportunity in the final rule to say it would, for the first time, allow in-service welding if that welding was done according to Appendix B of American Petroleum Institute Standard 1104

Welding of Pipelines and Related Facilities

.

EWI, an engineering and technology organization dedicated to advanced materials joining and other manufacturing technologies, said welding directly onto in-service pipelines is beneficial in saving time, labor, and expense.
