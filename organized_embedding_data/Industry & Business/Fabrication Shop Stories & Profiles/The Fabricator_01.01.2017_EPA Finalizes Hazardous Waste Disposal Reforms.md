New rule shows some flexibility for small shops

T

he new Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) rule on hazardous waste generation and storage will affect manufacturers across a wide range of sectors, including those in the metal finishing and plating sectors where solvent use is heavy.

The good news is that the final rule clears up some earlier concerns that generating companies would have to treat scrap metal as a solid waste and account for it. Joseph Green, counsel to the Specialty Steel Industry of North America, which raised the issue, said the final rule makes clear that the hazardous waste determination does not apply to materials such as scrap.

The rule’s provisions, which go into effect at the end of March, make some fairly significant changes to the Resource Conservation and Recovery Act (RCRA) Subtitle C regulations. The provisions affect companies based on whether they are large quantity generators (LQGs), small quantity generators (SQGs), or very small quantity generators (VSQGs). The annual costs of compliance across all three categories are somewhere between $6 million and $13 million, making this, frankly, one of the less expensive environmental regulations to have come out of the Obama administration.

The EPA contends that some of the changes in this new rule will benefit generators. One of those changes allows a VSQG or an SQG to maintain its existing generator category, even when it might generate a quantity of hazardous waste in a month that otherwise would have bumped it up into a more stringent generator regulatory category in the past. Another change allows VSQGs to send hazardous waste to a LQG that is under the control of the same person and consolidate it there before sending it on to management at an RCRA-designated facility, provided certain conditions are met.

The hazardous waste generator regulatory program was originally promulgated in 1980.

Imported Tool Steel Could Get More Expensive

The metalworking industry is concerned that three U.S. steelmakers are pleading for import tariffs or duties on imports of steel plate from various foreign countries.

ArcelorMittal USA, Nucor, and SSAB Enterprises want the International Trade Commission (ITC) to assess import duties on carbon and alloy steel hotrolled and forged flat plate products not in coils. The domestic steel mills argue that they are being "materially injured or threatened with material injury" by the imports.

The annual costs of compliance for the new EPA rule on hazardous waste generation and storage across all three categories are somewhere between $6 million and $13 million, making this, frankly, one of the less expensive environmental regulations to have come out of the Obama administration.

However, the National Tooling and Machining Association (NTMA) and the Precision Metalforming Association (PMA) are arguing they cannot obtain all the tool steel they need from U.S. producers and have to buy from foreign companies, whose prices sometimes are even higher than their American competitors’.

A key issue in the ITC’s determination, expected in early 2017, is how broad or narrow to classify the steel imports at issue. The NTMA and PMA contend that the steel plate category being looked at has two distinct subcategories. Tool steel has high wear resistance, toughness, and hot/red hardness, while other steel plate demonstrates low levels of those characteristics. Tool steel has relatively low ductility and weldability, while other steel plate has high ductility and weldability.

If the ITC finds injury, the Department of Commerce would determine the level of tariffs or duties.
