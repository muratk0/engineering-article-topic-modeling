The main reason for this never-ending push to stay in front of competitors is that the U.S. economy refuses to stop expanding, even if it is doing so slowly. While the rest of the world hopes for positive GDP growth, the U.S. is riding the wave of robust automotive, aerospace, and transportation industries. Even as the energy sector halts new exploration and sheds thousands of workers, the U.S. economy should continue in a positive direction, Dr. Chris Kuehl, the economic analyst for the Fabricators & Manufacturers Association (FMA), told conference attendees during the opening presentation on market trends.

FMA’s "4th Quarter 2014 Forming & Fabricating Job Shop Consumption Report" supports Kuehl’s conclusion. The survey revealed that 31 percent of the 250 companies that responded believed neworder activity was expanding in the fourth quarter, when compared to the third quarter of 2014; 49 percent reported that new-order activity was stable. When asked what the outlook was for the first quarter of 2015, 53 percent of fabricators responded with a positive outlook and 37 percent predicted stable business.

The manufacturing environment is ripe for those fabricators that want to initiate change to take advantage of new business opportunities, and some of those tales were shared during the conference.

Jim Schollett, CFO and vice president of finance and administration, Leaf Industries Inc., Plymouth, Minn., explained how his company is in the midst of moving its entire information technology infrastructure to the "cloud," where company information is kept on remote servers belonging to a third party and accessed over the Internet. The company had already moved payment and banking operations to a similar hosting model, so it made sense to do the same for its operational software, said Schollett during a panel discussion on enterprise software.

"We needed to understand what we wanted to do next," he said. "We didn’t want to buy something that did what we already did."

What was new for Leaf Industries was quick-quote and online ordering portal modules. These provided new tools to improve customer service.

Similar conversations continued during a fabricating technology panel. Of course, case studies revealed the impact that fiber laser technology is making on the shop floor. Perhaps more interesting is looking at newer fabricating technology options coming down the pike. One presenter shared how Alto-Shaam Inc., a Menomonee Falls, Wis., commercial appliance manufacturer, adopted automated tool-changing technology when it installed a new press brake. Before the new brake was put in place, shop floor research found that the operator spent an extraordinary amount of time determining what tooling to use, selecting the bend sequence, and conducting test bends. The automated tool changeover and bend correction technology changed that, increasing green-light time from 11.3 percent to 39 percent for bending operations.

Another presenter discussed the potential of laser welding. This particular case study focused on CBV Blechbearbeitung’s adoption of a 3-kW fiber laser power source that it uses to run a laser cutting machine and a laser welding cell. The Laasdorf, Germany-based manufacturer has to switch the power source between the two operations, but the impact has not been great because the new laser cutting machine is much more productive than the 10-yearold CO

2

laser it replaced.

The shop works with mostly stainless steel and aluminum, and it has found that the laser-welded joints actually don’t require postweld finishing. This has resulted in significant reduction in finishing activity—a necessary evil for fab shops all over the globe.

Admittedly, these types of changes come with a noteworthy price tag. But change doesn’t have to start by writing a check; sometimes it can start with a simple conversation.

At the 10th edition of the FLS, fabricators had multiple opportunities to connect with their peers from all over North America to discuss topics such as political trends, economic factors, marketing, customer satisfaction, and workforce development. This peer-to-peer learning took place during semiformal roundtable discussions, formal breakout sessions, and perhaps over an evening cocktail.
