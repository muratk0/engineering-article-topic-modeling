resident Trump opened a second front beyond steel and aluminum tariffs in his efforts to upgrade the U.S. industrial manufacturing base. And he’s doing it again in the name of national defense.

The new initiative coincided with the release of a White House report in October. Called "Assessing and Strengthening the Manufacturing and Defense Industrial Base and Supply Chain Resiliency of the United States," the report discusses the myriad weaknesses of the U.S. defense manufacturing supply chain. Peter Navarro, director of the White House Office of Trade and Manufacturing Policy, who led the report-writing effort, specifically alluded to the dwindling supply of American welders.

"As America’s manufacturing base has weakened, so too have critical workforce skills ranging from industrial welding to high-technology skills for cybersecurity and aerospace," the report states.

The report directly relates a shortage of metalworking skills to potential future problems in Navy shipbuilding. The Department of Labor predicts that U.S. jobs in occupations critical to Navy shipbuilding, such as metal layout (shipfitting), welding, and casting, will decrease 6 percent to 17 percent between 2018 and 2026. As the number of available jobs overall in the U.S. decreases, the number of workers entering into these fields also will decrease.

"Left unaddressed, a lack of skilled workers will significantly impact the shipbuilding industry’s ability to meet the Navy’s long-term demand," the report states.

The action plan produced by the interagency committee that wrote the report is classified. The unclassified version provides some general recommendations, such as expanding direct investment in the lower tier of the industrial base through programs such as the Pentagon’s Defense Production Act Title III initiative to address critical bottlenecks, support fragile suppliers, and mitigate single points-of-failure. Making that a reality would require the Trump administration to propose additional funding for programs or create new ones aimed at defense manufacturing suppliers.

The budget for the Department of Defense for the current fiscal 2019 (started Oct. 1) has essentially been approved by Congress. It wouldn’t be until the fall of 2019, aft er the fiscal 2020 budget is approved, that any new funding would be approved.

Reacting to the report, Scott Paul, president, Alliance for American Manufacturing, said, "I encourage the White House to dedicate funding to close already-identified gaps and increase materials stockpiles."

Another Report Also Highlights Manufacturing Needs

The White House issued a separate report at about the same time called "Strategy for American Leadership in Advanced Manufacturing," this one compiled by the National Science and Technology Council Subcommittee on Advanced Manufacturing. The report lists three goals:

Develop and transition new manufacturing technologies.

Educate, train, and connect the manufacturing workforce.

Expand the capabilities of the domestic manufacturing supply chain.

The report supports establishment of a central repository or registry that assists job seekers with identifying manufacturing-related apprenticeships and industry-recognized credentials. The Department of Labor established such a database just before the report was published:

www.apprenticeship.gov

.

The report argues that such a database, besides helping individuals identify a program that fits her or his needs and schedule, also could assist employers in finding job seekers with the right credentials to fulfill workforce needs. It also could further increase exposure to credentialing programs for U.S. veterans like SkillBridge, a program that provides training in areas such as welding, pipefitting, and information technology.

However, simply establishing a convenient place for people to find apprenticeship opportunities is not the end of the challenge. The report emphasizes that states and local communities will have to do their part to increase the number of individuals participating in and completing these programs.
