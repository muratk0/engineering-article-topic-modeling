ccording to recent statistics from OSHA, more than 30,000 fork truck accidents resulting in serious injury occur annually in the United States.

"Most industrial accidents are forklift related," said Nevzat Atakli, CEO of Trio Mobil, a Marietta, Ga.-based technology company focused on mobile safety in industrial environments. "And globally, the number is even greater. People are dying, and too many get seriously injured. This is a tragic fact that we need to address."

Say an accident occurs because someone rushed to retrieve material and, in doing so, failed to watch for pedestrians. The truck swerves, and the load collides with a column—sending thousands of dollars of raw stock to the scrap bin. Yes, the fork truck driver might have ignored safety protocols, but why was he rushed in the first place? Could raw stock be stored closer to the point of use? Not least, was the driver given the best material handling tools and technology for the job?

Airplanes in the Fab Shop

Paul Short is president, North America, at Combilift, an Irish manufacturer of multidirectional forklifts with U.S. offices in Greensboro, N.C. He described what he called "airplaning," when a fork truck driver lifts a long load just to clear nearby obstructions. The practice breaks at least one fundamental safety rule: Never travel with a raised load (see

Figure 1

).

"We call it ‘airplaning’ because it looks like an airplane going through the facility," Short said, adding that the practice puts the load outside the operator’s field of vision. This practice also causes the forklifts to be top heavy, which easily destabilizes them when accelerating, braking, or turning. Additionally, if the load is ready to slip, the driver probably won’t know until it’s too late.

Multidirectional sideloading fork trucks can help here (see

Figure 2

). "Say your load is 40 ft. long by 40 in. deep," Short said. "We’ll only need 48 in. in travel plus the dimension of the [fork truck]. That’s how you can handle a load safer and closer to the ground, within the operator’s field of vision." He added that some units "retract the mast, which sets the load on the platform. It’s not carrying the load on the mast. It’s carrying the load on the truck chassis, which increases the safety."

Walking vs. Driving

Ideally, a plant floor should have separate aisles for fork trucks and pedestrians. Some plants might even have physical barriers that prevent pedestrians from crossing the forklift aisles, except at designated crosswalks. Drivers are supposed to honk to alert pedestrians, and plants can invest in sensors and lights that tell workers a fork truck is approaching— especially helpful for blind corners.

In greenfield plants, fabricators can design a layout that minimizes or even eliminates those blind corners. In fact, some of the safest plants out there make limited use of fork trucks. You’ll see fork trucks unload sheets from trucks and then deliver them directly to a flexible manufacturing system with live inventory. As soon as the sheets are loaded into a tower, the shop knows exactly what it has and where. When parts are cut, they’re placed on carts, pallet jacks, or other manual material handling equipment. The scenario illustrates how minimizing fork truck use creates inherent efficiencies. Of course, not every material handling job can be managed by a simple cart.

But as Short explained, this doesn’t mean a shop needs to rely solely on the traditional fork truck. "We’re seeing some plants moving away from ride-on equipment toward pedestrian equipment," he said, adding that fabricators can now choose from a range of so-called "walkie" products that allow for operators to walk alongside a device carrying products that could weigh hundreds of pounds (see

Figure 3

). "Our multiposition tiller arm can be turned to the left or right and enables the operator to remain in the safest position, at the side of the truck and not at the rear. This removes operators from the crush zone and gives them better visibility. It also gives them a lot of precision when working around equipment."

Functionally, walkie equipment allows operators to maneuver in ways those driving even a multidirectional fork truck would find difficult. Beyond this, Short said, walkie products promote safety in several ways. First, they naturally slow movement to a walking pace. Second, they tend to make operators much more aware of their surroundings.

"When you drive and bump into an obstruction, the driver might not always notice," Short said. "But when you operate a walkie piece of equipment, you’re much more aware of your surroundings. Some manufacturers today use walkies to move more than 30,000 lbs.—loads that would have traditionally been handled by a fork truck. Now, the operator can walk alongside this heavy load and move it through the facility very safely. He never exceeds a walking pace, and the tiller arm allows him to stand to the side of the load and look down the aisle, giving him better visibility while operating the unit."

Whether this walkie strategy works or not depends on the application and existing facility. As Short explained, many plants use the equipment in facilities where it’s impossible to fully segregate pedestrian and fork truck traffic.

"Regardless, we recommend safety barriers that you need to open before walking across an aisle designated for fork truck traffic," Short continued. "Pedestrians should be able to make eye contact with fork truck drivers, especially if they’re walking anywhere near them, so that they’re aware of you. We also see plants moving toward lighting activated by an approaching forklift."

Tracking Fork Truck Movement

This dovetails into another emerging technology that could affect material handling safety and efficiency in the coming years: sensing and location-tracking technologies. Some operations today have systems that utilize GPS, which can be particularly effective outdoors. As Short described, these work well for remote diagnostics, monitoring, and service planning.

Inside the plant, ultra-wide-band (UWB) technology is showing promise for monitoring material handling safety and efficiency. Trio Mobil’s Atakli described the company’s technology in which workers wear tags that work in concert with separate UWB controllers placed on fork trucks or other mobile equipment, like automated guided vehicles and autonomous mobile robots.

When workers approach, the tag vibrates, alerting the pedestrian. Audio and visual alarms are also triggered. At the same time, an alarm is triggered on the fork truck with a UWB control module. On certain fork trucks, the control module can force the fork truck to slow down (the feature is subject to the fork truck OEM’s approval). Similar technology can help prevent forklifts from colliding with other mobile equipment and, in some setups, exceeding a certain speed in specific zones of a plant.

"UWB is not the only technology being used," Atakli said. "Many are moving toward AI cameras, which means pedestrians don’t need to wear tags." He added in these setups, cameras with AI-driven image-recognition technology can be mounted on the fork truck as well as in certain high-risk areas. "With edge processing, the AI cameras work instantaneously. They process the image data and react to a potential incident within milliseconds."

The company also offers systems that combine the AI camera technology with UWB proximity alert systems. The AI cameras identify pedestrians and trigger instant alerts. Simultaneously, the UWB technology ensures blind spot coverage (see

Figure 4

).

The technology can be integrated with a real-time location system (RTLS) that tracks the movement of mobile industrial equipment and, for that matter, any other physical asset or person. Such systems can produce significant data to help improve efficiency and optimize flow.

To be sure, technology can’t solve every problem. Ideally, training and overall shop culture should always put safety front and center. Fork truck drivers should never be incentivized to rush in the first place, and they should never be penalized for following best safety practices. Safety should trump everything else.

But the real world is messy. People call in sick or just don’t show up. Fork truck drivers can have bad days. Changes in customer demand can spur a shop floor scramble, creating an environment that invites human error—hence the value of having the right equipment, audio and visual alarms, and even real-time sensors and cameras that use AI-driven image processing.

Automate Safely

According to Combilift’s Short, high-product-mix environments can be difficult for material handling automation, simply because of all the product variation. Even so, high-product-mix operations do have at least some areas with less variability.

Consider structural fabrication. Downstream has plenty of variety, but what about in raw stock inventory? A raw structural beam might have different lengths and dimensions, but it’s still a beam and, hence, can be a good candidate for material handling automation—specifically, the autonomous guided forklift truck.

"These can scan a load to make sure it fits within the envelope," Short said, who added that to make use of driverless fork trucks, "an operation needs to have a certain level of technology to drive the system. So, you’ll need ERP [enterprise resource planning] or a WMS [warehouse management system], so you know where everything is in the building."

Many driverless fork trucks available today don’t require sensors on the floor but instead rely on sensors on the fork trucks themselves, which can sense pedestrians and other potential obstructions. If a pedestrian gets too close, the fork truck slows down. If the pedestrian continues to walk toward the truck, it will need to be reset to restart operation.

Short added that because autonomous fork trucks require significant standardization, they aren’t a good fit for every operation. But the act of working toward standardization can help create a safer, more efficient environment for fork truck operators in general.

For instance, Short described how an autonomous fork truck scans a bay before picking a load. "It needs to make sure the load is centered and the length is what’s specified in the WMS," he said. If the load dimensions are outside what’s expected, the truck will stop and require a manual override.

Similar checks occur when an autonomous truck places a load in the rack. "When it places the load, it scans to make sure the opening is sufficient, checking each side of the space, and ensuring that the opening on the rack is indeed empty. It can also detect whether a rack arm is bent up or down. If it’s too high or low, it will detect it. It won’t uncover what the issue is, exactly, but the system will show a failed load placement because something it scanned was out of tolerance."

All these measures ensure smooth operation, but they do require standardization that can make various operations safer, whether they’re fully automated or not. Short added that many plants work their way toward automation by identifying best practices. They standardize inventory locations and identification methods and ensure all loads staged for the fork truck are centered, secure, and safe to carry.

A fabricator likely wouldn’t automate material handling throughout its plant—at least not anytime soon. The product shapes are just too various. But automating raw stock handling is a real possibility.

The devil’s in the details, of course. But even if full fork truck automation doesn’t make sense, Short said, the very act of standardization can improve material handling efficiency and safety. If a designated sheet, plate, or beam is always staged where expected, fork truck drivers won’t waste time hunting for material. This means they’re less likely to be in a rush and scrambling to catch up. That ultimately makes the workday calmer, more predictable, and much safer.
