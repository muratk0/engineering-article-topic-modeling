# Ready for anything

[TARİH: 01.03.2025 The Fabricator]

Biz Talk

Agility matters in the choppy waters of world trade

TIM HESTON

I

magine quick tool and fixture changes. Imagine a staged setup on a brake, designed to handle a variety of parts. Now think of a dynamic nest on a laser that changes its nozzles and centers its beam automatically. Or a flexible fixture in a welding robot cell. Now imagine software that tracks it all and can help everyone turn on a dime.

All these points paint a picture of metal fabrication’s unique selling point: its agility. Outside of robotic welding fixtures and special press brake punches and dies, most fabricators don’t have to worry about custom tooling. Programs at the CNC might change, but tooling is universal.

Such agility quickly entered the conversation when I spoke with Kevin Matkin, CEO of Maysteel, a contract fabricator headquartered in Allenton, Wis., with four other locations, including one in Monterrey, Mexico. I called him a few days after the Trump administration announced, and then delayed, broad tariffs on Mexico and Canada, all while moving forward with tariffs on goods imported from China.

The trade war has begun. How will fabricators react? "We don’t want to overreact," Matkin said. "We’ll stay agile and communicate closely with customers, so we know how the situation is affecting them. We’re taking a disciplined approach."

What makes agility possible? Think of a diversified investment portfolio that’s ready for any kind of market—bull, bear, and anything in between. A metal fabrication business can be set up like this too, ready for any kind of external disruption that might arise, including the potential of tariffs.

It’s an ideal contract metal fabricators like Maysteel continually strive for. Maysteel’s plants have duplicate capacity, meaning that it can shift production quickly, and every operation runs under one enterprise resource planning (ERP) platform. Certain specialized weld fixtures would take some time to adapt to another plant, but otherwise, Matkin said that shifts in production would be counted in weeks, not months.

"As a contract manufacturer, we need to be balanced, and we need to be agile. All the tariff conversations do is allow us to make sure we can exercise on that agility, and we know we can."

—Kevin Matkin, Maysteel

About 80% of the components fabricated in Mexico are shipped to companies in Mexico. "These contracts come from U.S.-based relationships, and many of these are Blue Chip companies," Matkin said. "Our U.S. plants are supplying their U.S. plants, and we have our plant in Monterrey that supplies parts to their plants in Mexico."

At present, the fabricator doesn’t import or export from Canada, and most of its raw material is sourced domestically. A small portion of purchased components do come from China, and these usually represent sourcing arrangements that customers have specified. "But even here, we have a Plan B, with alternative suppliers already identified in the U.S.," Matkin said. "We had these in place even before the tariffs."

As of early February, Maysteel hadn’t made any drastic moves, mainly because it had no need—particularly as news from Washington changed by the day (and sometimes by the hour). Case in point: Just two days after I spoke with Matkin, the Trump administration announced tariffs on steel and aluminum. Media sources reported tariffs would cover "everybody," but the details had yet to pan out. Of course, I’m writing this long before the dust has settled. In fact, the dust might not settle for a long time. Any business that makes hasty moves on false assumptions can find itself in a world of hurt.

Most economists will tell you that the situation isn’t ideal. They say it’s difficult if not impossible to plan when you don’t have at least some level of certainty. But reality is what it is, and the best operations have a diversified portfolio of business to hedge risk and prevent what they can’t control—be it tariffs or anything else—from sinking their business. That portfolio can be comprised of diverse customers with distinct supply chains and demand patterns, as well as software and manufacturing technology that allows shop floors to change direction on a dime and meet customers’ changing needs.

As Matkin put it, "As a contract manufacturer, we need to be balanced, and we need to be agile. All the tariff conversations do is allow us to make sure we can exercise on that agility, and we know we can."
