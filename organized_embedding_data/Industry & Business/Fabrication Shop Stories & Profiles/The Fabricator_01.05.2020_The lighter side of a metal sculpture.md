eeping willow trees that grace the Boston Public Garden inspired a sculpture comprising over 1,270 metal leaves suspended from 139 stainless steel cables.

Commuters and visitors crossing over the Bass River Bridge into Boston’s historic Southie community or walking along West Broadway can see the sculpture framed by the multistory windows of the Cambria Hotel Boston’s lobby. Seating below the sculpture and upper-floor walkways invite hotel guests to view the floating leaves from all angles.

The hanging sculpture, created by Boston-based RODE Architects, and the lobby space were designed to work in tandem to bring the outdoors and indoors together as a transition from the busy street to the building’s calm interior.

"We carved out a section between the first and second lobby floor spaces thinking that a lighting element would fill the space, but we ended up incorporating a nonelectrical sculpture that relies on surrounding light. It has the natural light through the windows during the day and is externally lit by lights that stay on throughout the night," said Jessica Haley, head of interiors for RODE.

"We wanted something metallic that would bounce the light. There is also a lot of brass in the hotel’s palette, and we thought bringing in metal finishes would add to the visual dynamics."

RODE Architects came up with the weeping willow concept and worked with Yellow Goat Design, a custom lighting company, for the sculpture’s design and construction. Components for the hanging sculpture were produced in Yellow Goat’s own fabrication studio in Cambridge, Ont.

Metal Reflects Light

"We collaborated with RODE to create something very custom to meet their design aesthetic. Our teams worked with a variety of materials but selected metal to ensure that the piece had enough of a presence to be seen through the curtain wall," said Nicola Casale, Yellow Goat director for North America. "As we thought about the shape, materials, and construction method, we landed on laser-cut leaf elements built from Octopus Octolux aluminum in a gold and silver finish with a mirrored finish on one side and a brushed finish on the other.

"We selected these finishes because they really reflect the light and we wanted passersby to experience the sculpture from the outside as well as the inside. It was important that the material itself would be prominent enough to stand on its own and to be seen from many different perspectives. It needed to pick up the surrounding architectural lighting and sparkle to give the sculpture that wow moment," Casale added.

The sculpture’s gold and silver finishes are distributed evenly and alternate throughout to feel balanced and proportional. The brushed side of the metal absorbs some of the light to provide the perfect amount of sparkle, according to Casale.

Aircra’ Cable Holds Leaves

Each leaf was laser-cut as a double leaf and bent before being individually attached by hand to a length of aircraft cable. Aircraft cable was used because it is fairly inconspicuous and it remains taut to keep the sculpture elements in place when air currents are generated from lobby activity.

Each strand of leaves is attached via wire grippers to one of eight silver powder-coated aluminum tubes that form the sculpture’s frame. The satin finish of the tubular frame allows it to blend in with the building’s architecture and helped keep the 15-ft. 5-in.-long, 3-ft. 3-in.-wide, and 13-ft.-high sculpture’s weight to a manageable 110 lbs.

Over 1,270 gold and silver leaves create a shimmering sculpture in the lobby of the Cambria Hotel Boston.

Photo provided by Erin McDonald, Choice Hotels.

After being fully assembled at Yellow Goat’s shop to ensure that placement of each leaf and strand of leaves created a pleasing balance, the sculpture was disassembled for shipping. Each strand was packed separately to avoid damage.

A two-man team from Yellow Goat spent three days installing the fixture. They wore protective gloves to prevent scratches or fingerprints while the sculpture was reassembled and hung within its space.

A little adjustment was required during installation so the sculpture could camouflage equipment that projects artwork on the lobby’s interior wall. Placement of the strands had to avoid the path of the light beam while the reflection and sparkle coming from the leaves help hide the projectors.

"One of the best things about the sculpture is the positioning of the building and how you see it through the windows," Haley said. "You can see the sculpture from halfway across the bridge, and you can’t see any of the hanging elements. It looks like the leaves are just floating there. That is very cool."
