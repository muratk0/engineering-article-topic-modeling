# A metal fabricator’s viewpoint on assembly operations

[TARİH: 01.06.2025 The Fabricator]

Assembly

EVS Metal shares how it stays on top of this value-added offering

By

Dan Davis

Metal fabricating is a complex business, and there’s always a customer willing to make it even more challenging.

Cutting, bending, and welding parts in low volumes isn’t a business to which many would migrate. These types of fab shops have to deal with too many customers, changing production schedules, a tight labor market, and detailed work. It’s not for the faint of heart.

And customers are always wanting to see if these shops will do more. After all, they want to push their production headaches down the supply chain. Why should they deal with these challenges if someone else is willing to take on the work and the risks for a reasonable sum?

That’s when a metal fabricating environment can really change. Seemingly overnight, it can go from a simple product-to-print operation to one offering many services, simplifying life for its customers.

Many times, that transformational path for a fab shop begins with this question from a customer: "Can you provide us with an assembly instead of a bunch of parts?" It’s an opportunity that is hard to turn down because it’s a way to extract more business from a current customer and make the shop that much more valuable—in other words, harder to cut loose when difficult supply chain decisions have to be made.

The Fabricator reached out to Joe Amico, executive vice president and director of sales for EVS Metal, a multilocation metal fabricator, to learn about his company’s experience with assembly operations. EVS Metal has a unique history in that when Amico and his partner Scott Berkowitz founded the company in 1994, it was to fabricate industrial rack mount enclosures for the growing PC market. It was in the assembly game right from the start.

Of course, EVS Metal has evolved to offering metal fabrication services and has expanded its assembly capabilities over the years. Today, it has locations in New Hampshire, New Jersey, Pennsylvania, and Texas.

The Fabricator:

How long has EVS Metal been involved in assembly activities? What did some of those early services look like?

Joe Amico:

EVS Metal’s been doing assembly for well over 20 years now. At the start, it was pretty straightforward—mostly riveting cabinets and installing basic hardware on enclosures we built. It just made sense early on as a way to give our customers a more complete product right out of our shop, saving them a step.

The Fabricator:

What led to offering more indepth services like electromechanical assembly and electronics/software installation? When did these types of sophisticated assembly operations start?

Amico:

Honestly, getting into the more complex electromechanical work came right from our customers asking for more and our sales team always looking to solve the next problem. We saw that doing the integration work here really simplified things on their end—fewer vendors to juggle and fixed assembly costs they could budget for.

As we started talking about those capabilities more, the projects became more sophisticated. While there was a learning curve and some early jobs were tough lessons, the commitment was always there: deliver exactly what the customer needed, period.

The Fabricator:

What was it like when EVS Metal got its first complete build-to-order job? Is this type of work common for the company today?

Amico:

That first true box build, handling everything from our metal to all the sourced components and final testing—that was a big step, definitely a challenge back then. It pushed us to build skills way beyond just cutting or bending metal—managing BOMs, sourcing, developing test procedures.

Now, though, it’s fundamental. End-to-end electromechanical assembly is a core part of what we offer across our facilities, especially for demanding industries like medical, transportation, and energy. It’s just expected now, and we’re set up for it.

The Fabricator:

How has the role of an assembler changed over the years? What new skills are necessary today that weren’t needed 10 years ago?

Amico:

Fundamentally, putting parts together is still putting parts together. But assemblers today need to be sharper. They’re interpreting more complex prints and digital work instructions and handling more in-process quality control documentation. Plus, the pace is faster; projects ramp up quicker.

So, while the hand skills are still vital, that adaptability, the ability to learn a new build quickly—those are really key skills now. It makes solid training and the ability for our experienced leads to show them the ropes more important than ever.

The Fabricator:

Do EVS Metal facilities have dedicated space for assembly operations? How does the company maintain flexibility in this area?

Amico:

Oh, we absolutely have dedicated assembly areas in all our shops. It keeps things organized and separate from the heavy fabrication. But those areas have to be flexible. We run such a high mix of custom jobs, maybe hundreds of different assemblies a year across the company, setting up a permanent line for just one product usually doesn’t make economic sense for anyone involved. We rely heavily on modular workstations; flexible layouts; and well-trained, adaptable teams that can pivot between projects efficiently.

The Fabricator:

What are the greatest challenges when it comes to staffing this area?

Amico:

The biggest headache, frankly, is the fluctuation that comes with custom, project-based work. Predicting the exact number of people needed week to week can be tough. So, we keep a solid core team of our own experienced EVS people, the ones who really know our systems and provide the training. Then, for the big surges or specialized projects, we partner strategically with good staffing agencies. It gives us flexibility, and honestly, it’s also a great way to find talented people we can bring on permanently later.

The Fabricator:

Are assemblers typically newer employees, or do they migrate from other areas of the plants?

Amico:

A lot of folks do start with us in assembly. It’s a good way into the manufacturing business without needing, say, five years of press brake experience right off the bat.

But the key is having that mix. You need the newer people bringing their energy, balanced with seasoned leads who provide more of the real hands-on guidance and can ensure quality.

Our detailed, often visual, work instructions are crucial too. In a custom shop running different jobs constantly, clear instructions make training much more effective and consistent.

The Fabricator:

With automation playing such an important role in blanking, forming, and welding, can it play a similar role in the assembly area? Has EVS Metal seen any success with applying automation tools in the assembly area?

Amico:

We look at automation constantly, but for the kind of high-mix, often complex custom assembly we do, it’s tough. Cobots and full automation often struggle with the dexterity needed for intricate wiring or installing a wide variety of fasteners. The payback just isn’t there when the assembly might change significantly on the next order.

Where we have found success and continue to invest is in automating the workflow around the assembler—things like roller lines for moving larger units, smart racking, and efficient part presentation. These are things that make the assembler’s job safer and more efficient, rather than trying to replace their skilled hands entirely for these types of builds.

The Fabricator:

How do you see EVS Metal’s assembly operations changing over the next five to 10 years? Will reshoring boost the need for more fab shops offering assembly services, or do OEMs look to take more control of their supply chains and bring manufacturing and assembly back in-house?

Amico:

Assembly’s only going to get more technically sophisticated, more integrated. Absolutely, the reshoring discussion boosts demand for capable domestic partners like EVS who handle the whole process. While some OEMs might talk about bringing assembly back in-house for control, honestly, many recognize how challenging that is—managing component sourcing, investing in the equipment and space, and especially finding and keeping the skilled labor. I think the bigger trend will be OEMs seeking stronger partnerships with suppliers like us who can act as that reliable, skilled extension of their own operation, taking on more complexity right here in the U.S. to simplify their lives and speed up their time to market.
