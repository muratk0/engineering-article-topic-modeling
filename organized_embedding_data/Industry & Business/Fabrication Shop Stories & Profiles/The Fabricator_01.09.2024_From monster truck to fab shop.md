Minnesota fabricator chases small-business dream

Rafael Guerrero

Y

ou could argue that driving a monster truck or starting a small business are scary propositions for some people. However, a 27-year-old welder/fabricator from Minnesota has navigated both scenarios and lived to tell the tale.

Earlier this year, Kasey Kromschroeder left her job of about two years at St. Cloud, Minn.-based Triple B Motorsports, where she was a monster truck driver and lead fabricator, and shifted her focus full time toward her small business, K2 Fabrication.

It’s been a long time coming. Kromschroeder started K2 as a side business in 2018, and going out on her own full time has drawn attention to her work and commitment to custom metal fabrications.

"I have these people come back to me multiple times because someone was willing to put a little bit of thought into their own projects that they’re passionate about, and I think that’s super cool," Kromschroeder said. "That’s what motivates me. That’s what drives me to continue doing work that is definitely not easy."

Up to the Challenge

Kromschroeder’s 24- by 32-ft. space in Princeton, Minn., is a "reasonable shop to start with," she said. She has several items at her disposal, including welding machines, a tubing bender, band saw, belt sander, and a plasma table is coming soon, she said. She also would like to get a finger brake someday.

Some of the challenging requests Kromschroeder has received from customers—jobs that perhaps other fabricators wouldn’t or couldn’t take on—include custom work, automotive welding and fabrication, off-road fabrication and chassis work, parts repair and rework, trophies and decor, or new products. Her most notable jobs that required skill and out-of-the-box thinking include a turbo kit for a pulling semi, a custom aluminum fuel cell, and a steerable pit cart for a racing team.

Kromschroeder’s K2 Fabrication specializes in custom projects, automotive welding and fabrication, off-road fabrication and chassis work, parts repair and rework, trophies and decor, and new products.

"That’s where I get the most satisfaction. I like a big challenge—something that makes you think and pushes you to learn something new and figure something out. It feels good," she said.

"The people who come and pick their projects up are always ecstatic to see it done," Kromschroeder added. "They’re working on something very custom and they’re trying to figure it out for themselves and then have this piece, but they can’t find someone else willing to put the time and heart into it. It’s cool, and it makes for very good connections."

It Runs in the Family

Before going full time with K2, Kromschroeder spent years working in machining, welding, fabricating, and motorsports. Most notably, she drove monster trucks and worked as lead fabricator for Triple B Motorsports. She comes from a motorsport family, with a dad and brother who have racing experience as well.

"The actual driving of the truck is like being on a roller coaster you’re in control of," she said. "The donuts, everything, you’re along for the ride but you’re in control."

For about two years, she drove and maintained the trucks, drove the rigs that carried the trucks, rebuilt chassis, repaired broken parts, and worked on multiple trucks. "I was the go-to for all the welding," she said about her experience.

Kromschroeder first began welding around age 15 or 16, in high school and at home. She said her dad helped her out by bringing her scrap metal to practice on.

"He dumped out the scrap bin and fired up our old welder and pretty much just said to start welding things together," Kromschroeder said. "So, there I was out in the garage welding all of our scrap metal together.

"Welding goes hand in hand with fabrication," she added. "I get to learn a lot of new things and think and be challenged every day in this career, which I think is what’s kept me going all this time."

Machining also runs in the family, with Kromschroeder’s dad having been a machinist at one point. She considered following in his footsteps and becoming a machinist, too, but ultimately she wanted to weld. After spending two years as a machinist, she switched over to welding and remained at a welding shop for six years.

"After those two years, I realized I wanted to work more with my hands and not so much standing behind the controls of a machine," she said of her switch to welding.

More to Learn

Right now, Kromschroeder works by herself and said she is OK with that. Someday, she would like a bigger shop with more space.

Going full time with K2 has allowed Kromschroeder to cross a big goal off her list this year. Now she can focus on some of her smaller goals, which are geared toward becoming a better, more well-rounded fabricator.

For instance, she wants to weld titanium.

"I’ve done it a few times, but I want to get better at it," Kromschroeder said. "I get asked about it and I find it very cool, and I want to spend time doing it."

An off-roading enthusiast, Kromschroeder said she would like to build a rock crawler chassis. Not only would it be a personal achievement for her, but such a project could attract more attention to K2 and her capabilities.

"I’m very passionate about rock crawling. I have a little tube chassis rock crawler that has grown with me over the last couple of years," she said. "I want to take my new knowledge from monster trucks, from welding, from fab, to build something that reflects all that learning and that I can take out to the trails."
