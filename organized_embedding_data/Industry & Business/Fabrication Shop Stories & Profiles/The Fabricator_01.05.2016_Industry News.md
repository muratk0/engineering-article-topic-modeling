FMA Safety Conference: Recognizing the impact of safety

"Cost is not an issue for us at Dupps. The owners are going to spend whatever they can to make sure that employees are safe," said Jim Vose, human resources director, The Dupps Co., at the opening of the Fabricators & Manufacturers Association’s 2016 Safety Conference.

That’s not a statement that those in the metal fabricating community hear regularly. In fact, most companies likely have a lot of work to do to ensure that shop floor employees are as safe as possible as they undertake their daily tasks. After all, a metal fabricating facility can be a dangerous—and sometimes deadly—place.

Vose was asked to address the 2016 edition of the Safety Conference, held in Dayton, Ohio, April 13 and 14, because his company won the 2015 Rusty Demeules Award for Safety Excellence. The Dupps Co., an 80-year-old manufacturer of industrial equipment used to recycle animal byproducts for such things as oils and protein for pet food, has maintained a stellar safety record in recent years, but it also has recognized that an investment in safety can be healthy for the company’s bottom line.

To illustrate the point, Vose pointed to the figures. Dupps has 150 employees at its Germantown, Ohio, facility, with 60 percent of them being on the shop floor. The average age of a Dupps worker is 46 years old, and Vose said the company is seeing about four retirements per year. Even with an older workforce, the company went 8.5 years without a lost-time injury. That’s 2.5 million work hours, according to Vose, which translates into a lot of productivity.

"We push safety down to every shop employee," Vose said. "That’s not meant to be negative. We just make everyone responsible."

Dupps has gone beyond a focus on shop floor safety, however, and is looking at the general health of employees. (To learn more about Dupps’ shop floor safety success, see "Safety: For the people, by the people,"

The FABRICATOR

, July 2015, p. 82.)

The company has an on-site medical office where a retired doctor sets up shop at least once a week. Company employees and their spouses can stop by if they have ailments such as a cold, the flu, or a sore throat. The doctor also is involved in preventive measures, such as skin cancer awareness efforts and stroke prevention education.

As part of an overall wellness campaign within the company, if an employee hits three goals, he or she can save $1,300 in health care.

Vose said the on-site medical visits, which are not billed to the health insurance provider, and the wellness program have helped the company avoid big increases in health insurance costs. Over the past six years, Vose estimated the savings at more than $800,000.

Considering the Bottom Line

That’s a noteworthy dollar amount, and it’s the sort of figure that gets the attention of owners and managers of metal fabricating facilities, according to Brian Roberts, director, ergonomic services, CNA Risk Control Services, during a presentation called "The Business Case for Lean." Those that control the budgets in metal fabricating shops bought into lean because they understood that it eliminated waste and improved processes, saving time and money. Safety offers the same potential for positive budget impacts, Roberts said.

"What does senior management understand? The bottom line," Roberts added.

For instance, Roberts referenced a company that invested in a pneumatic bander for a worker responsible for banding together material to be shipped to a customer. Prior to using the new equipment, the worker needed about 70 seconds to band a load, and he banded about 145 loads per day. The manual task posed an ergonomic risk to the worker’s wrist, which might have led to an injury down the road.

One of the highlights of the annual FMA Safety Conference is the awards ceremony in which facilities with minimal injury and illness incidence rates are honored.

The pneumatic bander addressed the ergonomic issue and reduced the banding task to 40 seconds per load. Roberts said that translated into about 1.6 hours per day (40 seconds × 145 loads = 5,800 seconds). If that is extrapolated to 250 working days, that’s 400 hours per banding employee. Depending on the hourly rate of pay, this could be a tremendous savings in labor costs.

Cam Valle, general manager, Plymouth Tube, Streator, Ill., speaks about some of the steps his plant has taken to earn FMA’s 2016 Rusty Demeules Award for Safety Excellence. He discussed employee engagement with safety practices and a daily stretch program to get everyone ready for the shift.

Roberts added that this type of critical thinking has to be applied to the everyday activities on the metal fabricating shop floor because manufacturing workers are not getting any younger.

"We are going to have to understand that the 54-year-old worker is not going to be able to do the same job as that 25-year-old. It’s time to start looking at the design of the jobs," he said.

Examples From the Real World

During a panel discussion, Michael Moses, quality and safety manager, Buhler Aeroglide, Cary, N.C., said company management gave safety more attention when they were able to witness a real impact on the company’s finances. After a couple years of operating without any lost time due to injuries, the company saw its employee modification rate (EMR) decrease. Insurance companies use the EMR to gauge both past cost of injuries and future chance of risk. In other words, when the EMR decreases, a company’s worker compensation insurance premiums decrease as well.

That attitude change toward safety started on the shop floor however, Moses said.

"When you used to ask people who was responsible for safety, they would look at me. Now when you ask them that question, they look at each other," Moses said.

On the same panel, Darrel Shankles, health and safety manager, PlayCore, Fort Payne, Ala., shared an example similar to Roberts’. At another manufacturing facility he witnessed a shop employee using an old, unreliable nail gun that added an extra two minutes to the job of putting together a pallet. When a past request for a new nail gun was sent to management, decision-makers didn’t see the importance. When the request was supplemented with a presentation describing just how much labor costs would be saved over 250 workdays with a new, reliable nail gun, management delivered a quick OK.

The Real Costs

Some fabricating company owners and managers may not take these types of savings seriously, but they still should take safety more seriously in their own facilities. The Occupational Safety and Health Administration is making certain of that.

The agency announced in late 2015 that fines for workplace safety violations would increase for the first time since 1990. The increase could be as much as 80 percent over current fine levels. The increases are expected to be announced in August.

C4 takes on Dallas

"If a Texas shop isn’t in oil and gas, business is probably busting at the seams right now. Everything is really strong now, in fact, unless of course you’re into oil and gas. Sure, West Texas and Houston are heavily involved in oil and gas. But that will change quickly. As soon as they put people back to work, they will start machines that haven’t been running."

So said Adam Smelewski, the regional sales manager for Wood Dale, Ill.-based MC Machinery Systems Inc. who serves the Texas market. On March 30 Smelewski and others were attending an event celebrating the opening of a C4 Industrial Supply showroom and technical center in Farmers Branch, Texas, just north of Dallas. With a home office in Tulsa, Okla., C4 has grown with additional offices in Oklahoma City, Houston, and, as of earlier this year, Farmers Branch.

"We’re very happy to be here to start this new adventure in the Texas market," said Bill Isaac, MC Machinery Systems’ vice president of sales and marketing. "The key to success is taking care of customers locally … we know oil and gas is down, but it will be back. We’ve been through this before, and we want to be ready for the recovery."

In a separate interview, Vivek Gupta, president of Texas ProFab in nearby Carrollton, added that Texas—and particularly Dallas—isn’t all about the energy market. "Oil and gas is highly regionalized in Texas," he said. "Beaumont, Houston, West Texas—those places are more exposed than others. But for the most part, Texas has so much more. And it is such a favorable environment to do business in. We’re doing business with so many companies that are moving in from out of state. They need a local supplier, and that’s good news for those of us supplying them."

Along with Mitsubishi lasers, EDMs, and Dener press brakes from MC Machinery Systems, C4 carries equipment from AKS Cutting Systems, HE&M Saw, LENOX, and others.

C4 founder Fred Raschen got his start in metal manufacturing representing steel suppliers, and oddly enough, if it weren’t for the steel shortage of the 1970s, C4 likely wouldn’t be the company it is today. When representing steel suppliers, Raschen and his team didn’t have enough steel to sell, so they did the commonsense thing: They asked what else companies needed.

It turned out that they needed drill bits. This was in Tulsa, what was and still is a major center of heat exchanger manufacturing. "I sold $80,000 worth of drill bits that first year," Raschen said.

In one sense, C4 grew into the company it is today by asking

what else

the customer needed—which may be one reason the organization carries such a wide variety of lines. And it all started with a few people in an office with a drawer full of drill bits.

Industrial laser systems market remains steady in 2015 in U.S.

Global sales of laser systems for materials processing increased by 10 percent in 2015 compared to the previous year measured in local currencies, according to the annual market survey conducted by Optech Consulting, Taegerwilen, Switzerland. However, in U.S. dollars, the increase was wiped out completely, and the market volume came in at $10.8 billion, level with 2014.

Market growth rates in local currencies of 10 percent or more were recorded for Europe, North America, Japan, and Korea. Sales in China increased by nearly 5 percent, with weakening demand toward the end of the year. Because of the shift of currency exchange rates, China surpassed Europe as the world’s largest consumer of industrial laser systems for the first time in history, in spite of the comparably low growth rate for the Chinese market.

High-power laser systems for cutting, welding, and surface treatment account for a 45 percent share of the market. Laser systems for microelectronics processing make up 20 percent. The balance of the market is low- and medium-power laser material processing systems, such as for cutting, welding, marking, additive manufacturing, cleaning, engraving, and soldering.

For 2016 Optech Consulting expects a sideward trending demand for laser materials processing systems worldwide. The expectation follows sales and order booking for industrial lasers and laser systems, as well as for machine tools and electronic manufacturing equipment.

OSHA updates eye and face protection standards in final rule

The Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA), Washington, D.C., has published a final rule that updates requirements for personal protective equipment for workers in general industry, shipyards, longshoring, marine terminals, and construction. The final rule, which reflects current national consensus standards, ensures that workers can use up-to-date eye and face protection.

The rule updates references in OSHA’s Eye and Face Protection Standards to recognize ANSI/ISEA Z87.1-2010,

Occupational and Educational Personal Eye and Face Protection Devices

, while deleting the outdated 1986 edition of that same national consensus standard. OSHA also is retaining the 2003 and 1989 (R-1998) versions of the ANSI standard already referenced in its standard.

In addition, the final rule updates the construction standard by deleting the 1968 version of the ANSI standard that was referenced and now includes the same three ANSI standards referenced above to ensure consistency among the agency’s standards.

OSHA’s final rule became effective on April 25, 2016.

U.S. manufacturing still most competitive worldwide, says report

Although U.S. manufacturing is currently facing meaningful headwinds from a stronger dollar and the collapse in investment in the shale energy sector, it remains the most competitive worldwide, according to a new report released by New York-based Oxford Economics.

The rapid and broad-based 20 percent U.S. dollar appreciation since mid-2014 has done some significant damage to U.S. manufacturing competitiveness. However, three factors have offset the hit from the stronger currency: the dollar was arguably the most competitive it has been before the surge; U.S. manufacturing productivity is the strongest in the world; and the U.S. has a stable regulatory framework, a flexible labor market, low energy costs, and access to a large domestic market, said Gregory Daco, head of U.S. economics with Oxford Economics.

The report makes the following additional points:

• Since 2003 productivity growth in the U.S. has outpaced most of its peers, with manufacturing output per employee rising about 40 percent (2.5 percent per annum on average) from 2003 to 2016 compared with only 25 percent growth in Germany and 30 percent growth in the U.K. While productivity growth has lagged that of Japan, the U.S. manufacturing sector remains 25 percent more productive. Likewise, while productivity has doubled in India and China, the U.S. manufacturing sector remains 80 to 90 percent more productive.

• Most advanced economies have seen similar compensation growth over the past 13 years, but stronger productivity in the U.S. has allowed it to maintain generally lower unit labor costs. Since wage growth in China has largely outpaced productivity growth, and the renminbi has strengthened, China’s unit labor costs are now only 4 percent lower than in the U.S.

• While reshoring is growing, especially in the most competitive and export-intensive U.S. manufacturing sectors, Mexico offers a nearshoring alternative with costs 10 percent lower than in China, a stable regulatory environment, and generally unhindered access to the U.S. market.

• The baseline forecast shows the U.S. dollar appreciating another 3 to 5 percent through 2017 before gradually depreciating thereafter. In this environment, the U.S. manufacturing sector will maintain its edge. Another 20 percent appreciation of the dollar, on the other hand, would certainly dent U.S. competitiveness, make China an attractive production hub again, and give Japanese manufacturers a significant advantage over U.S. firms.

FMA names Plymouth Tube as winner of 2016 Rusty Demeules Award for Safety Excellence

Rockford, Ill.-based Fabricators & Manufacturers Association Intl. (FMA) has announced Plymouth Tube of Streator, Ill., as the winner of the 2016 Rusty Demeules Award for Safety Excellence. The award, presented by FMA and sponsored by insurance provider CNA, was created by FMA’s Safety Council to recognize a company with a demonstrated safety culture. The award is named in honor of Rusty Demeules, a significant volunteer contributor to FMA, who was president of the FMA Safety Committee from 1989 to 1996.

Plymouth Tube has 13 locations. Its Streator plant manufactures cold-drawn, seamless alloy, and carbon tubing from steel hollows, specializing in small diameters. It also provides shaped tubing such as squares and rectangles.

Plymouth Tube’s commitment to safety includes its Safety Engagement Program, which focuses on safety tours, meetings, observations, and safety suggestions from employees and helps to generate improvements throughout the facility. Employees are also required to participate in a daily stretch program to help ensure their minds and bodies are ready for a safe day at work.

Los Angeles-based National New Markets Fund LLC has closed $21 million in New Markets Tax Credit (NMTC) allocation that will enable

Commercial Metals Co. (CMC),

Irving, Texas, to build an environmentally sustainable steel manufacturing facility in Durant, Okla.

When completed in late 2017, the micro-mill will use an uninterrupted production line process called "continuous-continuous" powered by a highly efficient electric arc furnace (EAF). The result will be a clean and green steel mill capable of producing 380,000 tons of rebar from recycled pre- and post-consumer scrap, reports the company.

The company anticipates the facility will reduce CO

2

emissions by at least 58 percent compared to a traditional iron ore-based steel plant. Gases produced in the steelmaking process will be used to preheat scrap, which in turn will reduce electricity consumption. The mill also will employ advanced air filtering and recycling technology to enhance recovery and reuse of common steelmaking byproducts. In addition, water used in the steelmaking process will be recycled.

The new mill is expected to create 300 jobs. The site is located within a multicounty area designated as a federal Promise Zone for the Choctaw Nation.

CMC and its subsidiaries manufacture, recycle, and market steel and metal products, related materials, and services.
