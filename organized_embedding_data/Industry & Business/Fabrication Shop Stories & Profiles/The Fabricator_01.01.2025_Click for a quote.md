n the fall of 2024, manufacturers with a need for tube fabrication began to get a taste of what instant quoting is like. Xometry, a digital marketplace with an extensive network of shops, began offering instant quoting for work involving tube cutting and rotary draw bending.

"Using AI and machine learning tools, we’ve been working to expand our menu to include services that are typically a headache to source. Tube has been in that category for a while."

That was Greg Paulsen, director of applications engineering and marketing at the North Bethesda, Md.-based company. That capability is the latest in the evolution of what has become a relatively new kind of company in the manufacturing space: a digital marketplace.

Considering the industry’s fragmentation, the business model already had potential when Xometry came on the scene more than a decade ago. Now with the ever-increasing information processing power of artificial intelligence (AI), the digital marketplace approach could find a long-term, sustainable place in the metal fabrication value chain.

The Digital Marketplace in Manufacturing

Digital marketplaces in manufacturing have existed in the form of online bidding for years. Xometry, however, takes a different approach. It gives an instant quote to a customer; when the customer accepts, Xometry posts the job at network shops, each vetted to ensure they have the capability to deliver the services the customer needs, be it laser cutting, bending, machining, or anything else.

Accessing available jobs through a platform called Work Center, shops see the price and can choose whether to accept the job. Work Center also serves as a kind of manufacturing execution system (MES) platform to track project milestones. (As Paulsen explained, the MES complements, but does not replace, any internal software a shop might use.)

"When a customer uploads a file, we know when that happens down to the second," Paulsen said. "And from the order, our suppliers are using our MES to track jobs and attach notes. This makes us incredibly auditable. We also get pricing feedback. This all plays into our machine learning feedback loop."

This gives Xometry the information it needs to provide what it calls a partner success score, or PSS. "A better score allows partners to take on higher-revenue, more complex work," Paulsen said.

At this point, Xometry has years’ worth of pricing data, and the company’s algorithms mine job characteristics to dial in those instant quotes. This data gives the company an idea of manufacturing attributes of specific part geometries from an array of manufacturing processes: machining and 3D printing; sheet metal or tube cutting, bending, and welding; stamping; plastic injection molding; and urethane casting.

The digital marketplace model seems to have some legs. A publicly traded company, Xometry has seen significant growth in recent years. 2023 revenue totaled $463 million, with more than 64,000 active buyers—almost double the number of buyers in 2022—purchasing parts from a network of thousands of manufacturers.

"Just last quarter, we grew our marketplace by 25%," said Randy Altschuler, Xometry CEO and co-founder, during a Google Cloud roundtable press event at IMTS in September. The company began working with Google Cloud Vertex AI in 2023. "We’re using algorithms to match jobs with the right manufacturer, and those are getting smarter and smarter as we get more data. Over time, our ability to predict what the manufacturing cost will be has increased substantially, and you can see that in our financial results … our margins have grown. As we’ve gotten more data, we’ve been able to train the [pricing and cost] models more efficiently."

In one sense, the business model sounds a bit counterintuitive, considering all the pricing and cost pressures fab shops face. Raw material has gotten more expensive, and customers want better prices. Is there room for a digital marketplace in the middle of such a hypercompetitive market?

"Our pricing is competitive with the direct-shop [approach] while still maintaining a margin," Paulsen said. "For companies who partner with us, we represent a consistent revenue stream. And they’re not putting in overhead or paying commissions to get a sales lead. It’s just pure work."

The model overcomes some inherent supply chain inefficiencies in manufacturing. First is in quoting. According to recent KPI surveys from the Fabricators and Manufacturers Association (FMA), average bid-win rates in custom fabrication hover around 30%, and typical quote turnaround times are between three and four days.

Then there’s the demand variability issue. Back to the FMA KPI studies, recent reports show that between six and 10 companies, the "whale accounts," make up half the revenue pie of the typical fab shop. When demand rises from most or all of those whale accounts at once, the shop runs at full speed and might subcontract to get all the work out the door. When demand falls, machines sit idle, and manufacturing capacity remains unused.

There’s also the delay in collections. Average days in accounts receivable (DAR) in custom fabrication have been between 45 and 50 days, according to FMA surveys. Shops might deliver in a hurry, but they’re not paid in a hurry.

Moreover, while fabricators might work to become a one-stop shop, OEM customers might dual-source and use other strategies to ensure consistent supply. Related to this are risks associated with being tied to specific manufacturing technologies and materials. What if the product design changes? What if once fabricated components become plastic or extrusions? One fabricator can only do so much, but what about an expansive network of shops?

All these factors could build the case for the digital manufacturing marketplace model. Shops can fill unused capacity with work from Xometry. Paulsen added that for many, Xometry work helps diversify the revenue pie. It also uses financial technology to facilitate faster payments. "In the MES platform, when they scan a shipment release, shops will get a notification showing the payment and when exactly it will be coming. We’re consistent payers, and the process is transparent. In this business, cash flow is king."

From the buyer’s perspective, Xometry’s network gives them geographic and supplier diversity, Paulsen said. "Instead of companies trying to locate different vendors to do numerous parts, they can go to one vendor, and we can assure quality. We’re ISO 9001, AS9100 for our aerospace customers, ISO 13485 for medical device manufacturing, and IATF 16949 for automotive manufacturing. For these, we have flowdown requirements for our suppliers. When you select these in the quoting engine, that initiates a filter. For instance, we offer the ability to do ITAR work, and so if you have an ITAR job, you click the box, and that narrows the network. The price and lead time might change. But here still, we have a network of redundant suppliers for this type of work."

He added that Xometry has an inspection lab in Maryland dedicated to shop vetting and qualification, as well as specialized inspection services that provide another layer of quality assurance, especially for mission- or life-critical components.

From Low Quantity to Production

Lower-dollar orders still make up a significant portion of Xometry’s work. That said, a growing number of customers order more than $50,000 a year in parts; more than 1,500 did so in 2024—up from 700 in 2021 and just 389 in 2020, according to the company’s Q2 investor presentation.

When you streamline the ordering process, the positive ripple effects could run up and down the metal fabrication value chain.

The spectrum of work Xometry sees uses AI and software automation at varying levels. Low- to medium-volume, discrete-part work is usually quoted automatically, though it also can give design for manufacturability (DFM) feedback—pointing out potential issues in manufacturing and shipping.

"Design for manufacturability is a big part of our business," Paulsen said. "We have DFM tools on our platform, including CAD plug-ins."

More complex orders, like those involving assemblies and other services, are handled by an in-house team. Software streamlines the process but doesn’t eliminate human intervention. "Certain orders might, say, require specific finishes," Paulsen said. "In this case, we’ll flag those in our system saying we’ll manually review the order before finalizing the quote. The customer clicks on the manual quote option, which sends it to our internal team. We develop a price, and if the customer accepts the quote, there’s a feedback loop for our training data for jobs that require specific surface finishes."

The company also offers consultative quoting. As Paulsen explained, "This goes beyond, ‘Here’s your part, here’s your price.’ With these orders, we have other factors to address to understand what you need. This again includes DFM feedback." He added that "many of our internal quoting team members used to run shops themselves, so they have a lot of expertise."

Some large orders, like a medical part or assembly that requires certain certified facilities, can be handled differently. So can certain fabrication orders involving very large parts or assemblies that require some complex shipping and logistics considerations. In these cases, Paulsen said, Xometry will work with specific manufacturers in its network and, via an online platform (called Teams Space), facilitate collaboration between all parties involved, offer digital job tracking, and provide a place to consolidate what can be an extensive bill of materials (BOMs). "Customers come to us for instant quoting," Paulsen said, "but they stay with us for the supply chain management."

Moving Forward With Tube

The more processes Xometry takes on—like tube bending and cutting—the more costing and pricing data it gathers. For laser tube cutting and bending in particular, the quoting engine "learned," after several months of trials with tube fabricators in the Xometry network, to identify what is and isn’t a tube in a 3D CAD file.

"We’ll use computational geometry to interpret a model," Paulsen said. "For instance, a tubular component should not be confused with a lathe-turned part. Here, our team built different techniques to first answer the question, ‘Is this a tube?’ And then, ‘Is this a straight profile that is laser cut, or does it require bending?’ The next question is, ‘How much does it cost?’ And that’s where we use costing models from machine learning. The system then knows, ‘Parts like this, in this quantity and with these features in these materials, cost this much in a competitive marketplace.’"

For bending of round tube, the quoting engine can identify issues with tolerances and the requested centerline radius (Xometry recommends a radius-to-tube-diameter ratio of between 2-to-1 and 5-to-1). It checks to see if the tube in the model matches in-stock tubes or is at least close to a widely available stock size. If it’s close, it recommends an alternative. It also checks for sufficient distance between bends that gives the tube bending machine something to grasp.

"The manufacturing standards are widely accepted," Paulsen said, "and it helps guide people toward designs that give them a lot of manufacturing options, rather than be limited to specialized equipment or techniques."

Paulsen added that if customers are OK with recommended changes, they can accept the quote and move forward with the job. For other DFM feedback, engineers might need to return to the CAD model and make adjustments.

As it does with other types of work, for tubing, the quoting engine doesn’t handle all types of jobs. For instance, it doesn’t handle the bending of rectangular tubing. It’s also limited to the rotary draw process, not compression or other types of tube bending applications. And quoting complex jobs still requires some manual intervention.

Still, as AI continues to learn and evolve, tapping into thousands of part files and pricing and cost data across thousands of suppliers and customers, quoting even the most complex work might become a lot easier in the years to come. Indeed, this could apply both to manufacturing networks like Xometry as well as to fabricators that implement automated quoting in-house. A handful of custom fabricators already offer automated quoting with direct, customer-facing web portals, and more may enter the fray in the coming years. When you streamline the ordering process, the positive ripple effects could run up and down the metal fabrication value chain.
