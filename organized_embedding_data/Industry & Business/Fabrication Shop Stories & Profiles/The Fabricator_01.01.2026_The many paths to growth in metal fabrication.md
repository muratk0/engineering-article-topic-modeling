# The many paths to growth in metal fabrication

Biz Talk

More consolidation on the horizon, but also opportunity

By

Tim Heston

I

t’s 2026—already. Will this be a year of growth and opportunity, or one defined by slowdowns and sluggish sales? The answer will depend on the customer mix and the industries a shop serves. All things connected to AI, including power generation and related infrastructure, remain hot; other sectors, perhaps not so much.

The AI boom has created some significant opportunities for organic growth. Here, metal fabricators sit in an advantageous market position. Their business model is sheet metal, plate, and tube manufacturing—or, more specifically, manufacturing using certain methods and machines: punching, laser cutting, bending, welding, powder coating. This means they can ride the AI boom and still not be beholden to it. If the AI boom falters, they have plenty of other sectors to pursue. Combine this fact with aging owners looking for an exit strategy, and you have a business environment ripe for dealmaking.

James Rutt of Prairie Partners and Gary Kane of Corporate Fuel Advisors both are watching the market closely. The two authored a report (now published on

thefabricator.com

) that details the sheer variety of deals happening in the metal manufacturing space. These include vertical plays that put distributors and contract fabricators under the same ownership umbrella, as well as horizontal plays that aim to expand a metal fabricator’s markets and technical capabilities.

The most dramatic M&A has occurred at the distributor level, the chief being the merger between Ryerson Holdings and Olympic Steel. Those two industry giants also offer plenty of first-step fabrication (cutting and bending) and own various metal fabricators.

The structure represents the quintessential example of vertical integration, at least when it comes to ownership. How integrated and consolidated on-the-ground operations become is another matter. Geography matters, and so do local sales relationships and branding.

Numerous other deals in the metal manufacturing space have occurred in just the past few years. A brief look at a few of them illustrates just how diverse the metal manufacturing space is becoming. Cadrex Manufacturing Solutions remains one of the more dramatic "horizontal plays" in the past five years. Over a period of just 15 months, throughout 2021 and the beginning of 2022, the private equity group behind Cadrex, CORE Industrial Partners, acquired 10 metal fabricators. Some were focused on aerospace and defense; others boasted large machining operations; still others had extensive automation focusing on precision sheet metal fabrication.

This series of deals sought to scale through both redundant and expanded capabilities.

Redundant capabilities

—like similar laser cutting, bending, or welding in multiple locations—allow Cadrex to move work to different plants as capacity and demand shifts require.

Expanded capabilities

allow Cadrex to offer additional services, like precision sheet metal and advanced 5-axis machining.

Rutt and Kane cited several more recent examples of horizontal plays in metal fabrication, including two by Mayville Engineering Co. (MEC). Its most recent deal with Accu-Fab, an acquisition that closed earlier last year, gained MEC a significant foothold in the AI infrastructure market. Another deal with Mid-States Aluminum Corp., in June 2023, helped expand the company’s capabilities into the extrusions space.

Rutt and Kane cited a transaction that highlights another kind of "horizontal play" in metal manufacturing; the diversified holding company. "[In August 2025], Brattle Technologies purchased Lockwood Manufacturing. This is a prime example of another acquisition type happening in metal manufacturing.

Brattle is a diversified holding company of specialty businesses, including a range of metal manufacturers that now include Lockwood, a sheet metal fabricator specializing in equipment for food service, health care, hospitality, and laboratory sectors."

With all the dealmaking and consolidation, not to mention the level of capex shops now need to compete, you might think the barrier to entry for a startup is as high as ever. Large corporations pushing out the "little guy" seems to be a common narrative throughout the U.S. economy.

Thing is, the "little guy" in metal fabrication remains remarkably resilient. Just recently, I talked with Jack Rosemark of Freedom Fabricators, Jupiter, Fla. The company launched, like so many others, out of a garage. Rosemark had worked at Sikorsky, and he likely could have spent a career there, but he had the entrepreneurial itch. He launched Freedom in 2015 with a fork truck, a welding machine, and (most important) significant knowledge about what a high-quality cut, formed, and welded product should look like. The business got off the ground with a Facebook ad and word-of-mouth marketing. And in 2025, it moved into a larger facility.

The story (which we’ll be covering further in a future issue) illustrates two elements that keep small shops so resilient, and why so many in metal fabrication have that entrepreneurial itch. First is hands-on manufacturing knowledge. Today, that comes not just from time in front a machine but also from smart use of software and sheet metal design. Second is the wide variety of demand for fabricated metal products. That has meant that there’s no one way to grow a metal fabrication business.

The customer mix really defines the fabricator. Sometimes, that mix benefits from some dramatic growth, be it organic or through acquisition. That’s the case now with many shops serving booming markets surrounding AI. Other times, fabricators grow less dramatically, shaping a process to fit the unique needs of a specific, often carefully cultivated, set of customers. Sometimes, a shop’s growth ends in selling a business to a larger entity. Other times, the growth leads to sustaining a multigenerational private enterprise. The variety of different customer demands means there’s a variety of different opportunities.

All this means that metal fabrication will remain a dynamic business for 2026 and beyond. For those of us lucky enough to be in it, it’s not a bad place to be.
