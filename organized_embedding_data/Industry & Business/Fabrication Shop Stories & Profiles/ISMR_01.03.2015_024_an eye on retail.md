# AN EYE ON RETAIL

[TARİH: 01.03.2015 ISMR]

## JC Metalworks has invested in fibre laser cutting technology from Amada

As the oft-used idiom states, ‘mighty oaks from small acorns grow’. Few fit this phrase better than UK-based JC Metalworks. Started by Jack Chauhan in his garage in 1986, it has taken less than 30 years for the company to grow into a £5.3 million turnover, 72-employee, 60,000ft2 enterprise.

JC Metalworks offers an in-house solution for all sheet metal subcontracting requirements, from design and precision engineering services through to product manufacture, assembly (including electrical work and the fitting of non-metal elements such as wood and glass), and delivery. However, the company’s differentiating factor, and the secret behind its success, is its carefully planned, ongoing investment in modern, automated manufacturing equipment. This enables JC Metalworks to be flexible and highly responsive to customer needs.

In combination with three other founding principles – customer service, investment in people and development of processes – this family-owned and run company has gone from strength to strength.

## A focus on laser

Point-of-sale is the main market served by JC Metalworks, which accounts for 85 per cent of its business. Over the years, the company has become an expert in retail markets, understanding the sector’s products, lead-times and quality requirements. Retail shelving and racking for products such as confectionary, beauty products and tobacco are among its core business. Laser cutting is a primary process, which is why the company’s most recent investment is an FOL3015 AJ 4kW fibre laser from Amada.

“I always look to buy the best and fastest machine when I do my supplier research,” explained Jason Chauhan, the company’s managing director and son of the founder. “We had an existing CO2 laser from another supplier but, with 90 per cent of our business involving sheet of less than 1.5mm thick, it made sense to think about investing in fibre technology. After thoroughly researching the market and the machines on offer, we chose the Amada FOL3015 AJ. Many machines were considered, but Amada represented the best value for us in terms of manufacturing efficiency and automation. Amada also offers total reliability with regard to service.”

Duly acquired, the FOL3015 AJ with MP-F automated load/unload spends around 80 per cent of its time cutting mild steel of less than 1.5mm thick, although zintec, pre-galvanised steel, stainless steel and aluminium are also processed on the machine. Anywhere from 20 to 100 sheets are loaded at a time.

“It’s a lot quicker than our existing CO laser; probably two or three times faster, depending upon the part,” said Mr Chauhan. “However, aside from the speed, we are also experiencing enhanced cut quality, along with less maintenance thanks to features like automatic nozzle changing and cleaning.”

The FOL3015 AJ represents high specification fibre technology from the diode to the workpiece. Both the machine and beam source are developed by Amada. Material processing is rapid, thanks to 340mm/min. linear drives and 5G acceleration, while users can expect to save around 70 per cent in energy running costs in comparison with a CO machine. “Point-of-sale is the main market served by JC Metalworks, which accounts for 85 per cent of its business.”

“As well as eliminating the cost of outsourcing some of our previous laser cutting requirements, the machine has given us a real opportunity,” added Mr Chauhan. “Not only have we generated extra capacity, some of which we still have to fill, but it’s an excellent marketing tool. All of our customers who have seen the machine are really pleased to see that we’re investing in the latest manufacturing technologies.”

Along with the FOL3015 AJ, the company has more Amada technology on-site, including an EMZ3610 punch machine with PDC automatic tool changer and load/part remover automation, and two HFP series press brakes with BendCAM software.

Working as a sub-contractor supporting retail agencies and their clients – often well-known and high profile high street brands with their point-of-sale manufacturing requirements, it is crucial to JC Metalworks that all products are made to exact specifications and high standards, whilst remaining competitive in the marketplace. With this in mind, JC Metalworks is now working with trusted partners in the Gujarat region of India, from where the Chauhan family originate, to undertake some of the company’s metalworking processes.

“Having India on-board allows us to manufacture larger volumes on a competitive basis without affecting quality,” said Mr Chauhan. “This will open up opportunities for our clients to support their brands globally, as cost effectively as possible, while still making the most of our team’s skills and experience.

“Our success is based on the ability to build long-term partnerships with customers. Our employees work hard to understand and deliver what matters. Only then can we be truly confident that clients will become delighted partners.” ■

Schletter Inc., of Shelby, North Carolina, has invested in automated sheet metal and longspan shelf storage from STOPA

Schletter Inc, the subsidiary of German company Schletter GmbH, has gained first-hand experience in the storage of metal sheet and long materials in Tucson, Arizona, where it was originally established. The material there lay in cantilever racks and was transported to the machine tools using fork-lift trucks. Christoph Hackner, vice president of manufacturing at Schletter Inc., explained during a tour why a new concept was selected for the plant at Shelby, North Carolina (USA).

“It was already clear in the early phase of building planning that we would be investing in automatic storage systems for sheet metal. The aim was to devise a sustainable and economical strategy for handling raw materials and semi-finished parts,” he said.

That has now been achieved, stressed Hackner. Machines have been linked up to one another at short distances from the store, which in this case acts as a conveyor system. This option is being used by the company to run unmanned shifts, or ghost shifts, at night and from Friday afternoon to Saturday morning. Fork-lift traffic inside the building has been greatly reduced, creating a safer working environment whilst minimising damage to materials. Internal logistics costs have also been cut.

## Sheet metal store as a supply axis

A Krasser CENTURIO system cuts the thin sheets supplied in coils to the required dimensions, and pushes the sheets onto a flat pallet resting on a scissor lift table. The latter takes the sheets into the STOPA COMPACT automatic store, where they are picked up by the two-mast storage and retrieval unit (SRU). Further material is supplied by the operator to the store (which came into operation in October 2013) at a manual insertion station.

The sheet metal store is around 51.5m long, 8.4m high and 5.5m wide. Like the longspan shelving originating from STOPA Anlagenbau GmbH in Germany, it handles six to eight tons of steel and aluminium per shift. Designed for large and medium format metal sheet, and boasting 781 pallet positions and two loading heights, the storage system supplies sheet to several machine tools. These include a TRUMPF TruLaser 5030 laser cutting machine linked to the storage system by a double car – consisting of a charging car and discharging car in gantry design – and by an automatic handling unit. The double car takes sheets from the store to the machine and brings back semi-finished parts.

“Return storage of semi-finished parts is routine for us, because the sheet metal store also acts as a supply axis to the other systems,” explained Hackner.

For example, a transport car takes lasermachined parts to an RAS Multibend-Centre, which is used by Schletter for upward and downward swing-folding and supplies a robot with sheet cut to size. In addition, two TRUMPF folding machines and a RAS sheet metal shear are linked by transport cars. The material is transferred manually to the folding machines and the sheet metal shear. Barcode scanners installed at the stations allow operators to scan the printed-out transport orders, making access to the required material much easier. At every insertion or return storage operation, a weighing unit records the weight. Since the operator also has an eye on the future, he has kept open an option for extensions, earmarking an area for two stations to link up to a TruPunch 5000 punching machine.

Hackner watched as a storage and retrieval unit (SRU), that has picked up a flat pallet to be placed in storage, now speeds away towards its storage position.
