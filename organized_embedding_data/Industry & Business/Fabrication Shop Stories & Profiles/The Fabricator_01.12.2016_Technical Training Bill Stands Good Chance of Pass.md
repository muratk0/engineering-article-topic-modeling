A House bill (H.R. 5587) making changes to the Carl D. Perkins Career and Technical Education Act has already passed the House with only five dissenting votes. The Senate bill was still awaiting a vote in the Health, Education, Labor, and Pensions (HELP) Committee as of Nov. 8. The Senate sponsor of the bill is Sen. Orrin Hatch, R-Utah. His bill is called Innovation for Tomorrow’s Workforce Act (S. 3344).

The House bill is more comprehensive and makes a number of changes in the existing program, such as revising requirements for core indicators of performance, including state-adjusted levels of performance. The Hatch bill simply authorizes a new grant program providing funds to identify and support innovative strategies and activities to improve career and technical education and align workforce skills with labor market needs.

"In an election year full of partisan bickering, it is nice to see our lawmakers come together and pass a bill that will support thousands of manufacturing companies across the country and countless of workers," said Precision Machined Products Association Executive Director Bernie Nagle.

The U.S. Department of Labor recently reported that manufacturing job openings hit a 15-year high. The August 2016 report showed the number of positions available across the country increased to 353,000, up from 311,000 in 2015.

EPA Decides Not to Tighten Lead Air Emission Standard

Normally when discussing the Environmental Protection Agency’s regulatory doings, the news is bad: Manufacturing operations are to be affected negatively because of air or water standards being issued for the first time or being tightened. That’s not the case, however, with its latest announcement. The good news is that the agency just decided not to tighten its lead standard, which was last tightened, and substantially, in 2008.

The EPA is obligated to review many air standards—formally called National Ambient Air Quality Standards (NAAQS)—every five years, though that timetable often stretches, as it has here. In deciding to stay with both the current primary and secondary lead standards in 2016, the EPA said: "Currently, the highest concentrations occur near some metals industries where some individual locations have concentrations that exceed the NAAQS." Legislation to Delay Overtime Salary Rule Hung up in Senate

Typical of the partisan legislation hung up in this Congress is the bill to delay for six months implementation of the Labor Department’s new overtime rule. It was scheduled to go into effect Dec. 1.

That rule raises the minimum salary that typically exempts a white-collar employee from being paid overtime. The old minimum salary was $23,660 annually, and the new rules put it at $47,476. So many more employees will qualify for the new overtime rules.

In September, Rep. Tim Walberg, R-Mich., chairman of the House Subcommittee on Workforce Protections, introduced the Regulatory Relief for Small Businesses, Schools, and Nonprofits Act (H.R. 6094), which requires a six-month delay in the effective date of the rule. It passed the House in late September by a vote of 246 -177.

Sen. James Lankford, R-Okla., has introduced the companion bill (S. 3462). Senate sources indicate that discussions continue behind the scenes to see if the bill can be brought to a vote in the near future.
