# 5 steps to metal fabrication profitability

How the right KPIs drive a business forward

T

he customer and product mix really define a metal fabricator, a fact that makes this industry incredibly diverse. A heavy fab shop building large industrial assemblies is completely different from a precision sheet metal job shop. But one reality all fabricators face is that some customers and products are more profitable than others.

That’s why, during a presentation at the Fabricators and Manufacturers Association (FMA) Annual Meeting earlier this year, Doug Watts pointed to a simple chart. As CEO of Cincinnati-based Metalworking Group, Watts uses that chart, among other tools, to improve and shape the custom fabricator’s overarching business strategy.

The chart showed a hypothetical customer mix—no real names or numbers—but the idea was simple. The horizontal axis showed profitability; the vertical axis showed revenue (see

Figure 1

). The goal: Move as many jobs as possible up and to the right, toward greater revenue and profits. As the five steps that follow show, establishing, tracking, and improving the right key performance indicators (KPIs) can help make greater profitability a reality.

1. Know What Jobs Cost

To know how profitable a job is, a fabricator needs to know how much it costs. "To be honest, years ago, we really didn’t have good profitability numbers by product line and part number," Watts told his audience.

He explained that this had a lot to do with underutilizing enterprise resource planning (ERP) software, adding that ERP has come a long way in recent years. Workers clock in and out of jobs at workstations, and those timestamps are incredibly important. These incorporate rates for people and the rates for machines, depending on the value of the work center and the space it consumes. "At least at our shop, we build overhead rates into the work center rate," Watts said.

Steve Zerio, co-presenter at the conference and president of Triumph Partners, added that exact costing practices can vary. Whatever the method, though, tracking activities to establish costs is critical. "You need to know where your costs are coming from," he said.

This includes material costs, which of course have been on the rise in recent years. Say that material incorporates a large percentage of a job’s overall cost, while the costs added by labor and machine time are limited. That implies a low-value-add job. That is, the fabricator adds limited value beyond what the material costs.

How to interpret this depends on a fabricator’s business strategy and market niche. At Watts’ Metalworking Group, the job mix has evolved to include more steps in the manufacturing value chain. "That’s why we’re now into powder coating, assembly, and other value-adding processes."

For Metalworking Group, at least, adding more value has increased customer loyalty and made each job less transactional. The more value a fabricator adds, the more difficult it becomes to go somewhere else. Again, some fabricators might be built to perform specific processes for specific end markets and, hence, look at material cost relative to value-adding activities a different way. "You just need to know what your whole story is," Watts said.

2. Analyze the Quadrants

Once a fabricator identifies profitability levels for each job or product, the analysis begins. Watts’ team groups jobs into four quadrants (see Figure 1). The top-right quadrant identifies

high-revenue, high-margin

jobs. In most cases, these represent the primary drivers of a custom fabricator’s business. It’s the sweet spot, which can help steer operational excellence—from initial quoting to order processing, all the way through the metal fabrication value stream. It can also help new business development. Are there similar jobs in different industries, ideally those that undergo different economic cycles?

The lower-left quadrant identifies

low-revenue, low-profit jobs

. For Watts, this quadrant is prime territory for culling. "For us, these are time-wasters," he said. "They’re not adding volume and they’re not adding profitability. I don’t want us to pull our hair out and try to figure out how to get all the work out the door, and not make money doing it."

Zerio added that, in general, how companies address low-revenue, low-margin work depends on a fabricator’s strategic plan. In rare cases, a customer might be worth keeping and improving, especially if the account is within a key growth industry the shop wants to serve.

Regardless, if a customer resides in that low-revenue, low-margin quadrant,

something

needs to change eventually. If the fabricator doesn’t simply drop the work, then the quoted price needs to rise to improve the top line, or the fabricator needs to uncover efficiencies to improve the bottom line.

The bottom-right quadrant represents

high-revenue, low-margin

work. Here, uncovering why the work produces low margins can be critical. Is it from rising internal costs or external pricing pressure? These days, it’s likely a combination of both. Rising wages and the skilled labor shortage have forced shops to get more out of the talent they have.

Zerio added that how to treat these jobs depends on the fabricator’s overarching strategy. What if a job has low margin and yet still brings a steady stream of cash in the door? On any shift, the job runs smoothly and really doesn’t affect productivity elsewhere. Yet, due to market pricing pressures, the margins remain low. These jobs might stay in that high-revenue, low-margin quadrant for some time.

Watts added, however, that if those low-margin, high-revenue jobs are difficult to produce, the story changes. "These jobs really can kill a business. For these, you really need to increase their profitability." Processes need to change or be refined, or perhaps manufacturing technology needs to be upgraded to make that once-challenging job run smoothly. Alternatively (or additionally), a price renegotiation might be in order.

Difficult, high-revenue jobs can steal capacity from the upper-left quadrant in the chart, which in many ways reveals a shop’s future.

Low-revenue, high-margin

jobs could one day move to the right. Scaled up with efficiency and operational excellence in mind, at least some of this work could enter that

high-revenue, high-margin

sweet spot.

3. Identify and Improve the KPIs

Now that a fabricator has the "current state" of its customer mix, it can tackle its KPIs. These can vary depending on the operation. "The trend is what we want to see," Zerio said. "We want to see these numbers improving over time."

Watts described a direct labor efficiency metric that incorporates variances between actual and estimated times. If the estimator’s quote assumed a job took one hour, yet it took the operator an hour and 15 minutes, direct labor efficiency would be 80%. The operation also has a "total efficiency" metric that incorporates a variety of activities, including quality assurance as well as planned work center cleaning and maintenance—critical to prevent unplanned downtime and reactive maintenance.

"The efficiency number might be, say, 60%," Zerio said, adding that the metric by itself doesn’t tell much. What it is depends on how activities are being measured and how the metric is calculated. Some operations today utilize Industrial Internet of Things (IIoT) technology to track job-specific data, like setup times and the times quality parts are actually being produced. Whatever is measured, it’s critical to repeat those measurements over time, at similar intervals, and calculate them in the same way. "We want to see how the metric changes over time," Zerio said, "and we want to see it improve."

4. Track your KPIs

Potential metrics abound. What’s important, Zerio explained, is for fabricators to choose the right metrics with the greatest operational impact that also promote the right behaviors, like machine cleaning and maintenance (versus, say, a metric that encourages an operator to skip preventive maintenance to achieve better machine uptime). Fabricators should track metrics regularly and report them on an easily digestible statement, or scorecard—preferably just one page, one that identifies the metric, the timeframes measured, and, most importantly, the champion who’s responsible for it.

During the presentation, Zerio pointed to one scorecard tailored for a specific custom fabricator (see

Figure 2

). At the top was safety—that is, the number of recordable incidents. A shop might be profitable in the short term, but rising safety issues put a shop’s long-term viability at risk. It’s also simply an ethical issue. If more people continue to get hurt on the job, nothing else really matters.

After safety is on-time attendance. This sets the foundation. As Woody Allen said, 80% of a job is showing up. If people aren’t showing up and attendance records continue to decline, the shop’s entire operation sits on a shaky foundation.

After this comes sales-to-budget ratio and new customer dollars, which tracks the shop’s sales growth. Sales might be 110% of budget, which sounds great, but if all that growth comes from existing customers, and new customer dollars are down, revenue concentration rises, and business becomes a bit riskier.

Below this on the scorecard comes on-time delivery and customer returns; the former needs to be high and the latter needs to be low. If ontime delivery is stellar and yet customer returns are rising, problems are brewing. Deliveries need to be on time, but they also need to be right. Poor or incorrect work reaching the customer not only damages a shop’s reputation but also represents serious operational inefficiency. All the work put into those faulty jobs now is basically wasted effort.

After this comes metrics on plant efficiency; margins; and earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation, and amortization (EBITDA); followed by financial data points that show how well the shop is managing its cash and debt. A fabricator might have amazing profits, but what if its days in accounts receivable (DAR) continues to climb and its inventory turns fall? That means more customers are treating the shop as a bank. Slowing inventory turns might point to excess work-in-process (WIP) or finished-goods inventory—another big consumer of cash.

Again, the actual number for some of these metrics varies with the operation, its niche, and overarching business strategy. But as Zerio reiterated, how they change over time is what really matters.

5. Report With a Click

Tracking these items regularly helps build a shop’s plan of action, both in the short and long term. Perhaps some price renegotiation is called for. Maybe new scheduling methods or shop floor layouts—like cells dedicated to high-volume accounts or types of work—would help improve efficiency and, hence, profitability. At the same time, the scorecard and job profitability quadrants help keep everyone focused on what really matters—and it’s usually not a low-revenue, low-profit job.

Another key to success, Watts said, is streamlining the reporting. More software these days offer customized reporting. In Metalworking Group’s case, an office intern developed an Excel-based macro to import data from its existing ERP platform. With a few clicks, managers can see the data.

"Manufacturing is all about efficiency," Watts said. "It’s about getting more output, and that’s something we track. We break efficiencies down by plants, by shifts, down to the employee level. … It’s so important that you have a process to create these reports. If they’re hard to create, you won’t look at them every day."

"All this really takes discipline," Zerio said. "It’s about reviewing the right details at the right time."
