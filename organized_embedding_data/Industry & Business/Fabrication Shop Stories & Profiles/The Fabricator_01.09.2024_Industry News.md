Scholarships make it possible for students to pursue careers in making things

Spurred on by the need to rebuild the domestic manufacturing workforce, Nuts, Bolts & Thingamajigs (NBT), the Foundation of the Fabricators and Manufacturers Association (FMA), recently awarded 60 scholarships totaling $120,000 to students across the country enrolled in manufacturing-related certificate and degree programs for the fall 2024 semester. This is a 105% increase in award dollars distributed over last year thanks to grant funding from organizations such as Subway Cares and other generous donors.

"We saw a 45% increase in qualified applications over last year’s summer/fall semester. It is very encouraging to see this growth in interest, and NBT is extremely proud to support these accomplished students in their pursuit of manufacturing careers," said Ed Dernulc, NBT Foundation director. "Our sincere belief is that this opportunity will help lay the groundwork for them to become valuable contributors to the ever-evolving metal fabrication industry."

The awards help defray educational expenses such as tuition, fees, books, and other school-related costs to allow students to focus on the task at hand: becoming part of the manufacturing workforce. All award winners have strong overall GPAs with high grades in relevant coursework. In as few as six months, some of the scholarship recipients will graduate from their programs and become employed full-time in manufacturing organizations across the U.S.

Job Shop Consumption Report reflects uncertainty in first half of 2024

The semiannual Forming and Fabricating Job Shop Consumption Report has been released for the first half of 2024. Conducted by Armada Corporate Intelligence for the Fabricators and Manufacturers Association and offered free of charge to FMA members, the study focuses on small and medium-sized shops. The latest FFJSCR reflects the current period of concern and trepidation as inflation and supply chain worries continue. Some shops are still experiencing a fairly solid year and expect to see a good conclusion, but others are seeing declines already. With the job market still historically strong but showing signs of weakness and the potential for major political change in a few months, there are far more questions than reliable answers.

An important indicator of overall manufacturing performance is capacity utilization. Traditionally, capacity utilization between 80% and 85% at a national level is considered "ideal." This suggests that machines are being efficiently deployed and that the workforce is productive. Anything less than 80% suggests there is slack. For much of the last two years, the national capacity numbers have been stuck in the mid to high 70s. That means there is little incentive to acquire more machinery or hire new people. The report shows that capacity usage is even lower than the national norm, at 67.09%. This is not unusual, as the survey is aimed at smaller companies that generally have more slack as they adapt to new technology and deal with staff turnover. The report shows that 33.2% experienced an increase in capacity while 21.2% saw a decrease. The majority (45.6%) saw no real change and reported stable capacity capability.

Another indicator of increased activity is new order indices, such as those from the Institute for Supply Management and S&P Global, which track the buying patterns of the business community. Numbers over 50 indicate growth; under 50 suggest contraction. The U.S. had been under 50 at the start of the year but has had readings over 50 for the last three months. The ISM version is still under 50 because it aims at small and medium-sized businesses. The FFJSCR shows that 33.6% of shops have experienced an increase in new orders compared to 2023. Another 40.8% saw stability in new orders, and for 25.6% of shops, the level of new order activity declined.

All eyes are on employment data, as this is the key motivator for Federal Reserve action. An increase in jobless numbers is a signal for the Fed to start cutting interest rates. Thus far, jobless numbers have stayed at historical lows, although they have come up a bit lately. The FFJSCR shows a similar trend, with 24% of the respondents hiring and only 14.8% laying people off or reducing their workforce in some way. That leaves 61.2% staying right where they were. Overall, there have been fewer job postings nationwide, but this has been attributed to the inability to find the people needed, and many companies have just stopped looking. There has also been a lot more investment in technology and robotics to cope with the shortage of skilled workers.

While in general there has been a decline in commodity prices, the report shows that 44.0% of job shops are reporting higher steel and aluminum prices, with only 15.9% experiencing a decline. Another 40.0% reported stable prices. Much of the price volatility, especially for copper (for which prices have set all-time records) and aluminum, has been attributed to the actions of speculators.

The most consistent jump in prices has been in the logistics sector. Many factors have combined to drive these costs up, including an imbalance in trucking capacity among different regions of the U.S., volatile maritime rates, and an increase of more than 300% in the rate for containers from Asia to Europe. The FFJSCR reports that 60.3% of respondents have experienced rising logistics prices and only 3.6% have seen them decline. For the remaining 36%, prices have remained stable.

Despite all these challenges, the FFJSCR concludes with some positive observations. A full 51.4% of respondents said that their planned capital equipment purchasing is on track. The worker shortage is still driving businesses to bring in robotics and technology. Reshoring slowed a bit last year but is still taking place. The bad news is that 21.2% are delaying these purchases indefinitely, and a cursory examination shows these are the companies that serve some of the struggling sectors, such as aerospace. Another 14.9% are delaying by a quarter, and 13.0% are delaying by two quarters. There is also evidence that buying decisions are waiting on Fed action, as a rate cut would make some of these purchases cheaper.

The other piece of good news is that most of the respondents are anticipating a good year in 2025. Fully 50.4% said they expect a good end to 2024 and a decent start to 2025. Only 14.2% think the rest of this year will be trending negative, and 35.7% think the year will end about where it is now, and that is not bad. Most analysts are asserting that the second half of the year will have GDP growth of about 2.0%. This is not breakneck growth, but it is far from recession, and this supports the idea of a "soft landing."

LZH develops multilaser beam welding process for maritime manufacturing

Welding thick sheet metal is challenging because of the high penetration depths required. Researchers at Laser Zentrum Hannover e.V. (LZH) aim to facilitate welding of thick-walled components (15 to 30 mm) with a multilaser beam welding process.

The process includes integrated quality assurance to help ensure consistent, defect-free, and high-quality welds. Various sensors capture features such as weld reinforcement, seam width, and distortion. Based on this data, they will develop an adaptive welding process control and an integrated quality assurance system that can classify weld quality as "acceptable" or "not acceptable."

Additionally, LZH will transfer the sensor data from the quality assurance system into digital twins that digitally represent the process and the weld seam based on various measurement data. With the digital twin, researchers can document and track the process. The goal is to reduce overall error rates in automated welding in shipbuilding, minimize necessary component inspections in production, and make the manufacturing process more economical.

The process combines three laser beam sources into a parallel root and filler layer welding process. By customizing beam shaping, they can control the characteristics of the molten pools, temperatures, solidification, and microstructure to prevent hot cracks. The goal is that the one-run process developed, with welding depths beyond those currently available, will significantly reduce the number of weld passes, manufacturing time, and consumption of filler material and shielding gas.

The technology being developed is intended to be transferred to industrial manufacturing plants in the maritime sector.

Summer program pays teens in L.A. public housing community to learn skilled trades

This summer, teenage residents at Jordan Downs, one of L.A.’s largest public housing communities, are earning a paycheck while learning hands-on skills like welding and carpentry, launching them on pathways to careers in industries facing strong demand and a critical lack of qualified workers.

The program is made possible by the joint efforts of Harbor Freight Tools for Schools, the flagship program of The Smidt Foundation, and Bridge Housing. Bridge Housing is a nonprofit housing developer participating in the $1 billion redevelopment effort of Jordan Downs. First built in the 1940s, Jordan Downs is now going through a physical transformation that includes a doubling of the number of residential units, the addition of retail and community spaces, and new parks and open space for residents.

"The City of Los Angeles is committed to providing opportunity to all Angelenos. The launch of this program will equip young Angelenos with hands-on experience and essential skills that can help them succeed now and in the future," said Los Angeles Mayor Karen Bass.

Operating like a construction workplace, the program uses the "earn and learn" model where students are paid while developing meaningful skills that can lead to future employment. The 15 high school-aged residents participating in the summer’s pilot program at Jordan Downs were recruited by community-based service providers to participate.

Students will complete 40 hours of hands-on project work to practice foundational trade skills, such as welding, plumbing, electrical wiring, and framing a mini-house. Students also will master basic skills that apply to a variety of construction disciplines, including safety, measurement, site prep, and cleanup.

The hope is that students who complete this introductory program will enroll in more comprehensive skilled trades training programs that will result in them earning high-value industry certifications.

Formtek-Maine delivers flexible fabrication system

Formtek-Maine has installed a new flexible fabrication system at a water heater manufacturer in Michigan. The system can handle 30,000-lb. coils of surface-critical cold-rolled steel up to 60 in. wide and 0.125 in. thick in blanks up to 84 in. long.

The system, which includes a stock reel and coil car, features options such as a spring-set safety brake, automatic brake tension control, a clock-spring guard, and automatic coil centering on the coil car via a laser sensor.

The 11-roll heavy-duty D series straightener includes chrome-plated rolls, backup flights on all work rolls, high-rise upper rolls for cleaning access, power to all straightener rolls, a powered hands-free cleaning system, and a hold-down peeler station for hands-free coil thread-up. A set of powered pinch rolls pulls the strip through the customer’s blast machine.

A four-roll servo feed guides the strip into a DieMation coil processor system, which includes two tooling stations servo controlled in the X and Y axes, space to add more tooling stations, and a scrap conveyor with scrap bin for the slugs.

A second four-roll feed is used to pull the strip through, and a hydraulic powered cutoff shear performs blanking. Parts exit the cutoff shear and drop onto a belt conveyor.
