# Tracking the whole shop at once

[TARİH: 01.08.2024 The Fabricator]

Software

Software helps longtime fabricator stay ahead of the game

By

Lincoln Brunner

Fulcrum’s cloud-based software allows users to track jobs from any connected device, wherever they are, whether on the shop floor or the beach.

Image: Fulcrum

G

ive Rodd Blessey points for perseverance. Blessey, president of Aldine Metal Products Corp. in New Milford, Conn., gave not one but two ERP systems a fair shot at helping him run his 40-person job shop more efficiently.

Fitting In

The problem for Blessey wasn’t what those other ERP systems did, per se—it was what they couldn’t do, namely accommodate his desire to move into fully digital part quoting, which he did with another firm’s software a few years ago. But because the ERP system Aldine was using didn’t integrate with that software, Blessey had to find something that did to help him speed up the shop’s workflow.

"I had a goal to run our production paperless," Blessey said. "We – were spending a tremendous amount of time and money on printing drawings and communicating with the shop, with people back and forth. We would print the routers; we would print the drawings. We would have somebody file those. We would have somebody copy those. We would have somebody bring them out to the shop. And then whenever there was a change made, we could make it in our ERP, but we also had to go back out and make it on the shop floor."

Changing Focus

Something had to give. After a concerted search, Aldine landed on Fulcrum, a Minneapolis-based company that started with its own goal: Instead of a database-driven mechanism that stores data and pulls reports about individual machines or operations, why not create cloud-based manufacturing software that connects all parts of a shop simultaneously?

In essence, rather than taking up-close snapshots of certain moments in time, Fulcrum created a raised platform from which any user in a shop could view all facets of operation in real time. The company, in business since 2015, has recently focused on sheet metal fabricators as its primary audience.

That means fabricators like Aldine, which uses the software to track a part from start to finish to out the door.

Aldine fabricates thousands of different sheet metal parts and assemblies for customers in many different industries. The company works with mild steel, stainless steel, aluminum, copper, and brass in thicknesses ranging from 0.025 in. up to ¼ in. or even ½ in., depending on the material. Founded in Brooklyn, N.Y., in 1938, the company is in its third generation of family ownership.

At Aldine’s 30,000-sq.-ft. shop, the production process begins in engineering and programming. Then it may move to the shop’s 3-kW fiber laser or its combination laser/turret punch machine. Parts then could move to one of the shop’s six press brakes (a seventh is on its way by year’s end) and then to insertion, welding, or assembly. It’s a lot to keep track of, considering that the shop caters to customers needing anywhere from a handful to several thousand parts—even after-hours part runs.

"All of those resources, all of those departments, are scheduled in the software, and [it] allows us to see how the job flows through, but also where the bottlenecks might be for us to attack—to be able to get those jobs done when we need to get them done," Blessey said.

The main difference between this software and other ERP platforms is embedded in the idea of live data, according to Fulcrum CEO Sunny Han. "It connects different parts of the shop live," said Han, who co-founded the business as a builder of custom software for different clients, many of them manufacturers.

"If you change the schedule, you don’t have to go update a board or whatever it may be on the shop floor—the shop floor employees know it. If a drawing changes from a customer, instead of having to run out, find the traveler, [and] replace a drawing, the drawing is automatically updated."

At Aldine, being able to adjust schedules on the fly has made a huge difference in how people communicate internally and externally, Blessey noted. Not only has it allowed the shop to be nimble with customer demands, but it also has fundamentally changed the way its departments talk to each other. Not only that, but because the software is on the cloud, he can log in from wherever—the beach, his living room, a customer’s factory—and see what’s happening now.

"I personally love the scheduler," Blessey said. "It uses an algorithm that allows for putting in jobs wherever there might be some time on a particular work center to make sure you’re utilizing all of your time. And it helps a scheduler to see what everybody’s doing during the day, but also to adjust and communicate with the shop because no longer are they just going off a handwritten list or a printed list. They’re seeing everything that the scheduler does in real time. "So, if you make a change to the schedule, as soon as they go back to their job-tracking, they’re going to see whatever change you had made. It’s just moving a lot faster."

Inventory Control

The system also can track and update raw material inventories in real time so that employees on the shop floor and in purchasing know precisely what their shop has on hand and can proactively replenish what they’ll need tomorrow and next week based on actual and anticipated orders.

"You don’t only have to wait until the end of the month or end of the quarter or end of the day to do a stop take to understand where the inventory levels are," Han said.

Blessey noted that the software has made a difference for his purchase planning and inventory control, as well.

"Our purchasing team is seeing all of those needs of fabrication, and when those materials are needed in real time, as well," Blessey said. "And remember … because it’s in real time, if your laser operator runs a job and he uses 10 sheets of 14-ga. steel, as soon as he picks it, it’s out of inventory. So, our purchasing department, looking at everything in real time, we don’t have to wait for a piece of paper to come to the office to subtract the material from inventory to make sure it’s reconciled. We’re seeing everything in real time, from hardware to screws to nuts to assembly components to inventoried subcomponents.

"Our customers, as well as ourselves, we want to make parts in real time. We don’t run a thousand pieces and put them on the shelf anymore. We want to make the lot sizes that are needed. So, we’re constantly running smaller batches and moving those through the shop faster so that there’s not inventory on the shelf."

Ports of Call

Communication in-house has been streamlined, but what makes communication with the outside world just as easy is a tool that Blessey never thought he would be able to offer his customers—a self-service portal that allows customers to get information on if their orders have been placed or put into production. That information gives customers peace of mind but also tends to make people interacting with the system look good to their bosses. "Our customers can log into their portal and see the orders that they gave us and see if that order has been entered and if that order is in progress," Blessey said. "A lot of times, larger customers that have portals and they’re working inside their system, looking at their information to enter orders, and then they have a question—’Where’s this order with Aldine? What’s the delivery date?’ They can go in and see that.

"I think that sets us apart a bit, to be able to communicate with our customers whenever they want. A lot of our industry at this point in time, they want shorter lead times, faster turnaround. And they want accurate delivery dates. When their boss asks them where something is, they want to have that answer right away. So, our customer service needs to be able to react to that."

Vision for U.S. Manufacturing

In Han’s mind, businesses tend to fall into three focus areas: culture, execution, and finance. The first tends to hire and nurture people better than most, the second tends to create great processes, and the third tends to garner tremendous bottom-line results. His short-term goal is to create a manufacturing software that serves all of them well.

His long-term goal is to connect all those types of businesses, from all kinds of industries.

In one sense, though manufacturers in the Western world consistently innovate better than their Chinese counterparts, they also operate at a distinct disadvantage to a central planning model that can create seamless supply chains and instant coordination among many players. However, using integrated systems technology, if enough businesses here in the United States were to empower their entire enterprise with live data, it could enable their people to cooperate internally and externally in a way that allows them to compete directly with the efficiency of state-run enterprises. "In America, in Germany, in Japan and places where it’s more democratic, more decentralized, we have hundreds of thousands of shops that are working together to make a bunch of cool stuff," Han said. "We believe that’s the better model. That’s where most of the innovation comes from.

"Well, there’s an incredible advantage to manufacturing in a centralized way," he added. "You have a whole city in the south of China that is manufacturing—all of our vendors, all of our suppliers, all of your toolmakers are in the same spot. That coordination is extremely valuable. Well, our vision is: What if we could solve that coordination problem, not with dictatorial central planning, but with technology, by having an internet of manufacturing connect everybody together and coordinate in a more automated way? That’s our long-term vision."

At Aldine, just having a tool that schedules like Fulcrum does and gives everyone visibility in a way that accommodates a just-in-time production mindset is enough. "We’ve used three [ERPs] now, and I can tell you that this one does it better than anything I’ve ever seen," Blessey said. "I believe it’s going to lead us to be way more competitive in the future and allow for the growth of this company. That’s why I’m so excited about it."
