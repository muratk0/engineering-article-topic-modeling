T

he customer mix defines the custom and contract metal fabricator. A shop could have a range of low-quantity and prototype work, some of which could grow into production orders. In this way, a fabricator’s specialty evolves, ideally with a diverse range of jobs that help it scale in a sustainable way.

Steering this growth is the humble estimator, a job that lies at the heart of any make-to-order business. Estimators work between the demand realities in the market and the cost realities on the shop floor. Financially speaking, they can make or break a business.

THE ESTIMATING SPECTRUM

Estimating practices in contract metal fabrication vary widely. "From what we’ve seen, some companies do activity-based costing, which covers the true nuts and bolts. How much does a part weigh? What does it cost to move these pieces between work centers? How difficult will this work be, and how will this work be moved through the shop and out the door?

"On the other end of the spectrum, we see heuristic pricing. This is pricing, not costing, and that’s an important distinction. If you do a heuristic-based quote, you base your price based on developed rules. This could be anything from some multiple of material costs to pricing in the purest sense, understanding what the market will bear, and setting your price slightly below that number, as long as they know the shop can make money."

That was Jason Ray, co-founder and CEO of Paperless Parts, a Boston-based company that offers job shop quoting software. He added that software has made it much easier to do costing, so fewer operations are taking a purely heuristic approach. "But are they going all the way to activity- based costing? That depends on the types of customers and the work a fabricator does."

If customers buy purely on price, they view the parts a fabricator ships as a commodity. "And if you enter that commoditization territory, you need to be very accurate in your costing." Heuristic pricing, on the other end of the estimating spectrum, works well "when the services you provide are unique and complex."

In custom fabrication, this often happens when job routings grow longer and services expand beyond cutting, bending, and welding. Shops might handle multiple outside services, from plating to heat treating and powder coating, while juggling inventory levels for purchased components, raw stock, work-in-process, and sometimes even finished goods—held for customers to pull from as needed. All that adds value and makes it harder for those customers to switch suppliers.

ESTABLISHING A COSTING BASELINE

Ray added that heuristic pricing isn’t just "throwing a number out there." It involves having the right guardrails in place to ensure costs are covered and desired margins remain in a reasonable range. Direct and indirect labor need to be paid, as do machines and facilities.

Traditional job shop quoting usually involves shop rates that incorporate direct labor and overhead rates, estimated run times, setups, and specific variables tied to job difficulty: part attributes, setup times, the number of setups required, and other minutiae. A job requiring highly skilled TIG welders or an expert press brake setup tech will cost more than jobs that don’t require them. Then comes the use of markups for certain items, like purchasing components or outside services, to cover the cost of coordinating those services. (For more, visit

The industry’s rapid adoption of quoting software, Ray said, has helped document and standardize a process that in the past was learned only through experience. "We like to talk about transferring tribal knowledge. [Quoting software] has helped people who sometimes did things on gut feel now estimate jobs in a more formulaic way."

The idea is to develop a costing template that considers the minutiae—machine costs, the range of labor rates involved, overheads, setups, material handling. "All this comes from documenting practices from owners and estimators who’ve been at this for decades," Ray said. "That gets you 80% there toward your estimated costs and establishing a good price. We’ll never get 100% there, because human judgment will always play a role: What’s going on in the world? How busy is the shop down the street? How many [request for quotes] did you get yesterday for the same material? What’s the relationship with the customer? What kind of demand is trending? All that is happening in the estimator’s head."

THE COST OF POOR MANUFACTURABILITY

Software doesn’t eliminate the need for a human estimator, especially on jobs that involve lots of value-added work. But it does automate specific aspects of pricing so that fewer items get overlooked, including updates on material and component pricing. And some of the latest software helps automate the drawing analysis.

As Scott Sawyer, Paperless Parts’ co-founder and chief scientist, explained, "Job shops receive prints that could have a multitude of specifications from an OEM. Software really helps estimators not miss such details. The system flags it, then uses a rules-based engine that allows estimators to take consistent action."

The issue could be with forming (like a hole near a bend line), hardware (like access issues for the insertion press), or any number of design-for-manufacturability (DFM) challenges. The idea is to catch these issues before orders are released to the floor.

Software is starting to automate "DFM intelligence" by flagging issues and leaving experienced, highly skilled estimators to do what they do best: working with production to make a judgment call about the DFM issue, establish pricing, and move forward based on a defined manufacturing strategy.

Sources added that those quoting guardrails help establish that strategy. An operation with narrow quoting guardrails keeps their accepted jobs in the center of the road, well within a fabricator’s operational sweet spot, where costs are predictable.

A fabricator might widen the guardrails and accept a certain level of manufacturing challenge as a competitive strategy. Those wide guardrails, however, increase potential variabilities on the shop floor, including the potential for rework and the need for specific (usually highly skilled) people to complete an order. The price and the production schedule need to account for that.

THE COST OF TIME

"Yield is one item that often gets overlooked," Ray said, referring to the cost of scrap and rework. "Often, a shop schedule doesn’t consider the time it takes to produce bad parts."

This could be anything from extra tryout parts at the press brake to extra parts sent to an outside coater or plater that inevitably damages a few pieces. A long-term solution would be scrutinizing and perhaps changing the sourcing strategy. Excess setup parts might involve more training at the press brake, or a refined DFM strategy in estimating and order processing. All this can make costs and production time more predictable.

In the short term, though, Ray has seen fabricators account for these variabilities with price markups—but those markups don’t accommodate for the extra time and material. "When you don’t factor in the extra part and the rework, your material costs are off and your scheduling is off. Everything trickles from there. At that point, you’re not able to produce the part in the lead time you quoted."

That trickling can push other jobs behind and lead to an unexpected amount of overtime. In the short term, a shop just needs to deal with lower margins and perhaps some money-losing orders. If issues aren’t addressed in the long run, though, a shop’s reputation can suffer, which makes the estimator’s job a lot harder.

"There are risks with job complexity," Sawyer said. "When complexity rises, it becomes difficult to predict your exact cost. And the best operations are quoting with enough margin that accounts for that job complexity, and they know it’s going to move more slowly through the shop. When jobs move slower, that additional time means more unexpected events can come up."

THE VALUE OF LOW-QUANTITY WORK

Jobs with numerous value-adding processes and longer lead times cost more, but what about expedited, low-quantity work? On a per-piece basis, these jobs often cost more if they involve skilled personnel who can perform quick changeovers efficiently and reliably, but as sources explained, this isn’t the only reason low-quantity and expedited work is more expensive.

After all, lean shops can scrutinize their operations to streamline changeovers. And modern metal fabrication technologies—like press brakes with automated tool changers, panel benders and folders, even intelligent welding automation—sometimes make changeovers inconsequential.

If it takes less time and fewer skilled personnel to process a low-quantity order, wouldn’t costs be lower? A quick response also means orders are less likely to change midstream. More time adds more complications. So, wouldn’t a quick-turn order be less expensive? After all, time is money and, well, quick-turn work takes less time—right?

As Sawyer explained, these observations miss two points. First, demand for prototyping is driven less by cost and more by value. "You need to think about the value of producing parts quickly and pricing work to account for that value," he said. "Put yourself in your customers’ shoes. The more iterations a customer can get during the product design cycle will help determine how good that product will be. If you’re able to deliver in a week [and allow customers to iterate designs] four times faster, comparing it to typical four-week turnaround times, you offer tremendous value."

The second point deals with opportunity costs. A production order with a longer lead time has lower opportunity costs, because it can be integrated with other orders to ensure a job shop’s constraint work centers remain effective and as efficient as possible. And even if the production order does consume capacity and prevents the fabricator from taking other work, the work will continue to be there for weeks or even months.

For low-quantity work, this isn’t the case. If a low-quantity order consumes capacity, the opportunity costs and associated risk would be immense. Sure, prototyping is profitable—much more profitable than production work—but consumed capacity means the shop can’t offer the same quick response to other customers. And when orders are complete and capacity does open up a few days later, those opportunities are probably no longer there. Customers needing quick response shop around until they can find it.

So, how does a fabricator reduce the opportunity costs? With excess capacity. "I recall walking the shop floor with [Paperless Parts’ co-founder] Jay Jacobs," Ray said. Before launching a software company, Jacobs ran a sheet metal and machining job shop largely dedicated to quick-response work. "I remember seeing a row of machines, and every other one was idle. I asked him why. He told me, ‘We’re never going to be unable to deliver on a promised delivery date because of an asset. I would rather keep these assets open for people who are willing to pay for quick response.’"

When estimators name a price, they’re establishing a competitive position and, ultimately, shaping the kind of business a custom and contract metal fabricator will become.

THE IDEAL JOB PROFILE

That excess capacity isn’t free, of course, which is why, on a per-piece basis, quick-response prototypes remain more expensive than production work. That said, having excess capacity is nothing to fear. In fact, idle machines ready to produce at a moment’s notice might represent tremendous money-making potential. As Ray explained, "Young startup job shops in particular frequently get into trouble because they’re not willing to let their machines sit idle and wait for the right job. They take every job they can get. They undervalue their time, and the owner often works for free. They create an estimating doom loop, because they’re filling their machines with parts that don’t make much money. And again, when they have an opportunity to turn around a job quickly, they can’t do it. We see this happening because they haven’t identified their ideal job profile."

Sawyer added that developing that ideal job profile isn’t just about being arbitrarily choosy and raising prices. Instead, it requires a collaboration between estimating and production, which in turn helps ground any operational change to market realities.

He described a hypothetical situation in which production dials in costing to the nth degree—every material move, every bend, every piece of hardware, every weld fixture. This drives improvement, reduces costs, and makes production extraordinarily efficient—but does the result match what potential customers are willing to pay for? The estimating team should be involved to answer that question.

"Costing needs to be pricing aware," Sawyer said, "and your pricing shows where you can actually make money, and where your ideal job profile really is." This could include markets with specific demand cycles, production quantities, part geometries, and material types. Complexity and the number and type of value-adding processes play key roles as well.

"If you don’t establish your ideal job profile," Ray said, "you might be perfect on costing and yet never win work, simply because competitors have structural advantages. Your rates are never going to be low enough." Say a fabricator wants to shift its focus away from low-quantity work and toward production. After all, production gives more stability. Costs would fall, which would make the fabricator more competitive—right?

Not necessarily. An incredibly efficient fabricator can still fail if those reduced costs are higher than customers are willing to pay. Bid-win rates decline, and the shop lowers prices to accommodate. Employees and machines then get busy producing large quantities of parts for money-losing jobs. And the struggles can snowball from there, especially as market pricing expectations solidify.

Developing an ideal job profile balances a company’s operational strengths with the pricing realities of a customer mix. Prices aren’t static. Higher bid-win rates might move prices higher; lower win rates might reduce prices or guide a shop toward a different market. Accurate costing helps absorb these variabilities, protecting shops from lowering prices too much and obliterating margins. But as Ray explained, "costing with blinders on becomes a futile exercise when a shop down the street can do it for less."

Unlike costing, an accepted price represents the market, where supply and demand curves intersect. This reality puts estimating at the center of the custom metal fabrication universe. When estimators name a price, they’re establishing a competitive position and, ultimately, shaping the kind of business a custom and contract metal fabricator will become.
