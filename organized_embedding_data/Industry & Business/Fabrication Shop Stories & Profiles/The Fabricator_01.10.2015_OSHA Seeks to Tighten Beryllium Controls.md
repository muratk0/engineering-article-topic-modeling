T

he proposed Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA) controls on beryllium will have an impact on some metalworkingrelated industries.

OSHA’s preliminary economic analysis lists fabricated structural metal manufacturing and sheet metal work manufacturing as two of the highest impact "small entity" sectors affected by the proposal. The No. 1 impacted sector is commercial and industrial machinery and equipment repair, which accounts for nearly 20,000 facilities in the U.S. Sheet metal manufacturing represents nearly 4,000 facilities, by comparison.

On one hand, OSHA said that relatively few of these facilities, on a percentage basis, will be affected by the requirements of the beryllium rule, if it is finalized as proposed. For example, OSHA reported that only 129 facilities within the industrial machinery sector will be impacted.

On the other hand, manufacturers are almost sure to disagree with OSHA’s figures. For the most part, manufacturers and related associations hotly contest these types of regulatory business-impact analyses. The OSHA beryllium proposal came out in August with a comment deadline of Nov. 5, 2015.

In its economic document accompanying the proposed rule, OSHA estimated the cost of compliance for each sector as a percentage of profit. Those figures are 2.59 percent for fabricated structural metal manufacturing, 4.67 percent for sheet metal work manufacturing, and 12.11 percent for commercial and industrial machinery and equipment repair.

OSHA’s eight-hour permissible exposure limit (PEL) for beryllium is 2.0 micrograms per cubic meter of air originally established in 1948 by the Atomic Energy Commission and adopted by OSHA in 1971. OSHA’s proposed standard would reduce the eight-hour PEL to 0.2 mg/m3. The proposed rule also would require additional protections, including personal protective equipment, medical exams, and training.

The nation’s primary beryllium product manufacturer, Materion, and the United Steelworkers, the union representing many of those who work with beryllium, pushed for the new standard. They approached OSHA in 2012.

Workers who inhale beryllium particles can develop a debilitating, incurable illness known as chronic beryllium disease and are also at increased risk of lung cancer. Dangers arise when beryllium-containing materials are processed in a way that releases airborne beryllium dust, fume, mist, or other forms.

White House Endorses Manufacturing Bill

The White House was quick to endorse a manufacturing bill introduced by Senate Democrats, though the endorsement was unsurprising because President Obama apparently had the idea first.

The Scale-up Manufacturing Investment Company Act of 2015 creates a program that allows private investment firms to leverage funds provided by the government to help emerging manufacturers commercialize their products. The program, modeled on the Small Business Administration’s (SBA) Small Business Investment Company (SBIC) program, would allow approved participating investment firms to invest in securities and issue debentures to raise capital that would then be invested into emerging manufacturers’ businesses. Any fees, interest, and profits received from the debentures and securities investments would offset the costs of the program.

"The capital intensity of these scale-up investments—which can range from about $40 million to $200 million—and the lengthy maturities required to bring new manufacturing capabilities online make these investments fall between the cracks of the traditional venture capitalists and private equity investors," explained Jason Miller, special assistant to the president for manufacturing policy and the deputy director of the National Economic Council. "They are too big for funds that take many small, high-risk bets and too risky for funds that make just a few sizable investments."
