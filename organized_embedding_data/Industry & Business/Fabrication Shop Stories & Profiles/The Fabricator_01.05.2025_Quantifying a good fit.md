# Quantifying a "good fit"

[TARİH: 01.05.2025 The Fabricator]

Tech Spotlight

How to uncover applicant employability

By

Tim Heston

W

hen you ask fab shop managers about their hiring strategy, they talk about the lack of skilled labor, of course. Paradoxically, the dearth of experienced applicants has moved the conversation away from the

actual skills

required to run a fab shop. Instead, more leaders in metal fabrication talk about the importance of those core traits that make people employable: work ethic, grit, consciousness, curiosity, problem-solving skills, an eagerness to learn, a positive attitude. In other words, they’re looking for someone that’s a "good fit." In fact, as more shops hire based on employability, they find that hiring curious novices with positive attitudes usually has better outcomes than hiring experienced people with bad habits and bad attitudes.

Problem is, how do you really know a person’s employability? Often, it’s just a gut feeling. A job candidate might ask good questions and exhibit a certain "something." That might come from answering questions a certain way or asking good questions that show curiosity and eagerness. The signals seem so subtle and complex, they must be impossible to measure—right?

"When you put job candidates on our platform, we give you a comparative report about their employability. We’re purely a Swiss army knife tool that gives recruiters better information about available candidates."

That was Ray Dick, co-founder and CEO of Piccadilly Cloud, the software-as-a-service provider behind TEQ Connect™. The assessment platform aims to help hiring managers measure employability. Specifically, it draws from certain psychological models (dubbed "psychometrics"), which executive recruiters have used for years, and adapts them for the skilled trades.

The roots of the platform go back to 2018, when he founded Project MFG, an organization that promotes the skilled trades through various competitions in machining and welding, with the finals streamed online in the "Clash of Trades" reality show. The events test not just a person’s raw skill in a particular trade but also their problem-solving capabilities.

"We’re real-world oriented," Ray Dick said. "We put competitors through engineering change orders, apply cost constraints, and add other elements to make the competition reflect the manufacturing floor."

The program brings to light just how broadly engaging a career in manufacturing can be. It’s not about laying down a weld bead better and faster than anyone else. It’s about working with others to solve problems. After all, customers aren’t paying for perfect welds; they’re paying for a shipped product that works. A perfectly welded product that doesn’t function as intended isn’t worth much. To uncover the problem, welders need to step out of their booths, start asking questions, and engage. In other words, they need those "soft" employability traits.

In the past, a shop might skip over those with less experience. With an objective measure of employability, they now might give those inexperienced candidates another look.

The platform aims to measure those traits and applies today’s computing power to data grounded in years of industrial psychology research. Applicants answer a few open-ended questions, which can change depending on what employability traits a company is looking for. There are no right or wrong answers, but the way people respond—usually by speaking into their phone— can tell the system a lot. The system doesn’t detect a tone of voice, but it does pay attention to the words people say and the order they say them.

"We run a natural language processing algorithm on it to uncover the motivational and behavioral traits they should possess to make them good at a specific job. There’s a lot of data out there [from previous research]. We’ve put together templates for more than 3,000 occupational titles in the skilled trades."

That was Abe Dick, the company’s chief technology officer, adding that the platform works with almost two dozen traits, like ability to cope, grit, and openness to new experiences. When people say specific combinations of words in a specific order, the algorithm identifies those patterns as high-confidence predictors of a certain trait.

As Abe Dick described, "Our natural language processing algorithm has been validated by third parties to show, for instance, ‘these words used in this combination create a high-confidence predictor that a person’s grit will be high.’"

With the data crunched, the platform gives an employee quotient it calls the TEQ Score™. The higher the score, the greater a candidate’s employability. Abe Dick added that the specifics that create the ultimate score (like the weighting of certain factors) change depending on the company and job. For instance, a code-level pipe welder will likely be very different from an artistic welder at an architectural or miscellaneous fabricator. Both need certifications, and both must have an eye for quality and detail. But the way they go about achieving that quality can be quite different.

A recruiter can review a job applicant’s employability score and ratings on specific core traits, including consciousness, grit, and openness to new experiences.

"Those variances in fit are inherently what we are able to distinguish," Ray Dick explained. He added that the platform helps make hiring more effective, yet it doesn’t replace the face-to-face conversations recruiters have with candidates.

Since its launch in November 2024, companies have used the platform in various scenarios. At one extreme, recruiters use the resulting scores to narrow down the applicant pool. A manufacturer might get only a handful of candidates with a lot of experience and hundreds of applicants with little to no experience. In the past, a shop might skip over those with less experience. Measuring employability, though, they now might give those inexperienced candidates another look. Someone with no experience but a very high employability score could be a great success.

At the other extreme, a fabricator could receive just one resume for a position. Without the platform, a manager might consider hiring the lone applicant as the default decision. Beggers can’t be choosers, after all. The employability quotient could add some objectivity to the situation. If that candidate has a very low employability rating, a shop might think twice before pulling the trigger on an offer. Hiring the wrong person can be costlier than hiring no one at all.

"We’re all about providing an unbiased perspective," Abe Dick said. "We provide the data to help you decide whether hiring them will be a net positive for your organization."
