f doing the same thing leads to the same results, that explains why many metal fabricators continue down that familiar path. It’s comfortable and, often, helps to keep the business afloat.

But when it comes to taking the next step, some risk is involved. Increasing sales and growing the business require shaking up the status quo. Only a different approach can lead to a different outcome.

Ryan Maley and his business partners purchased Gulf Coast Welding, Foley, Ala., from Maley’s dad in 2021. The metal fabricating business, founded in the mid-1990s, had carved out a successful niche working on heavy-duty jobs, such as structural steel for residential and commercial construction and equipment for nearby manufacturing and processing companies. Gulf Coast Welding also offered field services, such as structural steel erection, staircase and platform installation, and welding repair. The company had a solid foundation of satisfied customers, and its location was close to growing areas in Georgia, Florida, and its Alabama home. But the new owners also saw the opportunity for more and embarked on a plan to target new markets.

One of those new areas was supporting projects for governmental bodies, like the Alabama Department of Transportation. A lot of those projects typically take years to materialize, but once a shop develops an understanding of what is required and gains a reputation for quality work and timely responsiveness, it can cultivate work from that sector on a consistent basis. Maley said it has been a nice and steady way to grow as well as diversify Gulf Coast Welding’s customer base.

With this focus on expanding the shop’s work, Maley recognized that some sort of automation on the shop floor might help to boost manufacturing capacity while allowing the company’s skilled welders to focus on more complex work. Gulf Coast Welding employs 20 people.

"Larger shops seem to go after jobs in a certain tonnage range. Then the really small shops can’t quite get into the type of work that we do," said Maley, describing where Gulf Coast Welding fits in the area’s metal fabricating community.

The company had no desire to chase after the work that much larger structural steel companies lived off of, but it did like the idea of utilizing the type of technology that some of these large structural steel fab shops needed to be competitive. Anything that could help expedite the processing of beams would help. The large material handling systems that moved beams from one station to the next and any robots weren’t the focus. This was to be the first step toward more efficient days.

Flexibility in Beam Processing

At FABTECH in 2018, Maley saw a beam processing machine that stuck with him. He immediately realized the impact that such a machine could have on a shop, but this was before he began overseeing Gulf Coast Welding’s operations.

"It just stayed in the back of my mind all of those years," he said. What Maley saw was an early-generation model of a CNC beam processing machine from Flex Machine Tools that offered precision drilling and milling capabilities. For someone whose day job involved laying out and manually drilling plenty of holes in structural beams, it’s no wonder he still remembered that machine so many years later.

Spurred on by the new direction of the business, Maley and his partners purchased a Flex-Beam FB-40-42 in late 2021. The machine, with a 40-ft.-long bed, is capable of drilling, milling, tapping, and scribing. It has an automatic tool changer capable of accommodating six tools.

Gulf Coast Welding has found automated drilling of holes to be a dramatic improvement in fabricating efficiency when compared to manual hole layout and drilling with a magnetic drill.

The type of horizontal drilling center is unique in the metalworking industry in that a horizontal foot comes down to hold the workpiece in place. This is unlike typical vertical machining centers where vises are needed to keep the workpieces in place during metal removal.

"We talked to them about the machine, and then we went to see several of them. As we looked at it, we just felt like it was something that was necessary for us, with the stories of labor shortages and everything else. It just seemed like a clear answer for what we were trying to do," Maley said.

Upon installation, the machine started to make an immediate impact. It simply delivered in a way that manual drilling couldn’t match, according to Maley.

To illustrate his point, Maley described a job that Gulf Coast Welding won to fabricate some "lids" for an interstate project. The fabrications, several beams placed side by side and then bolted together, cover trenches in the road and allow vehicles to drive over the trenches without causing damage to vehicle suspensions. This was a job that wasn’t necessarily very large in terms of units, but it was something that required quick turnaround because taxpayers don’t enjoy being delayed in traffic because of construction.

Each of the 26 lid assemblies had about 120 holes, which meant there were 3,120 holes to drill. (Maley indicated that the project also included holemaking on some plate that was part of the assemblies, but he didn’t include that as part of this example.)

Maley estimated that if the job had to be done manually, the process to create each hole—laying it out, setting up the magnetic drill, and drilling the hole—would have taken about five minutes.

Working at that brisk pace, the fabricator would have needed 260 hours to drill all 3,120 holes. That’s about six-and-a-half weeks of work for one person, without overtime, of course.

Using the FlexBeam, Gulf Coast Welding was able to drill a hole every 30 seconds, completing the entire job in 26 hours. It also finished the job without any doubts that the holes were where they needed to be. The automated drilling offers a consistency and speed that a manual fabricator can’t match.

"We wouldn’t approach a job like that if we didn’t have that machine," Maley said.

In another instance, the company was approached to do some surface milling on inlet drainage grates for a test track belonging to a vehicle manufacturer. Like the interstate trench covers, this was another rush job.

The grates, each weighing about 100 lbs., required bolt holes to keep them in place as cars raced over them. Maley said his team was able to configure some fixtures that held these grates vertical on the FlexBeam.

Several of them were set up on the machine at one time. As the gantry moved from one grate to the next milling the holes, the machine operator was able to swap out the finished grates with new blanks. When it reached the end, the gantry returned to the other end and began milling again.

"It’s been a pretty flexible machine for us," Maley said.

In a final example, Maley recalled one more transportation-related job where structural beams are bolted vertically on the side of the bridge with barrier rails attached to them. These beams need holes perfectly aligned on both sides of the flange.

Even with the FlexBeam, Maley said he originally thought a beam would have to be flipped during processing so that the drill could reach both sides of the 6-in. beam. However, he discovered that by using a longer bit, the machine could drill both holes simultaneously with no need to flip the beam.

"We never had to worry about alignment because it was held in the same position and drilled straight through," he said. "When you don’t have to worry about any misalignment of holes, that’s a good thing."

A New Culture of Automation

Metal fabricators are used to figuring out solutions to problems. Because of that, they are used to relying on their own wits and experiences. They don’t automatically seek out an owner’s manual when they get a new gizmo.

An automated beam processing line is hardly a gizmo. Even with that being the case, the Gulf Coast Welding crew wanted to see if they could get the machine up and running right from the start. They were excited to see what they could do.

"I don’t know how much money and tooling we went through before we decided we’d stop and wait for Flex to tell us how to do it, but we eventually got there," Maley said.

Flex Machine Tools did offer the training, and that’s when everything started to click. Once the basics were covered, the Gulf Coast Welding team got more confident with the machine operations, eventually asking questions that challenged the Flex technical support team.

Logan Skelly, national sales manager, Flex Machine Tools, said he believes the FlexBeam’s user-friendly controls help to get fabricators going with their new automation.

"Our machine has a built-in graphical programming software that’s really easy to use. You can see the part, the holes or slots you are making, and where it will be sitting on the bed before running the program. This helps with the learning curve and eliminates some of the mistakes that can happen when jumping into CNC-controlled equipment for the first time," Skelly said.

Maley said that any fears the employees might have had about the new equipment coming in to take their jobs were quickly alleviated when they saw the machine at work. They recognized how the FlexBeam was going to be able to process more work than they ever could if they were to rely on strictly manual processes. More work means more revenue for the organization, which is a positive for the entire team.

"We have confidence on the drilling and milling side with what we know about this machine. We don’t have to worry about that side of things anymore," Maley said. "Now we can focus on the next thing."

There are a lot of things that could be happening in the near future for Gulf Coast Welding. Maley said he and his partners have acquired some property, and they are trying to figure out just how large of a facility they want to build. With new space, he then can focus on better material flow, such as the best way to feed material to the FlexBeam. The success with his beam processing automation also has him thinking about adding a CNC pipe profiler and even maybe channel processing equipment.

This new automated approach to metal fabricating appears to be leading Gulf Coast Welding down a promising path for future success.
