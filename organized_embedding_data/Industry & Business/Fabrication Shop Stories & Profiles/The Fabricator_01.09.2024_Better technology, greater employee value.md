Why sales-per-employee metrics are heading skyward

dvancing technology has helped fabricators navigate market demand after a tumultuous few years. According to recent financial and KPI surveys from the Fabricators and Manufacturers Association (FMA), sales growth has been softening after a dramatic fall in 2020 (14% decline on average) and a big rebound in 2021 (35% increase on average). Sales kept increasing through 2023 before a noticeable slowdown (at least for some) this year.

All this said, the sales-per-employee metric within FMA’s survey has been on a tear. Four years ago, the average from FMA survey respondents was $214,653; as of last year, that number has grown to $273,206. Material costs contribute to that, but so too does increased productivity. With better technology, the same number of people can produce more and, ultimately,

add more value

.

All these metrics set the stage for this year’s FABTECH in Orlando, an event that can inspire and perhaps, at least in some small way, help fabricators build good teams and even ease the hiring struggle. This applies as long as the focus remains on helping each employee, as well as each new hire, produce more value.

This gets to be a touchy area. I’ve talked to some who choose not to invest in the latest and greatest technology on purpose, to ensure people on the floor get a chance to learn. More than one shop owner has told me, "I don’t want a company full of button-pushers." Still others find that when they invest in technology, they open new career opportunities, be it simulating an offline brake program or setting up a robotic cell. They’re anything but button-pushers. As the automation ramps up, so do the sales opportunities and, ultimately, that sales-per-employee metric.

That success might have to do with the way organizations think about technology investment. It’s not just to relieve a constraint but instead is considered just one slice of the operational-excellence pie. That starts with open communication and collaboration across the enterprise.

About a decade ago I visited Cambridge Air Solutions outside St. Louis. The place had all the tenets of a mature lean manufacturing program with overall operational excellence: tight control of work-in-process, documented processes supported with images that made instructions visually clear and unambiguous, a clear factory layout with obvious value streams, clean air, everyone wearing appropriate personal protective equipment, ample machine safeguarding, good lighting.

But a table in the corner stood out. On it were jobs that required rework. What made that table so memorable was the way, on an adjacent cork board, the company documented mistakes without judgement, and with the realization that rework happened not due to

operator error

but instead from a breakdown in

processes

, both on the shop floor and in the office. They weren’t playing the blame game.

Such nonjudgemental examination can spur real improvement and, not least, some smart investments. Perhaps cheap material isn’t so cheap after all, so purchasing makes a change. Perhaps certain bend tolerances were too tight, so engineering (or sales in communication with a customer’s engineering department) makes a change. Or perhaps it’s time for a new machine or software, maybe combined with more automation and even a new approach to how work flows through the business.

This holistic approach to problem-solving can avoid tunnel vision that, in truth, can lead to some frustratingly ineffective technology upgrades. Say a fab shop can’t find enough welders. In fact, the welding department has seen high turnover for years—no surprise, considering how in-demand talented welders are.

So, the shop invests in several welding cobots. Turns out, because these processes are downstream, all the issues upstream (distortion from cutting, inaccuracy in bending) wreak havoc on the cobot. Manual welders could "make it work," but the cobot can’t. It does what it’s programmed to do, and if one part varies significantly from one piece to the next, good luck making the automation work. Sometimes, the process just isn’t feasible—so the cobot goes into the corner.

To be sure, more adaptive forms of automation continue to march forward. Many setups today use real-time feedback to adjust to variation on the fly. But the real issue here isn’t the fact that a cobot (or any robot) has issues dealing with variation. The problem is a lack of communication between every person who added value to the workpiece: the brake and laser operator, the programmers, the engineers, even the purchasing personnel.

Imagine the same or similar situation, yet now with open communication and root-cause analyses that cross departmental boundaries. They see the value of good material flow being managed through modern software and well-maintained machines. Parts are cut and formed consistently, which opens the door to reliable automation downstream, where cobots (or any other form of automation) are implemented as intended—augmenting, not replacing, the skilled worker.

With all this as a foundation, the shop can build career paths. Some might thrive as cross-trained "floaters," moving where demand dictates. Others might grow as hands-on craftspeople, true experts with the welding torch or gurus of bend programming, pushing the technological limits. Still others could grow as mechatronic technicians, troubleshooting systems, programming machines offline, optimizing individual processes or even entire value streams.

The worst new technology can do is create an expendable front line of disengaged (and probably low-paid) workers, mere pawns in the quest for efficiency. The best that technology can do is create companies of curious people who aren’t pulled in by this-is-the-way-we’ve-always-done-it inertia. They question the status quo and know the real challenges people on the front lines face.

And they benefit from smart technology investment, like the kind on display at next month’s FABTECH in Orlando. They might have to learn and adapt, but in doing so, they become more valuable than ever. For the ever curious, that’s not a bad way to spend a career.
