W

hen the news broke that 20-year-old welder Amber Czech had been murdered on the job, the welding community was shaken.

But what followed—the flood of responses across social media— revealed more than grief. It exposed cultural divides, blind spots, and lived realities that shape women’s experiences in the trades every day.

This column isn’t just about the tragedy. It’s about what those reactions say about who we are as an industry—and who we need to become.

SPARKING REACTIONS

After learning about Czech’s death, Josh wrote this raw and unfiltered social media post:

In our early years of interviewing young trades workers, it was eye-opening to me how many women we talked to who had first-person experiences of not just sexual harassment, but of physical/sexual assault. Stalking, physical violence, attempted rape, actual rape. Like nearly every woman welder we talked to had horror stories. I wish this were hyperbole. S--- is way beyond the pale.

I think it is easy to diminish a lot of things as a guy, and it’s hard to step into their shoes. Any blue-collar job you’ve been on, you’re the OVERWHELMING majority. It’s been a good ol’ boys club for years. There’s a certain language and mentality that’s pervasive. That, in and of itself, is incredibly intimidating for a newcomer to break into. Add in the very high chance that some knuckleheads are going to take it way too far when they see a girl around. Now put yourself in her shoes. Would you have the guts to get into a trade where you have to deal with that s---? But they do. Because they love welding (just like you). Because they NEED a job to provide (just like you). Because they have every right to make a living JUST LIKE YOU.

On Nov. 11, a 20-year-old welder named Amber Czech was killed on the job by a 40-year-old a--hole with a sledgehammer to the back of her head. He said he "just didn’t like her." He also said he’d been planning it "for some time." I can’t imagine how he kept his hate to himself.

It isn’t enough to not be an a--hole. Or to be neutral. If you hear or see something, say something. DO something. One of the things the women who make it often say is how they found an ally—a coworker, a boss— who keeps others in check. And the best experiences are when that basic human kindness is exemplified by the workforce as a whole.

I don’t know what else to say. This isn’t groundbreaking s---. Just be a decent human.

Rest in Peace, Amber.

What happened next—what women and men said in response—revealed patterns that deserve close attention.

PATTERN 1: WOMEN RESPONDED WITH RECOGNITION, NOT SURPRISE

Women welders were horrified—but few were shocked. They saw Amber’s story as the extreme end of patterns many face daily. We received permission to share their stories, but ensured anonymity to protect their privacy and jobs.

Here’s what they shared:

• "We need to change the culture of not intervening. We all deserve to come home whole."

• "Sad reality is we risk losing our jobs if we speak up—or just get treated worse."

And then there was this story—one that exposes where systems often fail women long before violence escalates:

"I was having issues with a guy causing problems for a while. I went to my supervisor. He simply said he’d talk to him. The problem never went away. After a particularly bad incident, I went to HR and filed a report. While I was in the office, the owner came in and told me he had big plans for me to move up—but I needed to learn to have thicker skin. You have no idea how thick my skin already was, and how much s--- I took before I finally became ‘that woman’ who went to HR."

Women don’t go to HR casually. They go as a last resort. And when leadership tells them to "toughen up," the message is clear: Your safety is negotiable. Your discomfort is inconvenient.

More stories from private women-in-trades groups echoed the same themes:

• "I keep pepper spray in my welding jacket after repeated cornering incidents from a co-worker."

• "I changed my break schedule to avoid being alone in the shop."

• "I don’t go to HR for anything. They are pointless, only there to save face for the company. They will say, ‘Oh, we are here to protect the workers,’ but who signs their paychecks is who they protect."

• "My boss didn’t know how else to protect me from a co-worker who showed hostility to me on multiple occasions. So he fired me from a job that I loved. In the end, that hostile co-worker stabbed a man and then himself."

• "I thought about quitting the apprenticeship nearly every day. I’m glad I stuck it out and got my plumbing license. I wish the trades were easier in terms of the culture, because the work itself is so interesting and satisfying."

These stories didn’t surprise women in the trades—but they should alarm all of us.

Support for Amber Czech’s family and a call for workplace safety came pouring in from all corners of the internet.

Josh Welton shared his thoughts on Instagram about Amber Czech’s murder shortly after it happened.

PATTERN 2: BLIND SPOTS

• "I’ve worked 20-plus years in the trades and never seen any woman treated badly."

• "Been in the trades for 35 years, and only once have I ever seen a guy be rude to a fellow female tradesperson. Stop with this bulls---."

These comments may feel like a defense of the trades, but they inadvertently dismiss women’s testimonies.

Here’s the reality: Just because you haven’t seen it does not mean it isn’t happening. It means it isn’t happening to or near you.

Women often experience harassment when they’re alone, they’re new, they’re the only woman on a shift. Others won’t intervene, and reporting is risky.

And it’s important to note this: Many men—like Josh—responded with empathy, accountability, and support for women in the trades. Their voices matter, and their willingness to listen matters even more. The goal isn’t to shame men; it’s to help them recognize that their experience may not reflect others’ realities.

One of Darla’s own encounters illustrates this blind spot. Years ago, a plumber diagnosing an issue in her home told her she should get in the shower naked so he could see how the water ran off her body. She screamed, shoved him out, and called his employer. The employer minimized it, then claimed he said "none of that."

A culture that only validates what men witness overlooks most of what women endure.

PATTERN 3: SOME RESPONSES REFRAMED THE CONVERSATION AWAY FROM WOMEN ENTIRELY

Another reply: "Why is this about women when more men are murdered on jobsites?"

This is true. Most workplace homicide victims are men because they make up the majority of the workforce in high-risk environments. But this conversation isn’t about homicide statistics. It’s about patterns of targeted, nonfatal violence, which women disproportionately experience.

According to the latest Bureau of Labor Statistics data, 72.5% of all nonfatal workplace violence victims are women. This includes assaults, harassment, threats, intimidation, and sexual violence. These facts are not contradictory; they describe two separate problems.

Men are more often victims of fatal workplace violence. Women are more often victims of targeted, escalating nonfatal violence.

Deflecting the conversation back to men’s homicide rates sidesteps the reality women consistently report: the hostility that often precedes the worst-case scenarios.

WHAT THESE PATTERNS TELL US

The reactions to Amber’s murder reveal that women and men often experience different versions of the same workplace. Some women describe harassment as commonplace, retaliation as real, and fear or apprehension as familiar. Some men describe workplaces where nothing is wrong; they find harassment nonthreatening or funny, and they’re skeptical toward women’s accounts. That gap—between lived reality and perceived reality—is where danger grows.

WHERE WE GO FROM HERE

For workplaces to be safer and better prepared to prevent violence, we have to confront these patterns openly:

Prioritize and respect every account of harassment.

Reporting is rarely easy and often involves personal and professional risk.

Treat early complaints seriously.

It’s rarely the first incident. It’s usually the tenth.

Intervene as bystanders.

Silence protects the aggressor.

Ensure HR systems protect—not punish—those who report.

If women "with thicker skin than anyone realizes" don’t feel safe reporting, the system is broken.

Stop using personal experience as proof. Someone

else’s reality may be entirely different.

Did women report good experiences? Absolutely.

• "My shop has always made sure that I feel safe. Guys that work with me are like family!"

• "I am so very thankful to all those that looked out for me in my 28 years in the industry."

Be a workplace or a co-worker who inspires sentiments like these.

AMBER DESERVED BETTER

Amber Czech loved welding. She showed up, worked hard, and deserved to be safe. This tragedy is a call to ensure that the next person who walks into a shop does not walk into silence, indifference, or danger. We owe that not just to Amber; all workers who show up because they love the work and need the paycheck deserve the same dignity and safety.

As Josh noted in his original reflection, it shouldn’t be groundbreaking to say this: Just be decent humans.
