# How AI can help the metal fabricator

[TARİH: 01.03.2025 The Fabricator]

Cover Story

Industrial sales and marketing might never be the same

By

Tim Heston

Kulpreya Chaichatpornsuk/iStock / Getty Images Plus

W

hen it comes to artificial intelligence (AI), people in the metal fabrication industry typically think of supercharged automation. Thing is, modern machines offer intuitive controls, fast setups, and some incredible throughput. Sure, breakdowns do happen, and they can hurt in the short term. Overall, though, metal fabrication capacity has gone through the roof in recent years—without AI’s help.

If a shop has the right equipment and culture, productivity on the floor usually isn’t the constraint. You have to look at the process chain—order processing, engineering, estimating, sales, and marketing.

Marketing, lead generation, sales, customer communication, and similar activities all are critical for top-line growth. But defining what’s wasteful can be a challenge, including the opportunity costs. How many sales calls lead to dead ends? How many no-quotes (requests that, for whatever reason, were never bid upon) could have been multimillion-dollar orders?

Is there a better way to market and sell in metal fabrication? According to Josh Blankenship, there is, and AI will lead the way.

Ignore AI at Your Peril

Blankenship is the Houston-based director of business development for OuterBox, a marketing firm with numerous manufacturing clients, including many in custom metal fabrication. To set the stage, he clarified that the most effective AI, especially in the B2B world, isn’t represented by customer-facing tools like chatbots. At best, they operate with a narrow set of parameters. They don’t answer open-ended questions, but they can help guide prospects quickly to the right people or department.

At worst, customer-facing AI bots are just annoying and slow customers’ buying journey as they desperately try to get in touch with a real person. At the very worst, they can spread incorrect or even incoherent information and damage a fabricator’s brand.

The experience can be so bad, fab shop leaders might choose to ignore AI altogether, at least at present. They see it as a curiosity and not really applicable to a niche technical business like precision sheet metal fabrication. But as Blankenship explained, fab shops that ignore AI do so at their peril.

Yes, having customers interface directly with AI tools might not be effective. But within the company, and especially for sales and marketing personnel, AI can be a true game-changer. Those employees can use AI to augment, not replace, the skills and knowledge they already have. The right skills help them make the most out of AI, and their knowledge helps catch and correct the errors AI tools make.

Just a Tool

When most people hear about AI, they think about the well-known brands: large language models (LLMs) like ChatGPT, Google’s Gemini, or Microsoft’s Copilot. They also might think about image generators like Adobe Firefly or Image Creator by Microsoft.

These might be fun to play with, but after an hour or two, they devolve into toys that consume our curiosity but really don’t change our workday in a meaningful way. Ask Copilot to draw you a picture of a press brake or laser tube cutter, and you’ll get images that might look nice, but they’re far from anything you might see in the real world. As Blankenship put it, "AI’s a little rusty."

These AI tools are just that—tools. Without the right strategy (carefully worded queries) and raw material (data), the tools aren’t very useful. According to Blankenship, "It’s like handing someone a hammer and saying, ‘Go build me an armoire. … Ultimately, the tools are only as good as the person using them. The questions and prompting you do greatly affect the output. If you ask AI more strategic questions, its outputs will be superior."

In early February, news of DeepSeek roiled markets, mainly because the Chinese firm accomplished more with less, implying that perhaps the industry doesn’t need the high levels of chip, data center, and energy infrastructure investments previously thought. More important for the fab shop, and really any business, is the fact that DeepSeek released its code as open source, meaning companies with the right know-how can use it as a backbone to their own AI-based technology.

The news could signal that AI tools are about to become more powerful than ever. But they’re still just tools. How can a fabricator make the most of them?

Company Policies and Data Security

"More than a third of marketers are using AI in some capacity," Blankenship said. "It could be for chatbots, coding, content writing, and even design and graphic work.

"Use of AI is already happening," he continued.

"So, the first thing we recommend is getting an AI policy in place within your organization. When is using AI OK, and how does it get touched once an AI tool produces a product?"

This last point is key. A fabricator might want anything created by AI to be reviewed by a knowledgeable person. AI can automate many office and communication tasks, but as has been well publicized, it’s far from perfect. And even if it’s accurate, it might not be the best way to present information. Sure, AI can generate an email, but the absence of that human touch can be quite conspicuous.

"People are starting to get an eye for AI-generated content," Blankenship said.

Another consideration is data safety and security. "If you use, say, ChatGPT and upload company documents to the platform, ChatGPT trains the AI on anything you’re uploading or sending to it."

Today, any company can create (or work with an outside coding firm to develop) their own customized AI tool based on application programming interfaces (APIs) made available by the major AI players. "When you do this, you can safeguard what it sees, including client data that you don’t want getting into the general knowledge base [used by the AI platform]."

This is what AI thinks a press brake looks like. You can see the resemblance, but clearly, AI has a ways to go.

Details matter here, so researching how the platforms use data—and ways to keep data private—is a good first step before diving into deeper AI waters. Blankenship added that data privacy should be taken seriously, but it also shouldn’t prevent fabricators from using AI in general. Its potential is just too great.

Data, Internal and External

AI’s seemingly magical power to create verbal, visual, and even audio content comes from processing power, the chips and data center infrastructure that has fueled the most recent tech boom. Still, for most metal fabricators, the most visible AI LLMs don’t seem like true disrupters, at least not yet.

In fact, many continue to watch the AI hype with a skeptical eye. According to a recent essay by David Wilcox and Tom Orlik of Bloomberg Economics, "For all the excitement, and the trillion-dollar valuations for AI firms, evidence of a boost to productivity remains thin on the ground." They added that this isn’t surprising. It took years for a range of technologies, from the electric motor to the personal computer, to show up in the productivity statistics.

Consider the evolution of PCs. At first, a PC was essentially a toy for hobbyists. Spreadsheets then became the killer app. But when accountants were marveling over Lotus 1-2-3 (and worried about job losses in bookkeeping), no one was thinking of the internet or smartphones. Few imagined we’d be carrying such powerful digital processing power in our pockets.

Today, those who create content are marveling at AI’s ability to create video, audio, prose, and computer code, and they’re worried about their jobs. Could AI’s "internet and smartphone moment" be hidden over the horizon?

Blankenship has no crystal ball, but he did point out AI’s enormous potential for metal fabricators, especially when it comes to AI engines working with both

external

data from across the internet as well as

internal

data within a fabricator’s four walls. Again, companies can utilize APIs provided by LLMs to customize their AI tools. This has helped develop chatbots tailored for specific uses, drawing from both public and company-specific data.

Information could include not just production data but also prior sales data from throughout the customer journey, from initial contact to order placement and even contract negotiations. Blankenship described how shops can use AI to address a contract renegotiation, drawing from past emails and other communications. What wording worked for which markets, and when? When do bullet points work? What phrasing helps push a deal forward?

AI also could draw information from enterprise resource planning (ERP) systems and manufacturing execution systems (MESs) to perform near-instantaneous data analysis. Blankenship described one application that entailed feeding proposal and sales data to AI:

Prompt: Can you please analyze this data for the correlation between overall sales and proposed volume?

AI immediately charts the data, with the number of proposals on the X axis and sales on the Y axis. It then gives the following response:

The correlation between the number of proposals and sales is approximately 0.71. This indicates a strong positive correlation, meaning that as the number of proposals increases, the overall sales levels tend to increase as well.

Making this correlation isn’t novel—but the speed and ease of the analysis is. With more data, AI could give other insights, like what sectors represent healthy bid-win rates for specific types of work at specific times of year. This could lead to targeted sales efforts that aim to boost manufacturing throughput, smooth demand cycles, and avoid the feast-or-famine environment many shops face.

AI also could help refine a fab shop’s ideal customer profile. This could relate to order characteristics like geometry, complexity, manufacturing requirements, volume, and the timing of orders. In other words, AI could help managers refine the customer mix. Combine this insight with streamlined quoting processes and perhaps automated quoting software, and a fabricator can quote more strategically—no more passing over a quote that could have turned into a massive opportunity.

All this hinges on shop data, both its quality and quantity. "So many businesses have fragmented data that’s disorganized," Blankenship said. "It’s also isolated in specific systems. That’s a problem. If you have bad data, then all the efficiencies you’re trying to gain by using these AI tools will be less than you hoped for. You’re putting in bad data, and you can’t overcome that."

This is where discipline over data gathering, organizing, and housing come into play. What performance indicators need to be tracked, how, and how often?

"You need to decide what metrics you want to feed into these systems," he said. "That’s step zero. If you’re not doing that, any AI system you buy or build will be fed an incomplete picture of what’s important to your business."

In the Customer’s Shoes

Think about the data that exists, both inside a fabricator’s four walls and across the wider internet. Every query about a specific technical discipline related to metal fabrication—mining, heavy equipment design, offroad vehicles, aerospace engineering, or anything else—builds data that AI tools can learn from.

This data allows for some creative use of LLMs, including what’s known as persona roleplay. Blankenship described a recent interaction with a customized chatbot, built off ChatGPT technology, that went something like the following.

You: Can you assume the role of an aerospace engineer?

ChatGPT: Of course, I’d be delighted to step into the shoes of an aerospace engineer. What do you need assistance with?

You: Can you tell me what your biggest concerns are when looking online for a new fabrication partner?

The AI tool then lists specific details about quality assurance, certifications, technical expertise, risk management, and more. Some of the results aren’t surprising, especially to anyone who’s spent some time in the metal fabrication business, but certain aspects can create fodder for sales presentations, marketing material, and even strategic growth efforts.

Fabricators often think of AI together with Industrial Internet of Things and advanced factory automation—but AI could be just as impactful, if not more so, in the office.

At the very least, the tool conveys what matters to potential customers and, hence, what should be communicated regularly and easy to find on the fabricator’s website. A prime example is a certification that specific customers value. "We’ve worked with fabricators that are certified to, say, AS9100," Blankenship said, "but they’ve got that information tucked away on a website footer or three pages deep in a certification library they have online."

A fabricator sees AS9100 as just one of many certifications. A prospective customer who’s an aerospace engineer sees it as a top criterion to look for when selecting a fabricator. An AI persona can reveal that so the fab shop can adjust its website and marketing communications accordingly.

"The AI persona gives me angles and approaches for content that I can query further. It’s so much better than saying, ‘Hey ChatGPT, write me some website content on aerospace fabrication.’"

Blankenship reiterated that such strategies really become useful when companies build their own custom AI tools, using APIs from one of the major AI players that incorporate a company’s internal data. And as with anything else in the digital age, better data yields better results.

Make Metal Fabrication More Effective

Recent KPI surveys from the Fabricators and Manufacturers Association peg the average bid-win rate between 25% and 35%, though actual win rates vary widely depending on a fabricator’s customer mix.

Low bid-win rates, seen as an unavoidable reality of custom manufacturing, contribute to inconsistent demand. Sometimes a laser might be running over multiple shifts; other times, it might sit idle; it’s not waiting for repair or holding until a downstream bottleneck clears. Business is just slow, and the shop has excess laser cutting capacity.

Consider again that most shops only win 25% of the bids they submit.

"By the time people send you a quote, they’re likely more than halfway through their decision to purchase from you," Blankenship said. "A Gartner study says they’re already 57% through their buying journey. They do their own research, and [after viewing your website or marketing material], if you can’t meet the standard they have in their own head, they just hit the backspace and go on." The idea, he said, is to target marketing efforts so well—thanks in part to smart use of AI—that the prospect is further along the buying journey

before

submitting a quote.

To think about that 25% bid-win rate another way, imagine if only 25% of parts emerging from the laser were good parts. That likely happened in the R&D labs back in the 1970s and 1980s. Since then, the technology has improved, and many fab shops enjoy first-part, good-part quality, not just in cutting but in forming and other processes downstream.

Could something similar happen with sales? Could bid-win rates rise? Could customer relationships grow even closer? Could machine utilization and overall throughput rise? Could the customer journey through sales, engineering and estimating, and order processing be streamlined?

Consider the implications in estimating and engineering. A fabricator could train an AI bot based on shop floor capabilities, like the grades and thicknesses of material the shop can cut competitively, or the material it can form based on the punches and dies in its tooling library. The AI tool might not get everything. It wouldn’t replace bend simulations or the input of an experienced brake operator. And a knowledgeable engineer should check any suggestion that AI makes.

Even so, AI could help engineers catch details they might miss and streamline the drawing-creation process. And every exception the engineer makes to what the AI suggested could be fed into the AI engine, allowing it to learn over time. This data in turn could improve sales and quoting. No longer would salespeople need to query engineers about basic questions.

"AI tools could accelerate staff who aren’t at the engineering level," Blankenship said, "yet they’re still customer-facing and answering requests."

He added, however, that such interactions need guardrails to ensure misinformation doesn’t muddy the waters. Office personnel shouldn’t use AI as a replacement for knowledge, lest a host of faulty drawings make it to the floor. The point, Blankenship said, is that AI will allow that engineer to spend more time on complex questions and less time on repetitive, time-consuming tasks.

In this way, AI shares similarities with shop floor automation. A sophisticated machine requires a knowledgeable operator. People with less training might be able to make good parts, but they might also produce a lot of bad parts in a hurry, especially if they feed that modern machine the wrong information (incorrect part revision, for instance).

A shop needs knowledgeable people to make best use of manufacturing technology. The same holds true with AI.

Casually playing with AI chatbots—asking AI to alter a picture or draw a diagram—is analogous to early computer hobbyists. Companies making use of image and LLM processing to streamline and automate business processes might be analogous to those early spreadsheet users. Those accountants who marveled over Lotus 1-2-3 knew the efficiencies the software enabled were profound, but they probably couldn’t have imagined the technological progress that ultimately played out.

The same could be said of AI in the business world today, and this includes the fab shop. What will AI bring in the coming decades? If history is a guide, we haven’t seen anything yet.
