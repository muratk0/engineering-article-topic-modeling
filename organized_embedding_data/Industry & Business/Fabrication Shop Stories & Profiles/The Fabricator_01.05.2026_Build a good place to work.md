# Build a good place to work

How to ease the hiring challenge

W

e often hear companies lament that it’s hard to find great people, but I’ll be honest: We’ve never had that problem. At OSH Cut, we employ 120 people and are growing the team fast. Our turnover rate is below 1%.

I think a few things make this possible. First, we hire for cultural fit instead of experience and perform all training internally; second, we pay a living wage; third, we create a clean, safe work environment.

HIRE FOR FIT, NOT EXPERIENCE

Hiring for cultural fit instead of experience massively widens the hiring pool. Very few people have years of experience operating press brakes and lasers, but there are hosts of hard working, smart young people who are willing to learn. When you don’t have to limit your potential employee pool, options abound.

Of course, hiring for cultural fit over experience has a downside: You can’t just hire a new hand, walk them to the floor, and let them work. It takes more time and energy to train a green employee. But it’s worth the effort.

To account for this, we developed training programs for every workstation and operation. For each job, our program includes classwork, safety training, job shadowing, and supervised operation. Employees earn a certification that allows them to run machines independently. Each training module also includes a quality section, where we highlight expectations and common failure modes. In bending, for instance, we are constantly fighting backwards bends on visually similar parts.

After a day of standard HR onboarding, every employee spends a week on a "shop tour," where they take a partial or full day shadowing an operator at a workstation: inventory, sheet laser cutting and bending, laser tube cutting and bending, finishing, powder coating, and shipping. This provides a holistic view of the operation so that every hire understands how parts flow and how work is performed. It also gives them a taste of where they might prefer to work in the future. Employees usually gravitate to one area or another eventually.

After the first weeklong shop tour, every employee is required to certify in final inspection. There, employees learn what passes and fails. Classwork includes a module highlighting both target quality and every kind of quality escape. After a few weeks handling tens of thousands of unique SKUs, our team members get a great feel for the wide diversity of parts that flow through the shop every day.

From there, training in new operations is more flexible, based on need and employee interest. Some people love running the lasers. Others prefer bending. Whatever people choose, I believe this kind of mobility on the floor helps our team stay engaged.

PAY A LIVING WAGE

After hiring for cultural fit and then investing time into training, the last thing you want is for your team to turn over. I’ve said before that if you want skilled, reliable, mature adults to work for you, you have to pay them enough to live. To help us meet that objective, we’ve set guaranteed, time-based minimum pay tiers that apply to all employees—whether they are packing boxes, running machines, or answering phones. Pay increases are available for team leads, shift leads, and specialist positions. Meanwhile, we of course pay shift differential at 15% for swing shift and 10% for night shift.

These base raises are increased annually at the rate of the Social Security Cost of Living Adjustment to help ensure that our employees maintain buying parity as cost of living goes up with inflation.

On top of base wages, we pay a weekly bonus equal to a fixed percentage of earned revenue (shipped volume, not incoming volume), distributed to employees based on hours worked in the previous pay period. This weekly bonus is decreased by the replacement cost of any quality escapes that occurred in the preceding week. Between hourly wages and weekly bonuses, a sizable percentage of our people make six figures annually.

I’m sensitive to the fact that decreasing the weekly bonus based on quality mistakes could feel punitive, especially because the decrease affects all shop employees, not just those responsible. But our philosophy is that we are all hitched to the same horse. When we have great revenue weeks, everyone gets a better bonus. If we have a bad week for quality escapes, everyone feels that too.

We communicate all the factors that affect the weekly bonus so that the team understands what happened and how performance can be improved. To that end, we have an all-hands standup meeting every Friday to discuss every quality escape, companywide. We also highlight all customer feedback, positive and negative. This helps ground quality outcomes and helps us understand how we can improve as a company.

GREAT MACHINES UNLOCK GREAT PEOPLE

It can be hard to increase wages when margins are tight. It can definitely be a big ask. For us, one major unlock has been perhaps surprising: Great equipment increases productivity, which allows more revenue to go back to great employees.

Our first press brake was an old hydraulic machine with no CNC. We’d set the single backgauge manually by rotating a hand-turn crank and watching a dial indicator. The ram depth stop was similar, though it was finicky. Mechanically, it was basically a bolt on an all-thread rod: twist the bolt, change the stop depth. It usually took several test blanks to get the angles just right on the limited set of air dies we had at the time.

It was a dangerous machine too: no safety equipment, coarse speed control. It was a machine that did exactly what you told it to do without second-guessing. That can actually be nice, but it’s scary. I don’t think it is worth the risk when there are safer options available.

We are a high-mix shop, so it didn’t take us long to realize that for a bending service to work, we really needed a press brake with modern controls. After we confided this to an equipment sales rep, he smiled knowingly. "In the old days, running a brake was like black magic. You had to put on a tin foil hat and do a secret dance to get bends to come out just right. Some old hands could really work magic." Maybe that was part of a sales pitch, but it worked out to be true.

Eight years later, we have seven press brakes. Some are hydraulic, some hybrid (servo-driven hydraulic), and some all-electric. But each supports offline programming; precise CNC backgauges; and tight controls for depth, angle measurement, crowning, and springback compensation. Setup is usually quick, and the first article comes out perfect to print. It takes maybe a couple weeks to get a new operator with no experience up to speed. Our more experienced operators get really good at tackling tricky edge cases and complex bends, but in general, most parts are manageable even for new team members.

The same is true of our lasers. Compared to older CO2 technology, our fiber lasers are simpler to operate. There’s still a lot to know and maintain, of course—protective glass needs to be cleaned, optics need to be focused, cut conditions tweaked, and nozzles replaced. But that’s the bulk of the challenge. Great operators can be taken from zero experience to functional expertise in weeks.

Importantly, these modern machines are not only more productive than older machines; they are easier to operate. That synergizes well with our "hire for fit and train internally" policy.

CREATE A CLEAN WORK ENVIRONMENT

Most people don’t want to work in a dark, dusty, unhealthy environment. If you want to attract people, create a shop that you are proud to show off to prospective hires. A coat of paint on the walls makes a surprisingly massive difference on its own; polished or epoxied concrete floors create a high-tech vibe; climate control makes the work environment less physically demanding; workstations paired with touchscreen monitors and great software help manage complexity and de-stress the job.

Creating a culture of cleanliness helps as well. During the last 30 minutes of all three shifts, we shut down machines to clean, sweep, and dust working surfaces. Parts are kept organized and labeled on rolling carts and shelves. To help reduce clutter, we tape off wide aisles and require that they are kept clear, both for safety and appearance.

We aren’t perfect on this front. But keeping spaces uncluttered and setting aside dedicated time for cleaning workstations and machines helps create an environment that’s inviting and fun to work in.

WHERE PEOPLE WANT TO WORK

The general rule is sort of a no-brainer. People don’t like to work in high-stress, dirty environments where people are yelling and cussing. People need to earn a wage that allows them to support their families.

If you create a positive work environment, pay at least a minimum living wage, and hire great people regardless of experience, you almost can’t help but come out ahead.
