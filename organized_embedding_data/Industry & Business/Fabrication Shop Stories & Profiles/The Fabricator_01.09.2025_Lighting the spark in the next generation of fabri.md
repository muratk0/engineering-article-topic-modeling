That’s Chris Ellingsen’s philosophy in introducing metal fabricating to those who have never really cut, formed, or welded metal. As an advanced manufacturing and fabrication instructor at Grayslake Central High School in Grayslake, Ill., he tries to get his students involved with projects that inspire them to focus their talents on turning out the best fabrication possible. In school, it’s been projects like stools and grills.

"I want them to work on a product where they want to see it through to the very end," he said.

He applied that same thinking to a week-long Dream, Design & Create Camp held at the school in mid-June. The camp, made possible with a grant from SparkForce, the FMA Foundation, had a primary focus on welding laser-cut parts to create a fire pit.

"A lot of these kids are being exposed to welding for the very first time," Ellingsen explained, noting that of the eight teenagers in the program, only a couple had family members who were involved in manufacturing.

The fire pit, comprised of a six-sided base and top receptacle, is made of 1038 mild steel. Laser Precision, a metal fabricator in nearby Libertyville, Ill., laser cut the blanks for the campers. Ellingsen said he wanted to have precisely cut sections so that they came together more easily when it came time for welding.

Wanting to maximize the time under the hood during the week, Ellingsen had the blanks ready to go for the campers upon their arrival the first day. After an introduction to the project and discussing the basics of how the parts would come together, the campers were introduced to the processes to be used in the fabrication of the fire pit.

The first station was the collaborative robot. Campers learned how to create a program that applied six tack welds to join the walls of the top receptacle and six seam welds to join those same walls to the base.

"I really like welding a lot. It’s fun, especially using the robot," said Cecilia Abalos, a Grayslake Central senior, who was a helper during the camp after having learned welding under Ellingsen as a junior.

It sounds like a commercial, but programming a cobot is easy enough that a 13-year-old can do it. The student pushes a button, maneuvers the cobot to the point where the weld begins, proceeds to guide the cobot over the prescribed weld path, stops the cobot where the weld ends, and pushes the button to end the programming for that portion of the job before starting the entire cycle again for the next weld.

Abalos said that she has enjoyed the experience in the advanced manufacturing class so much that she is thinking about pursuing welding accreditation once she turns 18. "It would be good to have that in my back pocket," she said.

Jonathan Colon-Bailey, 13, worked diligently on his fire pit, smoothing out his stick welds with a grinder so that he could go at it again and fill in some of the craters left on his original joint attempt.

"This has been super fun," he said.

Even though he had no experience welding, Colon-Bailey said that he did enjoy working with his hands. He’s gone so far as to do some woodworking to build a bridge that he used with some of his Lego creations. He added that he liked the idea of expanding his skill set.

"It seems like [welding] might be something that I could do as a job or career in the future."

Ellingsen said the campers shared their fire pit fabrications with their parents on the final day of the class. He’s also had the campers describe the work that went into it.

In the past, the summer camp projects have included metal mazes and light sabers inspired by "Star Wars." Projects like that get young people excited to sign up and attend these classes. Ellingsen said he typically fills the eight slots in these camps in a little over a week.

Meeting the Realities of the Manufacturing World

When kids get bored over the summer, many head straight to a video game or TV. Some hop on a bike or even head to the beach.

But a few, like 13-year-old Jasper Busson, grab a welding torch. They might even wake up at 5 a.m. and tack together a surprisingly lifelike flower from a horseshoe, a long bolt, and a fistful of washers.

Busson, a student at Hastings Middle School in Hastings, Mich., took part in a SparkForce Manufacturing Camp at the school in July. In addition to designing and building their own jump-apeg game boards with TinkerCad software and the school’s jigsaw, Busson and about a dozen other campers visited metals-related businesses like Co-Dee Stamping in Hastings and Lewis Welding in nearby Grand Rapids, Mich.

"That was really cool," Busson said of the visit to Lewis Welding. "I learned a lot about telling the difference between aluminum and steel and what different welds look like."

William Renner, who teaches innovation and design at the school, is in his second year of heading up SparkForce camps there after many years of running camps aimed at connecting STEM courses and the trades. He said when he takes students on field trips to area manufacturers, he hopes that students begin to connect the ideas they talk about and the reality they see at different companies.

Hastings Middle School teacher William Renner (right) helps student Logan Guernsey, 13, line up a jump-a-peg game on a drill press during a recent camp in Hastings, Mich.

"We tried to take that model that we had done with the trades and built it into a manufacturing basis," said Renner, who’s been in his current job for seven years and teaching for 20. "We do a lot of tours of local industry. Why we’re doing it—the idea of the program—is, ‘How do you connect what they’re doing in a regular classroom to the skills that become employable?’"

One connection that Renner’s 13-year-old son, Matthew, made was the relationships some area businesses have with each other—a mainstay of regional shops everywhere.

"I found it interesting that the places we went to, most of them actually had to work together a little bit," Matthew said. "One would make parts for the other one, and one would make repairs on the machines."

For his part, Busson also seems to have quite a bit figured out already, starting with his little side project, which he brought in to show Renner and his classmates.

"It’s not my best welding, but it holds," said Busson, who started welding about a year ago when his grandfather gave him a welding machine. "I built this all this morning. My dad helped a little bit. He showed me the basics, but I really have gotten better.

"I want to get into a welding career when I’m older," he added. "I heard we were going to some welding shops, and I wanted to check out what it would be like and what I should look for in a good welding shop."

With all the site visits, even Renner is learning things he didn’t know from companies like Ketchum Machine Co. in nearby Freeport, Mich. Add that exposure to the classroom knowledge the kids received at the camp and from a visit to Kellogg Community College’s Regional Technology Manufacturing Center, and students just might have the eureka moment that leads to a career someday.

"We’re really emphasizing the idea that there are so many different paths to success, but in any of these paths, you need to be skilled," Renner said. "You need to have a trade. You have to have something that you’re able to do that not everybody else is able to do.

"And then also have the students get exposed to understanding just how much money will be paid in terms of skilled labor, as I’m talking to electrical apprentices who are making more than I did until I’d been teaching for 15 years. So, it’s not a piece of paper that gets you to where you want to go. It’s the skill and finding the employment."

That message is exactly what organizers of the Reimagined Rosies STEM Makers Camp wanted to impress on middle and high school students attending one of the many SparkForce Manufacturing Camps hosted nationwide this summer.

The two-week Reimagined Rosies camp, held this past June at Alessandro High School in Hemet, Calif., wrapped up with a two-hour drive north for tours of NASA’s center at Edwards Air Force Base in Edwards, Calif., and the Rio Tinto Borax Mine in Boron, Calif., the world’s largest borax mine.

At AFRC’s Experimental Fabrication Branch Sheet Metal Shop, campers got a close look at how a real fab shop operates—from fabricating simple aluminum brackets to reconfiguring critical aircraft structures. Staff explained how the equipment works, how the stations collaborate, and how the sheet metal shop coordinates with the weld shop.

"You guys are on the right path if you stick with this," Lead Aerospace Engineering Tech Toby Brittain told the group.

The Reimagined Rosies camp is the brainchild of STEM and skilled trades advocate Demi Knight Clark.

"It’s not just a welding camp. I want to put STEM back into the narrative of welding," Clark said. "I hear from some educators that students aren’t getting enough opportunity to sharpen critical thinking skills. The whole point was not to be in a classroom."

Clark partnered with Josh O’Neal, who leads Hemet Unified School District’s CTE welding program, and welder/educator Nate Bowman.

By the end of the camp, O’Neal, Clark, and Bowman had the students operating like they were in a job shop.

"Show up. Come in. Make it happen," said O’Neal, who has expanded the district’s weld shop with more Lincoln Electric booths. "I gave them a lot of freedom to do a bunch of different projects. It is a job shop."

Projects included everything from building a scaled-down load-bearing drawbridge and fabricating roses from thin-gauge sheet metal to creating 6-ft. scrap metal sculptures (a ninja and a robot) and designing chassis for a go-kart.

The instructors also brought in industry leaders for hands-on demos in oxyacetylene, abrasives, and automation.

"With [the campers] having that kind of access, they were immersed. They were happy," O’Neal said. "I know when they leave here, they’re going to talk about robots. They’re going to talk about welding."

O’Neal, Clark, and Bowman hope those experiences show campers there’s a clear career path after high school.

"You gotta show them there are a lot of options," said Clark. "I think that’s part of the success this week."
