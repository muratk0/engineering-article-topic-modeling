f this survey shows anything, it proves that if a shop delivers on time nearly all of the time, it stands out in a crowd.

More than half of those surveyed reported on-time delivery rates of less than 95 percent, and some reported rates of 80 percent or less. True, a majority reported that delayed jobs are just one or two days late on average, but a not-insignificant 18 percent reported that many orders are delayed by five days or more.

So what’s really going on here? This survey aims to answer that question.

Anyone who has spent time on the shop floor knows what it’s like to deal with expedited jobs, to multitask, and generally to bend over backward to get the job done. But sometimes no amount of back-bending can prevent an order from being late.

How does this happen? Many pointed to workforce challenges—no surprises there. Still, among all the causes of delay that shops cited, one dominated:

spikes in demand, when many orders come in the door at once

.

A majority reported that more than half of company revenue came from their top three customers. These customers often are in the same or similar markets and have similar demand cycles. That is, they tend to order more products at the same times of the year.

Many customer’s sales forecasts, if they’re provided at all, aren’t very accurate. And when planning production, most shops described their relationship with top customers as "somewhat transparent" or "opaque." Translation: They don’t really know what’s coming.

On top of all this, more shops are taking on significant design and product development work; half cited this kind of work as the fastest-growing part of their business. This adds customer value, for sure, but it also makes an operation more complicated, especially in engineering.

These variables add up to what has been a perennial challenge in any manufacturing situation with highly variable demand (which really is most of manufacturing, period). Companies need to invest in enough capacity to handle demand spikes, but not so much as to be a burden during the slow times.

So they outsource. Yet outsourcing remains a big reason many jobs are late. So they bring a process in-house, which in turn adds even more complexity to scheduling, routing, and maintenance. A good number of shops cited unexpected machine downtime as a significant cause of delays.

Many have uncovered more capacity and efficiency through better welding, bending, and cutting technology. Even more have gained efficiency through better materials planning, engineering, and—most of all—scheduling. This isn’t surprising, considering that most reported that their greatest bottlenecks occur in the front office.

Interestingly, when they do need to work through demand spikes, many rely on overtime and temporary labor. Few reported having excess machine capacity, and even fewer rely on flexible automation.

Having stellar on-time delivery may boil down to developing and perfecting flexible capacity, both in the front office and on the shop floor. Those who uncover it, especially without increasing labor costs, truly stand out in a crowd.

The value of a stopwatch

Dan Aronson, President

P&A Metal Fab Inc., Clackamas, Ore.

Welding remains a common shop floor bottleneck, and it’s easy to see why considering the nature of the process. But as Dan Aronson has discovered, the reasons behind his shop’s welding bottleneck were a bit more nuanced.

Aronson is president of Clackamas, Ore.-based P&A Metal Fab, a shop that fabricates many repeat orders, and it uses its four welding robots whenever it can. But the company can’t use robots for everything. For instance, extremely large workpieces just aren’t practical to fixture in a robot cell.

The company needs more welders and, like so many shops, is having a tough time finding them. So for now, P&A is contracting out some of its manual welding to relieve the bottleneck.

The one benefit of robotic welding, and a big reason P&A has aggressively adopted the technology, is that the robot welds at a set cycle time, no more and no less. This means that if a customer increases demand for a certain subassembly, figuring how long robotic welding will take is just a matter of doing the math. That’s the theory, at least. Reality is a little messier.

About six years ago, one top customer in the fitness industry ramped up its order for welded frames, increasing demand on a robot welding cell from 45 units over two 10-hour shifts to 90 units over the same period. As Aronson recalled, "I looked at this and said, ‘Well, we need to buy another weld cell.’"

But then he got out his stopwatch. Welding one frame took 12 minutes and 45 seconds. "I did the math and said, ‘Well, we could be doing 90 frames a day at that rate. What’s the problem?’"

The robot had a turntable, so in theory the system should have had only a few seconds of arc-off time between parts. The thinking was that operators could finish loading parts into the fixture on one side of the turntable before the robot completed welding the current job—but it turned out that the operators didn’t finish loading the fixture in time, and the robot sat idle for a few minutes as people gathered parts for the next job. "There was just a lot of wasted motion and time getting parts into and out of the weld fixture."

P&A’s solution was straightforward enough: Organize, label, and stage parts so that operators no longer needed to spend time searching for parts. "We leaned out the cell, and we increased production to 50 a day, 60, and finally 90 frames a day," Aronson said.

The fact that efficiency increased so dramatically was one thing, but another fact really caught Aronson off guard. "I realized that if the customer had never increased its demand for welded frames, I probably would never have noticed it. It would have stayed the same until the end of the project."

Truth about capacity and productivity occurs on the floor, and if it’s not measured, it can’t be improved upon. Today, Aronson and others at P&A never hesitate to get out the stopwatch.

How good intentions can go awry

Chis Boycks, Vice President of Lean Initiatives

Jay Manufacturing Oshkosh Inc., Oshkosh, Wis.

An employee taking ownership over his or her work can be a good thing, but this good intention can backfire if people don’t consider the big picture. This became all too clear earlier this year at Jay Manufacturing Oshkosh Inc., a contract fabricator in Oshkosh, Wis.

Chris Boycks, vice president of lean initiatives, became keenly aware of this when he and an improvement team put together a process map. As with any high-product-mix manufacturer, Jay’s order-to-ship cycle time varies depending on the job, but in general the team found that part flow on the floor wasn’t the company’s biggest problem. The holdup was in the front office.

Many front-office improvements in this business involve streamlining communication, especially between engineers, estimators, salespeople, and customers. But Boycks and his team uncovered something else: order-entry people who worked with good intentions and yet, in doing so, held up production.

Besides gathering the information required to perform the job, order-entry personnel would gather setup sheets and other material for all the processes on the floor, tweak nests that were already programmed, and personally let purchasing know what to buy. In effect, order-entry took ownership over the job and shepherded the work through the front office. The aim was that once the job hit the floor, it would be smooth sailing. That was their thinking, anyway.

The reality was quite different. It turned out that jobs ended up sitting for weeks in order-entry, buried within cluttered inboxes. The result was a serious bottleneck.

"We were using 21 to 26 days to get the purchase orders processed and to the floor," Boycks said. "That didn’t leave much time for a product to be produced."

Today that process time has been slashed from three weeks to just two days. The fabricator did it by identifying the overprocessing problem, instituting procedures that emphasized flow, and fully utilizing the enterprise resource planning system. The company also instituted clear channels of communication between project estimating, engineering, order-entry, and purchasing. Estimators and engineers now sit in the same room, and both use the ERP system to view current and future capacity levels in the shop.

Boycks added that overcoming this constraint showed how bottlenecks can be rooted in the company culture and, specifically, in how people think about their jobs and how they’re valued. Longtime employees remembered inefficiencies that occurred years ago, so they took ownership to ensure these problems didn’t happen again. But software, machines, and procedures change over time, and what applied in the 1990s doesn’t necessarily apply today.

It goes back to communication and looking at the big picture. For instance, Boycks recalled how one person in order-entry would spend time tweaking already nested jobs, reorienting parts to achieve slightly better material utilization. In that person’s mind, this work saved the company money. After all, material is the No. 1 cost on the balance sheet, right? Of course, this only added to the order-entry bottleneck. Although this good intention saved a little material, it also spent much more of a fabricator’s most expensive resource: time.

Retirement, bottlenecks, and inevitable change

Greg Williams, President and COO

Southern Metalcraft Inc., Lithonia, Ga.

Earlier this year a press brake operator approached Greg Williams, president and COO of Southern Metalcraft Inc. (SMI), a 31-person job shop in Lithonia, Ga. It was time.

At the end of 2016, the bending veteran—someone who has worked for SMI for 25 years—will be retiring. He knew he needed to give his employer plenty of notice, allowing time for him to pass on his knowledge; a younger technician has been shadowing the guru for several years. "We’re trying to get as much out of his head as we can," Williams said.

But his departure at the end of next year is significant all the same. In fact, over the next five years, almost a half dozen key employees—including a quality manager, a lead salesperson, and a welding supervisor—will be wrapping up their careers. The plant manager will retire within the next seven years.

Low turnover has been one of SMI’s strengths. Average tenure at the shop is 20 years. People have worked together for so long, they have a feel for the business, and this includes part flow. They know each other’s work habits, and they know how long certain jobs will take. They help each other when needed, and know how to keep parts moving. But what will happen when they retire? Will new people be able to keep parts moving as before, or will constraints rear their ugly head?

This has been top of mind for managers lately. People are the most important puzzle piece of any shop strategy, but part flow (including bottlenecks) and the customer mix are critical too. Williams is looking at all three.

He knows that the shop has a bottleneck in the laser cutting area, mainly because of demand variability. One week the phone may ring off the hook; another week, all may be quiet. During busy times, the shop’s two lasers can’t produce fast enough, so he’s considering some level of automation beyond the dual-pallet load and unload shuttle tables in operation now. "This would gain me more hours without having to hire somebody," Williams said.

But, Williams asked, how will SMI fill that increased cutting capacity during the slow times? "This is why we’re asking, ‘If we add that automation, where do we want to go, to ensure we keep it busy?’"

This is where the second puzzle piece comes into play: expanding the customer base. He doesn’t plan to serve OEMs directly (though he certainly wouldn’t rule it out), but he wouldn’t mind taking on some of the overflow OEM work from larger fabricators. He also may seek out smaller-volume product-line work, helping people with product development and serving their fabrication needs as their companies grow.

That work would fill his laser capacity, but how about the press brakes? At present, the shop’s product mix is such that two press brakes can keep up with the work flow. But will that be enough if the shop takes on more jobs, especially after the shop’s bending guru retires? Some type of bending automation may be in SMI’s future.

Williams added that he has no plans to increase the headcount, and he doesn’t see SMI growing into a large contract fabricator anytime soon. But change is coming, and the puzzle pieces soon will be in motion. Considering the number of people on the payroll nearing retirement age, SMI will be a very different company in 10 years.
