# Good questions drive innovation forward

Biz Talk

Themes from this year’s NASCC: The Steel Conference

TIM HESTON

s tariff talk continued to dominate the news cycle, I was absorbing the future of structural metal fabrication, and that future might look quite different. Among all the uncertainty and American politics in utter turmoil, innovative people on the ground, those who

actually build

things, push forward unabated.

I was in Louisville, Ky., at NASCC: The Steel Conference, organized by the American Institute of Steel Construction, an event where, I must admit, I did not expect to see a picture of a quadruped robot.

"I work with these robots now," said Kereshmeh Afsari, a professor at Virginia Tech’s Myers-Lawson School of Construction. "These can walk autonomously across the jobsite to collect visual data and monitor the process of construction." She added that such robots could be used to, say, monitor inventory levels in a steel yard and, using advanced AI, enable continuous coordination between purchasing and business development, so that a shop always has the right amount of inventory for upcoming projects.

From a metal fabrication perspective, technology and topics discussed at the early April event fell into two broad, complementary categories:

communication

and

methods

. Communication technology will affect how fabricators operate and collaborate with others. Afsari’s quadruped robot, monitoring and sending data to those who need it, falls into this category.

So does the concept of

connected design

. Instead of a fabricator bidding on PDF drawings, a digital model serves as a single, detailed information source that can answer various questions weeks or months before any structural beam hits the fab shop floor.

"We’re used to carrying the full scope of work, from detailing to fabrication and erection. We’ve seen a huge pickup in this connected model delivery platform, especially over the past five years."

That was David Wright, director of preconstruction at Arizona-based Schuff Steel, one of the country’s largest structural metal fabricators. In recent years, the fabricator has relied more on 3D structural steel models that are far more detailed than typical design drawings used by an engineer of record (EOR). Steel connection design and detailing begins in the design stage, and the fabricator gets involved early in the process. The whole concept reduces time-consuming request for information (RFI) paperwork to the EOR and helps optimize the steel fabrication, connection techniques, and on-site erection.

Of course, all parties need to have buy-in on the approach, which operates by the building information modeling (BIM) framework. To use BIM jargon, the models reach a level of design (LOD) up to 400, meaning that they include a plethora of structural steel information and go far beyond the traditional "stick" drawings that leave a lot of open questions.

A pedestrian bridge’s connection "branch" at AISC’s booth shows the kind of innovative connections made possible by additive manufacturing.

And as Darren Hartman, managing principal at Thornton Tomasetti Construction Engineering, explained during a presentation, the model itself doesn’t answer every question, like the project scope or which documents control others. Parties still need to communicate early and often. But the benefits of a well-executed connected model project are hard to ignore.

"We had a project involving 3,500 tons of material," Hartman said, "and we had only four steel RFIs. It’s almost unimaginable that could actually happen, but it did."

After realizing such dramatic efficiencies, all project stakeholders, including owners, might be more likely to buy in and even embrace the concept. But even if they don’t, structural fabricators still have options.

"Across the structural steel industry, we hear a lot about labor shortages and scheduling constraints, all while the documentation associated with jobs [are declining] in quality. On top of this, we’re dealing with pricing fluctuations. All this, if you think about it, is really quite aligned with AI technology, if you think about what AI is really trying to do."

That was Grayson Ingram of Edmonton, Alberta-based

SketchDeck.ai

, an AI-based platform that scans PDF-based bid material (including drawings) for key inputs, including structural steel members, and even helps perform a connection analysis to ensure they integrate well into shop floor processes. The company is also working on automatically converting 2D drawings into 3D models. All this accelerates the takeoff process at the structural fabricator.

"Given AI’s ability to handle large amounts of unstructured data, it can accelerate the process of going through extensive, and often ambiguous, documentation [associated with a construction job]. … It also allows fabricators to focus on more strategic tasks.

"There’s a perception that construction and the structural steel space are slow to adopt new technology," Ingram continued. "My experience tells me this is not true. If you identify a good fit between a problem and a technology to solve it, people in the structural steel space are very receptive to adopting it."

Connected model design and AI-enhanced workflow improve communication among the myriad players in construction. That in turn frees people to focus on strategic tasks that add value—like applying new fabrication methods, even 3D printing. Put another way, innovative

communication

can help push innovative

methods

forward.

A prime example of this was perched near the back of the exhibit hall: a small pedestrian bridge that made innovative use of gas metal arc additive manufacturing. Its design was like no other, with printed connections resembling tree branches and a 3D-printed overhead arch, its radius far too small for a conventional angle roll to create. The bridge’s printed connections looked organic, and not just for artistic reasons. Organic design often makes extraordinarily efficient use of available material.

The research project—supported by AISC and carried out by Lincoln Electric Additive Solutions and Cast Connex—spurred some serious curiosity at the show.

As the pundits on TV pontificated about on-again-off-again tariffs, the structural steel industry—architects, engineers, fabricators, erectors—met to learn and, perhaps most important, ask questions.

Sure, 3D printing entire structures probably won’t happen, but what about some innovative connection designs that add so much strength that they reduce the amount of material a building might require, and perhaps even simplify construction?

In the long run, such curiosity pushes innovation forward.
