# Tube fabricator moves big into branded parts, power gen market

[TARİH: 01.05.2026 The Fabricator]

Tube Time Lincoln Brunner

Morton Industries’ Ober aims to even out workforce among facilities

By

Lincoln Brunner

I

f there’s one thing that Morton Industries President/CEO Chris Ober knows how to do, it’s roll with the punches.

After 80,000 sq. ft. of the tube fabrication giant’s North Facility in Morton, Ill., went up in flames in April 2023, instead of taking a breather, Ober and his team immediately focused not just on rebuilding what was lost but continuing the expansion efforts already underway before the fire. At the time, that meant adding tube bending, cutting, and blast room capacity.

Since then, the company also has dived headlong into supplying tubing for diesel engines used for power generation and several other initiatives that share the twin aims of diversifying the company’s end-use markets and capitalizing on the boom in AI and the data centers it depends on.

That is one of the key reasons Morton recently purchased Superior Tube Products in Davenport, Iowa, about 90 minutes away from the company’s main facilities in Morton, Ill. The acquisition gave Morton the ability to bend 7-in.-OD tube, primarily for air movement in those large diesel motors, as well as equipment that bends large steel shapes that provide rollover protection for industrial equipment.

DIVERSIFYING TO STAY RELEVANT

And it’s not just tubes that Morton (No. 15 on the 2025 FAB 40 list) wants to supply to the data center market, although it does plenty of those for rooftop units, tubes inside server rooms, and tubing manifolds.

Two years ago, the company bought a contract manufacturer near Fort Worth, Texas, that was making parts for Morton and created Morton Enclosures to manufacture the metal boxes that hold many of the electrical components in those data centers.

Right about the time it established Morton Enclosures, the company also acquired Road Armor, also based in Texas, which manufactures heavy-duty aftermarket bumpers and grill guards for trucks—naturally, mostly from tubular steel components.

The branded products represent a stark departure for an 80-year-old company that’s always majored in contract manufacturing, mostly of tubular components for the mining, agricultural, and construction industries.

"We’ve just always done contract manufacturing, but we wanted to diversify, so Morton Enclosures was one way," Ober said. "People say, ‘Chris, you guys are in construction, ag, mining—you should do things that are countercyclical to that,’ and I just get a chuckle because I say, ‘OK, if nobody’s building anything and people aren’t eating, what are they spending their money on?’

"There’s nothing countercyclical, but there are things on different business cycles, so that’s why we decided to invest so heavily in equipment and sales efforts in the data center and power infrastructure portions of the market. Those are on a different track than they ever have been historically."

PROXIMITY MATTERS

In the three years since the fire, Morton has added businesses—not only for new customer lists and revenue streams, but also to place company facilities physically closer to its customers.

"Tubes are hard to ship, and so proximity matters to our customers," Ober said. "It’s what we love about it—because they’re hard to ship, they’re also hard to internationally source. There’s just not the expertise abroad, and even if there is, they don’t pack well in a container. So, proximity to our customers is important, even in the U.S. We have found a little higher success rate to acquire our way into a geography than to just try to show up."

MULTIPLE BASES OF PRODUCTION

One shining example is Road Armor—a business made for Texas if ever there was one. While the Lone Star State doesn’t even crack the top 10 in terms of per-capita truck ownership, it leads the nation in total pickup trucks sold annually, according to multiple sources. In fact, Road Armor has been such a hot business for Morton, it has had to rethink how it can fill orders without succumbing to the ramp-up-layoff cycle so common in factory work. Specifically, that has meant adding machinery at various facilities among the six that it operates in Illinois, Texas, and Tennessee to share the work more equitably among the 750 employees it already has.

"We wanted to do that [acquire Road Armor] not only to diversify, but it also gives us the ability to manufacture these products at all of our different facilities," Ober said. "We can move production around to help level the workload amongst the different factories, being in a very cyclical market. It takes a long time to diversify, but if we are making our own products, we can literally design and manufacture products that are a good fit for each of our different facilities and then move it around as the markets cycle on us so we can keep our labor pools even and growing as opposed to letting the labor pools flex with the markets."
