# MillerMetal Fabrication

[TARİH: 01.12.2018 The Fabricator]

MillerMetal Fabrication’s quotes are comprehensive, come complete with any special bending tools needed, and have detailed bill of materials (BOM) and routing information—all based on a costing and project management software that the fabricator developed in-house. The software fills in the BOM in the ERP automatically. When a customer pulls the trigger for an order, much of the engineering work has already been completed.

One recent order, for instance, consisted of 150 DXF files, each with its own routing information. When the order was accepted, the project manager opened the software, hit "accept," and the order was ready to be released to the shop floor almost immediately.

Until recently the company’s front office was separated by department: estimating would send a job to purchasing, then to engineering, and so on in order to build an accurate quote—a process that took up to five working days. Now the front-office team is cross-trained in estimating, engineering, programming, and nesting. Still, purchasing is centralized for better efficiency; why have three delivery trucks show up one day with three small orders of the same material?

Regardless, the shop’s front-office transformation, which began around the time of the Great Recession, is built on the concept of project management, in which one person shepherds each job through the entire work flow. He or she may hand off, say, a more complicated design project to someone on the team who really knows SolidWorks; regardless, the project manager owns the customer’s order, from the time it’s sold to the time it’s released. The goal is to move work through as quickly as possible and avoid time-wasting hand-offs. As Miller has found, just as jobs can spend a lot of time as work-in-process on the floor, they can also spend a lot of time in an email inbox.

Time between order receipt and the start of nesting is five minutes, if the drawing checks out; one or two days if re-engineering is required.

40 percent of what engineering receives are 3-D CAD models; 40 percent are DXFs; 20 percent are hand sketches or PDFs.

25 percent scrap rate or less, on average. Remnants, which are on about 10 percent of the sheets processed, are tracked manually.

Today, Miller returns most quotes in a day. Its win-to-bid ratio has gone through the roof as well. When Miller quotes jobs in its "sweet spot," where they know they can bid competitively, the shop wins as much as 70 percent of the work.

Viewed "within the cubicle," an individual today actually processes fewer items, considering that person is carrying the quote through the entire process. But altogether, the front office answers far more requests for quotes than it did before, which in turn has increased flow to laser cutting—which, thanks to recent investments in solid-state lasers, has the capacity to handle more work.

Interestingly, the purchasing manager also performs the nesting. For years the programmer and purchasing manager worked side by side, sharing notes, communicating constantly. So, managers thought, why not cross-train? This way, the purchasing manager receives the order releases and BOMs, orders the materials, and nests those same materials. For a shop that keeps minimal raw stock inventory and orders material just in time for the job, the arrangement works well.

Hot jobs are slipped in where possible, usually walked to the shop floor and added manually to a nest, often to a remnant. The company has one rotary-axis flatbed laser that cuts tube the majority of the time, two CO

2

lasers, and one new solidstate laser system. The fast solid-state laser mainly handles the higher-volume jobs.

After laser operators sort and stack parts, the jobs are then retrieved not by a dedicated material handler, but by designated operators of downstream equipment, be it the press brake, deburring equipment, or otherwise. The reasoning is that operators know best how they want their parts presented. And presentation matters. The better those parts are positioned, the faster the bending operators can process them.

Laser cutting order snapshot: During one day in September, 170 orders were in process.

Assist gas source: bulk nitrogen and oxygen cylinders. All thin gauges are processed with nitrogen; half of thicker gauges processed with nitrogen, other half with oxygen. No cutting with shop air. 90 percent of the time, the lasers cut with nitrogen.

Hot jobs are walked out to the machine with a special routing packet. One machine is dedicated to most hot jobs.

10 percent of parts are smaller than 12 in. long or wide; 80 percent of parts are 12-48 in.; 10 percent are larger than 48 in.

Carbon steel is 50 percent of work; stainless steel is 30 percent; aluminum is 20 percent.

Cutting sequence snapshot: 27 sheets of ⅜-in. thick carbon steel; 9 sheets of ⅜-in. stainless steel.

The solid-state laser runs three shifts, each shift with one operator; one operator runs the CO

Operators use suction lifting devices to load material onto shuttle tables.

Operators de-nest using magnets, air hammers, and pry bars; sort parts onto pallets; and place scrap in bins. This occurs immediately after cutting.

Parts are sorted on wood pallets, are transported on the pallets with a fork truck, or by using steel frame rolling carts.

Every Saturday, employees spend four hours cleaning and maintaining machines. Slats are cleaned once a week for an hour. Every two months, technicians spend four hours replacing slats.
