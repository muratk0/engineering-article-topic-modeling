# Running successful youth apprenticeships

[TARİH: 01.08.2024 The Fabricator]

Youth Apprenticeships

Early investment in employees can pay off for companies willing to take the risk

By

Lincoln Brunner

Jesse Even operates at a Haas milling center at Addison Machine Engineering’s facility in Reedsburg, Wis.

D

aryn Bormett didn’t grow up yearning to work for a tube mill manufacturer like Addison Machine Engineering. Today, the former youth apprentice doesn’t want to be anywhere else.

When Bormett kicked around a few career ideas at age 16, working in a machine shop in his hometown of Reedsburg, Wis., wasn’t one of them. But he let his dad, who had worked with Addison through his job at a local foundry, convince him to go see the shop for himself one day. After that initial visit, Addison asked him to be one of its first youth apprentices. "I was a young kid that didn’t know anything," said Bormett, now 27 and an assistant shop supervisor at Addison. "I knew a little bit about micrometers when I started. Now I feel comfortable on a lot of the different things that I do."

That quick entry into the business set a pattern for Bormett’s relationship with Addison—get in, learn fast, and take on as much as you can handle.

It seems to be working. A recent addition to Bormett’s list of "things that I do" is working with another employee to install and recommission a used Mazak CNC lathe at Addison—a sizable investment for the fourth-generation, family-owned business with 42 employees. It’s also several steps up from where Bormett started in the pre-inspection department at Addison, where he broke into the business taking measurements and looking for major problems on used tube mill tooling that needed regrinding—one of Addison’s bread-and-butter services.

"Originally, I didn’t really want to be a machinist," Bormett said. "So, when I got here, I wasn’t really that open to it. But once I started working and seeing what we did here, I thought it was kind of cool how a set of tooling would come in and it would not look good at all. And then, all of a sudden, after it would go through the shop, it would come back, and it looked super clean and nice. So, I thought that was really cool, and that’s kind of what got me to stay."

After high school, Bormett enrolled in a nearby community college. When he graduated, he came back to Addison. Six months later, company President Jay Brunken and Vice President Patrick Brunken opened up a second shift and rewarded Bormett’s loyalty (and skills) by making him shift supervisor.

He was 19. However, the Brunkens’ wisdom proved sound: Bormett remained Addison’s night supervisor for six years.

"It was definitely not easy," Bormett said. "I mean, some of the older people didn’t so much like a 19-year-old being their boss, you could say. It was hard for me, but I really leaned on Jay Brunken to help me through it. He would come through some nights and kind of be my backbone. Each person is different to work with, and once they gain your trust and you gain theirs, you can really work together well.

"It was difficult, especially starting out," he said. "I was so underqualified for the position at that age. I’ve talked to Jay about that. I think we had nine guys on nights, so it wasn’t that full, but it’s still a lot to ask for a 19-year-old, and I was definitely not ready for it when I started doing it."

Patrick Brunken said taking a chance on young talent (like Bormett) has paid off nicely for Addison, which operates in a rural town of 10,000 people and isn’t exactly fighting off hordes of job seekers. Over the past decade, the company has hired eight full-time employees through the apprenticeship program and currently has four interns on staff—two in the shop and two more in its engineering department.

"It’s been working extremely well," Patrick Brunken said. "We get first crack at good potential employees. They get a benefit by securing a full-time job before they graduate high school."

Preparing for Now

Across the state in Manitowoc, Wis., Brunner Fabrication had much the same idea when it started its apprenticeship program last year with another 16-year-old student, Teddy Dhein. The growing business focuses mainly on production-scale part runs for customers like Ariens Co., specifically for its lawnmowers.

Co-founder/COO Matt Brunner (no relation to the author) put Dhein to work two and a half days a week doing virtually every task on the shop floor. Dhein, who recently took another job but parted with the company on the best of terms, proved himself early on with a project that Brunner gave him to test his problem-solving skills.

Brunner, like the Brunkens, paired his apprentice with more seasoned employees and had them show their younger co-worker the ropes. That handson approach has worked well for both shops.

Case in point: The shop needed a "no semi traffic" sign, so Brunner gave Dhein some metal, a welding machine, some paint, and a chop saw and told him to have at it. Dhein didn’t have much experience to go on, but given his mechanical aptitude, Brunner knew he had no reason to worry. However, also learning Dhein’s personality and tendencies before he launched into projects like the sign was important, Brunner noted.

"It took him a while to figure it out, but eventually he did a really nice job," Brunner said of the sign project. "The first thing we needed to do was figure out his strengths and weaknesses. If you gave him a task with no direction, it wasn’t the easiest in the beginning to get him to where he needed to be. So, we started laying out a timeline on tasks and how they should be done. Once he got rolling, he did an awesome job."

Jake Even operates the control panel on a Mazak turning center at Addison.

"I think the youth apprenticeship has been all positives," he added. "It’s a good feeling watching the fire grow in a young person. Our youth apprentice’s willingness to learn and work hard at a task was a very big help in the program’s success."

Brunner, 35, is a big advocate for the fabrication industry. He said that robotics and technology could be a huge draw for students—if only high schools would push manufacturing careers and industrial trades like they once did.

"These kids are the first generation that grew up with everyone having cell phones, video games, and computers," Brunner said. "They already understand a lot of the technology. I think if we push them more, today’s generation will take to manufacturing more than past ones. We just need to show them how cool and rewarding today’s manufacturing technology is."

The Growing Need

When it comes to young people getting on board, manufacturing seems only to lose ground. On a national scale, the numbers daunt.

According to a recent report from The Manufacturing Institute and Deloitte entitled, "Taking charge: Manufacturers support growth with active workforce strategies," the U.S. manufacturing industry will need 3.8 million new employees between 2024 and 2033. Meanwhile, as the need for workers rises, the education and certificate programs responsible for preparing those workers are not keeping pace (and probably can’t), according to graduation data from the National Center for Education Statistics.

"While the number of bachelor’s degrees awarded in all fields of study from 2011 to 2022 has increased, the number of associate degrees—which tend to prepare graduates for high-skill trades—has remained stagnant [at about 1.1 million per year]," the study said. "The number of certificates has experienced a moderate increase over the same period … even surpassing the number of associate degrees awarded.

"The key question becomes: Given the talent challenges, how can manufacturers build the workforce needed to seize the growth opportunity at hand? Our study found that there is a shift underway in the sector and, in general, companies are currently taking a more active approach to addressing both the skills gap and the applicant gap."

While the report focused on partnerships between industry and education, it also noted a sharp rise in apprenticeships nationwide.

"The number of apprentices in advanced manufacturing occupations increased to 59,500 in fiscal 2023, which is nearly triple the total in fiscal year 2021," the report said. "Nearly half (47%) of survey respondents in the ‘2022 Deloitte and The Manufacturing Institute Perceptions Study’ indicated that apprenticeships, work studies, or internships at manufacturing companies would be the most effective way to increase interest in manufacturing as a career choice."

Brunner seems to agree.

"I would recommend a youth apprenticeship program to anyone," Brunner said. "Not only are you helping a young kid see a rewarding career in manufacturing, but you are also helping yourself and your company learn how to teach the younger generation.

"Get involved with your local high school tech programs," Brunner added.

"Donate scrap material and old measuring equipment. Have them come in for tours."

Addison’s Bormett said youth apprenticeships are "a very good idea," whether it’s a young person looking for a possible career or a business looking to fill a current position.

With more than a decade of experience now, Bormett said he’s "learned a ton," from how to spot flaws in roll tooling to purchasing a new machine to leveling that machine properly when it arrives at the shop. And it started with an apprenticeship he wasn’t even sure he wanted.

"Whether it’s machining … or being able to program on a computer instead of at the machine to make other people’s jobs easier, yeah, it’s a huge difference from where I started to where I am now," Bormett said. "I would love it if I could work here for another 30 years and retire here. That would be awesome."
