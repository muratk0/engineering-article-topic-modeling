Tech Talk

Accessibility and speed are the new table stakes

CALEB CHAMBERLAIN

Y

oung people are primed to expect a different experience from manufacturers. This is the age of Amazon. Speed, accessibility, and simplicity are the new normal. This is obviously true for consumer products, but young people are bringing their expectations into businesses as well.

I say "young people," but the truth is, most people have learned to expect speed and simplicity. I’m in my 40s and didn’t grow up with the internet, but I’m accustomed to the transparency and easy access offered by internet companies like Amazon.

The other day, I reached out to an industrial vendor to inquire about pricing and lead time. We are an established, mature company with good cash flow. We are ready to buy, cash in hand. We never received a response. Why is that? Another vendor of ours took a month just to give us a price and told it would take two months for delivery. We placed an order and only then received word that it would be delayed yet another month. The glacial response baffles me, especially considering I can go online, buy a custom $1,000 widget, and have a more transparent, streamlined, better-curated experience. Many industrial companies still lumber along at a pace best matched to paper mail.

The difference does make sense, though. Online retailers leveraged tech to make buying dead simple and then used the ubiquity of the internet to aggregate massive volume. At scale, they had no choice but to streamline processes around buying, shipping, communication, and customer support. They couldn’t have functioned otherwise. Those in the industrial space haven’t faced the same kind of pressure—at least not yet.

Change Is Coming

The beauty of free markets is that when incumbents provide a bad experience, new companies can challenge them. If I could price and order products from vendors online with an accurate and reasonable lead time, I’d do that 100% of the time. Industrial markets are more specialized and more niche than online retail, but better experiences will still allow modern companies to aggregate volume, build scale, and outperform competitors. That doesn’t just improve existing markets. It creates new ones. Better accessibility means more people will buy. Tech can democratize access to industrial products and manufacturing services.

There are quite a few great examples of this. McMaster-Carr is a wellknown, beloved vendor not because it is the cheapest, but because it offers a massive catalog of industrial products, accessible online, with most products available within one day. Its customer support is also legendary. The other day we ordered some critical parts, scheduled for next-day delivery. FedEx dropped the ball, so McMaster-Carr offered to ship replacement parts via same-day courier at its own expense!

Another standout example is Teknic, a New York-based servomotor manufacturer and engineering company. Teknic offers a large catalog of standard servos and controllers. Many industrial companies hide prices, and even product offerings, through an opaque, lengthy sales process. But Teknic built a web app that allows customers to view all options, filtered and sorted by motor properties like form factor, input voltage, torque, speed, and control method.

Prices are visible instantly online, and orders ship within three business days. I recently used Teknic servos to build a motion platform. Days before a tight deadline, I managed to drop a $1,500 motor and ruin it. Teknic had a replacement motor on-site within one day. Amazing experiences like this are becoming increasingly common.

The wheelchair manufacturing industry also is beginning its own transformation. Today, buying a wheelchair is an expensive, months-long bureaucratic process involving 10-plus layers of brokers, insurance companies, and other service providers. Worse, wheelchairs wear out long before insurance companies will pitch in to replace them. Like most of our healthcare system, the wheelchair market is convoluted, expensive, and inefficient.

Zack Nelson, the face behind the JerryRigEverything YouTube channel, noticed the problem and started a company to fix it. Ironically named Not A Wheelchair, Zack’s company offers an online web portal where customers can configure a custom wheelchair, view a rendered 3D model, order online, and have it delivered in two weeks for just $5,000. Zack is making accessibility products … well, more accessible.

Tuff Shed is another great example of this tech-enabled strategy. It built a custom web app that allows customers to build their own custom sheds online. You can select from 15 different styles and configure options like base size, height, door type and locations, windows, interior shelves, vents, roof pitch, and even color. The customized shed is rendered in your web browser, and prices are dynamic and adjust as options change. Finally, when customers place an order, a service rep calls to confirm details, ensure that the property is prepared, and schedule delivery. Tuff Shed makes each building to order and engages a network of professional installers to build the finished sheds from custom-manufactured components. My wife and I designed and ordered one recently. It was built and installed in less than three weeks. Amazing.

My own company also leverages tech to improve speed and accessibility. When my brother and I struggled to find sheet metal vendors that could make parts fast, we started OSH Cut to provide instant pricing and rapid turnaround. Our system accepts 3D models, runs automatic manufacturability checks, and provides instant pricing and lead times. Orders can be submitted online or via purchase order, and our systems automate scheduling, purchasing, and machine programming.

Automating these systems has allowed us to capture a "long tail" of high-mix, low-volume work that is unattractive to most legacy shops. We aren’t the only shop to integrate software in this way. Together with our competitors, we’ve made sheet metal among the most accessible manufacturing technologies for businesses and consumers alike.

A New World of Software Development

AI is accelerating these trends. While there is a lot of debate over the practical impact of AI, one of the most compelling use cases is already widely used: accelerating software development. Coding platforms like V0 and Replit make it extremely easy to build and deploy new applications, often without writing a single line of code. It’s hard to overstate how magical that is. You can have a conversation with a computer, ask it to write a custom app for you, and be using that app online in minutes to hours. It’s incredible.

I’ve watched new manufacturing businesses form around that capability: one recently built an app to provide instant quoting for custom manufactured cable assemblies. Customers can choose the connectors on each end of the cable, drag-drop to make connections on each end, select wire type and length, configure the cable jacket and strain relief, get instant prices in any quantity, and then place an order. What an incredible experience! And that capability was enabled after just a weekend of conversational coding with an AI agent. Of course, fulfillment is the biggest challenge, as manufacturers already know. But it’s becoming extremely easy to build great customer experiences online.

There are many, many more examples. Great companies are finding bad experiences and fixing them with modern technology. Every time this happens, markets will get more efficient, customers will have better experiences, and legacy companies will feel a little more pressure to modernize.

Massive Opportunity

Many markets, including most of manufacturing, still have a long way to go. In FMA’s 2023 "Financial Ratios and Operational Benchmarking Survey," nearly 50% of responding job shops reported an average quoting time of more than two days,

23 and nearly half reported a bid-win rate of less than 50%. To be sure, that survey incorporates a wide variety of operations, from piece-part job shops to fabricators that sell complex assemblies that understandably take longer to bid.

Still, if you just need a straightforward part that has to be laser-cut and bent, two days is a long time to wait for a quote. In our increasingly agile ecosystem, there might no longer be a job to win after two days. Customers value speed.

Soon, all companies that keep offering subpar customer experiences will start facing serious challenges, even in the manufacturing arena. Someone is going to come along, fix these horrible workflows, and soak up a bunch of demand from frustrated customers like me. It’s both a massive opportunity and a massive risk. Manufacturers today can’t afford to ignore our changing environment. Those who respond will thrive, and those who fail to modernize will be left behind.
