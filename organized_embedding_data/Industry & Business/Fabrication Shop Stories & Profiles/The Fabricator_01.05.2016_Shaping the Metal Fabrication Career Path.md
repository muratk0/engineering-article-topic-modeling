# Shaping the Metal Fabrication Career Path

[TARİH: 01.05.2016 The Fabricator]

Contents, Executive Summary

T

he alarm bells have sounded about the skilled labor crisis for years, and nothing seems to change. So what’s a shop manager to do?

These survey results give no easy answers, but they do provide insights. Many said that they have the greatest challenge finding welders, quality assurance personnel, and press brake operators—no surprises there, considering the hands-on skill and knowledge these jobs require. It’s also not surprising that fabricators have difficulty finding people in sales and estimating—roles that require business savvy along with technical knowledge.

Some said that their typical entry-level employee on the shop floor has a high school diploma and no vocational education. Meanwhile, most in the front office come to the job with at least a two- or four-year degree, but many positions in metal fabrication do not require college experience. Many entry-level front-office employees have no education beyond a high school diploma.

For policymakers, this may be worth noting. Sure, if you look across all industries, a four-year college degree pays for itself and then some. But in metal fabrication, an applicant need not spend a lot of money on a postsecondary degree to have a successful career. Good attitude and potential go a long way.

How successful will their careers be? Herein lies the rub. Some new hires find success, others don’t, and there doesn’t seem to be a direct correlation with experience. Some novices come to the job with no preconceived notions, receive training, and find success—but not an overwhelming majority.

One reliable path to success in this business may be through technical certificates and certifications. Almost 80 percent said people with such credentials find success at their companies, becoming valuable experts and sometimes rising to the supervisory or managerial ranks.

Is training the issue? Here again, people gave a mix of answers. Most notably, 61 percent said that new hires undergo no formal training but do shadow experienced workers. And most companies did report they were within 30 miles of a technical school or similar manufacturing training facility.

The biggest challenge may relate to front-line personnel. The vast majority said that’s where they experience the greatest turnover. Employees who do stay for more than 10 years usually become a well-paid technical expert or are promoted through the ranks. Few long-term employees remain in their entry-level position.

Mirroring most of metal fabrication, most said they work for small companies, and many said that their company’s small size makes promotion opportunities limited. At a small shop, people have only so many career ladders to climb.

But is this really a problem? Not necessarily. In fact, only 8 percent said employees left for promotional opportunities within the past five years. Almost a third of those surveyed said that employees left for more money or better benefits—no surprise there, especially in labor markets where both large and small manufacturers compete for workers.

However, just as many said employees left because they just weren’t a good fit. So what’s a shop to do? How can a fabricator ensure a new employee will indeed be a good fit? There is no definitive answer, of course, but focusing on a person’s career path may at the very least set a fabricator apart from the competition. Only 32 percent said they give career-path guidance for employees.

An assembler may be inserting hardware today, but what will that person be doing 10 years from now? Asking that question doesn’t solve the problem, but it may be a first step in uncovering new opportunities for good people.

Traditional fabrication versus the modern shop

Michael McCarthy, President

Metko Inc., New Holstein, Wis.

Michael McCarthy misses family dairy farms. He’s the president of Metko Inc. in the heart of dairy country, in the aptly named town of New Holstein, Wis. Years ago most of the fabricator’s employees grew up on those farms.

Working on a small farm is a little like working at a small job shop. People looked at the challenge in front of them, rolled up their sleeves, and, with a heavy dose of common sense, just got the job done.

Today most of the small dairy farms are gone, replaced by corporate agriculture. "Now most young people don’t have this hands-on experience," McCarthy said, "and we’re putting them in front of million-dollar machines."

McCarthy has worked in custom metal fabrication for decades— but the reality may be that he’s actually worked in two kinds of industries, "traditional" and "modern." In traditional fabrication, people grew up in an environment that fostered the just-get-it-done work ethic. They didn’t have the luxury of modern machine controls or software, yet the jobs weren’t as complex or precise either.

Fast forward to the modern shop, where jobs are intricate and lead times are short. To maintain high customer expectations while also increasing throughput, Metko continues to put an emphasis on new technology. It installed a new welding robotic system—programmed and simulated offline—as well as a new high-end press brake. It also purchased a new enterprise resource planning system that, among other things, helps the company track its work-in-process.

Because jobs move so quickly, worker engagement is more important than ever. One small mishap can lead to hours of costly downtime. Consider a recent mistake in which a worker failed to close a clamp in a robot welding cell. He called up a program, and the robot crashed into the open clamp on the fixture table. The entire cell was down for three shifts.

McCarthy added that, yes, the company could error-proof the system with hydraulic or pneumatic clamps. But completely error-proofing an operation in a high-product-mix environment is easier said than done, and it can get expensive. "High-volume plants can spend the money to error-proof to the nth degree," he said. "We can’t do that. The best thing we can do is focus on employee performance. In fact, the best counterpart to our advanced technology is talented, dedicated people."

The shop has worked with local technical schools and bolstered in-house training. But it’s also instituted something that’s arguably just as important: The Metko Mentorship Program. Each less experienced employee is paired with a veteran mentor.

It’s different from the traditional apprenticeship, which focuses on learning a manufacturing technology. The mentorship instead focuses on continuous improvement of the entire manufacturing process. The program fosters candid and collaborative relationships to share best-practices and different perspectives. It’s not just about knowing your job; it’s about being engaged and curious.

Because Metko now has such productive technology, mentally disengaging at the wrong time, like forgetting to clamp a workpiece, can have big consequences. This is why, perhaps, McCarthy feels that the new mentorship program has become such a necessity—especially for the fast-moving modern shop.

Why defining a career path matters

Nora Harding, Director of Leadership Development

Special Products & Mfg., Inc., Rockwall, Texas

"It isn’t about money. That we can fix."

So said Nora Harding, director of leadership development at Special Products & Mfg., Inc. (SPM). She was talking about the custom fabricator’s challenge in attracting and retaining top talent. "In fact, attracting talent actually isn’t difficult," she said, "but retaining them is another story."

Being in Rockwall, Texas, northeast of Dallas, you wouldn’t expect retention to be a problem, considering the downturn in the energy sector. But Dallas is not Houston or West Texas. In Dallas, opportunities abound for people with the right skills and experience.

At SPM, most people weren’t leaving for more money; they felt they lacked opportunity. The 200-employee custom fabricator has various departments, including one dedicated to continuous improvement. In other words, it has plenty of opportunities for career growth. Still, too many were calling it quits.

"It’s not that they didn’t have a career path here," Harding said. "It just wasn’t organized."

This, she conceded, was a mistake. "Millennials are driving this. So we said, ‘OK, we need to institute systemized talent development.’ We’re trying to create a learning organization and standardize the development of all associates."

This goes beyond the fabricator’s previous practice of providing career development for only certain positions; now managers discuss careers with everyone. "Every employee not only gets a performance appraisal but also gets a training plan," Harding said.

Training and coaching takes place in a program called SPM University. The shop hired a mechanical engineer whose sole job is to train and coach. All this helps managers determine what employees want to get out of their job and what they want that job to look like down the road.

This goes beyond the basics of print reading and other shop fundamentals, and involves cross training as well as classes taken outside the company. The training plan balances an employee’s career goals with his aptitude (gleaned through testing) and company needs.

Harding described one employee in the assembly department who wanted to be a supervisor. Managers had assessed his strengths and knew a supervisor role wasn’t a good fit. But they also knew that he grew up wanting to be a firefighter. Today he works on safety within the fabricator’s continuous improvement team.

Harding added that such employee investment builds loyalty, especially important for millennials who grew up with globalization, layoffs, and a seemingly pervasive lack of trust between employer and employee. Young people aren’t just looking for a job, she said. "They now come in the door and ask, ‘What is my career path going to be here?’"

At SPM, the employee, his coach, and managers all work together to answer that question.

Eye on the goal

Hernán Luis y Prado, Founder and President

Workshops for Warriors, San Diego

"I saw many of my fellow service members depressed, suicidal, with no path to a successful civilian life. I needed to change that."

So said Hernán Luis y Prado, a Navy officer with 15 years of combat experience in Iraq and Afghanistan. From that sentiment sprang an organization that would affect hundreds of lives, with the potential to change thousands more.

In one sense a military base is a microcosm of productive society. Planning, infrastructure, logistics—it’s all there, concentrated in just a few square miles. Instead of spending his limited free time on base playing pool, Luis y Prado took walks and looked at all the different areas that made the military base tick, especially the machine shop.

"I’d talk to the pony-tailed white-haired gentlemen running the machines. I’d ask them to teach me how to weld, how to machine, and how to repair machinery." Soon he was buying old pieces of equipment, refurbishing them in his garage, and selling them.

Later he walked through a naval hospital in Washington, D.C., where he witnessed many of his friends injured in the line of duty, sitting idle. He invited them over to his garage, just one or two at first, then five, then 15, then even more.

As Luis y Prado recalled, "My wife, who’s the smartest person I know, said to me, ‘You know, this shouldn’t just be a VFW with power tools. You could train them.’"

From this came San Diego-based Workshops for Warriors, a school that has trained 238 veterans and wounded warriors who have qualified for more than 700 credentials and obtained jobs in advanced manufacturing immediately after completing their programs.

Luis y Prado added that the nature of military training, with its intense focus on clearly defined goals, often serves graduates extraordinarily well. He recalled one story in which an alumnus working as a machinist waited outside the door of his busy production supervisor’s office. He looked at his watch, then left before the supervisor could meet with him. The machinist came back two days later to report that the job he had been given was complete.

The supervisor asked, "Why were you standing outside my door several days ago?"

The machinist responded, "When you assigned the job, you told me it was due today. I was going to tell you that we didn’t have the tool we needed. I went online, made sure it was certified for the job, and bought it myself. I gave it to the purchasing people so that they could put it into inventory, and I finished the part. The tool was just $15."

His supervisor was furious at first, but then something occurred to him. Procedures are important. But his machinist knew that, in this case, following those procedures could have prevented him from accomplishing the goal: to get a quality part out on time—which, by thinking on his feet and developing a new plan, this veteran did with flying colors.
