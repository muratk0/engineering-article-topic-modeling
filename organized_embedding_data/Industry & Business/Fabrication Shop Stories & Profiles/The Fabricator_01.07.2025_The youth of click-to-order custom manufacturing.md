# The youth of click-to-order custom manufacturing

[TARİH: 01.07.2025 The Fabricator]

Tech Talk

The digital-first model, still new, is growing rapidly

CALEB CHAMBERLAIN

diego_cervo/iStock/Getty Images Plus

I

t’s shocking to reflect on how much the world has changed over the last few decades. I’m in my early 40s, demographically young in the manufacturing space. Even so, I vividly recall when personal computers were still a novelty. My first computer was an old DOS-based "IBM Compatible" 286 with 5¼-in. floppy disks and a green monochrome display. I learned to program on it.

In high school, my friends and I lugged our big CRT monitors and computers into a friend’s garage to have "networking parties," where we’d play multiplayer games on a local network. That practice dissolved when the internet became mainstream. And speaking of the internet, I still recall using mail-order catalogs. The internet sure unraveled that kind of buying.

I bought my first cell phone, a flip phone, as an undergraduate. As a kid, we planned trips using paper maps and highlighters. That changed when MapQuest let you plan trips online and then print maps for later use. But I recall being absolutely floored when I first saw a Blackberry phone that could display maps in real time.

It’s funny how all these changes creeped up on us and utterly transformed the way we live, work, spend our free time, interact with others, buy products, and engineer designs. Life in 2025 is so utterly different, but we adapted so comfortably that we sometimes forget how momentous, and rapid, all these changes have been. Yet we aren’t finished. Many markets have been transformed, and others have been created, but manufacturing has in some ways lagged the tech curve. Digital manufacturing markets are still young, immature, and growing rapidly.

Still Fragmented

I don’t mean to say that manufacturing hasn’t changed at all. Modern equipment enables more complex products, produced with higher accuracy and repeatability, more quickly and economically. Software has allowed complex organizations to manage production more efficiently at scale. Offline programming and simulation make programming equipment more efficient and predictable. On the balance, it’s safe to say that today’s manufacturers are far more productive than they were just a few decades ago.

On the other hand, the shape of the market, particularly in the U.S., hasn’t really changed. Sure, most manufacturers today have a website and email, but those are "old" technologies. A handful of companies have grown on the back of digital-first ordering experiences, but the bulk of the productive manufacturing output in the United States is driven by 1990s-era technology. The space is fragmented, and customer experience is inconsistent.

For all but the largest of customers, quality of experience depends on winning a three-part lottery—geography, capability, and capacity. Customers might have a good experience if they are close to a responsive shop that can meet their needs and has the capacity to take more work. With most U.S. capacity spread across tens of thousands of regional shops, the odds can be poor.

Manufacturing Isn’t Amazon

In contrast, companies like Amazon and McMaster-Carr have set new standards for fast, streamlined buying experience at scale. Customers can comfortably and painlessly browse and buy, with same- or next-day delivery, at a fair price, anywhere in the U.S. These great experiences are enabled by dynamic "web 2.0" websites, cloud computing, tightly integrated physical infrastructure, and scale. Young people today never knew a world without this kind of immediacy.

Manufacturing is about to get easier, faster, and more accessible. Perhaps in 20 years we’ll look back and marvel at how much the business has changed.

With that backdrop, today’s custom manufacturing marketplace feels incredibly dated. The experience is almost jarring. To be fair, manufacturing is far more complicated than retail, and it spans industries in different ways. Amazon might be the "everything store," but warehousing and shipping books isn’t all that different from warehousing and shipping cutlery or anything else.

But

making

cutlery isn’t anything like

making

books. Manufacturing is far more complex and difficult to do well at scale across industries and product categories. This is in part why the manufacturing space remains so fragmented: There is no such thing as an "everything manufacturer," nor will there ever be. Even so, customers demand better experiences, and some companies are listening.

The scope of the opportunity is highlighted by how quickly web-first manufacturers and marketplaces have grown. For example, Xometry, an online manufacturing broker, scaled to nearly $600 million in annual revenue in just over 10 years. Xometry’s platform provides instant pricing and certain manufacturability checks for uploaded 3D models. Their network of third-party manufacturers then fulfills orders.

Xometry is an interesting case study. All other things being equal, brokers are de facto worse than direct manufacturers: They’ll be more expensive, slower, less communicative, and less consistent than a dialed-in manufacturer. But "all other things" aren’t equal. Most manufacturers can’t provide instant pricing and design for manufacturability, and Xometry’s network acts as a one-stop shop for customers who might be otherwise frustrated with a fragmented marketplace running on 90s-era tech. There isn’t an "everything manufacturer," but Xometry brings lots of manufacturers to the table.

Xometry’s rapid growth highlights customers’ appetite for speed and simplicity. It has aggregated over half a billion in revenue, and they are still growing. My personal conviction is that Xometry nails half the problem. They add a layer of coordination over an inefficient, fragmented marketplace, and while that’s an improvement over the legacy state, it’s still not optimal. They resemble what Amazon might have been without vertically integrated warehouses and shipping infrastructure, perhaps more like eBay than Amazon. The true optimum is more vertically integrated.

Will Auto-Quoting Become the Norm?

The good news for direct manufacturers is that the type of job online brokers can aggregate is exactly the kind of job a direct manufacturer can tackle at scale through in-house software automation. In other words, if a broker can auto-quote a part, then so can a direct manufacturer. It’s only a matter of time before manufacturers bring that capability in-house and pair it with great execution, yielding better prices, faster turnaround, better communication, and more relationship-based growth opportunities.

There are scale benefits at play here as well. A scaled broker might aggregate billions of dollars in volume, but that scale makes little difference on the factory floor if it’s spread across 1,000 different network shops. A scaled direct manufacturer, on the other hand, gains buying power, leveled demand volatility, and process power (do a lot of something, get great at it).

This won’t be easy, and it won’t happen overnight, but the trajectory is obvious. Customers will increasingly demand quick, easy access to manufacturing services. Savvy manufacturers will respond by building systems to streamline online quoting, ordering, and account management. Soon, that’ll become the table stakes, and customers will gain unprecedented access and mobility. This will be true whether quoting is instant and online, or whether it’s simply accelerated through back-office automation.

High customer mobility will in turn motivate manufacturers in new ways. Rather than competing on price alone, manufacturers will differentiate by broadening their capabilities, improving quality, and/or decreasing lead times. Short term, this will help the leaders preserve margin and sustain growth. Long term, this back-and-forth counterpositioning will cause manufacturers to develop parity in capabilities and quality. At this stage, the likely next step is growth through acquisition. This long-awaited consolidation phase will culminate in a short list of scaled direct manufacturers that own the web-enabled manufacturing space.

This particular story isn’t novel. It’s how tech has evolved and transformed other industries in my own lifetime. Manufacturing is simply next on the list. Manufacturing is about to get easier, faster, more accessible, and more economical than ever before. Perhaps in 10 or 20 years we’ll look back and marvel at how much the business has changed.
