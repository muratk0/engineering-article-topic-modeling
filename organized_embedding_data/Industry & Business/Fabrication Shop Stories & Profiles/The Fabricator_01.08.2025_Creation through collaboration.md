The House of RAD provides a workspace to local artists, including those who work with metal.

B

randon Minga figured the House of RAD—an acronym for Resident Artist Doers—would be popular among artists in and around Milwaukee.

After all, artists want space to work on their projects, regardless of their art medium. House of RAD not only provides that space, it also allows artists to connect and collaborate.

What he didn’t expect was its exponential growth. House of RAD went from a 1,000-sq.-ft. facility in 2020 to a 35,000-sq.-ft. facility in 2022. And in 2025, it began relocating into a 67,000-sq.-ft. space, which will expand the number of artists housed at the collective from 60 to 100.

"I had an idea but didn’t want to put any expectations on it," said Minga, House of RAD founder. "But at the same time, we knew we had to come in strong and have the momentum to really push this thing forward, and I think we’ve accomplished that."

The House of RAD is a place Minga hopes spurs collaboration and creativity among Milwaukee-area artists, some of whom work with metal, including Minga.

"The art community here is pretty vibrant and strong. It’s also really collaborative," he said. "Whereas before, you would keep to yourself and not really talk about what you were doing because someone could take it. It’s not like that anymore. It’s pretty open and friendly. Everyone wants to collaborate."

All Under One Roof

Currently, about 10 of the artists at House of RAD work with metal. Minga said metal artists have access to multiprocess welding machines, plasma cutters, and pretty much anything else to fabricate large public sculptures. The new location will double the metal shop workspace, and its higher ceilings will allow for bigger and taller things, he added.

Minga is a multidisciplinary artist who often works with metal. However, his origins are in graphics and illustration. He didn’t learn to weld until after college, but once he did, he was hooked.

Minga’s work can be found throughout Wisconsin, including the mixed-media triptych for the Milwaukee Bucks’ art collection at Fiserv Forum, the Beerline Plaza project along Milwaukee’s Beerline Trail, and the "Salva-Jelly Bloom" jellyfish sculpture in downtown Green Bay.

"I treat everything like a collage. A lot of what I’ll do is with found objects, welding and creating or building onto antique pieces, that type of thing," he said.

Minga noted the jellyfish project—of which 25% of its steel came from a former sculpture of his—is a product of the unique setup and community of the House of RAD. Thirteen artists were involved, each contributing something to make it come to life.

House of RAD was meant to be more than just rental space for artists, Minga said.

"You could just fill a warehouse with a bunch of painters, but that’s not what we wanted to do. We wanted to be inspired daily by many different things and be able to collaborate," he explained. "We wanted to make the House of RAD a one-stop shop when it comes to the bleeding edge of creativity and collaboration."

The House of RAD in Milwaukee started in a 1,000-sq.-ft. facility in 2020, expanded into a 35,000-sq.-ft. facility in 2022, and began relocating to a 67,000-sq.-ft. space in 2025.

More Than Just a Workspace

Jill King, a mixed-media sculptor who likes to mix fabric with welded steel, is among the resident artists at the House of RAD.

A recent transplant to Milwaukee, King said she found the House of RAD workspace perfect for what she does.

"When I saw the House of RAD, I thought, ‘Well, I can weld there, I can have a space there,’" said King.

Setting up at the House of RAD has helped King immensely. In one example, Minga and others helped her brainstorm and devise something for a project that was to be mounted on walls.

Multidisciplinary artist Brandon Minga, whose metal sculptures can be found in a variety of Wisconsin communities, founded the House of RAD.

King has been an oxyacetylene welder for 10- plus years but has not yet branched into arc welding. Conversations and collaborations with some of the artists there have her seriously considering learning MIG and TIG, especially as she eyes larger sculptures for outdoor display.

"Having that knowledge and having people with those different skills around me has made a huge difference," she said. "It makes me feel more comfortable as I move in that direction."

She looks forward to the new location, where there will be more lighting, higher ceilings, gallery space, and it’s closer to downtown Milwaukee.

"It’s a wonderful move for everyone involved," King said.
