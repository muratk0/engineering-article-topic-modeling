Automation represents all these things. It fundamentally changes how people do their jobs and, in some cases, does the job for them—an intimidating reality for office and manufacturing team members alike. But it is leadership’s job to push boundaries and integrate the team into the process. When you involve people at all levels in automation planning and implementation, fear gives way to curiosity, then excitement. When they help select, test, and refine the automation, they take pride in the results and actively work to make it successful.

At Hebron, Ky.-based Skilcraft, where I served as CEO, innovation was part of the company’s DNA for 40 years. With a union workforce, we still managed to integrate automation across every aspect of our production process—laser systems with auto load/unload, panel benders, robotic welding cells, robotic press brakes, automated powder coating lines, and more.

Shop culture really matters. Smooth implementation of automation requires that all stakeholders—including the workforce and (if a shop is unionized) the union leadership—be part of a culture of innovation and continuous improvement In my experience, automation done right is simply another form of continuous improvement. And if continuous improvement is not part of your company’s core values, it should be.

Involving your employees fosters trust. When workers help select, test, and refine automation, they take pride in the results and actively work to make it successful. Buy-in is earned through transparency, inclusion, and training. When employees see that automation makes their work more efficient and valuable—not obsolete—they will embrace it.

At Skilcraft, we involved the team from the getgo as we learned about and selected the automated equipment. We trained them, gave them ownership, and encouraged experimentation. If they were

not

involved, they did

not

use the equipment—plain and simple.

We took this approach after some hard lessons learned. I remember our first attempt at automated powder coating. We bought automated guns, installed them, then told the team to use them—without much guidance. The result: We produced inconsistent coating patterns, and we ended up scrapping thousands of dollars’ worth of panels.

The production team, firm in their resolve, said they could do better manually. In fact, they eventually stopped using the automatic guns altogether. As their resistance continued unabated, we in management grew more frustrated. In truth, though, our approach fed their resistance. We never involved them, nor did we invest in training.

We repeated the process several years later, this time with full team involvement. The new automated line was not only used—it was celebrated. The team worked closely with the equipment manufacturer, which conducted on-site training for several weeks. They shared their challenges and resolved them by working closely with the equipment supplier. Better quality and higher productivity resulted. They took pride in the line, and it became a center of excellence, an area where individuals coveted working.

How Automation Is Perceived

People fear job losses as they see their roles become redundant. This is an especially big concern for skilled workers. A welder sees a welding robot and perceives a job loss on its face. In a culture of acceptance, that same welder sees the robot as an opportunity to increase output, which in my experience always pulls along more value-added work—including more welding work that the robot simply cannot do.

Implemented well, automation increases productivity per person and reduces the need for long hours of repetitive, physical tasks. It makes work safer and more engaging. It enables workers to think more strategically, improve processes, and collaborate more closely with engineering. Conversations shift from "work harder" to "how can we make the machine more productive?" That mindset shift is powerful. Automation may change their jobs, but it also elevates their role and helps build a healthier company.

Map the Current State

Before anything else, observe and analyze your current process. What skills are required to perform tasks manually? How reliable is the available automation, and could it perform the task better than a skilled person? Specifically, does it produce less scrap, increase quality, lower labor costs, and boost throughput?

Key performance indicators (KPI) are essential here. They tell you whether a process, system, or team is achieving the desired results, including on-time delivery, quality, productivity, and profitability.

If a machine cannot match the consistency of a skilled person, automating the process might not be worth it—at least for now. In fact, not every process needs automation. Identify where automation delivers the highest return, where it complements your workforce, and where the technology is truly ready for your specific application.

There must be a payback. Automated systems, especially customized ones, are not cheap, and they aren’t always reliable. We all have automation sitting unused in a corner because it was not properly vetted. You need to evaluate the development cost, the probability of success, and the return on investment.

Resources for Automation

That said, the vetting process shouldn’t dissuade you from pushing the envelope. A challenging application may be a good fit for automation, but only if your maintenance and operations teams are up for the task.

At Skilcraft, we needed to fabricate some complex parts that involved INCONEL and other high-temperature alloys. The parts had many countersinks and features that were being fabricated manually. The existing process couldn’t meet the application’s tight tolerance requirements consistently. Countersinks would be missed; rework and scrap rates rose.

In a quest to automate that countersinking process, we looked at various punch/laser combination machines. We evaluated different equipment manufacturers, each of which ran test parts for evaluation. We found one machine that could perform the task—yet we were told that the application pushed the equipment to its upper limits.

We were told what we needed to do to keep the equipment running, including robust daily maintenance, tool sharpening, careful programming, and comprehensive operator training. We involved engineering, production, operation, and quality team members. It took us six months to validate the automation.

Even after all this, our CFO calculated the cost of the equipment and compared it to the labor and scrap cost. The payback was there. The machine, when it worked, paid for itself. And we made it happen—but only by getting all stakeholders involved, from front-line operators to top management.

Over the next several years, we doubled our output while still producing good parts. We succeeded, yet we had to be vigilant. Again, we were pushing the machine to its limit. We made our share of mistakes. Failure to maintain the equipment, properly train operators, or involve engineering at the right times cost us dearly. When we stayed on top of our game, though, we reaped the benefits and then some.

We learned similar lessons when integrating a punching and panel bending flexible manufacturing system (FMS). The FMS replaced several blanking and bending machines, and the way it boosted our throughput was truly astounding, when it worked. But frequent downtime, due to poor preventive maintenance, hurt us.

The issue stemmed from us not truly knowing our current state, including the 50-year history of "firefighting" maintenance. To get the most out of our new system, we needed additional maintenance staff; a reliable spare parts inventory; and, most important, a culture change that embraced a proactive, robust maintenance program.

Over time, we improved significantly, especially when we hired an exceptionally good director of engineering. Hiring such expertise earlier would have made our automation investment even more beneficial. With that new hire, along with an intern, we started tracking our maintenance and repair issues. We also worked with experts at our machine tool manufacturer, who audited our maintenance program and helped us refine it.

The experience illustrates an immutable fact: When you invest in automation, you need to invest in the resources to support it. The effort challenged us, but it ultimately ended in success.

Of course, knowing what I know now, I would have done things differently. Still, even with the downtime and maintenance issues, the KPIs proved automation’s value, both for the FMS and the punch/laser combo. When the machines ran, the productivity and quality gains were obvious, and the KPIs confirmed it.

A FORMULA FOR CHANGE: LESSONS LEARNED

At its essence, automation represents change. If it’s implemented well, the change is positive—but it’s not guaranteed. To make change successful, I often refer to David Gleicher, a former senior executive at consulting firm Arthur D. Little. He developed a formula for change management. Various versions have been used over the years, but the one I like goes as follows:

Change = Dissatisfaction × Vision × First Steps × Creative Leadership.

If I had considered this formula before some of the automation implementations at Skilcraft, the entire process probably would have gone a lot smoother. Consider our powder coating line initiative:

Dissatisfaction.

People need to be dissatisfied with the status quo. At Skilcraft, we experienced coating inconsistency and high scrap rates.

Vision.

We thought that automation could eliminate the cost of scrapped panels, improve our delivery, and improve our quality metrics.

First Steps.

You need to establish several concrete steps to start the implementation. This is where things went wrong with the powder coating line. We just bought the equipment before pulling together a team to implement the automation.

Creative Leadership.

This includes having a supportive culture and core organizational values that accept automation for what it is—a valuable (though not the only) tool for continuous improvement.

If you have no support in any one of these areas, you insert "0" into the equation. Multiply anything by zero, you get zero—and your initiative goes nowhere.

Here, we turned to software automation—specifically, electronic data interchange (EDI). We needed a company to write software that could interface directly with our customers’ systems. To find a software provider, we took the same approach as we did when buying machinery. We got the customer service team, IT, accounting, quality, and production teams involved early. Together, they worked with our software provider to create a system that worked best for us.

The software automatically entered customer orders into our ERP. It also identified any changes in demand or lead time issues. The team focused on the exceptions—that is, the orders that, for various reasons, couldn’t be processed automatically. The system automatically released jobs to scheduling; batched certain orders together to streamline processing; printed part number labels at workstations; and created all the necessary shipping, quality, and other documents.

The experience exemplified two realities of automation in the complex world of contract metal fabrication. First, bad data gives bad results. Automation is only as good as the information you feed it. This applies to equipment on the floor as well as to the software that streamlines your business. If algorithms work off flawed assumptions or inaccurate demand signals, they can wreak havoc on your operation.

Second, automation does not end on the shop floor. Office and administrative functions often represent a third of a company’s workforce, and inefficiencies here—from quoting and estimating to order entry, scheduling, and data collection—can be just as costly. Artificial intelligence (AI) is already having a measurable impact here. I encourage you to work with your office team to see where AI and related technologies can automate the manual aspects of their jobs.

Measuring Success

A successful automation investment improves key metrics without sacrificing safety or employee engagement. Success isn’t guaranteed, of course. If automation consistently fails to outperform the manual process, and you see no viable ways to improve, it is time to move on.

That said, never make this decision based on emotion or gut feelings. Turn to your KPIs. Do your employees simply need more training? Perhaps your maintenance program needs to be more robust.

The idea is to create a culture that embraces improvement, not the status quo. When you have this, you have a culture that embraces automation—a place where people high-five each other when the equipment runs well. Operators take pride in their machines. They feel a sense of ownership and responsibility, not resentment. That’s the culture you want, where automation success is celebrated at every level.
