# Pursue the not-so-obvious targets

[TARİH: 01.07.2026 The Fabricator]

Executive Perspectives

Chasing the crowd can distract from great opportunities

By

Lisa Wertzbaugher

tadamichi/IStock/Getty Images Plus

I

just returned from the June FMA Board meeting. At the end of our time together, each board member gave an outlook report on what we are seeing in our respective regions and what we expect for the remainder of 2026.

The reports varied from member to member and region to region, but one thing was consistent: Everyone is attacking the data center market.

We are also seeing this in the eastern Iowa/western Illinois region, where the metal fabrication business I own with my husband is located. And we are following suit—adding web marketing, building prospect lists, and cold and warm outreach.

There are some challenges. We are small, and it will be more capital-intensive for us to onboard these large projects. The sales journey can be long and expensive. The market is saturated—everyone is running at the same thing.

Recently we were approached by a much larger company about taking on some of its standard production so it can absorb more data center work.

Now, that is doable—overflow work for a larger company that has ironed out the production bumps. Picking up projects to support stable, longterm customers can be very profitable, short- and long-term.

This has got me thinking: What are some of the not-so-obvious targets we are missing?

INDUSTRY FOCUS

Space

. Everyone is focused on data centers and the tech industry right now. However, space exploration is planning for significant spending and investment over the next decade.

As the market in this segment changes from a small, government-dominated niche to a fast-growing industrial economy, growth is expected to top 9% annually for the next 10 years. Total value is placed at over $600 billion with a jump to $1.8 trillion by 2035. Our capabilities include laser welding, which is a great fit for this industry. I’m spending some of my sales energy marketing to these space-intensive companies.

Heavy Ag

. Another industry I’m targeting is heavy agriculture. Yes, that is correct—agriculture.

Markets like agriculture and trucking are known for their cyclical nature, and after 12 consecutive quarters of downturn, upward momentum is expected in 2027. The time to sell to these customers is now. Introduce them to your company, pick up work others have let go, and be ready to onboard new projects when the market turns.

OVERFLOW SUPPORT

Back to overflow.

Some large companies are asking for help. As the manufacturing economy continues to grow, capital investment and infrastructure will have to keep pace.

Typically, there is a lag. We are partnering with companies we would generally consider competitors, but the real competition are low-cost global companies and industries that convince potential workers to go into other professions. Partnering on projects, sharing capacity, and pursuing oversized opportunities with a partner are all great strategies to consider.

STAFFING

Every time I attend a conference with a session on workforce, we discuss Gen Z and millennials—even Gen Alpha (people born between 2010 and 2024). I have spoken on and written about this extensively, and we have focused on Gen Z for hiring in the past few years.

It’s all about young people. However, we recently we interviewed and hired a few employees who have retired from the manufacturing industry but decided they wanted to continue working part-time. Why send them to a different industry? We should reintegrate these individuals into a schedule and capacity that fits their retirement expectations. Their expertise is invaluable, they are great coaches, and they fill a gap that no one else currently is.

EXPORT

Domestic manufacturers spend a lot of time fending off global competition. Global companies often have a cost structure that gives them an aggressive price advantage.

Maybe we should consider playing less defense and more offense.

I have been surprised by recent inquiries from international companies into some of our unique welded assemblies. Cost is not always the driver. Delivery, quality, and design all figure into the buying decision.

There are excellent trade shows outside of the U.S. that provide a platform for scoping the market. There are global companies looking for U.S. partners to manufacture and sell their products domestically. We have been approached by two in the past year.

There is also the consideration of acquisition expansion. This may sound overwhelming, but not all acquisitions are large companies buying large companies. Buying another company is a consideration for entering a new market.

This economy is still deep and broad enough that small companies should aim for the targets hiding in plain sight, outside the latest hotspots. As our economy continues to grow, make a point to listen for the opportunities others don’t hear in all the noise.
