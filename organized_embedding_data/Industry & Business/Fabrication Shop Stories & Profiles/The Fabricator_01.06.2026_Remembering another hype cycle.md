# Remembering another hype cycle

[TARİH: 01.06.2026 The Fabricator]

Editor’s Corner

Additive manufacturing didn’t revolutionize manufacturing, but it did turn out to be a useful tool

By

Dan Davis

AI is a tool, just like additive manufacturing. But it’s also fuel for the advancement of technology. For example, it’s why you are seeing more conversation about humanoid robots.

I

n fall 2018, The Fabricator launched a sister magazine called The Additive Report, dedicated to covering the additive manufacturing industry. Honestly, the timing of the publication was a bit off, as it came toward the tail end of what was a pretty dramatic hype cycle for the technology.

The publication lasted for a full three years after that initial launch. A lack of advertising—and perhaps reader interest—ultimately made it difficult for the publication to move forward.

But even in 2018, I sort of bought into the hype. I wrote this in that first issue of The Additive Report: "The point is that additive manufacturing (AM) likely isn’t going to become the main production tool of any job shop or manufacturer with a high-mix, low-volume workload. But it is likely to play an important role for these businesses. The key is that they have to be able to see just how AM can work for them."

Talking with Paul DeWys, founder of Forerunner 3D, Coopersville, Mich., for the story "A 3D-printed option for bending easy-to-mar metal" for this issue (p. 52), I was reminded of my interviews and trade show visits for the magazine. Years have passed, and the topic of 3D printing doesn’t come up as much. But additive manufacturing is still around. DeWys’ story of the 3D printing offshoot of his engineering and design business encapsulated the technology’s recent history.

"I went and bought a bankrupt 3D printing company sort of by accident, and I said, ‘We’re in the 3D printing business, boys,’" DeWys said. "We had been trying to use it for a while, so I’m like, ‘This is going to be our ticket. We’re going to make so much money off this. It’s going to be crazy.’

"And then we didn’t make any money for like three years. We lost money," he added. "Then finally we figured it out. We figured out what technology our customers really needed. We figured out what niches to go after on the shop floor. That’s when we finally started to turn the ship around."

DeWys started targeting the markets that the larger 3D printing companies and technology developers were underserving—metal fabricators, machine shops, and injection molders. Meanwhile, aerospace, health care, and defense dominated the attention of the rest of the additive manufacturing world.

So additive manufacturing never went away; it just evolved into a tool for modern manufacturers. Today, it’s a $7 billion market in the U.S., and the technology actually is being used in high-volume production. (For comparison, the U.S. fabricated metal product manufacturing industry, defined by NAICS 332, has total combined annual sales of roughly $350 billion.)

Now an entire generation that has grown up with additive manufacturing technology at home, school, or even the local library is entering the manufacturing workforce. They likely will have 3D printing at the forefront of their thinking as they try to solve engineering and production puzzles. They won’t need a reminder of what they can create on a printer.

It might be a bit of an apples-to-oranges comparison, but I kind of feel like we are living through another hype cycle with AI. The big difference is that the latter is going to have a much larger impact on the world—not just from a business standpoint, but from a societal and environmental aspect as well.

With the focus on manufacturing, however, you see that AI is already having a tremendous influence. Metal fabricators with the time and curiosity to try AI engines are creating their own estimating programs and quality management systems. Pointed prompts and patience are required to guide the AI engines, but the results can be impressive.

AI is a tool, just like additive manufacturing. But it’s also fuel for the advancement of technology. For example, it’s why you are seeing more conversations about humanoid robots. Critics of this type of automation are correct that much more work has to be done in terms of hand dexterity and spatial awareness before these robots can match human performance. What critics don’t realize is that this type of robotic teaching is being sped up with the help of AI.

In fact, the phrase "physical AI" is being used to describe AI agents that are helping these robots to become better at perceiving their surroundings and operating safely in unstructured environments. This type of technological advancement would be years down the road if it weren’t for the use of AI.

Ten years from now, however, fab shops still will be cutting, forming, and welding metal. Front offices will be slimmer than they are today, and perhaps a humanoid robot will be moving a push truck of parts from one area to another.

Hype fades, but evolution is real. Additive manufacturing is here to stay. The impact that AI will have on the future manufacturing landscape has yet to be fully understood, but the early returns are promising. The one thing we do know is that those results likely will manifest themselves a lot sooner than people realize.
