OSHA launches initiative to protect temporary workers

The U.S. Department of Labor’s Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA) has announced an initiative to further protect temporary employees from workplace hazards.

OSHA has sent a memorandum, viewable at

http://s.dol.gov/zm

, to its regional administrators directing field inspectors to assess whether employers who use temporary workers are complying with their responsibilities under the Occupational Safety and Health Act. Inspectors will use a newly created code in their information system to denote when temporary workers are exposed to safety and health violations. Additionally, they will assess whether temporary workers received required training in a language and vocabulary they could understand. The memo underscores the duty of employers to protect all workers from hazards.

Of the thousands of workers who die each year on the job from preventable hazards, “many… are temporary workers who often perform the most dangerous jobs, have limited English proficiency, and are not receiving the training and protective measures required. Workers must be safe, whether they’ve been on the job for one day or for 25 years,” said Dr. David Michaels, assistant secretary of labor for occupational safety and health.

In addition, OSHA has begun working with the American Staffing Association and employers that use staffing agencies to promote best practices ensuring that temporary workers are protected from job hazards.

In recent months OSHA has received a series of reports about temporary workers suffering fatal injuries, many during their first days on a job. OSHA has issued citations when the employer failed to provide adequate protections, including safety training.

Rethinking manufacturing education

Educators, guidance counselors, manufacturing technology providers, and metal fabricators have to rethink how they present manufacturing education if they want to generate more excitement for manufacturing careers among students.

The discussion moderator of Strengthening Manufacturing Education: An Experience Summit in late June posed a question that sums up the battle to replenish the ranks of retiring manufacturing workers: “How do we get young people interested in a career in welding?”

The question was focused on welding, primarily because the two-day event in Burr Ridge, Ill., was sponsored by The Lincoln Electric Co. and several attendees were directly involved with welding and industrial technology education, but it definitely could be expanded to include all facets of metal fabricating. The U.S. Department of Labor says that 23 percent of the people employed in U.S. manufacturing are 55 or older, which means a significant chunk of the manufacturing workforce is seriously thinking about retirement. Getting new blood into the industry is a primary concern for many in the industry.

The discussion that followed the moderator’s question focused on several points, including recruiting students who might be interested in making money without the need of a traditional four-year college degree; stressing that welding can act as the foundation for an exciting manufacturing career, whether it is as a job shop owner or a certified welding educator; and positioning welding as a modern career with advanced tools and monitoring equipment, not the dark and boring career of 50 years ago. While those suggestions are being enacted sporadically in school districts and towns all over the U.S. , others have taken formal—and successful—steps toward changing the way that young people look at welding. They shared their successes during the educational summit.

David Landon, manager of welding engineering, Vermeer, Pella, Iowa, told the crowd that his company has turned to virtual welding training as a means to boost the company’s all-around welding performance.

“This is a tool to improve the training of our welders, but what that also has really done is allowed our trainers to become better trainers,” Landon said.

Vermeer, a manufacturer of heavy-duty agricultural and industrial equipment, has been using the virtual welding technology since 2008. Today it uses Lincoln Electric’s VRTEX™ 360 to evaluate new welding hires and provide refresher training to current welders.

Landon said that less experienced welders using the tool—which replicates the gas metal arc welding process without the live arc—learn the important aspects of a good weld: placement of wire into the joint, work positioning, gun angle, travel speed, and center-tip-to-work distance. When welding simulations are complete, welding trainers get quantified scores and an idea on areas for improvement, which can be difficult to decipher simply by watching someone lay a bead.

To get an idea of the impact that the tool has made on the company’s more than 300 welders, Landon said you only need to look at welding scores for those who take a pretest and a posttest after training with the virtual welder. He said that welders show a 12-point improvement, which translates into more refreshed skills on the shop floor.

W. Richard Polanin, professor and program chair, Illinois Central College, East Peoria, Ill., stressed the importance of actually working with manufacturers to ensure introductions to the best possible welding technology, access to welding expertise, and availability of job opportunities for welding students. His department’s willingness to work with any manufacturer to develop training programs designed specifically for one type of application or to make it part of a general welding curriculum, he said, has made the school’s welding program much more relevant to the surrounding community. It also helps him when materials used in welding training are donated.

Erica Swinney, program director, Manufacturing Renaissance, Chicago, shared her tale of trying to establish a high school with a focus on technology and engineering in the Chicago Public School System. Over the past six years, her organization has worked with local manufacturers, the school system, elected officials, and nearby residents to set up a curriculum that puts the school’s graduates into a position to become anything from a CNC machine tool operator to a candidate for engineering school. Swinney said that it has been a slow build, but increasingly each graduation class from the 150-student school is showing more interest in manufacturing.

In the end, all participants agreed that any change in manufacturing education will occur only if people get involved.

Metal & Flesh series sells manufacturing to the next generation

It starts in a studio at the back of a barn somewhere near Toronto. It’s a manifestation of an in-your-face passion for exposing the importance, the need, the excitement, and the human connection in manufacturing to the next generation. It’s fast-paced, it’s loud, it’s relevant. It’s the Edge Factor.

After a dozen years as a CNC programmer, Jeremy Bout, Edge Factor producer and host, combined his dedication to manufacturing with his love of electronic media and art to create the Metal & Flesh series—actionpacked films meant to reveal the impact of manufacturing. Episodes are carefully crafted to pique the interest of students, educators, and parents.

One episode takes viewers inside the progression of designing, engineering, and building the rock drill that penetrated 2,200 ft. of earth to save 33 trapped Chilean miners. Another walks viewers through an amputee’s conceptualization and design of a prosthetic leg that put the snocross champion back in competition. His design now gives other amputees a chance to participate in extreme sports.

Bout chatted with FMA Communications (FMAC) before his keynote presentation to more than 500 attendees at the Rockford Chamber of Commerce Manufacturers Council Annual Dinner in Illinois.

FMAC:

What is the bottom-line focus of Metal & Flesh?

Bout:

Metal & Flesh episodes are designed to inspire, to tell stories of people using new technologies to make a difference in lives. A good story is made up of how manufacturing engineering touches the human element. You can tell about the most amazing piece of technology out there, but if it doesn’t touch a human life, you’re wasting your time.

FMAC:

Where do the stories come from?

Bout:

Subjects for episodes come to us, but every once in a while I’ll hear a snippet of a story that sounds right. About 5 percent of the opportunities give us something to pursue. A story has to have the technology and the people, and we have to have access to the core story. Am I allowed to release details? Can I bring a film crew in and spend three days in the facility? It’s a major screening process.

FMAC:

It’s not just entertainment. How is Edge Factor educating?

Bout:

Every episode spawns a GameChanger kit of resources for teachers. The kit includes a license to use the episode itself and follow-up about the team behind the technology. We show what careers are involved and how they work together to give students the tools they need to make a decision about a manufacturing career, the educators what they need to inform, and parents the ability to make a better decision on what to encourage their children to do. The Chilean mine rescue kit, for example, delves into engineers working with designers who feed the machinists operating the equipment that created the rock drill … to save lives.

FMAC:

How are you creating interactions with young people already in manufacturing?

Bout:

Right now I’m laser-focused on my eduFACTOR and its distribution in the United States. Postgraduates share who influenced them, what they studied, who they work for, and what they do on a day-today basis. We are painting a series of 15 very diverse pathway videos that will directly give students resources to decide what to pursue.

FMAC:

How are Edge Factor projects funded?

Bout:

This journey started with product placement sponsorships. They’ve grown to include contributions from educational foundations whose goal is to get young people involved in manufacturing. SME and Gardner Publications have been a big help. Our biggest sponsor to date, for sure, has been Sandvik Coromant. They are a very valued partner. Funding is an evolution. Other partnerships are forming.

The next level of automation

The rear view of this laser cutting machine shows fabricators taking in the cutting speed of the Bystronic ByAutonom 3015. The equipment is designed to attain a top positioning speed of 6,650 IPM.

Ask metal fabricators if they have automation, and more than likely they will base their answers on whether their facilities have automated load/unload tables or material storage and retrieval towers connected to their blank cutting machines. For them, automation is related to material handling.

But the act of creating a blank for downstream processing or final delivery to a customer involves so much more than simply moving the sheet metal into the equipment and then removing the cut shapes. For instance, a laser cutting machine operator typically is needed to ensure that the cutting nozzle and focal length are optimal for the cutting job at hand, look for tipped-up parts that the cutting head could collide with, and generally be aware of maintenance schedules to keep the equipment up and running. The operator’s active participation is necessary for the optimal performance of the machine over a manufacturing shift.

Machine tool builders recognize that and want to make the process of laser cutting as operator-independent as possible. With the unveiling of the ByAutonom 3015 laser cutting machine at its open house in early June, Bystronic Inc. President Robert St. Aubin said he thinks this new machine can provide “autonomous fabricating” that metal manufacturers can use to be even more competitive.

“People wondered why you couldn’t just put in the same numbers as you did [for a laser cutting job] last week and get the same kind of results,” St. Aubin said. “That’s because laser cutting was more art than science. This is an attempt to bring laser cutting into the world of science.”

The new laser cutting machine, which won an MM Award from the German manufacturing technology journal

Maschinen Markt

for innovative technology on display at EuroBLECH 2012 last October, has automated lens cassette changing, nozzle changing, and nozzle alignment. Operators don’t have to take time to switch out the cutting head and focus the lens properly each time a new cutting job is initiated.

In addition, the machine has a collision detection system that helps it to keep cutting parts even if the cutting head runs into a tipped-up part. In the past such a scenario would have resulted in a machine shutdown until an equipment operator could restart the job and realign the laser. The By-Autonom can recenter the laser cutting head after a run-in with a tipped-up part and work around the problem area. If recentering is not possible for some reason, the machine can arrange for another lens cassette to be retrieved from one of the five storage spaces in the laser cutting area.

During operation, the laser cutting machine is constantly collecting data on the condition of the machine components—presenting the information in easy-to-understand charts for the operator. If a component requires checking even before routine maintenance is required, the machine alerts the operator to look into the potential problem area. When regular maintenance is required, the machine notifies the operator as well.

“The better the [laser cutting machine] operator, the more parts you’ll get. We want to say, ‘No matter what operator you have, you get more parts,’” St. Aubin said.

Bystronic engineers designed the equipment to have a lower operating cost than typical CO

2

laser cutting machines. It shifts into a power-saving mode—running about 30 percent of what it would be when cutting—when it is idle, and has solid-state circuitry as part of the power supply. Bystronic also recommends that premix laser gas be used with the laser, which can cut the consumption of costly helium dramatically during typical operating use.

At the open house Bystronic also debuted its BySprint Pro 4020 laser cutting machine with a 4,400-W CO

2

resonator and its Xcite 80 E electric press brake to North American fabricators. Like the ByAutonom, both pieces of equipment were introduced to the metal fabricating community for the first time at EuroBLECH 2012.

ANSI approves new RIA robot safety standard

The American National Standards Institute (ANSI) has approved a new U.S. national robot safety standard. Developed by Robotic Industries Association (RIA), Ann Arbor, Mich., the ANSI/RIA R15.06-2012 standard has been updated for the first time since 1999 and now is harmonized with the ISO 10218:2011 standard for robot manufacturers and integrators.

The adoption of the international standard allows systems designed and built in one country to be freely moved to other countries while maintaining compliance with safety regulations. A key feature in the standard is collaborative operation, which is the introduction of a worker to the loop of active interaction during automatic robot operation. Systems can be designed for the operator to load and unload the robot directly or drive the robot manually to a selected location.

Hypertherm hosts Building America Conference at New Hampshire headquarters

U.S. manufacturers’ productivity has tripled, and products of value are still made in the U.S. That was the message that Evan Smith, president of Hypertherm Inc., Hanover, N.H., gave to attendees at the company’s Building America Conference in early May.

Smith went on to point out that American workers are increasing productivity throughput with fewer workers than their Chinese counterparts—11 million compared to China’s 110 million. And a lot of that has to do with substituting capital for labor. The U.S. is second only to Japan in the use of industrialized robots. Spending on equipment and software has increased by 31 percent, Smith added.

The two-day seminar provided attendees with tips to promote continuous improvement, operational excellence, and leadership and a peek at why some businesses—despite being led by smart individuals—fail. Hypertherm employees presented on topics such as branding, lean manufacturing, strategic planning, cutting efficiency, and the future of air plasma.

Kevin Duggan, president of Duggan Associates, author of

Design for Operational Excellence

, and a consultant to Hypertherm, spoke on the importance of integrating a continuous improvement plan—a series of improvements and sustaining measures—into a business.

“Companies that move forward are the ones where its leaders have the same answers to the tough questions. If you want to achieve operational excellence, you create flow only to know if flow is stopping. Any good company can create flow, but great companies know what to do when flow stops,” Duggan said.

He went on to say that operations excellence isn’t just about creating flow, it’s about creating self-healing flow and immune systems for flow to ensure that your operations are able to start every time.

Sydney Finkelstein, professor of management at the Tuck School of Business at Dartmouth College and author of

Why Smart Executives Fail,

cautioned attendees, saying even good leaders make bad decisions because they let emotions overcome rational thinking.

“Sometimes the same things that have led to your success will eventually lead to your demise,” Finkelstein said.

He said that leadership capabilities that differentiate winners from losers include intellectual honesty, adaptability, self-awareness, and accountability.

Synalloy’s Bristol Metals subsidiary files antidumping petitions

Synalloy Corp., Spartanburg, S.C., a holding company owning subsidiaries that engage in a number of diverse business activities including the production of stainless steel pipe and fabrication of stainless and carbon steel piping systems, has announced that its Bristol Metals LLC subsidiary, joined by co-petitioners Felker Brothers Corp. and Outokumpu Stainless Pipe, has filed antidumping duty petitions with the Department of Commerce and the U.S. International Trade Commission. The petitions allege that welded stainless pressure pipe imported from Malaysia, Thai-land, and Vietnam was being dumped in the U.S. market.

From 2010 to 2012 imports of welded stainless steel pressure pipe from those three countries increased from 14,000 tons to 17,000 tons and have taken a significant share of a relatively stagnant U.S. market. The petitions allege dumping margins of 15 to 17 percent for Malaysia, 13 to 15 percent for Thailand, and 70 to 71 percent for Vietnam.

The DOC preliminary dumping determination is due Dec. 12, 2013.

Schuler Automation shows transfer systems in operation

Visitors from Germany and abroad attended a Technology Day held by Schuler Automation in Hessdorf, Germany, in mid-April. As well as receiving information on the latest developments in part transport, they were able to observe eight different transfer systems in operation, including the new Intra Trans.

To save space, the new system works within the press rather than through the press window. Parts are transported by carriages mounted on the transfer rails, so the full width of the window is available for material feeding, unrestricted by the transfer rails.

Each carriage can be moved individually, allowing varying distances between stations.

Material can be fed from both sides. Suitable for retrofitting on existing presses, the system needs the same space as the progressive die.
