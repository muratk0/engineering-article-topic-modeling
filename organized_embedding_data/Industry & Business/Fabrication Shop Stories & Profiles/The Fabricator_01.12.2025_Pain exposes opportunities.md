# Pain exposes opportunities

[TARİH: 01.12.2025 The Fabricator]

Tech Talk

Manufacturers thrive when they alleviate that pain

CALEB CHAMBERLAIN

T

his almost goes without saying, but I’ll say it anyway: Manufacturing is tough. Running any kind of business is complicated, but there’s something special (or maddening) about manufacturing in particular. Building physical products adds layers of complexity and risk that pure service or software companies don’t have. Safety is a massive risk and must always remain top of mind. Hiring talent is an ongoing challenge. Inventory and receivables consume working capital. Scheduling is an impossibly complex and mostly unsolved problem, especially for highmix manufacturers. Quoting tends to be labor-intensive and inefficient. Equipment is expensive. Competition is unforgiving. Margins can be slim. Cash flow often is a challenge.

One experienced shop owner recently confided that he doesn’t think many manufacturers in the U.S. are profitable, at least sustainably. Many run paid-off, fully depreciated machines that don’t touch the income statement or the balance sheet, yet they still just scrape by with single-digit margins. He thought that new equipment, with associated debt service and depreciation, might push some manufacturers into the red.

I don’t think that’s true. I think that most manufacturers are generally productive, but it does highlight the challenge: In spite of these headwinds, I believe that manufacturing is filled with opportunities that avoid many of the downsides traditional manufacturers might face, especially in terms of margin and cash flow. Software is enabling new business models and new services, creating "blue ocean" opportunities where there is less competition, more margin, and lots of latent customer pain. Want to know how to build a better-performing business? Follow the pain.

My company, OSH Cut, is one of many examples. We aren’t the only business in our space, and we aren’t the largest, but we’ve managed to grow into a niche where there was less competition and more customer need. My brother and I started the business because it was hard for us to buy prototype sheet metal parts—the kind of low-volume, one-off, inconsistent jobs that understandably don’t interest most shops. It worked out that lots of other people needed those kinds of parts, so we grew fast, with healthy margins and without outside investment.

It’s been a wild ride. I wish I could say we had a deep understanding of the market before we started. I wish I could say we had a master plan that fully captured the scope and scale of the opportunity. But it was mostly accidental. We bought a machine to solve our own problem, developed software to make our lives and our customers’ lives easier, and things just took off. Today, we are growing faster than ever.

Find a Niche, Create Value

I bring this up to highlight the power of solving customer pain in a niche. If an industry, service, or product category is known for customer pain, there’s value to be created. From my experience, pain is the rule, not the exception. Many industrial products and services have yet to adopt modern software and processes like other industries have. Quoting as a rule remains slow. Lead-times often are measured in months. Websites lack relevant information.

This highlights the scope of the problem—and the opportunity. Influenced by Amazon, people today expect immediacy and transparency. Those aren’t words I’d use to describe the bulk of the industrial supply chain in the United States.

Real-World Example

Digital Metal is taking a new approach to investment casting: instant prices, rapid lead-times, no minimum quantities. The founder, Connor Kapoor, told me about a painful experience that motivated him to start Digital Metal. It’s worth recounting here in full:

"When I was working at Siemens, we had an older frame scheduled for an outage. When you schedule an outage, it’s critical to avoid overrunning the allotted time. If you do, you’re liable to the customer for lost revenue in the form of liquidated damages. The outage was originally scheduled for eight days but ended up taking 26 because we were missing two parts: a small high-pressure valve body and a row-one compressor blade.

"Since this particular frame was old and originally built in America, the supply chain was expected to be primarily domestic. But when we contacted the original vendors to remake the parts, we learned that the tooling no longer existed. The lead-time was six weeks for one part and 12 weeks for the other. We eventually found spares in a warehouse in Germany, but the inability to manufacture the parts locally in any reasonable time frame cost the company roughly $1 million in liquidated damages for parts that together cost less than $10,000. It could’ve been far worse if we had to remake them in the U.S.

"The casting supply chain is fragmented and fragile, and it’s only getting worse as foundry owners age out. That’s why we’re building the fastest foundry in the world."

Ouch! A million dollars in damages for a couple parts that together cost less than $10,000! This experience motivated the original business, but since then, it’s found an unexpected market for production-grade parts at low volumes. Ordinarily, customers find alternatives to casting for low volumes and prototypes, because it is inaccessible, long-lead, and expensive. Now that Digital Metal makes investment casting accessible, customers are starting to shift their expectations. Accessibility is creating new markets and new demand.

Kapoor’s story highlights exactly the kind of customer pain that begs to be solved through innovation. Digital Metal already has software to enable instant pricing. Its team continues to develop new software tools to improve quote accuracy, automate mold creation, and streamline processes. It is still a young company, but given the current state of the investment casting marketplace, I’m confident that it will scale fast and thrive. For the record, I have no relationship with Digital Metal, financial or otherwise. But I’m watching it with great interest.

Find the Pain

Opportunities abound. For every story about a new company successfully reimagining a service, there are perhaps hundreds of stories about customers having bad experiences. I recently posted a thread on X asking people to share poor manufacturing experiences they’ve had. Many responded.

One person, attempting to reshore manufacturing, tried to source custom packaging in the U.S., only to find that his mid-volume business wasn’t interesting. Multiple vendors required five-plus years of packaging purchases up front just to do business. To me, that sounds like an opportunity to reimagine the domestic packaging supply chain.

Another individual wanted a custom 10- by 8-in. aluminum electrical enclosure. He was quoted $2,000 on a four-week lead for a single unit of a single SKU. That wasn’t going to meet time or cost limits, so he ended up buying a cheap product on Amazon (probably from China) and then modified it to meet his needs. This story got my attention. My company doesn’t make boxes; we are more of a blanking and bending component supplier for sheet metal, plate, and tube. We don’t currently make consumer-ready products. But I’m 100% confident that there’s an opportunity for a company to solve on-demand, custom metal enclosures through online-first ordering. Design your box, place your order, get it in a few days.

Yet another customer reported ordering wave springs from a manufacturer: "Lead-times were always months long, following up weeks after accepted PO and would routinely find out they didn’t actually have receipt or started on it. Makes no sense. Would barely ever respond to emails; always had to call."

I won’t include all the feedback I got on that X thread. Let’s just say some of it was colorful. The key takeaway is that in many industries and product categories, these kinds of experiences are the norm. In defense of the existing ecosystem, one underlying thread among all these complaints is that people are asking for services that businesses don’t habitually provide.

Indicators of Opportunity

High-mix, low-volume manufacturing is a particular kind of business, and most companies that provide it seem to be structured for higher volumes and higher-touch service.

It’s hard to do both. Meanwhile, shops that don’t respond might not be a good fit for the work, may already be at capacity, and might be unable to grow easily. That said, we are talking about pain being an indicator for opportunity. There are a lot of people in the world, and if you can make life easier for them, you can create and capture extraordinary value.

I opened this article talking about how hard manufacturing can be. It’s operationally difficult, highly competitive, expensive to scale, and often characterized by poor cash flow. But manufacturers can transform their businesses by noticing customer pain and fixing it, and exploring new business models that create good experiences where frustration and delay are the norm. By serving underrepresented niches extremely well, manufacturers can avoid cutthroat margin competition, improve cash flow, and delight customers in the process. Now is the time. Services and products are being transformed all around us. We need more manufacturers to jump on board.
