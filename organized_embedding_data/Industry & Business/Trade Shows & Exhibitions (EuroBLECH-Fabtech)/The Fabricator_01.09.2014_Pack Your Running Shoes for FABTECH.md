Dispelling the Mystery Surrounding Manufacturing

Manufacturing operations have always been mysterious to the uninitiated. They are just big buildings with mostly unfamiliar names on them and relatively few windows, so most people just drive by them every day without a clue as to what goes on inside. If they give it any thought at all, they might assume that inside is an inferno of noisy machines like something out of a 1950s newsreel.

The fact that everything they touch and interact with had to be made somewhere rarely dawns on anyone. The mysterious nature of manufacturing can be a problem on a variety of fronts. Young people don’t know that they can work there and build a solid, family-supporting career for themselves; the local population doesn’t understand why a little traffic congestion around the plant is worth it; and the local politicians don’t understand why they need to be mindful of their taxes and regulations.

When I was a kid I toured a lot of manufacturers. My dad was an engineer and fascinated with how machines worked. Every electronic device in our house was from a Heathkit

®

— he made them all himself so he would know exactly how they worked. On vacations we rambled all over the place on the way to visit family, detouring at every opportunity if there was some manufacturing place we could tour. My favorites were a place that manufactured telescopes and the company that made washing machines. AstroPhysics had just opened in Rockford, Ill., and it was on one of the last family trips we ever took that we visited Maytag, which had been building its appliances in Iowa for years.

Manufacturing Day

is coming up on Oct. 3, and every maker of things across North America has been invited to dispel some of that mystery. Here is an opportunity to replace it with awe and excitement — to show a whole new generation of kids how cool these machines are — and an opportunity to show their parents the kind of work that goes on in our manufacturing and fabricating shops.

Those in manufacturing are proud of what they do and what they make, and now is the time to share that pride. The U.S. is back where it belongs as a nation of makers, and that is something we can all be proud of. To continue that rise, we all need to show what we do.

Dr. Chris Kuehl FMA Economic Analyst
