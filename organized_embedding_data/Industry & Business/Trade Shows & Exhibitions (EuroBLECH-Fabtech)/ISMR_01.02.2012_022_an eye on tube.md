# AN EYE ON TUBE

[TARİH: 01.02.2012 ISMR]

ISMR previews the Tube Duesseldorf show, which also incorporates wire.

ISMR “At around 13 million tons, European SAYS: steel tube manufacturers achieved an increase of 13 percent in 2010 compared to the previous year”

Together, the two exhibitions, Wire 2012 and Tube 2012, cover more than 100,000 square metres of display area for the first time. Since 1988, the trade fairs have been held jointly every two years at the Düsseldorf Exhibition Centre in Germany.

Approximately 1,142 exhibitors from 47 countries have already booked a display area of over 56,000 square metres. Today, 47,000 square metres have already been booked for the Tube exhibition alone. So far, 918 companies from 44 countries have registered.

Tube occupies Halls 1 through 7a. Tube accessories can be found in Halls 1 and 2, whilst the tube trade and tube manufacturing are in Halls 2, 3, 4, and 7. Metal forming occupies Hall 5. Tube processing machines are presented in Halls 6 and 7a. Parts of Hall 7a are also dedicated to machinery and systems.

"On display will be the entire product and service range, from tube manufacturing via tube processing to tube machining. This spans raw materials, tubes and accessories, machinery for the production of tubes, used machines, process technology tools and auxiliary materials, measurement and control technology plus test engineering. Pipelines and OCTG technology, profiles and profile technology, control technology and special areas such as warehouse automation as well as monitoring and control systems complement this," the organiser told ISMR.

## Steel tube on course for growth

"Following the economic crisis in 2009, the steel tube and pipe industry has noticeably benefited from the economic upturn in 2010. Manufacturers have been able to increase utilisation of their production capacities – however, rises in raw materials prices and energy costs had a dampening effect on revenues. On a worldwide scale, the production of seamless steel tubes increased particularly strongly in 2010, but the welded tube sector also registered growth. The driving force behind this development was once again the energy sector," said the organiser.

"With an increase in worldwide production of 13 per cent to around 124 million tons in 2010, the steel tube industry was able to leave the previous year's slump behind it more quickly than expected. The remarkable aspect of this is that, after only one year, the record results of 2007 and 2008 were achieved once again. A large part in this development was once again played by Chinese steel tube manufacturers. With an increase in production of 9.5 per cent to 58 million tons in 2010, they reached a global market share of 46 per cent. In the rest of the world, compared to 2009, production even increased by as much as 17 per cent to 67 million tons. This strong growth in percentage terms should, however, not disguise the fact that this was, after all, clearly below the record value of the 2008 boom year (almost 80 million tons)."

Compared to 2009, all tube segments were able to increase in 2010, even if at varying rates. The highest growth was registered by the seamless steel tubes segment with a worldwide production increase of 19 per cent to around 39 million tons. The recovery in demand for small welded tubes (up to 406-millimetre external diameter) and large welded tubes was smaller, with increases of 11 per cent for both. With well over 60 million tons, the small welded pipes and tubes market segment was by far the largest product sector ahead of seamless tubes with around 39 million tons.

The driving force behind this trend was once again the energy sector, which as a result of the renewed increase in oil prices, attracted increased investments. The increased demand from the automobile manufacturing sector, coupled with the recovery in the mechanical engineering sector later in the year, contributed to growth. At around 13 million tons, European steel tube manufacturers achieved an increase of 13 per cent in 2010 compared to the previous year, which corresponded to the average worldwide growth rate.
