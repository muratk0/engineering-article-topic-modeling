# What I learned on my trip to Euroblech

[TARİH: 01.12.2024 The Fabricator]

Editor’s Corner

As the metal fabricating industry grows, the world gets a little smaller

Dan Davis

F

ollowing the close of FABTECH 2024, where almost 30,000 attendees showed up in Orlando, Fla., to check out the latest in metal fabricating technology, I got on a plane to visit arguably the most international of sheet metal technology tradeshows, EuroBLECH 2024 in Hannover, Germany. It turned out to be the

wurst

of times.

Actually, having a currywurst at the trade fair is an attempt to immerse myself into the German culture. It’s a lunchtime favorite of the German working class, becoming popular in the years after World War II. In fact, Volkswagen has its own branded currywurst, which for years has been offered in cafeterias to its workers.

While selling currywurst in Germany is good business, manufacturing in the country hasn’t been as hot. That’s a major issue when manufacturing represents almost 20% of the country’s overall gross domestic product (GDP). There’s even been talk of the aforementioned Volkswagen perhaps closing some of its factories in Germany to help offset declining vehicle sales.

Germany is not alone, though. In fact, the economies of the European Union have been struggling for a while now. According to the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development, real GDP for the Eurozone as of Q2 2024 has only grown 4.2% since Q4 2019, before the pandemic. (It’s only 0.2% for Germany.) Meanwhile, the U.S. GDP has grown 10.7% in that same time period.

Needless to say, people are looking for the EU and the manufacturing economy to bounce back. But even against the backdrop of slow economic times in Europe, nearly 39,000 visitors from 114 countries walked the fair grounds for the four-day EuroBLECH. The metal fabricating visitors were there to see technologies that can help them be more competitive, which is something with which metal fabricators in North America can identify.

At one time, EuroBLECH acted as sort of a launching point for metal fabricating technologies that would eventually work their way to the rest of the world. It made sense as the tradeshow was home base for key Germany-based machine tool companies, and European manufacturing typically was dependent on automation to make the most of tight shop floors and expensive labor.

Today, however, a metal fabricator is just as likely to see a world debut for a machine tool at FABTECH. After all, the North American manufacturing scene is hopping, and manufacturers and job shops are just as thirsty for automation as their European counterparts.

With that in mind, here are three observations that jumped out at me during the show:

1. European machine tool vendors have developed their own equipment and strategies to take on the proliferation of metal fabricating machinery from China. For example, LVD debuted its LaserTwo laser cutting machine at EuroBLECH. The laser, which the company calls "cost competitive," comes in three configurations: a single-bed machine without protective covers (available only for non-CE markets); a machine with only local covers and light guards over the laser power source; a laser with a full enclosure and an integrated shuttle table. The first configuration is a direct counter to laser cutting machines being marketed and sold in countries where the safety and health of workers aren’t top concerns. Other European machine tool builders are offering up similar cost-effective machines or are quietly working with Chinese manufacturers that are making equipment to European standards but are sold under another brand.

2. Digital connectivity has evolved beyond marketing hype, and metal fabricators now are actually asking, and even demanding, this type of functionality. Even in Europe, both metal fabricating companies and machine tool manufacturers struggle to fill job openings. That only makes machinery uptime more critical because automation plays an important role in helping shops to produce parts and make on-time deliveries. If something goes wrong with the machinery, the odds of a machine technician being available immediately to fix the equipment are slim. Having the ability to troubleshoot machines via the internet can help techs get the machines up and running in a quick timeframe. Remote diagnostics can help to alert metal fabricators that the machine might be on the verge of a breakdown and needs attention.

3. The arms race associated with laser power shows no signs of slowing down. Cutlite Penta had a 50-kW laser cutting machine, which was heading to Cupples’ J&J in Jackson, Tenn. Another manufacturer had a 60-kW machine, and another had a sign for a 150,000-kW model. Where does it stop? Obviously, most metal fabricators don’t need that much cutting speed or power to cut thick materials, but there appears to be interested parties out there.

According to the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development, real GDP for the Eurozone as of Q2 2024 has only grown 4.2% since Q4 2019, before the pandemic. (It’s only 0.2% for Germany.) Meanwhile, the U.S. GDP has grown 10.7% in that same time period.

The metal fabricating industry finds itself in the midst of great technological change, even if it might not be as obvious as the shift from punching machines to laser cutting. The velocity of information transfer, speed of cutting and bending machinery, and automated material handling are helping fabricators to decrease lead times and reduce the cost of parts.

The big difference nowadays is that metal fabricators from around the world are facing similar challenges. As the pace of manufacturing quickens, the world grows smaller.
