2015,

like so many other years, dawns with all the usual conjecture regarding what might happen next. The fact is that 2014 was not all that awful and actually performed far better than 2013; in some respects, it actually beat expectations.

As we bid 2014 a fond farewell, we acknowledge that it was a year that offered solid job growth, GDP expansion over the last two quarters that was as good as anything seen since before the recession, and a marked improvement in some of the measures used to assess the state of the economy. The Purchasing Managers Index has been close to the 60 mark, and there have been improvements in capacity utilization, industrial production, durable goods orders, and overall factory orders. Not that these improvements have been startling and they certainly havenât been enough to undo the damage created by the recession, but everything is certainly trending in the right direction. Does that suggest that 2015 will be better and that recovery has some real legs this time? To go out on a limb â it would seem so.

Several factors will contribute to the success or failure of 2015, as this will most certainly be a year of decision on key economic issues. At the top of that list will be the Federal Reserve Boardâs decision on what to do with interest rates. Right behind that decision will be discussion regarding the value of the dollar. The U.S. is an export-centered nation, and no sector is more attuned to overseas demand than manufacturing.

This will be a critical year as far as the labor force is concerned â everything from immigration to minimum wage, as well as real efforts to boost training. The big question will be whether the growth rates of the last few quarters will be matched this year, and much will depend on what is going on in the other global economies. This will not be a year focused on an election as was the case in 2014, but given the open presidential race in 2016, a lot of political maneuvering will have an impact on the progress of the economy. The most important decision to be made this year is whether interest rates will rise and by how much. Thus far the Federal Reserve Bankâs comments have been less than instructive, as there is still intense debate within the ranks of its members. The hawks have been demanding that rates rise for the past two years (or longer), and the doves assert that the economy is in no shape to handle higher rates. The latest comments have come from Vice Chairman Stanley Fischer, who has tried to give some structure to the conversation. The point he made was that decisions on the Fed funds rate will be dataâdriven, which means that close attention will be paid to the labor market as well as to signs of inflation.

The labor market has improved a little, but the unemployment rate is still far from healthy â especially when looking at the U-6 measure that includes the discouraged worker and the involuntarily part-time employee. The inflation situation will be discussed later, but from the Fedâs perspective, there is no imminent danger, which tends to favor a cautious approach to rates. In the end it would appear that interest rates will stay where they are through the majority of 2015 and that when they start to rise at the end of the year, the movement will be slight â perhaps a rise of 1 percent.

The factor that could throw this estimation off would be inflation. The current rate of inflation is far below what the Fed considers normal â 2 percent at the core. Remember that the rate of inflation that triggers Fed action doesnât include the volatile categories of food and fuel, and that rate has been staying at around 1.6 percent. There is more concern at the Fed regarding deflation at this point, although no real signals are evident to suggest that the consumer is about to collapse. The three factors that most affect inflation are generally money supply, wages, and commodities. The prices of most industrial commodities have fallen, and in the case of oil, prices have collapsed. The wage inflation that might have been expected by now has not happened either; a substantial pool of unemployed are still holding down wage growth, but more important, many businesses are simply not hiring at all as they are turning toward automation and technology to replace workers.

The money supply issue is perhaps the most important because a lot of cash is in the system. There has not been nearly the activity that the Fed expected, but that hardly means that this money will stay on the sidelines permanently. At this stage we do not see inflation rearing its ugly head in 2015. There may very well be little spurts from time to time, but nothing that affects the core rate significantly.

The value of the dollar will be a big issue throughout the year as the U.S. needs its export market now more than ever. The manufacturer in the U.S. is as dependent on overseas markets as sectors like agriculture and mining. The only part of the economy that is not living and dying with export levels has been the low-level service sector â but even it has been affected indirectly. The U.S. has been holding its own as far as exports are concerned, which has been an important part of overall growth. The problem is that some of that strength is related to the fact that the value of the dollar has been down for most of the last few years. The dollar is not weaker than some of the other currencies as they have all been tumbling to some degree, but it hasnât been getting stronger until recently. Now the U.S economy is powering along at a clip that others are envious of, and the dollar is responding. We think this process will continue and the dollar will gain against the euro, yen, and pound, as well as the other currencies, possibly by as much as 10 percent by the middle of the year. This will have a negative impact on exports and will encourage a lot more imports.

The overall growth of the U.S. economy is likely to be close to what it has been for the last few quarters. Even with the big drop recorded at the start of the year, the annual rate of growth in 2014 will be close to 3.5 percent, and this year the rate should be over 4 percent and perhaps as robust as 4.5 percent. There are always factors that can change that outcome, but for now the prospects look good.
