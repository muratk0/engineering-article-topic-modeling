Midyear Review

It is that time of year again — too deep into the thing to pretend that everything will work out and too far from the next year to be nostalgic about this one. It is the economic version of halftime. Your team may have a chance yet to win the game, but if they are behind 56 to zip at the half, it isn’t looking good. From an economic point of view, it looks a little like bad news 21 and good news 7. It isn’t impossible to stage a comeback, but it had better start quickly.

The bad news is that unemployment remains stubbornly high, industrial production is low, and capacity utilization is still not where it should be. The troubles in Europe and Asia have worsened, and that is dragging down export numbers. The Fed is still in a mood to stimulate, but investors are poised to send the markets crashing if they get a hint of a reversal in policy. Meanwhile, that continued loose monetary policy is still creating an inflation threat. It isn’t all that imminent according to the current signs, but the setup is there for a surge.

The good news is substantial, but it isn’t clear that it is robust enough to offset the bad. The housing sector is rebounding nicely, but other construction sectors remain pretty weak. Consumer confidence had a surge, but it is very dependent on the performance of the market and housing. It appears to be fairly fragile. There is some euphoria over the fact that the sequestration process was not any worse, but evidence shows that it is getting a little more meaningful every month.

The overall conclusion for the economy at midyear is that it could be far worse, and it could be far better. The real question is which way it will trend for the remainder of the year. The majority of the forecasts are calling for a steady but slow improvement for the rest of the year, but at the same time there are plenty of warnings. Several sectors of the economy could drag the whole thing down in the months to come — most notably the banking sector.
