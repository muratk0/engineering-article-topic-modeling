# Manufacturing is capitalism at its finest

[TARİH: 01.01.2025 The Fabricator]

Tech Talk

Customers win in our industry—elsewhere, not so much

CALEB CHAMBERLAIN

Adding barriers for customers preserves immediate revenue. Someone, somewhere stared at a spreadsheet and figured out that they could rake in millions by making their customers mad. Brilliant.

T

he other day I needed to remove a software license from our company Adobe account, and it was way harder than it should have been. You probably know the drill. It’s incredibly easy to add another license: log in, click the big "Buy" button, and now one more person in your company can use an Adobe product.

In contrast, the reverse process is deliberately difficult. Ideally, software licenses would be offered a la carte. Need more licenses? Add them. Need less? Remove them and prorate the unused subscription fee. This would be the most customer-friendly way of doing business, and particularly well suited for software, since software companies incur basically zero marginal cost to let one more person download and use a piece of software.

Instead, executives at Adobe figured out that they could collect more money by making it extremely easy to sign up and extremely difficult to cancel. Instead of a simple button click, I was required to reach out to Adobe support and talk to a person, who spent the better part of an hour arguing with me. "You can just keep the license in case someone else needs it in the future." "We can bundle more products instead of canceling the ones you don’t need." And so on. I finally stopped engaging and repeated, over and over, "The only acceptable resolution is to remove the unneeded licenses." That finally did the job.

Frustrated Customers With Few Choices

This short-sighted strategy is inevitable when companies start to focus on short-term profit over customer experience. It’s absolutely true that adding barriers preserves immediate revenue. Every retained subscription is money in the bank. Someone, somewhere stared at a spreadsheet and figured out that they could rake in millions by making their customers mad. Brilliant.

Of course, companies that engage in these kinds of anticonsumer practices end up universally hated. When markets work correctly, enraging your own customers isn’t a great long-term strategy. When markets don’t work correctly— like when a big company has a monopoly—it’s time for regulators to step in. Fortunately, they are noticing. In June 2024, the FTC issued a press release indicating that they are "taking action" against Adobe.

Said the FTC, "When consumers reach out to Adobe’s customer service to cancel, they encounter resistance and delay from Adobe representatives. Consumers also experience other obstacles, such as dropped calls and chats, and multiple transfers. Some consumers who thought they had successfully canceled their subscription reported that the company continued to charge them until discovering the charges on their credit card statements."

Huh. Yeah, that sounds familiar. Six months later, it’s still nearly impossible to cancel a subscription, but I suppose that these things take time.

Adobe isn’t the only offender. I’ve had similar experiences with telecom companies, VPN service providers, gym memberships, and ad platforms like Google. In fact, Google recently admitted in court that it secretly raised ad prices, euphemistically "shaking the mattresses" to hit growth targets while disguising it as bid competition. Google enjoys a monopoly on search traffic, one it’s maintained by paying tens of billions of dollars annually to remain the default search engine on other platforms. Its prolonged market dominance has led to a predictable, gradual erosion of service quality. Organic search results are now buried "below the fold" underneath paid ads. Organic results have been gamed to show affiliate marketing pages that also host paid ads, which Google profits from. And Google notoriously holds brand names hostage, extracting tolls on branded searches so that companies must pay to rank above the fold when people search for them.

Manufacturing Stands Apart

It’s all a predictable product of short-sighted thinking and plain old-fashioned greed. But do you know where this almost never happens? Manufacturing.

There are exceptions, no doubt. But as a manufacturer, can you imagine setting up your company to manipulate your own customers? Is there a world where making your customers mad is considered a good idea? Can you deliberately make your customers’ lives harder and expect them to stick around?

Obviously not. I believe that manufacturing is perfectly illustrative of how markets are supposed to work. For starters, customers have options. Lots of them. There are tens of thousands of manufacturers in the United States, spanning hundreds of industries, and serving millions of people and companies. Customers have a lot of mobility and power. If my company starts shipping poor-quality products or charging too much, my customers can just go elsewhere. That’s as it should be.

In manufacturing, fragmentation is a feature, not a problem. The outcome is a cutthroat market where great quality and customer experience are the table stakes, and manufacturers are highly motivated to become faster, more efficient, more price-competitive, and better able to meet customer and industry needs. It’s one of the rare markets where the phrase "build a better mousetrap" actually applies, both literally and figuratively. Incentives are perfectly aligned to produce customer delight. Again, as it should be.

Meanwhile, barriers to entry are low. No one starts with a seven-figure fiber laser with automation, of course, but someone with a garage, a welding torch, a right-angle grinder, and perhaps a small plasma cutting table can launch a fab business in no time. It’s relatively easy to start a new manufacturing business to address an unmet need, and people do it out of their garages all the time. Small, bootstrapped companies can’t do everything, but the fact that they exist and often succeed means that they are filling unmet needs.

Another delightful thing about manufacturing is that garage shops rarely stay that small. It’s inspiring to watch a small company deliver great service, reinvest in new equipment and more shop space, and level itself up year after year. Deliver value, reinvest to grow, deliver even better value at higher scale, reinvest … and so on. What was once a small garage shop now has that seven-figure, high-powered fiber laser with automation. Such growth and reinvestment encapsulate the best capitalism has to offer. Manufacturers benefit greatly from the snowball effect, and so do their customers.

Lest I sound too self-congratulatory, I feel I should point out that people are people. I don’t know that manufacturers are more honest, caring, or ethical than Big Tech monopolies as a rule. Charlie Munger said, "Show me the incentive, and I’ll show you the outcome." That applies here.

Publicly traded companies are pressured to grow, and the bigger they are and the more mature their markets, the more difficult it becomes. If those companies also happen to enjoy monopolies, and anticonsumer behavior doesn’t hurt the immediate bottom line … I imagine the temptation is hard to resist. And I imagine that Adobe’s executives were hired to do precisely what they are doing.

Healthy competition is the cure. Manufacturing has resisted consolidation for reasons we’ve discussed at length, and that dynamic has forced manufacturers to stay honest. Competition is a fantastic motivator, as it should be.

Manufacturers also tend to be privately held and founder controlled. They are interested in manufacturing for its own sake, and while creating wealth might be a motivator, it’s a natural consequence of providing real value. The "realest" value, for lack of a better phrase. You don’t get more real than making physical stuff. The contrast is stark. I feel proud to compete in a market where the best product truly does win and customer satisfaction reigns supreme.
