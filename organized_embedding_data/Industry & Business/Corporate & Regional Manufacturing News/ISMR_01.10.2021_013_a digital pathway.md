# A DIGITAL PATHWAY

[TARİH: 01.10.2021 ISMR]

FICEP S.p.A. (Varese, Italy) has become a leading supplier of structural steel and plate fabrication equipment. Established in 1930 and based in northern Italy, 50km north of Milan and 16km from the border with Switzerland, it focuses on the engineering and manufacturing of machine tools for the metalworking industry.

With 17 sales and service subsidiaries globally, the manufacturer employs 600 people worldwide (450 in Italy). It also runs a manufacturing plant in Bordeaux, France, for stand-alone products destined for the French/Northern African region.

ISMR sat down with its CEO, Ing. Christian Colombo, to discover his views on market and industry sector growth as well as the company's exciting new product, software and digital transformation developments.

FICEP's CEO, Ing. Christian Colombo, outlines his views on market and industry sector growth, as well as the company's new product, software and digital transformation developments.

## Continuous improvement

FICEP's portfolio of machinery includes steel section and plate fabrication equipment, automated handling and forging systems. The company manufactures a wide range of fabrication machines with options to punch, mark, drill, scribe, machine and shear or thermal cut. Its system simulation software allows it to evaluate potential layouts based on work force requirements, capabilities, throughput and production bottlenecks.

"We offer a variety of products that can fulfil the requirements of a wide range of industries. For example, a steel fabrication shop needs cutting technologies (plasma, oxy-fuel, sawing, shearing), which we can supply. We started to add milling and automatic handling functionality to our machines to make them full process lines. Then we made partnerships with complementary providers of products such as painting and welding etc. so that we could offer complete turnkey solutions. By doing this, we are able to continuously grow with our customer, offering different solutions to him throughout each stage of his development. We provide solutions, not just machines, and have done so for many years," Christian Colombo told ISMR.

"Machine tool builders tend to design, assemble and sell (directly or indirectly) their machines. We are a little different.... We have a large factory and can machine parts and components for our machine tools, which makes us more self-sufficient. This has been a major investment for us over the last ten years and we now have a variety of automated FMS (flexible manufacturing systems), in manned and unmanned shifts, supplying JIT components for our machine tools. We adopted lean manufacturing years ago to streamline our production and this has meant more agile processes across sales, manufacturing and services. It is a continuous improvement process and has become the engine that keeps the company alive. No company can afford to stop growing. The important thing is to grow at the right pace," he added.

## COVID-19 recovery

FICEP aims for more targeted market penetration as it opens new subsidiaries every two-three years around the world. Despite the ravages of the COVID-19 pandemic, global supply chain disruption and material shortages/delays, the company is back up to speed with new orders and Colombo anticipates steady turnover results for this year. He hopes that supply chain issues will be resolved by the end of the first quarter in 2022 and expects market recovery and solid growth for the future, pending no emergence of new COVID variants.

"During the pandemic, we were lucky that our projects were mainly just postponed until after Summer 2020. Very few were cancelled. We closed 2020 with roughly the same order levels as 2019. Our order book in 2021 was quite high so we were able to honour our deliveries and service returning business when demand rose again. In February/March this year, we saw similar order levels to those before the pandemic. Business has returned although there has been a change in the spread of geographical demand as regional markets recover at different paces," confirmed Colombo.

FICEP took strict internal health and safety measures to control infection such as staff temperature measurements; facility disinfection; social distancing rules; masks and control of staff flows in offices and workshops. Its engineers adhered to local quarantining measures, when travelling, so that they could service or install any machines required.

Although the manufacturer had previously run online demonstrations and video conferences, the COVID-19 restrictions really brought these to the fore and at a much higher level. It now launches webinars and live machine demonstrations from its workshop every two weeks, which attract hundreds of customers.

"We were able to prepare pre-sales work using vital online tools - this was particularly valuable for our subsidiaries. This also negates the need to travel so much. All our internal meetings also took place online which was better, quicker and more flexible for staff. We could record video meetings so that we could review what was discussed and any requirements/actions that flowed from the meetings," confirmed Christian Colombo.

"Now, we can support our sales teams, if they travel, on video calls to customers and involve the project managers right from the beginning, rather than just before the order is closed. This means better information flow, management and communication so these new online processes have brought a lot of value to us and our customers. We intend to continue with many of these practices, going forward," he added.

## Entering new markets

Since the 1950s and 1960s, FICEP has aimed for an international presence with a foot in every geographically important area. Its strategy is to supply different regions with products in line with their economic cycles. This also diversifies its markets and revenues and has been particularly important during the pandemic, especially during periods when its personnel could not travel.

The Italian manufacturer has also successfully transferred the technologies it uses for the structural steel fabrication industry (transmission towers, bridges, buildings etc.) to other industry sectors such as metal service centres; job shops; shipyards; steel agricultural machinery and, most recently, the wind tower industry.

"We started using plasma/oxycutting technologies in the eighties, but our strength was always our ability to combine different technologies on one single machine for different types of operations i.e. drilling, marking, scribing, cutting etc. Once we decided to move into parallel markets, such as job shops and service centres, we were asked just to deploy specific, for example, plasma cutting technologies with multi-heads/torches. Now, we are doing cross-transfer deployment of technologies between different industries," explained Colombo.

The offshore wind tower industry, which is currently booming worldwide, has been a key new sector for FICEP. Colombo saw an opportunity to use FICEP's automated processes for pre-rolling and post-rolling operations in the wind tower industry. This includes processes such as assembly, welding, shotblasted, painted, handling, milling, cutting etc. Boilermaking is another industry sector of interest to FICEP.

"We are also taking the same approach for customers who manufacture chassis for earthmoving equipment (e.g. truck loaders, pallet handlers, bucket elevators etc.). The chassis are made of very thick steel plate, with lots of drilled holes and some weld preparation. Our multi-technology machines handle these processes so that parts can go directly to assembly, with no further operations needed. This shortens lead times, makes processes more accurate and reduces costs. We are streamlining the value chain for customers, enabling them to bring down all their internal logistics costs (not just machine/capital asset costs)," Colombo explained.

## Digital transformation

A key differentiator for FICEP was its purchase, several years ago, of a French software company which specialises in manufacturing execution systems (MES) to control production operations. This helped propel FICEP on its path to providing total solutions to its customers and gave it a broader outlook on Industry 4.0, as well as a new digital transformation path, that has driven its machine development ever since.

"We saw real opportunity for Industry 4.0 and the chance to do something concrete with the data being gathered by our machines. This prompted us to buy the French MES software firm. Previously, we had focused on the internal value chain (delivery of material to machine, moving material, overall quality control, material sorting etc.). Now, we deploy Industry 4.0 to the maximum and are now involved with secondary operations like deburring so, even if we don't supply the actual deburring machine, we do supply the software to manage material movement to the next operation so that customer parts can be tracked etc.," explained Colombo.

"For example, we were the first to deploy a concept called 4D or 5D in structural steel fabrication. You can use a 3D model with different colours and information added to the model to show the different operations on a part. The 3D model therefore becomes a virtual communication tool for part traceability, cost, availability etc. These concepts bring multicommunication tools to another level, and we are bringing this to industry sectors like job shops etc.," he added.

FICEP has moved on from teleservices that connect to machines for troubleshooting etc. It now stores continuous streams of data from its machines and can process it, create reports and key indicators from it as well as development of the HMI. So, we also decided to develop a new customer-centric, knowledge management software platform, where we record the customer from the marketing stage (as a lead) until he becomes a prospect. This includes all pre-sales communications; feasibility studies; offers; quotations; time studies and technical and commercial aspects (down to order fulfilment; service; spare parts etc)," added Colombo. This was a huge software investment of two million euros for FICEP. In essence, it allows the manufacturer to control the customer's machine lifecycle, from beginning until end (scrap/ trade-off etc.). It can also be interfaced with PLM (Product Lifecycle Management) software.

"This also allows us to monitor remote interventions and problem resolutions so that everything is stored in a knowledge management system and can be shared across our organisation to spread experience and expertise. We want to bring this valuable knowledge to our service teams, as well as to our customers, so that we can all grow together. It is important that this data is stored properly and can be re-used," Colombo told ISMR.

"There are two major differences now. One is data availability: with the new HMI/customer centric system, all data will now be available and cloud/web-accessible from any device. The second difference is combining machine data with market/customer tracking data; you then have valuable, powerful knowledge that can be held in a knowledge management system," he commented.

## An eye on the future

"Of course, we continue to develop machines and technologies (such as plasma) that are faster, cleaner and more capable but, as a machine tool fabricator, our attention is also shifting to ancillary and complementary processes. A thermally treated operation means that the material must be treated (cleaned etc.) further downstream. So, our customers do not just want faster machine speeds and better performances (i.e, how many parts they can produce per hour) but now also focus on how many finished/ assembled products they can make in their factory," explained Colombo.

"Many companies talk about using AI in manufacturing. However, if you really want to make a difference, you need to invest in one or two Big Data experts who can properly mine data and generate usable data. Our machines combine different technologies which generate massive amounts of data. Invest in the right people who can understand the data and decide on the right algorithms," he added.

"Because we supply not just the machine, but also the infrastructure to support the machine asset and the customer, we realised that this project was much bigger than just the development of the HMI. We will be launching our new, redesigned, web-oriented and cloud-connected HMI at EMO Milano 2021 (4-9 October 2021)," confirmed Colombo. "During the pandemic, we redeveloped and redesigned this product so that the front-end can evolve faster with the new technologies that are coming out (javascript, Python, WPF)."

FICEP is also adding a lot of robotic applications to its machines for loading and unloading, in collaboration with partners, as automation is always in demand. The manufacturer makes its own CNCs and the trend towards Industry 4.0 drove it to develop its own cloud-connected, human-machine interfaces (HMIs) for its machines. It also creates value for customers as it shows them how to generate extra business from their machine assets. It flags when they need certain services such as retrofits, servicing, tooling, preventative maintenance and operator retraining. These key services, in an Industry-4.0 chain, help to keep FICEP machines up and running. We are working on VR and have now completed a digital twin for one of our machines.

This includes all ancillary and complementary functions in a factory (such as machine-to-machine process, reworking, weld preparation etc.). FICEP's customers are asking it to add quality control to a part so that when a part comes out of the machine, and is going to assembly, it must be in tolerance and not need rework etc. Their focus is on the entire value chain, and not just on the machine.

Colombo points out that this broader customer view also correlates with the changes driving its Industry 4.0 practices.

FICEP is already using Augmented Reality (AR) tools, connected to its CRM, so that its service teams can see instructions from its competence centre on their iPads, iPhones etc. for machine troubleshooting and maintenance purposes. That data is stored in a knowledge management system.

Christian Colombo sees future utility in Virtual Reality (VR) tools, for applications such as training, but not for the next five years (at least).

"It takes a lot of time and hard work to create 3D digital twin representations of machinery etc. on VR goggles, phones etc. so it is not an immediately efficient process. It is easier to use Augmented Reality for services, rather than VR, right now. However, we are working on VR and have now completed a digital twin for one of our machines but, of course, it will take a lot more time to be ready to support this properly." he told /SMR.

In the meantime, he hopes that robotics, automation, AR, VR, Big Data and AI in manufacturing will change the perception of 'dirty' heavy industry manufacturing in the minds of Gen Zs/Millennials.

"The young are the lifeblood of this industry. We have a training academy and offer apprenticeship programmes to attract young engineers. We partner with the polytechnics of Milan and Turin, as well as local schools, to bring in more young people. We are also trying to set up this culture in our subsidiaries. The quicker we can set up our knowledge management initiatives, the more this will help us to attract the interest of today's young engineers," he concluded.
