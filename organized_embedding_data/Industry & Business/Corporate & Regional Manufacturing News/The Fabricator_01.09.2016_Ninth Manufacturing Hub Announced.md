T

he Smart Manufacturing Leadership Coalition (SMLC), centered at UCLA, has been heavily involved in developing two manufacturing test beds, one of which is focused on machining. The group got a recent boost in its research efforts when it was tapped to receive a $14 million-a-year grant to become the ninth U.S. manufacturing hub.

The coalition, which eventually will be called the Smart Manufacturing Innovation Institute, is a consortium of nearly 200 partners from across academia, industry, and nonprofits—hailing from more than 30 states—hoping to spur advances in smart sensors and digital process controls that can radically improve the efficiency of U.S. advanced manufacturing. Two of those companies have been developing test beds, one in a hydrogen production plant (Praxair) and the other in a forging/machining/heat-treating manufacturing line operation (General Dynamics). Sensor-driven modeling, measurement, and simulation systems will be used in the development and operation of the test beds. They also will be key in managing energy consumption of the lines in real time.

Julie Tran, the SMLC program manager, said she expects the two proprietary test beds, developed at the cost of $11 million over three years, to be operational at the end of November 2016.

"The applications and toolkits … that are developed under the project are reusable and can be applied in other industry areas," she added.

By making the Smart Manufacturing Platform available to all potential users, program operators expect costs typically required to develop business case-specific control and management systems to be significantly reduced. The U.S. Department of Energy and other federal departments will supply $70 million over five years, which will be matched by private and academic donors.

OSHA Delays Controversial Anti-Retaliatory Provision

The Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA) pushed back to Nov. 1 enforcement of one aspect of its final rule issued last May to improve tracking of workplace injuries and illnesses. One of the provisions in that rule requires employers to inform employees of their right to report work-related injuries and illnesses free from retaliation and requires employers to give workers reasonable avenues for reporting those injuries. The provision was supposed to have gone into effect Aug. 1.

The new mandate proved controversial enough that business groups have filed a lawsuit in a Texas federal district court to stop the provision from going into effect. The National Association of Manufacturers and other groups filed a motion for preliminary injunction against OSHA. The emergency motion to the court protested OSHA’s prohibition, in the final rule, of "incident-based" employer safety incentive programs and routine mandatory postincident drug testing programs. In doing so, the business groups called them unlawfully "retaliatory." (The court had not ruled on this motion at the time this was written.)

The court filing may have had some role in OSHA’s decision to delay implementation for three months of the anti-retaliation provision. On July 13, Dorothy Dougherty, an OSHA deputy assistant secretary, published a memorandum to regional administrators delaying enforcement of these provisions until Nov. 1. She said the agency is currently developing educational materials for employers and enforcement guidance for their staffs.

The new anti-retaliatory provision has special resonance because on July 22 OSHA announced a settlement with U.S. Steel in a case the agency filed in February 2016. In that case, OSHA alleges the steelmaker retaliated against workers who reported injuries after the "event" or "incident." As part of the settlement, U.S. Steel agreed to establish an occupational illness and injury reporting policy that developed with input from OSHA. U.S. Steel did not admit fault or liability in connection with the allegations, nor was the agreement a confession of guilt or liability.

Two of those companies (that are to be a part of the Smart Manufacturing Innovation Institute) have been developing test beds, one in a hydrogen production plant (Praxair) and the other in a forging/machining/heat-treating manufacturing line operation (General Dynamics).
