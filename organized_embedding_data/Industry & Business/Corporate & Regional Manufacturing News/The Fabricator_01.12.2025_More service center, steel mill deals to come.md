ould service center consolidation and foreign investment in the domestic steel industry signal changing trends in the metals landscape?

In October, Chicago-based service center group Ryerson announced it was merging with Cleveland-based chain Olympic Steel, creating the second biggest service group in the U.S. And at the end of that month, South Korean steelmaker POSCO announced a "transformative partnership" with U.S. steelmaker Cleveland-Cliffs. South Korean news agencies reported an investment of more than $700 million and at least a 10% stake in the Cleveland-based company. Recall that POSCO is partnering with fellow South Korean company Hyundai to build a $5.8 billion electric arc furnace mill in Louisiana as well.

It’s not a bold statement to say there’s been a big shift in the metals space, and in globalization in general, since President Donald Trump took office last January. From Section 232 tariffs to reciprocal tariffs—even on USMCA partners Canada and Mexico—long-held norms have been upended. Service center consolidation and foreign investment in U.S. companies predates Trump 2.0 by a long while. Still, what kind of trends can we pick up coming out of these events, or any at all?

Well, I enlisted the help of both man and machine to find out.

The Mighty ORAC Says

First, I consulted with SMU parent company CRU’s proprietary artificial intelligence ORAC. I plugged in SMU’s articles on these topics and asked what trends to expect in the next six months based solely on those articles. Here are a few of the forecasts, and their likelihood of happening, according to ORAC.

Forecast No. 1:

More strategic alliances and cross-border deals will be made to satisfy origin-based trade rules. (Highly likely)

The Cleveland-Cliffs relationship with POSCO—explicitly timed with the U.S.-Korea trade agreement—signals a near-term wave of partnerships in which foreign mills use U.S. footprints to meet country-of-origin requirements. That will increase competitive pressure on purely domestic mills and change near-term flows of product into U.S. channels.

Forecast No. 2:

Acceleration of mergers and acquisitions among service centers is on the horizon. (Moderately to highly likely)

The scale rationale and $120 million synergy case from the Ryerson-Olympic Steel merger show that buyers and service centers will pursue deals to cut costs and diversify product mix while demand is weak. Expect at least one more deal or announced merger activity among regional service centers within six months.

Forecast No. 3:

Policy and trade signals will trigger heightened market reaction to any U.S. trade policy or U.S-Korea agreement implementation details. (Highly likely)

Tariffs and trade rules are already shifting behavior. Any clarification, waivers, or enforcement updates will quickly move flows and pricing in the short run.

An Analyst’s Observations

Next, I spoke with Josh Spoores, head of Steel Americas Analysis at CRU, to widen the picture.

Unsurprisingly, he also was bullish on the pace of service center consolidation.

"I think the momentum is just increasing in the consolidation of service centers," Spoores said. "But we’ve seen it for a while."

He pointed to acquisitions by Ryerson, Reliance, Kloecker, Russel, and Worthington as examples.

Additionally, he said that as some steelmakers diversify their businesses (such as SDI and aluminum, and Nucor and garage doors), it is not improbable that mills could jump deeper into the service center game.

From one point of view, it could solidify where their volume goes. He pointed out that some service centers do really well because they can buy from one mill one month and a different mill the next month.

"So if you have a steel mill that owns a service center, you might find that some of the profitability is no longer there," he said. "It depends on the exact service center in their operating model."

Driving this overall search is the current capacity growth in steelmaking. As the mills can produce more steel, they need some place to send it. Looking for steel-intensive businesses wherever they sit on the value chain "makes sense," and some services centers have done that as well, according to Spoores.

But mills want to add the right supply in the right place. They don’t want to have to compete on just "generic hot roll," he added. The mills want to compete on higher-value products, such as galvanized, painted, and processed steels.

The Role of Reshoring

A boom in U.S. manufacturing could definitely be a wild card for the pace of consolidation. But Spoores backs up and divides it into two questions:

Are we going to see any reshoring of businesses?

If we are, what mills are going to service that new demand?

He points to Nippon Steel’s recent "partnership" with Pittsburghbased U.S. Steel that closed in June. The Japanese steelmaker is on a growth trajectory and has entered the U.S. market with existing assets and customers. If a reshoring boom does materialize, it would leave Nippon Steel well-positioned to capitalize on it. Perhaps South Korea’s POSCO is looking to do the same thing with the Cliffs announcement, as well as the proposed Louisiana mill. However, Spoores noted that a lot depends on whether that manufacturing materializes on domestic soil.

"If we start to really see reshoring take place, we’re going to see rising demand for steel," Spoores said. That steel could come from current companies operating domestically, or it could come from foreign companies coming in to invest. Whether it ends up affecting North America or just the U.S. probably depends on how future trade deals evolve. (The USMCA agreement is up for review in 2026.)

"But if demand is rising, we’re going to see well-capitalized companies come in here and compete," Spoores said. "We will see more steel production and more investment."

Whether predicted by man or machine, rising demand is an encouraging forecast.
