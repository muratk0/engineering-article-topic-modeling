# INNOVATE TO ACCUMULATE

[TARİH: 01.09.2016 ISMR]

Terry Scuoler, CEO of the EEF (the UK manufacturers' organisation) discusses Brexit, UK manufacturing trends, the skills sector and the need for an industrial strategy with ISMR.

ISMR SAYS:

“Britain needs a long-term industrial strategy to anchor large, mobile investors and to support investment and growth in domestic supply chains“

ISMR: Please give a brief background to EEF and explain your mission and objectives?

TS: EEF is the manufacturers' organisation – we are the representative voice of UK manufacturing, with offices in London, Brussels, every English region and Wales. This year we celebrate 120 years of backing Britain's makers.

We represent companies of all sizes, from start-ups to multinationals, across engineering, manufacturing, technology and the wider industrial sector. Everything we do – from providing essential business support and training to championing manufacturing industry in the UK and the EU – is designed to help British manufacturers to compete, innovate and grow.

From HR and employment law, health and safety to environmental and productivity improvement, our advice, expertise and influence enables businesses to remain safe, compliant and future-focused.

ISMR: What are your views on the current UK business climate? What do you foresee for the next six months?

TS: Following the referendum, exchange rate volatility is by far the biggest concern at the moment. But other risks on our sector's radar are political uncertainty and increased costs. Following the vote, just 5% of firms have not identified any risks to their business in the year ahead.

While the subsequently weaker pound is an opportunity for over half of the firms we polled, a third of manufacturers have already seen input costs rise, with over half expecting this to be the case over the next six months. It is very much a mixed blessing.

All of our forecasts now point to manufacturing growth being subdued for the near future. This means that now, more than ever, the Government must step up to provide a stable business environment, scrap burdensome and costly policies and reinstate a long-term national industrial strategy.

ISMR: What is your view on the impact of Brexit on UK manufacturers and on the UK's industry/economy as a whole?

TS: The decision to leave the EU is not the outcome that many businesses wanted. Six in ten of our members wanted the UK to remain in the EU, while just 5% wanted the UK to exit.

Our first survey following the referendum vote shows that demand conditions in the next six months are expected to be weaker, with demand from UK customers the greatest area of concern. Almost three in ten firms (29%) are bracing themselves for UK orders to decline.

Confidence has been knocked. The referendum outcome was followed by the biggest drop and lowest scores in both business confidence and confidence in wider UK economic conditions since we started measuring two years ago (Q3 2014). Undoubtedly, this has been driven by the broad range of risks that manufacturers have identified for the year ahead.

ISMR: Where do you see the greatest challenges and opportunities?

TS: We are in a very different place this year compared to last. The Brexit vote has fired a starting gun on a period of political and economic uncertainty as the UK attempts to strike a deal with the EU and forge a new path in the world.

Manufacturers have fought their way back from the last recession but, following the referendum, we now expect our sector to remain subdued for some time. The challenge for firms in our sector will be to continue investing and innovating against this more uncertain and difficult backdrop.

With heightened awareness of risks, we need to ensure that opportunities also register. A number of large infrastructure projects, such as Heathrow airport expansion, are still awaiting decisions. We want to see the Government, as a priority, give these projects a green light and get them underway.

Not only does it open the door to valuable contracts and supply chain opportunities, but it sends out a powerful and positive signal to British business, and indeed to the wider population, that our Government is confident about investing in our economy. Not unhelpfully, it also sends out a clear message to the world that Britain is open for business.

ISMR: What would you urge as important next steps in the aftermath of Brexit?

TS: Government and business must now get on with the important job of determining what is in the best interests of the UK in forging a new relationship with the EU.

For manufacturers, there are three main priorities - maintain tariff and barrier-free access to the EU market for goods and services, ensure regulatory stability and continue to address the UK skills' gap.

Trade will be one of the most important measures of success for our sector. The negotiations must deliver an ambitious and comprehensive EU trade deal, unimpeded by tariffs and other barriers, which also ensures that UK manufacturers have access to the developing single market in services – an important growth area for our sector.

At the same time, the UK benefits from Free Trade Agreements between the EU and more than 50 partners. These must be rolled over with the conditions of trade maintained, without having to be renegotiated.

While trade is a priority, manufacturers will want to see a clear roadmap for how the process of disentanglement would be managed and consultation on the development of a comprehensive legislative review. As a key stakeholder, business must be allowed to play its part in helping to assess realistically what legislation could and should be retained.

When it comes to immigration, the Government will have to strike a difficult balancing act. As part of this, there must be recognition that skilled overseas workers make an important contribution to our economy and are part of the solution to our sector's unresolved skills' gap.

Manufacturers must be able to continue to access these skills (as well as have the freedom to send employees to other sites in Europe without incurring additional new costs or getting caught up in red tape). This has to be the tenet of any new immigration policy and we would also urge the Government to seek and consult with industry on creative solutions, such as a highly-skilled STEM fast-track system.

Whether or not we agree that Brexit was the right decision, with these building blocks in place at least businesses will again feel confident that the UK Government supports their ability to invest and grow.

ISMR: What is your stance on apprenticeships and training in manufacturing? Are there any initiatives in this area that you would like to highlight?

TS: Access to skills remains a significant ongoing challenge for our sector and, depending upon the outcome of the Government's Brexit negotiations, it could get worse.

While a key part of our role as the manufacturers' organisation is to work with Government and employers to ensure a supportive policy framework is in place, we also provide practical support through our apprentice training services.

We are investing heavily in this area. Our state-of-the-art technology training centre in Aston near Birmingham officially opened in April 2014 and has been a resounding success. The centre currently works in partnership with over 80 employers and we are now investing a further £5 million so that it can expand into a second site and increase the number of apprentices it trains from 250 to over 400 per year when fully operational.

This latest round of funding takes our total investment to £11 million. The new facility will house a wide range of areas devoted to developing technological skills, including robotics, electronics and rapid prototyping. It will boast an industry standard CAD (Computer Aided Design) suite, an automated factory and an industry-standard tool room.

The move will keep our centre at the forefront of new manufacturing and engineering technologies, ensuring that apprentices continue to learn both traditional and the most cutting-edge techniques. This will fully equip them for a sustainable and exciting career in modern industry.

The new centre will also host our soon-to-be-launched Leadership Institute and Manufacturing Academy, which will play a key role in ensuring that UK manufacturing stays at the forefront of 4IR. This investment is all about keeping our sector one step ahead as British manufacturers compete in an increasingly global and technologically advanced marketplace.

ISMR: Which issues are of prime importance for your members at the moment and how are you addressing these issues for them? What is your strategic and technical focus/ vision over the next few years?

TS: Britain needs a long-term industrial strategy to anchor large, mobile investors and to support investment and growth in domestic supply chains. It must provide a comprehensive and ambitious vision for how the industrial sector can play a full role in rebalancing the post-Brexit UK economy, reducing the trade deficit and driving improved productivity.

The fact that industrial strategy has been included in the new Secretary of State for Business's title and remit is a very positive sign. But we want to see it backed up with action and with a commitment to a long-term strategy, and not just a plan for the life span of this Government.

Policies supporting this aim include reinforcing the tax system for capital investment and higher levels of support for research and development through Innovate UK. Decisions on major infrastructure projects should not be further delayed, with critical UK supply chains able to invest with confidence in order to deliver major transport programmes that will support productivity growth in the UK.

One immediate step should be to delay the rollout of the Apprenticeship Levy. A delay will allow the Government to view the levy within the context of a broader long-term industrial strategy and work with industry to develop and improve its design in line with this. Secondly, it will protect manufacturers from additional costs at a time when they are fighting off uncertainty and other growth inhibitors in the aftermath of the referendum outcome.

Our sector is also on the cusp of a new industrial era – 4IR (the 4th industrial revolution) is being driven by rapid advances in technology and digitisation. An industrial strategy will help the manufacturing sector to capitalise on the immense opportunities to come from this, ensuring that manufacturers have access to the right skills, R&D and innovation support and also nurturing greater cross-sector collaboration – working with the tech sector for example, will be key.

ISMR: Which trends do you see developing in manufacturing/ sheet metal markets?

TS: UK manufacturers will be gearing up for the 4th industrial revolution (4IR) by prioritising investment in staff and management skills over software and machinery. It sounds counter-intuitive, but our research suggests that rapid advances in technology have left skills trailing in their wake, but manufacturers are alert to the danger and have quickly grasped the need to invest.

Over eight in ten (83%) say that they will need to invest in staff skills in order to adopt new advances in technology. At the same time, over six in ten (65%) recognise that they will need to invest in management skills as they set about boosting their company's knowledge and understanding at a strategic level.

Companies say that getting fit for 4IR will require investment in new machinery (51%) and software (38%). But, over a third of manufacturers (34%) also say they will need to invest in new staff. This indicates that firms are deeply aware that this latest industrial revolution will bring new skill requirements to the fore and that their success and ability to harness opportunities will hinge on bringing these skills into their business.

Despite this, firms are already investing in multi-purpose production lines (38%), capital equipment with embedded sensors and controls (33%) and cloud solutions (29%). Even the more futuristic technologies are on their radars - within the next five years, almost two in ten manufacturers will have invested in the 3D simulation of manufacturing processes (18%), augmented reality (17%) and fully autonomous robots (16%).

New technologies will start to change the way that factories look in a relatively short space of time. Within the next five years, almost three in ten manufacturers will be using rapid additive manufacturing techniques (3D printing) while those using cloud solutions will have leapt to 46%. Almost four in ten (38%) will have invested in capital equipment with embedded data analysis - Big Data will have started to become business as usual in factories across the UK.

This latest round of funding takes our total investment to £11 million.
