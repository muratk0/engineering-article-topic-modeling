# AN EYE ON RESEARCH

[TARİH: 01.09.2014 ISMR]

In the second of two articles, ISMR examines the strides that Europe’s largest applied research organisation has made in the manufacturing and production sector this year

Through their work on research and development projects commissioned by industrial firms and companies in the service sector, the Fraunhofer Institutes help to transform the results of research into real-life products, thus playing a central role in Germany’s

innovation process and that of Europe as a whole,” explained Professor Neugebauer, President of the Fraunhofer-Gesellschaft.

Contract research is Fraunhofer’s key activity. The Fraunhofer-Gesellschaft continued to grow in 2013 – the organisation’s total business volume increased by four per cent compared to the previous year, passing the two-billion-euro mark for the first time ever. In 2013, Fraunhofer spent a total of 235 million euros on improvements to its research infrastructure. It is also one of the most active and prolific sources of patent applications in Germany.

For the sheet metal and industrial production sectors, Fraunhofer has contributed valuable R&D expertise to help improve efficiencies, bring down costs and take advantage of new technology. A selection of its latest new initiatives in these sectors are highlighted here.

## Customised surface inspection

Fraunhofer researchers recently engineered a high precision modular inspection system, for the quality control of component surfaces, that can be adapted on a customer-specific basis and integrated into the production process.

“Every material substance has its own unique surface structure. To evaluate its quality, the testing procedure has to be gauged precisely to these specific properties,” explained Markus Rauhut of the Fraunhofer Institute for Industrial Mathematics ITWM. The size and shape of a component play a role as well as the desired resolution of the images.

Even though there is an array of surface inspection systems on the market already, these are only configured for specific materials and dimensions. Standard solutions cannot cover the entire spectrum of possible testing objectives. Researchers at ITWM have now closed this gap – scientists from Kaiserslautern engineered a modular inspection system, dubbed ‘MASC: Modular Algorithms for Surface InspeCtion’ which can be modified to customer-defined specifications.

“Our system is suitable for the most diverse materials – like metals, leather, textiles or paper – and covers a size range from tiny components for medical technology through to entire sheets of rawhide or ceiling panels,” Rauhut added. MASC-STeX for the inspection of cover plates and MASC-Dehnzelle for the inspection of expansion cells are already in practical use in the industry.

First, the surface of the workpiece is illuminated and scanned using multiple cameras set at a wide range of angles so that impact points or fractures, that are only visible from one side, can be picked up. With freeformed surfaces, regions are also recorded that are covered by curvatures or corners. The more complex the geometry, the more cameras are needed, as a rule.

For analysis of the images, the scientists have developed mathematical evaluation algorithms and, from this, built up a comprehensive software library. The basic version of the software alone comprises more than 300 algorithms that could also be combined, depending upon the testing task.

One particular challenge includes inspection procedures that require very high resolution. No surface is quite homogenous – they feature small scratches or fluctuations in brightness. For projects where resolution reaches the microscopic level, it becomes increasingly difficult to differentiate between anomalies in the surface texture and actual defects. The consequence – components that are actually free of defects are sorted out as defective.

“That is another advantage of our procedure – with the aid of our algorithms, we can refine the analysis to such a degree that incorrectly identified flaws are virtually eliminated,” Fraunhofer explained.

Once all testing parameters are set, the procedure is integrated into the production process with the customer. The cameras are either installed directly on the assembly line for this purpose, or applied using robots. When a defect is found, the production process automatically stops while the machine operator is notified. The detected defects are classified and the test object is divided into quality classes.
