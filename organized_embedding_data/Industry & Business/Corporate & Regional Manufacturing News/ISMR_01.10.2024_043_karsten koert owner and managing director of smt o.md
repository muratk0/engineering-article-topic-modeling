ISMR SAYS:

SMT, is a German, family-owned machine-tool manufacturer with a long history. The company was founded in 1896 and is based at Siegen in Germany.

It started manufacturing products for the steel industry such as steam engines, steam hammers, pumps and roll forming machines. The company quickly grew and then began developing heavy shears, bending and dishing machines, pickling machines and other equipment for the metalworking and automotive industries. Today, it is also a global specialist in cleaning and spray lubrication machines for the automotive industry (blank and coil preparation).

In 2005, Karsten Koert bought the company and turned it into a global supplier for the automotive, metalworking and steelprocessing industries. With over 70 employees (in design, electrical and controls engineering), a very deep vertical manufacturing range and a 4200m² production and assembly facility space, the company flexibly reacts to market changes and new product requirements from dynamic markets in Europe and worldwide.

Kasten Koert outlined global market trends, new customer requirements and his strategy for SMT's development, as well as the company's latest new product launches, to ISMR.

ISMR: Please highlight any notable successes and achievements for SMT over the past two-three years.

KK: Over the last couple of years, SMT has enjoyed great success with its dry-cleaning machine, the Cross Brush Cleaner CBC, for the automotive industry. In the forming sector, we have also developed new applications and very flexible machines for producing tank heads, as well as hemispherical tank segments, with 2D and 3D formed body panels and heads on one machine with the necessary manipulation and handling equipment.

Our strategy is to continuously adapt our technologies to each customer and increase our technical expertise. We always aim to be in the top three of potential suppliers to our customers, with our focus on reliable machinery and competitive solutions in a global, demanding marketplace.

ISMR: What are your views on the current global business climate for sheet metal professionals?

KK: SMT manufactures products for a defined group of customers and these customers' requests are almost constantly in demand, so that a continuous requirement exists. Our great strength is a main product line that can be adjusted, within a short time frame, to the customer's specification and requirements.

In the global market, we currently see great demand for the metal forming and production of tank and vessel heads, as well as 2D or 3D forming of plates and segments in sequential production, for the energy market and in the shipbuilding industry. These markets are growing, due to the investment in energy storage and energy transport in the broadest sense worldwide. This applies to new machinery as well as to retrofit projects.

SMT can cover these demands with its portfolio on dishing and segment forming presses as well as on the engineering of complex production lines.

In press automation, the sales market for our own products (like the blankwasher, dry cleaner and spray lubrication systems) does not really depend upon sales of motor vehicles.

Some OEMs in the United States are also building new plants. These are all SMT's long-term customers. ISMR: What, in your view, are important industry trends and challenges? How are you responding to these?

KK: It is a real challenge for us to find suitable and qualified personnel to expand with and match requirements on different projects. We are always looking to hire experienced and skilled personnel with the relevant machinebuilding experience. We also see, on the customer side, longer periods of decisionmaking and planning horizons.

We keep a sharp eye on global developments and requests for new trends and technologies that are part of customer requirements. As far as I can see, over the last two-three years, specific customer requirements and trends are more focused on maintenance-friendly (or even maintenanceless) equipment that is fast and flexible, but also very robust. This is, of course, already part of our philosophy and we have made machine upgrades to match these requirements.

ISMR: Which areas of growth/opportunities can you highlight?

KK: All our product lines have great potential now and in the future. Growth is not the main aim for us; we prefer strong and continuous utilisation of our technologies. Growth will then follow in some areas.

In the automotive sector, we have identified the trend of using more and more lightweight materials, such as aluminium for the outer body. This requires new or updated equipment features on our cleaning and spray lubrication machines, like the hot steam cleaning device for the brush belts and integration of additional tank and filter stations for two or more different spray oils, which we can integrate onto new machines or in retrofits of current machines.

ISMR: Which issues are of prime importance for your customers and how are you addressing these issues for them?

KK: Knowledge, experience and training is decreasing. Our aim is to provide machines with almost a simplified operating experience and less 'sensitive' machine equipment. For the customer, the equipment must be reliable, robust and very flexible to match different production scenarios. We can help customers with our experience and knowledge of the machines in production and we offer our service technicians for support.

So, in short, the answer is high quality levels, realistic delivery times and good service.

ISMR: Are there any products/technologies/ services that you have released or are releasing this year (including at EuroBLECH in Hanover) that you would like to discuss?

KK: This year at EuroBLECH, we will showcase a dry-cleaning machine, the SMT Cross Brush Cleaner CBC 30-R, on our booth. This machine is the latest updated model of our very successful line of dry cleaners. The cross brush cleaner is used where blanks and coils with light-to-medium surface dirt must be cleaned before the forming process or further processing. Our cross brush cleaner features a compact and maintenance-friendly design as well as a high degree of automation.

For the first time in public, we will also launch our Hot Steam Cleaning Unit as an integral part of the dry cleaner for modern production and high-quality cleaning results.

## CALCULATING THE CARBON FOOTPRINT

Today's automotive OEMs are placing even greater emphasis on energy efficiency and sustainability for their products. With the global drive towards netzero, designers are looking to optimise the fuel efficiency of their vehicles to keep the carbon footprint as low as possible. One important way to do this is to reduce the weight of the vehicle's structure without compromising on safety or performance. This calls for the use of lightweight materials, combined with innovative design and construction methods.

But how can automotive OEMs calculate the true carbon footprint of the materials that they use in vehicles? If they are using lightweight materials in vehicle design, then which materials could be used to truly lower the carbon footprint of a vehicle?

To find an answer to these questions, ISMR took part in a roundtable discussion on 11 September 2024 at the Engine Rooms in East Finchley, London (UK), hosted by stainless-steel provider, Outokumpu, and its automotive specialists.

The event focused on the carbon footprint of stainless steel in automotive production and included the corrosion resistance, high strength and energy absorption capabilities offered by the latest generations of specialised stainless-steel grades.

## Product carbon footprint (PCF) cradle to gate

## Calculating the carbon footprint

A key area for debate was Outokumpu's latest research to evaluate the carbon footprint of a real-world vehicle component during both production and use.

"A first-of-its-kind investigation by us and researchers at FKA Aachen has shown stainless steel to have the world's lowest carbon footprint compared to standard carbon steel and aluminium, when considering both the production and use phases," announced Outokumpu at the roundtable discussions.

“The results are counter-intuitive because, while the natural assumption is that aluminium is the best material for reducing carbon footprint as it allows the construction of energy-efficient vehicles, that benefit is far outweighed by the energy needed to produce it. So stainless steel, with its inherently low carbon footprint due to production based mainly on recycled scrap, emerges as the clear winner," Outokumpu explained.

This new research takes into account the ISO 14040 environmental management standard which provides a framework to assess the CO₂ emissions of products and raw materials. It breaks down their carbon footprint into three scopes:

■Scope 1 covers direct emissions from a producer's own operations. For stainless steel, that could include burning of fossil fuels to heat furnaces. Addressing coke and fuel use has a significant impact on Scope 1 emissions.

■Scope 2 covers indirect emissions. These arise from the generation of electricity used on production sites.

■Scope 3 covers indirect emissions from the supply chain such as the use of raw materials and transport. This is where particular care is needed as not all material producers provide data under Scope 3. The three most important raw materials amount to about 66 % of our value chain emissions.

"Variations in reporting can make it difficult to compare the carbon footprint of materials from different suppliers. While two products can appear identical in terms of composition and mechanical dimensions, their carbon footprints can be significantly different. For example, steel mills often take differing approaches to energy efficiency and procurement of low carbon energy for their processes, as well as sourcing low carbon raw materials," explained Outokumpu.

The challenge for automotive designers is that while a growing number of manufacturers strive to publish data on the carbon footprint of their materials, there is no standard on how it should be calculated (especially in terms of covering Scopes 1, 2 and 3). It is therefore hard to ensure that 'like' is always compared with 'like'.

"Another challenge is that carbon reporting varies between suppliers and countries. Not all reporting methods consider carbon dioxide (CO₂) emissions from 'cradle to gate'. That makes it important to seek the right data so that 'like' is compared with 'like'," it continued.

There is also very little practical information on how the construction material impacts the overall global warming potential (GWP) of automotive components throughout their service life. This dilemma prompted Outokumpu and FKA, its Aachen-based research partner for the automotive industry, to collaborate on this project to evaluate the carbon footprint of a real-world component during both production and use.
