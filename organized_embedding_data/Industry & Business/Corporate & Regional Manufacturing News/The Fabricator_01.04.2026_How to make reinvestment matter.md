e all need to keep our corporate profits inside our corporations. We need to reinvest those profits in machines, equipment, factories, and people. If we do that, manufacturing in America will be stronger."

So said Troy Berg, president/CEO of Dane Manufacturing, during a panel discussion at the Fabricators and Manufacturers Association Annual Meeting. Fellow panelists Jag Reddy, president/CEO of MEC, and Kent Brown, president/CEO of O’Neal Manufacturing Services, agreed wholeheartedly. Brown described OMS’ recent software investment that should help push the metal fabrication giant into the ultra-quick-quoting space. And Reddy described MEC’s aggressive capex investment in the face of relentless demand from data centers and the industries supporting them.

Reinvest

has become the industry’s North Star in the turbulent waters of geopolitics and the overall economy—and a theme that threaded its way through the FMA Annual Meeting in late February in Clearwater Beach, Fla.

The theme went beyond just buying new equipment, though. The event covered looking outward to tariffs and other economic headwinds (like, say, a war), as well as looking inward to operational excellence, management techniques, and even specific strategies on interpersonal relations and communication. Reinvesting without looking, without deep consideration of all internal and external factors, is like throwing time and money at a challenge without first identifying the true problem and its root causes.

Regarding external factors, Omar Nashashibi, founder of lobbying firm Inside Beltway, named plenty, including the tariff roller coaster. The Supreme Court struck down much of the administration’s broad-based collection of tariffs, though Section 232 tariffs are still in place. The administration has found alternative tariff strategies that have been broadly reported, but less known are some of the new tariff proposals centered on manufacturing machinery.

Proposals have varied, from having a flat rate of 25% to charging different fees based on a machine’s weight. As Nashashibi told his audience, "How would this impact the cost of manufacturing in America? If the administration wants to grow manufacturing, why would it increase the cost of buying machines? [Lawmakers and government officials], when they walk through your shops, they see an overreliance on Japanese, German, Italian, and other equipment on the floor that’s not made in the United States."

He added that these and similar tariffs are being proposed as being supported by Section 232. "The president can declare virtually anything he wants as a national security risk.

"You all need a seat at the table here," Nashashibi continued, adding that targeted communication is critical. "We understand the administration’s need for targeted tariffs, but [regarding the tariff of machinery], how does that impact our growth potential as manufacturers based in the U.S.?"

Of course, tariff proposals are all subject to change, but the mere fact that such tariffs are being talked about is worthy of the industry’s attention. At the same time, fabricators need to cut through the noise and focus on what matters, a fact that applies not just to external factors like tariffs but also internal factors—and at their core is effective communication.

Jared Detter, a leadership development consultant at Bartell & Bartell, State College, Pa., brought up a good point during his presentation: A shop can pay its people well, spread knowledge, and be laser-focused on overall throughput. But if dysfunctional communication practices continue to fester, nothing moves forward and nothing improves.

What fosters effective communication, exactly? Many factors play a role, but a central one involves fact-based, information- and context-rich, adult-to-adult communication. Listening and empathy take center stage. Detter added that empathy doesn’t necessarily mean you agree with someone, only that you can imagine life in their shoes.

All that stands in stark contrast to what Detter referred to as "child mode" and "parent mode." Poorly behaved children whine about perceived injustices, and frustrated parents patronize ("How many times do I have to tell you?").

In a conference session across the hall, Josh Blankenship, director of business development at OuterBox, a Houston-based marketing consulting firm, showed how certain manufacturers communicate with specific, highly targeted audiences. While Detter focused on connection within a company, Blankenship described connecting with those outside the business, including potential customers. This includes not just smart website design and search engine optimization but also some incredibly innovative uses of AI, including chatbots that can help bring prospects farther down the sales path by answering specific, sometimes extraordinarily technical questions: This grade of aluminum needs a radius this tight and a flange this short—is this possible?

Still, all the slick web design and AI-driven functionality won’t work if the connection—the listening, the empathy—is lacking. That reality applies to co-workers, suppliers, customers, prospects, and (as Nashashibi knows well) the governmental powers that be. Good communication guides strategic reinvestment, which in turn can really move a metal fabricator forward.
