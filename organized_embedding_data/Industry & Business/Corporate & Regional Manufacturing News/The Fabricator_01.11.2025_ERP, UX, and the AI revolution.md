arlier this year, David Lechleitner was working furiously. He was pursuing a doctorate in business administration, and he didn’t want to be EBD—everything but dissertation. His focus: the implications of the user interface (UI) and user experience (UX) on enterprise resource planning (ERP) software. Specifically, he wanted to find out if ERP with a good UI and UX improved job satisfaction.

As founder and president of LevelUp Consulting in Minneapolis, Lechleitner has decades of experience in the ERP sector, especially with platforms that aim to serve job shops. Even with all his industry experience, he still had trouble wrapping his head around all the qualitative and quantitative data he collected. His professor had allowed the use of large language models (LLMs). To help him put the finishing touches on his dissertation, he used

Sider.ai

, a tool that combines multiple LLMs and other AI models. After being fed enough data, the AI tool could (if given permission to) start asking the user questions, including other ways it could help. After a week or so, the software gave a prompt he wasn’t expecting. "It asked, ‘Would you like me to create an ERP software application based on the concepts you’ve proven in your research?’ And you know what? It did. That’s utterly game-changing."

To be sure, the software wasn’t ready for launch, but the fact that AI could create a demo ERP, just from its experience interacting with Lechleitner and his data, might be a harbinger of how small and medium-sized businesses—including custom metal fabricators—will adopt and interact with software in the years to come.

"Get ready," Lechleitner said. "Things are changing at light speed."

ERPs and the Job Shop

Lechleitner’s research focused on both UI and UX. There’s a difference between the two. UI has to do with a software’s look and the way it organizes information and functionality. A much broader concept, UX deals with every interaction between the customer (in this case, the metal fabricator) and the software provider. The two are related, of course. Someone who deals with a clunky UI can grow frustrated and, hence, have a poor UX.

UX also involves a software provider’s response to requests about certain functions. And as Lechleitner explained, meeting functionality needs hasn’t been easy. A vessel shop will have different business needs than, say, a thingauge, precision sheet metal fabricator. Their unique natures make job shops impossible to pigeonhole, and this in part has shaped the history of the ERP platforms that serve them.

"In many cases, a customer wouldn’t buy an ERP platform until [the software provider] added a particular feature," Lechleitner said. "So now, you have all these features and functions thrown together, and you end up with a platform that’s complicated and unintuitive."

Break Down the Silos

Lechleitner emphasized that no one is to blame. The history of ERP, especially in job shops, is a story of good intentions with unintended consequences. ERP providers bend over backward to develop functions customers request, but excessive features hurt the UX. Shops take advantage of some features but never get around to using others.

Of course, a custom fabricator’s processes need to be solid, too. The issue lies in how companies analyze their business processes.

"When companies do their current-state analysis, they tend to look vertically," Lechleitner said. "Quoting, order entry, engineering, scheduling, and other functions operate in their own silos, and they have their own way of doing things."

If a fabricator implements ERP to satisfy the needs of individual silos, they end up with a software platform that serves certain departments well, but to the detriment to the whole. As a result, the velocity of work slows throughout the enterprise.

"The hand-offs are where the ball gets dropped," Lechleitner continued. "We’ve all heard the phrase, ‘Throwing the job over the wall.’ Quite often, there’s no one there to catch it. That’s why I’m a fan of the horizontal, cross-functional, quote-to-cash analysis."

Software and Human Behavior

"The greatest takeaway from my research is, we need to focus on the individual, what they feel when using the software," Lechleitner said. "That’s going to be almost more important than a software’s features and functions."

Guiding those feelings are, again, the UI and UX. If they improve, so will the employee’s workday. So, how do you improve the UX? Lechleitner named some common improvements, like better customer support—that is, how quickly and effectively an ERP provider can solve a customer problem. Another element involves software design that promotes the way adults absorb new information, especially kinesthetic learning.

"If we’re kinesthetic learners, we like to be hands-on," Lechleitner said. "We learn by doing." Here, UI directly impacts the UX. The better that UI adapts to the way a user learns and performs work, the better the UX.

Market Focus

Large or small, job shops are complex and extraordinarily diverse businesses; catering to them all doesn’t seem practical. Considering this, how can ERP providers effectively serve the market?

Focus can help. Instead of trying to be everything to every kind of job shop under the sun, certain ERP providers focus on specific job shop niches or operational methods. "They are laser focused on a specific job shop model," Lechleitner said, adding that such providers often work to collaborate with other platforms to develop an ecosystem, not unlike the app store on an iPhone.

Companies buy the ERP not because of all of its features and functions, but because it matches an organization’s overarching way of doing business. This might include the ecosystem the platform offers as well as how well the ERP "plays with others." If, say, a shop wants to integrate sensors to track utilization of certain machinery, it can use an application programming interface (API) to make it happen, without much fuss.

AI Changes Software Development

Imagine a custom fabricator uses an ERP with a broad ecosystem and great customer response.

Igor Borisenko/iStock/Getty Images Plus

Someone needs a specific function that the ERP provider doesn’t offer, and others within the ecosystem can’t help either. Here, AI-driven software development could give software users, including custom fabricators, more options than ever.

Wayne Byrne supported this assumption with a demonstration. The CEO and founder of tangle. io walked through the capabilities of Milo, the ERP platform’s coding agent, a technology that’s becoming ubiquitous in software development.

"There’s a new generation of coding agents now," Byrne said. "Instead of copying and pasting bits of code, you now have an agent build the entire system you need. It just goes and goes until it’s achieved its goal."

Byrne demonstrated how Milo refined a fabricator’s quoting system to incorporate various tariff markups. "You start with the quoting module within the ERP. Say you want a 40% markup for certain components. You just tell it [Milo] to make those customizations, and it moves fields around on the form, changes the logic, edits the templates, and does everything a human would do."

He demonstrated Milo at work, showing how the agent "takes control" of the screen. "It walks you through the changes it’s making, step by step. It shows how it’s incorporating code. The user can literally watch the AI customize the system in real time."

Byrne added that, for testing purposes, all these customizations can happen in a separate "virtual sandbox," a complete digital copy of the shop’s enterprise platform. Once proven, the feature can be rolled out to the shop.

Tangle.io

launched in 2021 as a low-code ERP, one that used the software equivalent of Lego building blocks to customize the system to suit users’ needs. The low-code aspect of the platform streamlined the customization process, but as Byrne explained, the AI coding agent has boosted customization capabilities even further. "The agent here almost feels like a consultant," he said. "You can converse with it, and then watch it carry out the changes you need."

He added that such intelligence can help fabricators make the most of available AI to streamline mundane processes. Consider the act of interpreting drawing PDFs during quoting. "Both Claude and ChatGPT have been really good at processing PDFs of CAD models, mainly because people have been using these AI tools for that purpose," Byrne said. "The more PDFs the AI models see, the better they get."

Time and resources remain a barrier, though, especially as PDF documents grow more complex, detailing hundreds of parts and myriad callouts. "There’s some prompting involved here, trying to explain to AI what you need from those PDFs. People on staff in the office aren’t going to sit around and experiment all day with an LLM. And you can’t necessarily trust everyone to get the prompts right all the time." Instead, coding agents can develop "idiot-proof wizards" that help them capture the needed data. This way, the agents themselves perform the detailed prompting in the background.

The same can apply to other aspects of software customization, everything from advanced scheduling algorithms to shop floor monitoring systems. Byrne described one manufacturer that invested in a few inexpensive sensors that could monitor machine uptime. The API connecting it to ERP was developed in about five minutes. "In this application, every sensor was about $150, and they literally put together a shop floor intelligence system for less than $1,000. You can then have the coding agent interrogate the data. And with that, you can start having deep conversations, like the effects of grouping specific parts together in a routing or costing trees for multilevel BOMs at different quantities of production."

New Kinds of Jobs

Lechleitner sees the job shop front office evolving in the coming years, especially as software automates previously manual tasks. Fewer people will spend their days on tedium, like cleaning up files or keying in data. More will take a holistic view.

"In the coming years, I think the role of business analyst will really take off in the job shop," he said. "Even at very small companies, someone will need to know how all the business processes fit together. With that, they’ll be able to, with a few clicks, actually create new [software] modules and user interfaces." The business analyst then will test the software, verify that the changes work, and manage the software improvement over time.

AI will make fabrication machinery more intelligent, but it also could utterly change how the industry adopts and develops software. No longer static until the next update, AI-driven software could adapt and automate to meet a custom metal fabricator’s changing needs.

Software companies will provide support, of course, which arguably will become more important as ERP platforms become more flexible. Regardless, there also will be nothing stopping fabricators from augmenting and refining software all on their own—without learning to write a line of code.
