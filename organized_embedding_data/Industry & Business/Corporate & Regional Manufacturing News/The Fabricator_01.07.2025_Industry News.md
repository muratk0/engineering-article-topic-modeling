Mitsubishi Logisnext Americas adds building, plans training facility in Houston

Material handling and automation system manufacturer Mitsubishi Logisnext Americas has completed a $20 million expansion at its Houston manufacturing campus with the opening of a 73,500-sq.-ft. electrification fabrication facility.

Construction was completed in spring 2024. Installation of advanced technologies, including powder painting and robotic welding equipment, took place throughout the rest of the year.

The plant will build the company’s Electric Class I and Class II products, including the pantograph reach truck, order picker, and stand-up counterbalance forklift.

The company also has announced plans to open a Customer Experience Center and training facility at its Houston campus in Q1 2026. Designed to deliver immersive, hands-on demonstrations, the new facility will allow customers and dealers to explore the company’s portfolio of material handling equipment and energy and connected technology offerings.

Capitol Engineering purchases 120,000-sq.-ft. facility

Metal fabrication company Capitol Engineering Co. has purchased an 11-acre, 120,000-sq.-ft. industrial facility in Phoenix that nearly triples the size of the company’s current operations there.

The new location includes a 6,000-sq.-ft. administration office, a 14,400-sq.-ft. flex building, and four production/manufacturing bays encompassing about 95,000 sq. ft.

"The new facility will not only help us meet our current workload but will support our growth plans by delivering a more modern and efficient workflow and additional equipment to support our team, operations, and capabilities,"said John Carney, president.

Capitol Engineering offers custom fabrication of structural steel, piping, ductwork, and tanks in carbon steel, stainless steel, aluminum, and other alloys.

TRUMPF expands its U.S. manufacturing footprint

The latest expansion of TRUMPF’s manufacturing facilities in Farmington, Conn., is a 55,800-sq.-ft. addition that houses the company’s newest Smart Factory, what company leaders call its automated fabricating systems that are tied to an automated storage and retrieval system.

In the midst of increased tariffs and bold political talk about rebuilding U.S. manufacturing might, TRUMPF formally commemorated the opening of the seventh expansion of its manufacturing operations in Farmington, Conn.—its 55,800-sq-ft. Smart Factory.

"This building is not just a manufacturing facility. It’s the next step in American manufacturing," said Lutz Labisch, president/CEO, TRUMPF Inc.

This $40 million investment in this digitally connected fabricating system is part of the company’s "local for local" strategy, according to Labisch. TRUMPF wants to manufacture machine tools in the geographic locations where it sells the tools, whether the U.S., Europe, or Asia, he said. TRUMPF has been manufacturing in Connecticut since 1974.

"The Farmington Smart Factory provides a blueprint that enables manufacturers to reshore production and remain cost-competitive while adapting to changing needs, overcoming skilled labor shortages, and meeting strict safety standards," said Burke Doar, TRUMPF’s executive vice president.

During the formal grand opening on May 20, attendees got to see the Smart Factory in action. A 700-slot Stopa material handing and storage system acts as the spine to the automated system. It not only feeds the different fabricating processes attached to it, but also provides temporary storage of work-in-process that might be moving from blanking to forming.

The Farmington Smart Factory, like the other 300 similar setups in North America, incorporates various blanking and forming machine tools. As it is currently set up, one side has an TruBend 5230 automated press brake with the company’s ToolMaster automated tooling changer; a TruMatic 5000 with automated parts sorting; and a TruLaser Center 7030 laser cutting machine, also with parts sorting capabilities. On the other side of the automated material handling and storage system is a TruLaser 5030 with a PartsMaster, which can feed a laser-cut skeleton directly to an operator for ergonomic breakout if the parts aren’t being fed back into the Stopa system; a TruBend Center panel bender; and a station with two TruBend press brakes for manual forming are located.

What is noteworthy about this Smart Factory concept, according to TRUMPF officials, is that it is also connected to a nearby powder coating line. Lidar is used to measure incoming parts to be powder coated, and the automated spray guns can adjust to accommodate spray patterns when those parts arrive.

Leaning on technology to keep the workforce safe

Technology was a big focus for attendees at the FMA Safety Conference.

People in the manufacturing community have a lot of love and skepticism when it comes to advanced technology. Just look at robots. They are more productive than humans for certain tasks, but they also are looked at as replacements for human workers. This is a case where both observations are rooted in fact.

Robots do take over certain tasks that were once done by humans, but those robotic installations also end up creating more job opportunities, whether they might be technicians or programmers. Will Healy of Teradyne Robotics told attendees at FMA’s 17th Annual Safety Conference in Elgin, Ill., on May 13 that the automotive industry added 80,000 robots to the shop floor between 2010 and 2021; during that same time period, the same industry added 230,000 employees. Robots make sense.

They also make the workplace safer, according to Healy, who pointed to the growth of his company’s collaborative robot applications. He said that Teradyne’s collaborative automation solutions company, Universal Robots, has about 100,000 cobot installations all over the world, with a lot of the growth being tied to their affordability and quick implementations when compared to typical industrial robots.

Surprisingly, he added that safety managers are often a roadblock to installing these cobots. They want to treat this type of automation investment like an industrial robot, which isn’t necessarily the case. The cobots are designed to work closely with humans, employing force limiting sensors, motion monitoring, and speed limiting settings to reduce the risk for injury.

Healy added that the growth in cobot adoption is likely to increase in the coming years, particularly as companies continue to fine-tune applications for welding, grinding, and forming. That trend is sure to make a large impact on the metals industry, where UR sells a quarter of its cobots, in the U.S.

Cobots aren’t the only thing being used to create a safer work environment in the metal fabricating industry. Artificial intelligence is playing a role as well.

Connor Kennedy, an account executive with Voxel, explained how his company’s AI-driven safety monitoring software is able to work with a manufacturing company’s closed-circuit television cameras to identify risky behaviors, which cause about 80% of workplace incidents. The AI is able to observe unsafe activities, such as people not wearing personal protective equipment or a forklift driver practicing poor driving techniques, and identify them as incidents that need review. A dashboard then collects and feeds these recorded incidents to managers or safety professionals who can use them as the basis for corrective action discussions.

Voxel

"Identifying risks and correcting behaviors early can eliminate accidents," Kennedy said.

He positioned the AI as more of a proactive solution for manufacturers. Whereas a lot of focus typically is on fatalities, lost-time injuries, and OSHA recordable incidents, the Voxel technology is able to zone in on near misses and unsafe conditions. It also gets smarter the more it’s used.

AI can be taught to recognize patterns captured on the shop floor via video or picture. The more AI sees such incidents, the better it becomes at identifying risky behavior.

"Ergonomics is the most common risk that our customers are seeing when using our technology," Kennedy said.

Now, that might not sound like an immediate issue, but repeated poor ergonomic practices can lead to lost-time injuries. Kennedy put the average cost for such a worker injury at $45,000 for a company.

In this case, the AI actually identifies the poor ergonomics, such as a worker bending straight down to pick up a load or leaning forward too much when reaching, by highlighting the worker. That way it’s easily identifiable when reviewed later.

"AI is just a tool. How you use it determines how effective it will be," Kennedy said.

Advanced technology can be used to reduce the risk of danger from overlooked areas as well. For example, how many shops have taken the time to learn about combustible dust? Every metal fabrication shop has to deal with it, but it might not have the needed safety devices on its dust collection systems to minimize the risk of property damage.

The National Fire Protection Association defines combustible dust as finely divided solid material that is 420 μm or smaller in diameter. In the metal fabricating world, this metal dust is generated by thermal cutting and welding activities. If this type of dust settled in a dust collection system where oxygen flow was plentiful, a spark from a dust collector motor or even static electricity generated when certain types of dust particles rub together can cause an explosion. Andrew Schonacher, executive vice president, RoboVent, said this type of blast is "much more violent" than a typical flash fire. He added that the average cost of a combustible dust incident for a company is $800,000.

"Everyone should get the dust produced in their shops tested to determine explosive potential," Schonacher said.

That type of testing could be done as part of an overall dust hazard analysis. This comprehensive survey of the manufacturing operations looks at the material to see how combustible it might be, the processes that produce the dust, how the dust is collected, where sparks might be created, and places to introduce controls and training to reduce the explosion risk. At the end of the analysis, an in-depth report with findings and recommendations is presented to the manufacturer.

If a shop is wondering if it might have a problem, Schonacher said management should try and answer these questions truthfully:

Is there a visible haze on the shop floor?

Does dust collect on flat surfaces?

Is there dust inside control panels?

Is the air unpleasant to breathe?

A "yes" to any of these questions could mean that the company is in desperate need of a dust hazard analysis.

When it comes to the control technology used to reduce the risk of combustible dust incidents, the approaches can be both practical and complex. For instance, a standard explosion vent is a simple metal panel designed to release the pressure of an explosion. The panel pops off when the pressure rises to unsafe levels inside the dust collector. Similarly, a flameless explosion vent performs the same pressure-relief function and also is designed to stop flames.

Other options help to reduce the risk even further. A rotary airlock, for example, can be installed between the dust collector filter chamber and the collection tray or bin. The airlock acts as a oneway valve, allowing dust to fall into the bin while preventing it from reentering the filter chamber, reducing the risk for collected dust adding more fuel to an explosion in the filter chamber. A highspeed abort gate, which can be installed on the outlet ducting of the dust collection system, is another option. It prevents flames and burning debris from a dust collector fire from entering the facility through the return air system.

"The dust collection system can be the No. 1 combustion risk if not designed properly," Schonacher said. MEC to acquire Accu-Fab

Mayville Engineering Co. announced its intent to acquire Accu-Fab LLC the Tuesday after Memorial Day. The deal is expected to close later this year.

Mayville Engineering Co., a contract metal fabricator that has topped The Fabricator’s FAB 40 list for more than a decade, is about to get even bigger.

On May 27, MEC announced a definitive agreement to buy Accu-Fab LLC, a major player in the critical power and data center infrastructure market, for $140.5 million, from Tide Rock, a California-based holding company. The deal is expected to close by the third quarter of this year.

"As we saw a line of sight to debt reduction in the past year, we activated our M&A pipeline and have been in conversations with a significant number of targets. We had three main goals: one, to diversify out of our existing end markets; two, to enter highly profitably end markets; and last but not least, enter end markets where we see long-term secular growth"—that is, growth independent of broader economic cycles.

During a May 27 investor conference call, Jag Reddy, MEC president and CEO, said that as the company has communicated with a number of potential acquisition targets, Accu-Fab LLC, with its significant presence in data centers and critical power infrastructure, stood out.

So said Jag Reddy, MEC president and CEO, who hosted an investor call on May 27. During it, he shared some sales data that show just how complementary Accu-Fab’s customer mix is. More than 60% of its sales come from the critical power and data center markets—in-demand sectors where MEC was looking to expand. In fact, Accu-Fab’s entire product mix, which also include a number of industrial electronics customers, has very little overlap with MEC’s customer portfolio, with its heavy presence in powersports and commercial vehicles. Once the acquisition closes, critical power and data centers will comprise about 10% of MEC’s overall sales.

MEC plans to retain all of Accu-Fab’s 250 employees. With locations in Raleigh, N.C., and Wheeling, Ill., Accu-Fab is highly focused on precision, light-gauge sheet metal fabrication. The two plants offer a combined manufacturing space of 200,000 square feet. As Reddy explained, Accu-Fab’s thin-gauge sheet metal capability expands and complements MEC’s existing fabrication expertise in heavier-gauge sheet metal, plate, and tube.
