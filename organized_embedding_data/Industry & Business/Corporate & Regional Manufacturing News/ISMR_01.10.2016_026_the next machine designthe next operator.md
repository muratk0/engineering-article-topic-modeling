# THE NEXT MACHINE DESIGNTHE NEXT OPERATOR

[TARİH: 01.10.2016 ISMR]

Swedish sheet metal contractor, Andrénverken, focuses on quality, versatility and productivity

Based in the small Swedish town of Smålandsstenar, sheet metal and press working subcontractor, Andrénverken, knows what works best to stay competitive and win business in a high-cost economy. As managing director, Johan Bredenfeldt, explained, 'keeping it local' is a good strategy.

"We only work with Swedish customers and that is deliberate. Our biggest customers have a policy that they only work with the contractors that are around them. It means short communication chains and rapid response times."

Andrénverken handles sheet metal processing, welding and assembly. Its customers demand smooth production processes and high delivery reliability.

## Keeping it local

However, 'local' is relative - Sweden is a big country, and it is as far to Andrénverken's customer, Luleå Generatorservice, as it is to Milan. It also takes two days by truck to deliver parts.

Another Andrénverken customer is Skeppshuts Guteri, which makes cast iron cookware just a few kilometres away. Its production manager, Erik van Dijk, puts the benefits of 'thinking local' in a nutshell: "Long distances and different cultures can cost more than it says on the price tag," he explained.

Andrénverken's biggest customer, however, is industrial lift truck manufacturer, Toyota Materials Handling. And, even if the volumes are not the same for Toyota's automotive business (10,000 parts per year rather than hundreds of thousands) the expectations and quality demands certainly are.....

Said Johan Bredenfeldt: "Just in this area, we have 200 competitors. They are the same size as we are and are doing the same things that we are doing. It's not easy. But all of us have one big customer – in our case, Toyota.

"It is hard to have more than one big customer because they tell us how they want us to work - we can't tell them. And, if you have two big customers, it's going to be very complicated because they will want different things."

Bredenfeldt told ISMR that working the Toyota way means a focus on Kaizen – continuous improvement. Daily shop floor meetings focus on identifying ways to do things better, reducing waste and minimising costs that do not add value.

"When you are supplying a large company like Toyota, delivering kits of parts to the production line on a just-in-time basis, you can't afford to get things wrong. If we fail to deliver on time, they have a big problem - and we have a bigger problem. We can't risk getting into the situation where they are missing parts for assembly," he explained.

"Our strength lies in the way we use the equipment, in our documented quality and in how we cooperate. Our customers require us to be able to take responsibility for several processing operations in the supply chain. And we do."

## Adding value and increasing competitiveness

Johan Bredenfeldt joined Andrénverken three years ago. His mission is to double the company's turnover.

As well as getting out into the market to win new customers, he has also instigated investment in new production systems to increase the company's capacity and production efficiency. These include an LVD ToolCell automated tool changing/bending system with automated tool set-up and CADMAN® programming software and a 4kW LVD Electra FL 3015 fibre laser with a Compact Tower (an automated load/unload system with a 6-pallet material storage). Both machines are in addition to an existing Axel CO2 laser system.

"We bought the Electra because it is designed to cut thin metal very quickly and 95% of what we cut is thin material. With the Electra, we can cut sheet of up to 5mm thick at twice the speed as we could before, so we are more competitive – and we are winning more business because of this," explained Johan Bredenfeldt.

"We chose an automated system so that we don't need to be there when the machine is cutting. We can leave it overnight, unmanned, so it is very cost-efficient. We can put at least a shift's worth of work in the tower. The driver for this is that wage costs here are high, so we want to have as few people as possible running the machines. It is also difficult for us to find skilled employees with the right training. Smålandsstenar is just a small town and the young people don't want to stay here. They want to live in the big cities like Gothenburg."

This makes it even more important for Andrénverken to exploit technology to the full - and integrated programming using LVD's CADMAN® software suite has made a big difference.

"The new laser made us more competitive, but it didn't really change the way we worked," added Bredenfeldt. "But the CADMAN software means that we are now processing work in a completely different way. The software automatically creates the program, tool set-up and bend sequence for us from the 3D CAD model."

Taking the technology to the next level, Andrénverken chose to combine integrated programming with the automated toolchanging capability of the ToolCell.

"It was the same philosophy as for the software. On small-volume batches, we are much more efficient and we can handle the work in a completely different way. In the past, it took from 30 minutes to an hour to change the tooling. Now, it takes perhaps five minutes and you get the program directly from the database. So, compared to programming on the machine and changing tools manually, we are saving hours. It is a revolution."

## An eye on quality

Andrénverken puts a lot of resources into tool-related manufacturing, since it is often the most competitive option. By manufacturing its own tools, it is very independent and highly flexible. It also has the capacity for non-toolrelated manufacturing, welding and joining inhouse and a supply chain that means that it can assume full responsibility as a total supplier.

"We have everything we need in our production plant for handling different types of sheet metal processing. Our mechanical equipment consists of edging presses, hydraulic presses, automatic presses, multioperation machines and robotic welding cells, as well as equipment for impulse welding, deburring, tumbling and laser cutting," it explained.

All the documentation that comes with a new item in its production is analysed and specified using PPAP (Production Part Approval Process) and MSA (Measurement Systems Analysis). A control measurement is performed for every delivery. Measurement values and deviation reports provide it with very valuable information that it can use in the construction of its own tools and in dialogue with its clients.

With FMEA (Failure Mode and Effect Analysis), it can identify possible errors that may arise during the production process.

This method gives it the opportunity to take preventative measures that can counteract any problems and minimise the risk of errors in production. The analysis work is run by a team of employees from different areas within the company. Andrénverken is certified under ISO 3834-2: 2005. The standard is an international quality management system for companies working with welding.

The company also has a rich and varied history. When Andrén Johansson's mechanical workshop was set up in Smålandsstenar in 1923, he manufactured pokers for cooking ranges, among other things. When Bengt Johansson took over the company in 1957, Andrén Verken AB was formed. The first subcontracted product that the company started to manufacture was a machine foot. This product still exists in its product range.

"Despite changes in the global economy, our company culture has proved to be vibrant. We never give up, even if things can get arduous from time to time. The commitment among our employees gives us the strength to manage our tasks, even in a tough climate. We have never advocated short-term gains at Andrénverken. We choose instead to focus on quality of life, corporate quality and long customer relationships. This is what gives us and our customers the most satisfaction," concluded Andrénverken. ■

INDUSTRY 4.0

FACTORY OF THE FUTURE

The fourth industrial revolution will be transformational with technologies such as augmented reality, robotics, Big Data and the Internet of Things changing production

With Industry 4.0, the physical world is merging with the virtual world...

In a vision of the future of manufacturing, the pervasive networking of people, things and machines will create completely new production environments. Manufacturers, researchers and governments are working together to explore and implement this vision for tomorrow's networked factory, which is embodied in the "Industry 4.0" concept. But what are the forces driving this development?

## Why the name "Industry 4.0"?

"The first Industrial Revolution was triggered by the introduction of the steam engine and the mechanisation of manual work in the 18th century, while electrified mass production drove the second Industrial Revolution in the early 20th century. The third followed in more recent decades as a result of the use of electronics and computer technology for manufacturing and production automation. The real and virtual worlds are now beginning to merge in production, which is why we're talking about 'Industrie 4.0' - the Siemens term for the fourth Industrial Revolution," German manufacturer, Siemens, told ISMR.

"Increasing digitisation and networking is changing the entire industrial production chain, and the volume of data worldwide is exploding. The total amount of data worldwide in 2005 was 130 exabytes, and that amount had grown to 462 exabytes by 2012. Experts expect the volume of data to grow to 14,996 exabytes by 2020 (roughly equivalent to about 15 trillion gigabytes).

To properly analyse and be able to use such huge volumes of data, we must first develop systems that enable us to understand their content. A precondition for this is to know how devices and systems function and which kinds of sensor and measurement technology can be used to access the most useful data."

Regardless of which expression is used, the idea is to reconcile the conflict between maximum efficiency and minimum cost (the driving forces of mass production) on the one hand, and maximum flexibility, customised offers and rapid product lifecycles on the other.

"In the future, the tool of the industrial age will no longer be the screwdriver but the tablet computer," claimed Eric Spiegel, CEO of Siemens USA.

The real and virtual worlds are now beginning to merge in production

Market researchers anticipate that, thanks to industrial automation, global sales in this area will increase from around 160 billion euros in 2013 to approximately 195 billion euros by 2018. German industry alone will invest approximately 40 billion euros annually in Industry 4.0 applications by 2020. In Germany, the industrial share of total economic output is already more than twice as high as in the UK, France or the USA. Digitisation, says Siemens, will essentially determine the success or failure of German industry.

## INDUSTRY 4.0

## Faster, more flexible and efficient production

As a result of Industry 4.0, in the future billions of machines, systems and sensors worldwide will communicate with each other and share information. This will not only enable companies to make production significantly more efficient, it will give them greater flexibility when it comes to tailoring production to meet market requirements.

Information technology, telecommunications and manufacturing are merging, as the means of production becomes increasingly autonomous. Yet it is impossible to say exactly what smart factories will look like in the future. Siemens suggest one possible scenario - in the factory of the future, the machines will organize themselves to a great extent, delivery chains will automatically assemble themselves and orders will transform directly into production information and flow into the production process. Yet people will remain essential in an Industry 4.0 world – as the creative leaders and thinkers who will use their intelligence to conceive all the processes and procedures in advance and who will write software to convey that information to machines.

Whether they involve digital planning methods (virtual reality), 3D printing or lightweight robots, new technologies for Industry 4.0 are already a reality.
