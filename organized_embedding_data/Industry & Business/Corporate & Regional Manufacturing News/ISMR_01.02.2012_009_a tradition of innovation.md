# A TRADITION OF INNOVATION

[TARİH: 01.02.2012 ISMR]

ISMR flew to Germany to visit TRUMPF's headquarters at Ditzingen

With sales totalling US $ 2.761 billion (€ 2.024 billion) and 8,550 employees, the TRUMPF Group ranks among the leading manufacturing companies worldwide. Its three business divisions are Machine Tools/Power Tools, Laser Technology/Electronics and Medical Technology. Its core business is machine tools for flexible sheet metal processing for punching and forming, laser processing and bending. For industrial lasers and laser systems, the company is a technological leader in the world market.

A constant focus on research and development as well as product and technology innovations, such as skeleton-free processing on punching machines, has kept the company at the fore of sheet metal expertise.

## An eye on the future

Dr. Nicola Leibinger-Kammüller, President and Chairwoman of TRUMPF’s Managing Board, is upbeat about TRUMPF’s future after one of the best business years the company has ever had. During the past fiscal year (ending 30 June 2011) the Ditzingen machine-tool producer saw an increase of 51 per cent, with sales rising by 683 million euros to 2.024 billion euros.

“We have an excellent business year behind us, with fifty-one per cent growth in sales – the highest growth rate in our company’s history. And with order receipts of 2.2 billion euros, we have set a new record there, too. This means that we are back at the same level as before the economic crisis. Within a single year, we made up for the losses we suffered in the preceding two years,” she told ISMR.

## FACE TO FACE

During the last fiscal year, TRUMPF invested more than ever before on innovation. Expenditure for research and development increased to € 158 million. The research and development quota was 7.8 per cent of sales.

One of the reasons for the company’s success, she believes, is the “German Wirtschaftswunder” ...

“The answer – or at least a part of the answer – is: ours is a family company,” she explained. “This means we can allow ourselves the luxury to think about the day after tomorrow. We think about the next generation, too and are not driven by quarterly figures and certainly not by profit warnings. Instead, we can afford to act in a way that is strategically adept, sustainable and appropriate for the long run. Let me give as an example the most recent major economic crisis, the one that started in 2008. In spite of higher labour costs in Germany, we did not lay anyone off. We did this out of a sense of responsibility to our own operations - because we knew we would be needing those same employees once the crisis had passed.

“We kept our expenditures for research and development high – at a level twice the average for our industry. And, in spite of a general hiring freeze during the crisis, we did recruit some engineers. These were recent graduates who were able to start out with us at special terms. That is another reason why – right after the crisis ended – we could go to market with new products and technologies. During the course of the slump, our family invested 75 million euros in the company, not because of pressure exerted by the banks, but rather as a signal, directed both inward and outward, that we believe in TRUMPF’s strengths.

“The outcome of all these steps – and of many others – was that we were able to profit from the worldwide growth that ensued immediately after the end of the crisis. That was made possible by our highly flexible production system. We were able to switch it from “crisis” to “vigorous expansion” practically overnight. We could ramp up production immediately, keep delivery times short and, in this way, acquire new market share,”

She is, however, keeping a wary eye on developments in the Eurozone and political discussions over the euro’s stability.

“The Euro crisis and the Greece problem now have to be solved quickly, efficiently and sustainably. Each week without any tangible result will only make markets more insecure,” she said.

## Sales and investments

Expressed in numbers, she confirms that sales in Germany, TRUMPF’s home market, grew by more than fifty per cent in the 2010/11 fiscal year. In Asian markets and particularly in China as well as in eastern Europe, it was also successful. Bucking the trend in another market, its business grew too in the USA, since its clientele there is strongly exportoriented and is profiting from the boom in Asia.

“Southern Europe’s markets are actually quite significant, Spain and Italy being two examples, but they are limping behind this trend,” she added. “We are hoping for an upturn, especially in Spain following the upcoming elections. Our plans are to pursue expansion in the growth markets and particularly in China. There, we are assuming continued vigorous expansion since the industrialization of the vast western regions in China has yet to take place. That is why we are doubling the capacities of our plant in Taicang, near Shanghai. The new structure being built there will be dedicated in February 2012. This will also be flanked by reinforcement and expansion of regional sourcing.”

TRUMPF is planning investments at many other locations, as well:

■ In Ditzingen, it is erecting two pre-fab buildings to quickly augment capacities for laser technology and mechanical engineering activities.

■ In Schramberg, in the Black Forest, a new development centre is now under construction.

■ In Hettingen, in Germany, it is building a new structure for final assembly of machine tools.

■ In Saalfeld, Thuringia, two additional bays are being erected for the medical systems subsidiary. Operating tables will be manufactured there.

■ In Grüsch, Switzerland, a new manufacturing building for marking lasers is being planned.

■ In Princeton, New Jersey (USA), a new clean room for diode production is now being set up.

“Why are we doing all this?” says Nicola Leibinger-Kammüller. “Because we believe that growth will prevail! It is true that we are expecting the business cycle to level off somewhat, but are still projecting a double-digit increase in sales.

“That is because we have the right products – high-efficiency products with which our customers can achieve advantages vis-à-vis their competitors. That was decisive for our clients during the downturn and that will continue to be crucial in the future.

“At the same time, we are continuing to put our faith in research and development. Some 1,200 employees are at work in these fields across the globe. This is in line with our objective of always being a step ahead of our competitors in terms of technology. We also provide our customers with direct consultancy on applications and custom development work, as well.”

## Laser in focus

ISMR also visited TRUMPF’s Laser Application Centre during its visit to Ditzingen. The concept – to develop solutions in laser technology for the customer and in close cooperation with the customer – is unusual. At present, TRUMPF has six such LACs – in Germany, the United States, Japan, China, Taiwan and Korea. ISMR’S visit to Schramberg was to see TRUMPF’s solid state laser plant, with a guided tour through the production area.

TRUMPF has introduced a method of directly connecting a marking laser to the Systems, Applications and Products (SAP) environment via a standard interface, allowing users to mark components from within SAP. Instead of printing onto a label that would then be stuck to the product or component, the TruMark directly and indelibly marks the product’s surface. This enables users to work from within the SAP system to apply company logos, model designations, codes and serial numbers to components using a laser without requiring any programming skills or additional software. The laser parameters required for a wide range of materials are included as part of the marking software package.

Taking an automotive analogy, the CO2 cutting machine is a versatile off-road vehicle, which, thanks to up to 8 kilowatts of power, is capable of high-quality steel cutting for sheet thicknesses from 0.5 to more than 30 millimetres – making it suitable for negotiating the rally challenge of quick design changes and short product runs that defines the workload of many suppliers.

Solid-state laser machines, on the other hand, are like racing cars in that they can get up to incredible speeds on certain ‘surfaces’: in this case, thin sheet metal – even copper or brass. They can handle thicker materials, too; indeed, their results on mild steel are comparable to those of a CO2 laser. But when used to process stainless steel, cut quality deteriorates relatively quickly – meaning these lasers prefer a racetrack of sheet metal that is no more than 5 millimetres thick.

The SAP driver connection is featured on the new TruMark Station 1000 (D). This compact, space-saving laser marking station is suitable for marking small components without the need for a mechanical z-axis.

Today, there are around 20,000 TRUMPF CO2 lasers and 15,000 of its solid-state lasers in use around the world. While the CO2 lasers continue to dominate the market for flatbed laser machines, there is a growing demand for its range of machines with fibreguided solid-state lasers, as these lasers complement each other. The TruLaser 5030 fiber machine achieves feed rates for thin sheet metal that are three to four times faster than the CO2 version.

“Lasers as a tool are still in their youth – even now, 50 years after the first laser was fired up and 40 years since they began to cut their teeth in an industrial setting at TRUMPF,” says Peter Leibinger, vice chairman of the Managing Board of TRUMPF GmbH and head of TRUMPF’s Laser Technology Division. “People are still constantly coming up with new applications for both CO2 and solid-state lasers. Some, such as the cutting of glass display covers for mobile phones, benefit from the speed of laser processing, while others, such as the cutting of lightweight materials, would simply not be possible without lasers.” ■
