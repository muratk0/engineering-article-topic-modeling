ISMR: In your opinion, which trends are currently dominating the sheet metal processing industry?

AW Added value is an important trend. The main question here is: How can parts be produced along the cutting and bending process chain as profitably as possible? With solutions that increase efficiency and reduce production costs, we support our customers in achieving this. In my opinion, a further trend is flexible machine systems, which can be easily upgraded according to the order situation.

In metal cutting production, the fiber laser is certainly another further trend.

We recognised the potential of the fiber laser at an early stage and adjusted the focus of our R&D developments accordingly

ISMR: Fiber laser technology is developing at an incredible pace and is advancing ever further into the CO laser’s fields of application. Where does Bystronic stand on this?

AW: We recognised the potential of the fiber laser at an early stage and adjusted the focus of our R&D developments accordingly. For example, we equip our BySprint Fiber with a 6 kilowatt fiber laser – this allows us to meet demands for rapid cutting times. We are also increasing the fiber laser’s spectrum of applications even further with new additional functions. With the new Power Cut Fiber, users can now achieve extremely fine cutting quality in sheet thicknesses of up to 30mm. The Detection Eye speeds up the detection of the position of metal sheets. This function enables users to save time and optimise raw material usage.

ISMR: Where do you see further potential for process optimisation in laser cutting?

AW: Next to high performance machines, innovative service products are increasing in importance. Throughout the entire process related to cutting and bending, there are many opportunities to optimise.

For example, the ByOptimizer, which we are presenting at EuroBLECH, is an exclusive online service that provides users with optimised cutting plans. With ByOptimizer, we expand the performance potential of our laser machines. This innovative service is currently unique on the market.

Thanks to innovative cluster technology, the cut parts are grouped on the metal sheet in an extremely efficient manner. This saves material, reduces cutting times and ultimately increases the profit per cutting job.

To achieve optimisation of the cutting plans and ensure process reliability, ByOptimizer factors in the geometry and the characteristics of the parts to be cut, but also the power of the laser in use. During the process, the technology accesses a comprehensive database.

ISMR: Which bending innovations will Bystronic be presenting at EuroBLECH?

AW: We will be presenting a new press brake – the Xpert 40 is a compact speed-machine with an innovative design. It is extremely well suited for efficiently processing small bent parts, regardless of small or large batches. The press brake has a maximum press capacity of 40 tons and a bending length of up to one metre. In line with the “plug and bend” principle, the machine is set up and ready to use in less than five minutes.

With the Xpert 40, we complete our range of press brakes. The sales launch is planned for Spring 2015. »

ISMR: Which technological leaps in waterjet cutting will you also be presenting at EuroBLECH?

AW: In waterjet cutting, we are supporting the trend towards flexible machine systems. Hence, we developed the ByJet Flex, a waterjet cutting system that enables the cutting range and the range of functions to be adapted to a wide range of customer requirements.

For example, the ByJet Flex’s cutting range can be extended from 2 x 3 metres up to a maximum of 10 x 3 metres, according to requirements. And, by exchanging the cutting head, users can convert the machine from 2D to 3D cutting technology. There are further additional functions with which the machine’s performance spectrum can be extended at any time.

A powerful software program, which is simple to use, significantly increases our customers’ productivity in all of these steps. Therefore, we are constantly working on the further development of our software.

At EuroBLECH, we will present BySoft 7 with numerous new functions. Stop by at our exhibition booth and take a look.

## Swapping from CO2 to fiber laser profiling boosts sheet metalworking productivity at MMP

UK sheet metal subcontractor, Midland Metal Products (MMP), bought a Bystronic CO2 laser profiler in 2005 to replace three smaller laser cutters. In May 2014, the firm part-exchanged the CO2 machine for a fiber laser version of identical capacity from the same supplier. The new machine is twice as fast when cutting zinc-coated and uncoated mild steel up to 3mm thick, doubling productivity when processing those materials.

As 90 per cent of throughput at the factory is mild steel and 80 per cent of material is 5mm gauge or lower, the impact of the new 3kW BySprint Fiber 3015 has been dramatic. Stainless steel and aluminium sheet is also being cut more efficiently up to the machine’s maximum capacity of 3 metres by 1.5 metres.

Productivity has been boosted even at 5mm, although material thicker than that can normally be machined faster on a CO2 machine, certainly when piercing and cutting in a straight line. Nevertheless, the fiber laser is able to tackle heavier gauges with ease and the small time penalty is far outweighed by the higher speeds when processing thinner materials.

Justin Sanders, production control manager at MMP, said, “We had been watching carefully the progress of fiber laser technology since 2012 and were monitoring the equipment on offer from four major OEMs. The decision was taken to return to Bystronic for the latest purchase due to the previous CO2 machine’s reliability, the speed and price of the BySprint fibre laser profiler and the successful cutting trials carried out at Bystronic’s technical centre in Coventry.”

The original CO2 laser was installed with an integrated ByTrans 3015 automatic load/unload system, which in turn was interfaced with a Remmert computer-controlled sheet metal storage system. This equipment has been linked to the new fiber machine to form a similar, automatic cell, complete with the original nitrogen generation plant. As the material being cut is thin, cycle time per sheet is relatively short. Even so, two to three hours’ lights-out production is gained every day.

The Desford factory, near Leicester, operates around the clock on weekdays and for 12 hours at week-ends. Business at the 125-employee firm has been growing steadily over the past 18 months. It is winning a new customer every month on average and demand is predicted to increase year-on-year.

Keeping prices competitive and, at the same time, maintaining quality is crucial in the metal sheet sector. All customers are looking for good value. MMP supplies this diverse range of products and fabrications in small-to-medium volumes of less than 100-off. One of its particular strengths that helps to keep costs down is being able to take raw sheet or tube and transform it into sometimes complex, finished assemblies under one roof, often with the assistance of robotic arc welding. Plate of up to 20mm thick is processed occasionally.

Further laser cutting plant will be installed at Desford in the coming years as demand for the company’s sheet metal products and services grows. Now that fiber laser technology has reached maturity, this will be the process of choice for MMP unless the proportion of thicker gauge metal that it handles increases significantly. ■

## BUILDING THE FUTURE
ISMR SAYS: “Adding value through aesthetics – industrial design in construction”

LiuGong engineers explain the factors that impact the current process of construction machinery design as well as what might lie ahead in the future…

The aesthetic appearance of construction machinery is immediately recognisable but, to a casual observer, the technical thought process that influences their designs can be overlooked. Legislative changes, the safety and comfort of operators and the continuous development of new technologies make the visual design of construction equipment a complex and intellectually challenging endeavour.

The Industrial Design team behind the forthcoming machines produced by Chinese construction manufacturer, LiuGong, shared their insights into the considerations impacting the current process in construction machinery design, and what might lie ahead in the future.

For over 55 years, LiuGong Machinery Corporation has been a leader in China’s construction equipment manufacturing industry. From building the country’s first modern wheel loader, LiuGong has evolved to become one of the fastest growing, global CE companies in the world offering a full line of extreme duty, intuitive machines for construction equipment owners constantly challenged to do more with less.

## The right look

Gary Major, Director of Industrial Design; Richard Killgren, Senior Industrial Designer and Ed Wagner, Director of New Technology and Director of Test, combine decades of design experience in the construction and automotive sectors, positioning them to provide expert comment on the matter.

“Today’s end-user is far more design-savvy than their predecessors so aesthetics is a hugely important factor,” said Major, formerly the Head of Industrial Design with JCB. “If something looks right and looks trustworthy, a little spark in the back of their brain immediately tells them it is going to be a good purchase.”

For a construction manufacturer attempting to succeed amongst a plethora of alternatives, differentiating the machines from their competitors can be difficult, but the influence of a tight-knit, in-house industrial design team such as LiuGong’s can be a defining factor before mechanical specifications even enter the equation.

“Consultancies can have a high turnover of staff so consistency in the development of the form language can be a little uneven,” explained Major. “But establishing a consistent brand identity is extremely important – often the end-user will want to tag a machine with their company’s branding, usually at the expense of the OEM branding with which the machine leaves the factory.

“Our own approach is already giving rise to LiuGong’s aesthetic evolution – in other words, the DNA permeating every design and each machine’s lineage will be so obvious that you could trace its family tree,” added Major. “Solid form language and strong DNA can communicate the marque just as effectively as a set of decals…. When was the last time you needed to see a badge before recognising a Mercedes-Benz or Aston Martin?”

Legislative changes, the safety and comfort of operators and the continuous development of new technologies make the visual design of construction equipment a complex and intellectually challenging endeavour.

Succeeding with this differentiation must take more into account than originality and consistency alone – the ability to influence a positive emotional response with the end-user is one of an industrial designer’s most powerful weapons.

“The perception of quality will help to sell a machine,” elaborated Killgren who, having spent years in the automotive industry with Lotus and Bentley, is now transferring his skills to LiuGong’s machine development. “When you make contact with a machine, whether from a visual or on a tactile basis, if anything looks or feels cheap you assume the whole machine is inferior on an emotional level, irrespective of its specifications and usability. The impact of emotional triggers from a design perspective can never be underestimated.

“As people, we are often unaware of the subconscious emotional tendencies that influence our decisions. This could even be as subtle as the colour balance between yellow and dark grey on a construction machine, which is easily relatable on an emotional level to examples in the natural world – think about the different ways you would react to a canary and a wasp. These things are second nature to us now – it’s one of the first things we look at when we see a machine, and acts as an overarching initiative in every LiuGong machine that we design.”

## Engineering and regulatory demands

The challenge in defining any construction machine with a unique identity stems from the varying specifications that must be adhered to, whether as a result of engineering or regulatory demands.

Major highlighted an example relating specifically to ever-increasing legislative demands. The gradual progression towards the energy-efficient Tier IV Final EU emissions standards has had as much an influence on the design of construction machines as it has mechanically.

“One golden rule around five years ago was that an operator needed to be able to see an object one metre high from one metre away from the machine,” explained Major. “Engine emissions have changed components such as powertrains and the particulate filter assembly, growing their size enormously. Because of that, engine enclosures are now huge which creates challenges in meeting sightline requirements, which in turn impacts the overall aesthetics.”

These challenges, however, can be offset by the continuous development of new technologies – an area which LiuGong is actively researching and incorporating into its designs: “Developments in materials – especially in the area of composites – have allowed us greater freedom to explore form, and the increased aesthetic awareness from the end-user has allowed us to push for bolder designs,” added Major.

“From a visual standpoint, the better the A-surface, the better the machine looks,” he elaborated. “Up until now, an awful lot machines in this category used pressed steel; when you do pressed steel on a car, because you’re working with a very thin gauge of material, you can get the sleek detailing that you would generally associate with a car’s body. With construction bodywork, we are talking about 2, 3, 4mm thick steel at times, so to get that level of definition has been very difficult. With composite technologies and the likes of injection moulding, it is considerably easier to attain the style of A-surface we want for future generations of LiuGong machines.

“From a performance point of view, the materials we are looking at have an extremely high impact resistance. For example with hoods, there will inevitably be a considerable amount of spoil and rock landing on the machine. Certain materials can also be self-coloured within the mould so, if a machine is subject to high levels of abrasion, the colour is not going to scratch off. As a result of all this, these higher grade materials help the machine to age better, both in terms of maintaining visual appeal and minimalising potential damage.”
