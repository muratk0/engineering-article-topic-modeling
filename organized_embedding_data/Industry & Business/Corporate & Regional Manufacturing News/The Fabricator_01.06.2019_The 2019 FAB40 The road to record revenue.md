# The 2019 FAB40: The road to record revenue

[TARİH: 01.06.2019 The Fabricator]

By Tim Heston

No. 1: MEC

In 2019 MEC will reach a milestone it set for itself five years ago: to become a half-billion-dollar custom metal fabricator. Its recent acquisition of Defiance Metal Products will send it over the mark. Company leaders are expecting 2019 revenue to close out at more than $560 million.

And if you turned on financial cable news the morning of May 9, you would have witnessed a first: A custom fabricator CEO, Bob Kamphuis, rang the opening bell at the New York Stock Exchange. MEC had its initial public offering and became a public company, with an opening share price of $17 and raising more than $105 million for the company. Until May MEC was owned by an employee stock ownership plan (ESOP).

"Employees continue to have a stake in the company," Kamphuis told Yahoo! Finance, standing on the floor of the NYSE a few hours after ringing the opening bell. "The only difference is, now there’s a public marker [for the price] versus an annual valuation process. So now they’ll see how we compete at a new level in the capital markets. And I think we’re going to do very well."

So why go public now? As Kamphuis explained in an interview with

, "We’ve grown the value of MEC significantly since we became an ESOP in 1985 in Mayville, Wis., and we have a concentration of shares in Mayville. And retiring baby boomers puts a dent in our repurchasing obligation."

That is, when longtime workers retire from an ESOP, they’re required to sell their shares—and because the value of those shares has grown significantly, these transactions take a lot of cash. "The proceeds from the IPO put our balance sheet in a very conservative position."

As a public company, MEC workers now have more options upon retirement. They no longer have to sell their shares back to the company; they instead can either hold on to the shares or sell them on the public market.

There’s good reason that employees might want to hold on to those shares.

"We saw practically every market we serve go in a positive trajectory," said Ryan Raber, executive vice president, sales & marketing, performance structures and tanks. "As we get into 2019, I’d say similar conditions will continue. The heavy-duty truck market continues at a record level, medium-duty continues to do well, the construction market is good, agricultural equipment is on the upside, and the military equipment business has a bright future. Same thing goes for the power sports and recreation market."

Another good reason to hold on to those shares: The Defiance Metal Products acquisition, which closed in December, has also gone smoothly. According to SEC filings, MEC purchased the Defiance, Ohio, company for about $115 million. "This acquisition was like getting a Christmas present, opening it, and seeing that there’s no assembly required and the batteries are included," Kamphuis said. "It’s just a really good business, and we’re pleased to have them as part of our team."

The move further diversifies MEC’s customer base. But as sources explained, achieving revenue diversity isn’t just a matter of tackling new markets and buying companies with complementary customer bases. The competitive position of those customers in various markets really matters. Building a relationship with the top players in each market can help further reduce the peaks and valleys of the business cycle.

"During a recession, the Nos. 1 and 2 players in a market fall less than the Nos. 3 and 4 players," Kamphuis said, adding that MEC identifies top players not only by market share, but by other factors too. "Who has the most dealers out there, and who has the best parts programs out there?" Heavy machinery, be it farm equipment or anything else, still needs repair parts even during the worst of an economic cycle, and MEC wants to be ready to provide those parts.

More customers in more markets also increases the complexity of the demand cycles and the associated capacity planning. "If you’re in numerous markets, now you also have to offer an agile, redeployable manufacturing capability, so you can shift when you need to and keep the revenue streams steady."

No. 2: O’NEAL MANUFACTURING SERVICES

"After having such a banner year [in 2018], we’re putting it back into the company and making some major technology investments."

So said Michael Richey, director of sales and marketing at O’Neal Manufacturing Services (OMS), Birmingham, Ala., adding that "there have been investments across the board, especially with automation. Labor is the biggest challenge we face." After stating this, he pulled up OMS’s internal job board. "As of today [in late April], we have 148 job openings."

OMS is in a similar boat as many successful shops on the FAB 40. It’s trying to meet solid growth in demand from customers across the economic spectrum. Like many fabricators, OMS has seen solid gains from nearly every sector it serves, particularly construction and rail. "Rail was particularly strong in 2018."

What’s made 2018 and 2019 especially positive is that much of the demand OMS has had is what Richey called "true demand, not smoke and mirrors." That is, demand isn’t there because of timing issues—like ordering products and building up inventory in reaction to the trade and tariff situation.

He added that OMS has had a slight slowdown in the agricultural equipment market, "but I don’t think it’s anything to panic over. We just had a little fluttering in the demand level.

"We’re also seeing a lot of reshoring," Richey continued, "particularly for work with large, heavy weldments. We’re seeing a lot of uptick [in business activity] and chatter about that."

With record-low unemployment, available labor is as scarce as ever. So what’s OMS doing to attract the rare talent that becomes available?

"We’ve been doing lots of studies to make sure our rates of pay are competitive and the benefits package is attractive," Richey said, adding that employee surveys have become more common too. Questions are about work-life balance, their relationship with their supervisor, and overall employee satisfaction.

No. 3: BTD

The Detroit Lakes, Minn., fabricator reported that 2018 was a year of "peak volumes," with strong demand across most markets—a common theme among this year’s FAB 40.

So what’s ahead for the remainder of 2019? "We had a lot of volume growth in 2017 and 2018, but now we’re starting to see the volume growth stabilize," said Jared Lotzer, BTD’s vice president of sales and quality. "We might be at a peak, when you consider the annual volume of products customers are selling."

The peak comes after several years of record growth. 2018 revenues were 20 percent over 2017, which is saying something for a $230 millionplus operation. That said, because of the recent material price volatility, comparing revenue numbers from year to year doesn’t produce an apples-to-apples comparison of overall business health.

"Of course, we’re still seeing very high revenue numbers, because steel [prices] were high in the first quarter," Lotzer said. "The volatility of steel prices has definitely changed the revenue numbers in our space."

Although a portion of that revenue growth can be attributed to high steel prices, a good portion of that growth still can be attributed to real demand. Lotzer estimated that—taking out the material price effects—BTD still had more than 10 percent growth between 2017 and 2018. "And that’s still remarkable, considering where we are in the business cycle," he said.

So where exactly is this "steel price-adjusted" 10 percent growth coming from at BTD? "For us, it’s coming from increasing our market share in markets across the board," Lotzer said. "It’s all incremental new business, from agriculture, recreational vehicle, and construction.

"The only slightly negative trend we’re seeing now in our customer base is the oil and gas piece of the energy space," Lotzer continued. "When oil prices dropped in Q4, it put many people on the fence as to what the future of oil looks like. So there was a period where the order books stopped a bit.

"We do complex pumps for that industry, and these complicated systems have long lead times. So when they stopped ordering in September of last year, it started affecting us in the spring."

Of course, oil prices have rebounded since last year, which in turn will affect demand. "The volatility in the oil markets has always been a challenge for us," Lotzer said, "and I know we’re not alone."

So how is BTD growing its market share, even during these times of peak demand? According to Lotzer, a lot of it might have to do with on-time-delivery (OTD) performance. "OTD is still a challenge for many in this business," he said. "We’re on a tear on the past 18 months where our OTD is in the high 90-percentile range; our last report had us at 99 percent. When you think of the cost of doing business at the big OEMs, that [high-performance] OTD has to be there."

No. 6: TENERE INC.

Several years ago Tenere Inc., with sheet metal operations in Dresser, Wis., began a new focus on market development. The company had grown strong by serving specific markets, and leaders wanted to know why. The fabricator’s performance of course had something to do with it, but the company also looked at external market forces. What exactly made the markets they served true growth markets for metal manufacturing?

For instance, one of Tenere’s major sheet metal fabrication markets deals with cloud computing, including the server racks, chassis, enclosures, and other sheet metal components that permeate server farms across the country.

"[The characteristics of this market] are the future of a lot of American manufacturing," said Audrey Hamilton, director of marketing. "It’s hightech, has short product-development cycles, scales quickly, and doesn’t always travel long distances very well."

Besides cloud computing, another growth market for Tenere is the self-service kiosk arena. Minimum wages are rising, labor is scarce across the board. For these reasons and others, kiosks and the "automated" customer service they provide have a big future.

Projecting growth isn’t a straightforward process, because past behaviors don’t necessarily reflect future market performance, especially considering the reshoring push going on in certain sectors. Just because a sector or prospect has relied on overseas suppliers for the past 20 years doesn’t mean it will continue to do so over the next 20 years.

Hamilton described a cloud networking company that had previously made its products in China but, as of last year, decided to bring its metal manufacturing back to North America. "Our Mexico facility is an attractive alternative to manufacturing in China. Our Wisconsin and Mexico locations collaborate daily, and our ability to transfer work between them was one of the main reasons the company decided to work with us."

As Hamilton described, having the Mexico facility helps Tenere start a reshoring or near-shoring discussion with a prospect. The prospect might decide to start a program with Tenere’s Mexico plant, which starts a relationship that could add more work to other Tenere plants in Wisconsin and elsewhere.

No. 11: MILLER FABRICATION SOLUTIONS

Many business leaders in metal fabrication can probably relate to Eric Miller’s forecasting experience over the past year. The president of Miller Fabrication Solutions (MFS), Brookville, Pa., expects business to start trending downward gradually this year. "And this doesn’t surprise me. Just look at the run everybody’s been on. I mean, holy cow."

That said, MFS’s initial forecast for 2019 was low. "Our sales are still strong," Miller said. "In fact, we’ve had to revise our expectations upward at least twice this year. Our second half of the year, which I thought would be pretty soft, has filled up significantly. And most of our customers are telling us to expect decent growth into 2020. Still, that’s far away at this point. But overall, business conditions are very positive."

As most businesses do, MFS’s management team weighs broader economic indicators with its customer base and competitive position in the market. Miller has been tracking North American industrial production, which has been trending downward in recent months. MFS tracks its historical performance in relation to that index and uses that to project where orders are headed. Based on that, "in the third quarter we expected to see some softening. Thing is, we’re just not seeing that."

He added that orders look strong for two main reasons. First, MFS’s accounts in the construction equipment and material handling sectors have been much stronger than expected. "The second reason is we’re onboarding quite a bit of new business," Miller said. "That’s had a very positive impact. So unless something significant happens later this year, we’re expecting very strong growth."

All this positive growth came even with material pricing challenges that all fabricators have grown all too familiar with. "We index based on the CRU," Miller said, adding that the dramatic price increases obviously presented challenges. "It was painful. But it’s leveled out, and we’re now in a much better place."

He explained that material prices "definitely inflated our sales numbers," but he added that the high material costs were a minor portion of MFS’s sales growth. "Even with the material out of the equation, our sales are still up significantly."

No. 15: CUPPLES J&J CO.

In recent decades Cupples J&J Co. has built a reputation for pushing the technological envelope. The Jackson, Tenn., fabricator has 18 lasers, including a collection of high-powered fiber lasers, complemented by 35 press brakes. Few shops can boast a cutting capacity that includes four fiber lasers with 10 kW or more of cutting power. The shop has three 10-kW models and one 12-kW system.

"We’ve always jumped ahead on the technology curve," said Jeff Cupples, vice president.

Being an early technology adopter, Cupples said the company has worked its way through cutting heads and other machine components in a quest to push and perfect the bleeding edge. But the work has paid off in a big way. For instance, as of May the shop’s 12-kW fiber laser has been running nonstop, and technicians have yet to replace a cover slide on the cutting head—a true feat, especially considering the aggressive piercing and rapid plate cutting it’s performed.

"Right now we’re cutting ⅜-inch material at 295 inches per minute," Cupples said, adding that this thickness and even thicker are ideal for the company’s high-powered systems.

When possible, the company avoids putting thin stock on the high-powered lasers simply because of the offloading resources required to keep the head cutting. Cutting plate slows the machine, but the cutting rate remains incredibly fast for such thick stock.

Cupples also pays careful attention to overall throughput, not just the IPMs achieved on the cutting tables. For instance, you won’t see flat-part deburring systems anywhere near the lasers. The company’s incredible cutting throughput wouldn’t be possible if parts had to be sent through a deburring machine.

"If we have to deburr it, we won’t run it," he said, adding that the company is cutting up to 0.4-in.-thick stock and achieving less than a 0.005-in. burr on the bottom of the kerf.

The company has modified cutting head nozzles and other components on its high-powered laser systems, sometimes achieving up to 20 percent greater productivity beyond what even the equipment-makers promised. "It’s not about power," Cupples said. "It’s about the power of the gas and having a good laminar flow around the beam. There are so many ways you can skin a cat."

No. 18: GREINER INDUSTRIES INC.

If you want to find a good bellwether for the industrial economy, visit the expansive shop floor of Greiner Industries in Mount Joy, Pa. The company performs the extremes of heavy fabrication, with plate measured in inches and press brake capacity tonnages measured in the thousands.

The company helps fabricate the industrial foundation for a range of U.S. manufacturing sectors—steel mills, food processing plants, beer and wine processing plants, even pet food plants.

"That’s a stable business," said Jim Gillespie, Greiner’s manager of business development. "Americans love their pets."

According to Frank Greiner, president and CEO, the business is seeing a steady rise in demand. "What I see overall is a bit of a trend upward. We don’t have an overabundance of work, but we’re not bidding down in the dirt either. Within our customer base, we’re seeing more positive trends than we’ve seen over the past two years. Industrial companies are spending money replacing equipment, or simply growing."

Greiner is getting a boost in some of its infrastructure work, too, with several large railroad bridge projects moving forward. And it’s challenging work, especially from a material handling perspective. The projects will minimize field fabrication and maximize fabrication in the controllable shop environment.

It will be quite a sight. "It’ll be some of the heaviest fabrication you’ll ever see," Greiner said. "[These bridges] are about 20 feet wide, 125 feet long, and weigh about 360,000 pounds—and that’s per bridge. And it’s all going to be fabricated in one piece." The massive fabrications will be trucked from Greiner’s Mount Joy plant to the nearby Susquehanna River, where they’ll be sent to the job site by barge.

"We’ve made railroad bridges for years," Greiner said, "but this is the first one we’ve done that’s welded entirely throughout. All the other ones were bolted, sent in single pieces or in modular sections. It’s all about making the operation at the other end [on the job site] more efficient. They’ll be able to pick one bridge out and set a new one in within a matter of hours."
