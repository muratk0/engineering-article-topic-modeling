ISMR travelled to LVD's headquarters in Gullegem, Belgium, to view new metalworking technologies at its recently built five-million-euro Experience (XP) Centre.

LVD is a global solutions provider for sheet metalworking fabrication – punching, bending, laser cutting and software solutions, serving twelve core industries.

The founding families of the LVD Group have a mantra 'Big enough to cope, small enough to care'. Approximately 1250 people work in the LVD Group which is active in 45 countries, with 17 subsidiaries. It has its main factory and headquarters in Gullegem, Belgium, and a central European factory in Slovakia (for European production) as well as a factory in the U.S. and factories in China (south-east of Wuhan) for its two Chinese Joint Ventures.

ISMR travelled to LVD's headquarters in Gullegem, Belgium, to view new metalworking technologies at its recently built five-million-euro Experience (XP) Centre. This serves as a demonstration centre (to help stimulate solutions to metalworking challenges), a training centre and a showcase for LVD's latest Industry 4.0-enabled technologies.

## Investing for success

"We have had a very good year, with strong sales being made over the months leading to the half-year point. We are therefore on track for double-digit growth this year," Carl Dewulf LVD's President and MD, told ISMR.

"LVD is also expanding – we started a new sales and service subsidiary in Mexico at the beginning of the year to help bring our technology and support much closer to our customers. We will also be at the forthcoming Blechexpo (Stuttgart, Germany) and FABTECH (Chicago, U.S.) exhibitions, highlighting our latest solutions.

As ISMR walked through LVD's factory, it saw how World Class Manufacturing principles have been used to increase throughput on LVD's press brake and laser production lines. New investments on LVD's shop floor, to further streamline its manufacturing processes and shorten lead times, included a new large multi-pallet machining centre for cylinder body production, a machining centre for the machinetool frames/beams as well as specialist grinding machines.

In the XP Centre, ISMR saw LVD's CT-L high-speed load/unload automated tower system for its laser cutting machines. The key area for automation, as users know, is to get the cost benefit ratio right. LVD has just introduced the next generation of its CT-L (Compact Tower warehousing) automation system, for increased processing speed and changeover times for additional throughput. It develops its own automation systems but has just announced, in the BeNeLux region, a distribution agreement with Starmatik Automation for automation systems for LVD's press brakes and laser cutting systems there.

## Industry 4.0 in focus

However, as well as launching new metalworking technologies, LVD's key focus has been on software/control developments (around which its products have evolved) and the new, and intriguing, implications of what it calls Industry 4.1. This is LVD's vision of the future, driving its product and technology developments.

"With falling skills, you need to put more intelligence and intuitive nature of operation into the machine control and software. It is getting harder to find skilled operators so we need to inject experience and intelligence into our software and controls for better production flow. Innovation courses through the veins of LVD. When we industrialise ideas, we also think about practicality, reliability, benefit etc. A new technology must always deliver significant benefits for the user," Matt Fowles, Group Marketing Manager, LVD told ISMR.

LVD’s control software drives its laser cutting, bending, tube cutting and punching technology developments – with an eye, always, on the direct cost per part as this affects the margins of its customers. Its CADMAN® software suite is designed to facilitate Industry 4.0, looking at the big picture and helping to streamline the complete fabrication process through integrated, database-driven software that gives real-time insights from the shop floor.

"Industry 4.1 is the future beyond Industry 4.0. Industry 4.0 is about the communication of Big Data and cyber physical systems (sensors detecting data). It is the fourth industrial revolution (communication). The first industrial revolution was about mechanisation, the second was about electrification and the third was about automation," explained Kurt Debbaut, Product Manager, CADMAN Software, LVD.

"Our message is that Industry 4.0 is not a solution – it is a tool to come to a solution. Where can we gain time across the entire sheet metal process flow? How can you reduce the number of times you touch the sheet metal part? How can you reduce hidden time (i.e. tooling calculations, preparation and set-up time etc.)? Making the machines faster can only really shorten the process time by a minimal percentage – reducing hidden time can make a much more substantial and significant reduction to the overall process flow. Machine speed, therefore, is not integral to this process."

This is a thoughtful viewpoint, based around intelligent planning of the overall process flow of a part (from beginning to end of production) for a smarter way of working that keeps margins down. Based on 'Just in Time' principles, with order status visible at all times, the software will need to be able to calculate optimum buffer times for reduced work in progress (order intake to invoice), shorter delivery lead times, efficient production and lower costs. This also means less waste, less material used, less inventory, greater output levels and faster production. All of this must also take place in an environment of smaller batch work and higher part complexity.

"So, how do we define the optimal buffers and order intake? KPIs (Key Performance Indicators) can be measured. Parts need to be made when they are due and not before – the more manufacturing that we can combine, the more efficient we can be… You must have a holistic view, however (not a view from one part of the production process) to calculate this. The thresholds will differ, depending upon the management, type of product, type of market etc. So, Industry 4.0 will give you KPIs but it is not a solution. You will come to a solution by applying the values and the data that you have. So, Industry 4.0 is a tool, and not a solution. We can calculate direct and indirect cost per part. Your optimal buffer time will be somewhere between overall flow efficiency and overall resource (OEE) efficiency – the scenario calculation, and eventual manufacturing process chosen, comes under the remit of Industry 4.0," explained Kurt Debbaut.

LVD's CADMAN-Job software already calculates best impact for manufacturing processes and, in concert with its Touchi4 system and various CADMAN production software modules, is able to provide most of this information. However, LVD's software vision is for a self-learning system, with central data storage which companies can access while the system learns from various manufacturing scenarios.

"We are already working towards Industry 4.1 – we are no longer focused on Industry 4.0," Matt Fowles told ISMR. "This is our vision and its evolution over the next five years will be huge. Alongside this, we are working on several collaborative ventures, on ideas and concepts in this area, with a leading university in Belgium."

## An eye on laser cutting

"From a laser perspective, the current key focus is fibre laser cutting technology. It has accelerated almost exponentially because of its perceived benefits, increased capabilities and increasing acceptance in the marketplace. It was originally introduced as an ultra-high-speed metal processor/finisher and now, with zoomhead technology and higher-powered sources, its capability has moved from thin material processing to a much wider variety of material thicknesses," explained Matt Fowles.

"We have therefore accelerated our development programme for fibre laser cutting products. Over the last 12-18 months, we have introduced more models in the Phoenix fibre laser cutting system range. We started with our Electra machine, built as a machine that could cut as fast as the process without dynamic compromise. This also applies to the Phoenix. Now with our Electra and Phoenix fibre laser cutting systems, power sources are increasing from 4kW to 6-, 8- and, eventually, 10kW. However, there is a finite line with the cost benefit ratio."

LVD plans to launch, at this year's Blechexpo and FABTECH exhibitions, a new Electra 8kW fibre laser cutting system. The 8kW machine has no dynamic compromise so it can not only position the head rapidly but also cut whilst maintaining 2G acceleration speed, which is crucial when users wish to maintain the fidelity of small details on a metal part.

"We sell laser cutting machines all over the world and have made various studies on the most efficient way to pierce metal and reduce piercing times on the Electra and Phoenix. "It means that less heat is generated in the material so it makes it easier to cut small holes," Kurt Van Collie, Product Manager, Laser Cutting Machines, LVD, told ISMR.

"We sell more Phoenix models, on average (available in three different sheet sizes now). We now have 6kW and 8kW versions of the Phoenix and they are compatible with automation. Now also coming into the mix is our Electra 8kW machine which is suitable for high dynamic cutting of intricate parts with speed and accuracy."

Kurt Van Collie pointed out that cost per part remains more important than speed or power. When cutting thick steel with oxygen, he said, it makes no sense to move from a 6kW to a 10kW because the gain in cutting speed is marginal. However, when cutting steel with nitrogen, you can double the speed gain from 6- to 8kW. LVD believes that an 8kW machine will currently cover the greater part of the market but will also take the step, when ready, to a 10kW machine.

Matt Fowles is also keen to highlight that there is still a place for CO2 laser cutting systems in LVD's product portfolio.

"We still have CO2 lasers in our product range and we feel that CO2 still has a fit for certain applications. We offer CO2 entry-level and XXL format machines. The majority of laser machines sold now are fibre laser cutting ones, but we still feel that there is a place for CO2 laser cutting systems in our product portfolio (particularly for the cut and edge quality on thicker, stainless steel materials)," he explained.

Although LVD has investigated direct diode technology for laser cutting, it currently feels that fibre laser technology (as prices have dropped and fibre laser cutting efficiency has now increased to 40% on its 6kW machines) offers better value for its customers.

## New tube cutting system

Another new innovation for LVD is its new TL 2665-FL tube fibre laser cutting machine, which it will be launching. The system can cut tube diameters of 165mm and can process a range of non-ferrous materials on the fibre laser i.e. mild steel, stainless steel tubes, copper, brass etc. Using LVD's CADMAN-T software, it extends LVD's product portfolio and fills a growing demand for the application amongst its customers (where tube processing is a complementary activity to sheet metal operations). ISMR watched a cutting demonstration on the TL 2665 at LVD's new XP Centre.

"We know that there are a lot of companies still doing conventional processing of tube and small sections (cutting, notching, drilling and welding fabricated tube profile products). We particularly see, with our existing customer and prospect database, that they would benefit from having another process. We think of this as a complementary technology to add to our product portfolio," Matt Fowles told ISMR.

"In Europe, we are just introducing it to the marketplace and it is here in our XP Centre for people to see. It will not be at the Blechexpo exhibition in Stuttgart but we will be taking it to the FABTECH exhibition in Chicago for its global debut."

## Bending innovations

In LVD's new XP Centre, ISMR saw the Dyna-Press portable electric drive press brake in operation. LVD is extending the range of the machine so a 12-ton (800mm), a 24-ton (1.2m) and now a new 40-ton version (1.5m) are available. The niche for this system is in highspeed bending of small parts and brackets etc.

"Our Dyna-Press press brake is for bending smaller, more complex parts. The operator can now sit down to bend the parts. It is equipped with our latest software and is small, compact and portable (12/8 and 24/12 models only). You can move it with a forklift next to, say, a lasercutting machine so that you can immediately bend parts that have been cut. It can supply more bending capacity, with higher throughput, to users," explained Steven Lucas, Product Manager, Press Brakes, LVD.

"The 40-ton machine can bend 1.5m (up to 5mm material thickness) and a variety of materials (such as stainless steel, aluminium, mild steel etc.) with high throughput. The customer can choose the type of tooling style he wants. Small batches and short lead times require rapid tooling change, as this affects the direct cost per part. Scheduling can be done through our CADMAN-Job software. No skilled press brake operators are needed – the maximum training needed on this is less than one day."

LVD's latest generation of flexible Easy-Form® press brakes features its adaptive-bending technology, employing LVD's 'Art to Part' flow philosophy, where bending the part 'right first time' is integral.

LVD's ToolCell press brake, created to reduce tooling set-up times and incorporating an automatic tool set-up system at the back of the press brake, has been a major product for the Group. LVD recently expanded the range of the ToolCell to the ToolCell Plus, a higher tonnage machine with extended height and stroke plus the capability to utilise larger tools, so that users can process heavier materials and parts with larger return flanges. ISMR watched a demonstration of the ToolCell at LVD's XP Centre. LVD's patented Easy-Form® Laser adaptive bending system provides in-process angle monitoring and correction for consistent bending results.

"Our CADMAN suite software decides the optimum solution for a job, generates the bending program and creates a 3D bending solution etc. We gain about a 45% increase in throughput with the ToolCell. While the machine is changing the tools, the operator can be taking away the parts from a previous job and fetching material for the next job – he is not being unproductive. It is not a stressful operation for the operator and productivity can be significantly raised. We have sold ToolCells all around the world," Steven Lucas told ISMR.

LVD’s award-winning Synchro-Form pressbrake was launched at EuroBLECH in Hanover last year – another form of adaptive bending. LVD will shortly be announcing new orders for this system and is now building a larger version of Synchro-Form. The Group is also planning an event in Q1 2018 at its facility in Belgium where it will be showing Synchro-Form technology in action.

"Synchro-Form is our next step for XXL bending. Large parts can be difficult and sometimes dangerous for an operator to handle and bend. Synchro-Form automatically controls the bending cycle, manipulating the part, continuously scanning and measuring the parts and automatically adjusts and corrects the bending sequences," added Steven Lucas.

## Packing a punch

LVD is the only major player that offers both turret punch press and single head punching technology. It is fair to say that market share of punching machines has been taken by lasers – that has been happening since the 1990s. However, some users have punching machines that are coming to the end of their working lives and they need to renew those machines. Punching, as a technology, still has a place in the sheet metalworking shop. We have found now that people are also analysing what technology they need to invest in for their next punching machine – the thought process is now different," explained Matt Fowles.

"Like anything, you have to find the right process for the application. Punch presses will continue to be part of the process as, for some applications, it can be more cost-efficient to use a punch press than a laser. There are some products that are more suited to a turret machine and others are more suited to a singlehead style machine. We have also combined punch/laser in the past on a machine."

ISMR watched demonstrations, at the XP Centre, of LVD's latest Strippit VX turret punchpress as well as its Strippit PX that punches, forms, taps and bend all-in-one (equipped with the FA-P automation system). The PX machine can cover several operations in one, eliminating several secondary operations.
