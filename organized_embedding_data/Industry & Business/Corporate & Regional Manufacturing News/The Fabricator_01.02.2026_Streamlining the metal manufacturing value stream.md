# Streamlining the metal manufacturing value stream

Biz Talk

And what it means for contract fabricators

› AUTHOR › TIM-HESTON

I

ndustry consolidation has commenced with gusto in the metal service center space. Just a few months ago, Ryerson announced that it purchased Olympic. Then, in mid-January, Worthington Steel announced it would acquire Kloeckner & Co., a service center with plants in Europe and the U.S. Both deals are slated to close later this year.

The Worthington Steel deal especially exhibits just how quickly a progressive metal manufacturing business can evolve. In December 2023 the company, then known as Worthington Industries, split in two. Today, Worthington Enterprises focuses on products for the building, consumer, and energy sectors, while Worthington Steel focuses on steel processing and distribution. Once the Kloeckner acquisition closes, Worthington will become the second largest service center in North America, slightly smaller than Reliance but slightly larger the soon-to-be-combined Ryerson and Olympic Steel.

"We’ve been looking at Kloeckner for two years," said Geoff Gilmore, Worthington Steel’s president/CEO, in an interview with The Fabricator last month. "When we separated and became our own publicly traded company, we felt the environment was ripe for acquisition. We looked at the best candidates, and after analyzing them over time, Kloeckner kept rising among the mix of potential companies. They were our No. 1 target."

"[Kloeckner] really rebooted their strategy. They wanted to focus on high-value-add processing. They’re not just a distributor. And they’ve been executing on that strategy."

—Geoff Gilmore, Worthington Steel

The Kloeckner deal is significant for contract metal fabricators, considering the company’s history of value-added fabrication and vertical acquisitions— the most significant being American Fabricators Inc., a Tennessee contract fabricator Kloeckner acquired in 2015.

In 2019, I spoke with Mark Zumbrun, Kloeckner’s director of fabrication, who described how the company at that time was sending a portion of its fabrication work to custom fabricators on a toll basis (that is, fab shops would charge for the services but not own the material). His overall message: Service centers and fabricators need to collaborate to help lower costs and streamline the supply chain, making life easier for the ultimate customer.

As Gilmore explained last month, along with its diverse product offerings and geographic footprint, especially in the Southeast, Kloeckner’s emphasis on value-added fabrication made the organization a prime candidate for a potential acquisition. "That made Kloeckner appealing," Gilmore said. "They really rebooted their strategy. They wanted to focus on high-valueadd processing. They’re not just a distributor. And they’ve been executing on that strategy. They actually sold seven distribution centers [earlier this year] so they can remain focused on high-value-add processing."

OEMs are demanding more value-adding fabrication, and service centers are meeting that need. So, where does that leave contract metal fabricators? The pragmatism behind Zumbrun’s comments from 2019—about fabricators as toll processors—applies here. Many fabricators covet those high-quantity orders that keep machines running and cash coming in the door. But with those whale accounts come the need for cash-consuming inventory. Keeping machines running without consuming cash on material sounds like a compelling proposition.

Metal manufacturing has a long history of expanding beyond its core processes, be they at a service center, fabricator, or OEM. All this is done to better serve the ultimate customer. And that ultimate customer is where success really matters.

Last year, our columnist Caleb Chamberlain of Utah-based OSH Cut described how he lost a bid for some tube cutting work from his own tube supplier. "They command enough scale to earn direct-to-mill buying power and can therefore drive prices down far enough to win the big jobs. This can be frustrating for shop owners like myself, but it’s actually an important, healthy part of our manufacturing economy. We need those kinds of efficiencies to help reduce manufacturing costs in the U.S."

The same could be said of OEMs expanding (and automating) their inhouse metal fabrication capabilities and relying less on outside suppliers. If the strategy works, and the in-house technology truly reduces overall costs, then that OEM becomes more competitive. That brings in more business and, if internal fabrication capacity is exhausted, the return of more contract work for area fabricators. The return of work isn’t guaranteed, but nothing in a free-market economy really is.

Typical custom fabricators have a handful of large accounts and a "long tail" of smaller accounts. The diverse needs of that long tail are what keeps the industry so fragmented. A small shop can tailor its value-adding operations to serve a specific low-and medium-volume niche—work that a giant service center or contract fabricator would never touch.

What

no

contract metal fabricator or service center wants is a large OEM (or another type of "ultimate" manufacturing customer) to pull up stakes and move elsewhere—or even worse, fail altogether. If the entire metal manufacturing supply chain works together to eliminate waste and reduce cost, that’s less likely to happen.
