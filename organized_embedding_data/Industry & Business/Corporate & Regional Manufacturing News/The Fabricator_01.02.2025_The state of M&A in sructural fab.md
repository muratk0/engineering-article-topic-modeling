# The state of M&A in sructural fab

Construction

Specific factors drive acquisitions in a unique industry

By

Tim Heston

S

tructural fabrication is in John Schuepbach’s blood. His father worked for several shops in and around San Antonio, Texas. Schuepbach himself worked for a few different organizations, acquired several fabricators in the 1990s and early 2000s (Trans-Tex Fabricating, Bexar Steel, and Jackson Steel), then sold the combined company in 2009.

"I grew up in this business, and when I sold in 2009, I couldn’t find anyone in M&A that specialized in steel construction companies. I saw that opportunity and ran with it."

In 2010, he launched a company now called Steel Mergers & Acquisitions LLC, and since then he’s visited hundreds of shops and pored over hundreds of financial statements. Anyone who looks at the numbers alone can plainly see what drives revenue: the volume and (especially) velocity of work that flows through each plant. Still, the nature of work varies tremendously. Levels of demand vary, too, and most companies deal with longlead-time projects that require some sophisticated project management.

So, why do people buy and sell structural fab businesses? Schuepbach outlined several key reasons, including the need to find capacity, construction market diversification, and, not least, available talent. He added the most successful deals share a common thread: They help an organization manage risk extremely well.

Finding Stasis

Private equity’s presence in contract precision metal fabrication has increased significantly in recent years. In the structural arena, though, PE isn’t nearly as present. There’s a good reason for that.

"Some structural fabrication companies are owned by private equity, but they are few and far between," Schuepbach said. "You have lower EBITDA multiples, and when you dig into them, you find they can be fairly risky businesses."

As managing director of Steel Mergers & Acquisitions LLC, John Schuepbach has visited hundreds of structural fabricators since launching the business in 2010.

One large construction project that fails could be enough to put under a small structural fab shop that doesn’t have other work lined up. Also, projects take time to ramp up, and managing many of them at once can get seriously complicated. This, Schuepbach said, is why the business is dominated by small family businesses that reach a certain level of revenue. Few make it past $1 billion in annual revenue, and most make $50 million or less.

"Most owners find a level where the cash flows they have and the amount of risk they’re taking give them an equilibrium, and they find this stasis."

About Expertise and Relationships

Some structural fab shops might also do erection and have in-house detailing, but they represent the minority. As Schuepbach explained, one reason has to do with expertise. It’s the unique selling point of every entity in the construction supply chain, and each entity requires a

lot

of expertise. A heavy fab shop might specialize in beam and plate while another one might focus on architecturally exposed structural steel (AESS). Still another might focus on handrails, stairwells, or other miscellaneous fabrication.

Put another way, the products and services companies sell aren’t unique, but the

experts

who work for them are. So, if a company is looking to grow, it wants to help make the experts it employs even more effective, which in turn makes the company overall more competitive. Consolidating different elements of the construction supply chain often doesn’t accomplish this.

All this creates a relationship-driven business. For instance, a fab shop builds relationships with specific detailers they trust. "Detailing is such a small portion of the total project, but it can have such a huge impact. There’s a risk premium there," Schuepbach said.

Consolidating various construction services under one umbrella also could increase risk by concentrating the revenue streams. A one-stop shop that offers detailing, structural fabrication, miscellaneous fab, erection, and even mechanical services could take on more and more work for a single construction project. That’s great, but what if that project stops unexpectedly? Maybe the project owner goes bankrupt? In this case, that one-stop shop just saw a lot of its eggs in one basket suddenly go rotten.

Schuepbach added that companies with multiple construction services under one roof do exist, of course. The successful ones work to manage risk by increasing their competitiveness in ways that work for the markets they serve. In structural fab, the project mix really defines the business model.

Building Capacity

Schuepbach described several reasons why structural fabricators buy other shops. All of them have to do with enhancing a shop’s existing expertise or strength—that is, the reason customers trust and (hence) buy from them.

"One reason is the need for additional capacity," Schuepbach said. A fab shop might have a growing list of clients, and the space under roof and in the lay-down yard just isn’t sufficient to serve them effectively. Another plant in the area might have not only space but also equipment that complements a shop’s existing work, anything from beam lines to bridge cranes of different capacities.

The need for "additional capacity" can also involve skilled labor. "Some shop owners tell me, ‘I’ve hired everybody I can. There are no more welders in our area. I could build a new facility, but I can’t staff it. But I can go across town and buy a competitor.’"

Market and Project Diversity

"Construction is not homogenous across the U.S.," Schuepbach said. "Different metroplexes run in different construction cycles, and different project types run in different cycles as well. The market in Houston is different from Dallas, which is different from Atlanta.

"Moreover, project types in those markets run in different cycles. Warehouses could be booming in Houston and dry in Atlanta, yet Atlanta could have opportunities in schools, hospitals, or data centers."

He added that in these cases, structural fabricators looking to acquire want to diversify with different project types in

different

locations that serve

different

markets.

Diversity in Capabilities

Every shop has its strengths and weaknesses. One shop might specialize in welding to the AWS D1.1 Structural Welding Code while another might be experienced in working to the AWS D1.5 Bridge Welding Code. Or they might fabricate to the AWS D1.8 Structural Welding Code—Seismic Supplement.

Depending on a fabricator’s competitive position, having more capabilities might expand its overall competitiveness. That said, the specific processes, procedures, equipment, material handling (like different crane capacities), and even the shop layout can differ depending on the mix of fab work a shop does. A growing fabricator with little experience in bridge fabrication might find it difficult to launch a bridge division—but acquiring a bridge fabricator, one with the right equipment and expertise, might make sense.

"You get better productivity out of employees when they’re doing similar work consistently," Schuepbach explained.

Again, all this depends on the current project mix a shop has. Consider a shop that specializes in AESS comes on the market. Some operations might not touch AESS work with a 10-ft. pole, subbing it out when the work just can’t be avoided. Other shops might have structural work that calls for AESS on a regular basis and, hence, work with those AESS specialists frequently. In the latter case, an acquisition might make sense; in the former case, maybe not so much.

The Processing Center Approach

Schuepbach described a recent innovation in company organization that could start to change the way some structural fab shops grow.

"All this new fabrication technology is clogging up a lot of floor space," he said, adding that to rectify this, "a lot of companies now are setting up parts processing centers that can serve two or three fabrication plants."

Separating out upstream parts-producing processes makes sense for several reasons. First, a person’s workday on the shop floor can be very different, depending on where one works. Upstream, at the plate cutting machines, beam processing systems, and even robotic copers, cross-trained workers learn the intricacies of different machines and move where needed to keep the parts flowing downstream.

Those parts then get sent to locations dedicated to specific kinds of work: beams, trusses, industrial fabrications, or anything else. The closer the work gets to completion, the more specialized the work becomes. This is why, organizationally, it helps to separate the "parts producers" upstream from the specialized fabrication work downstream. At least that’s the theory.

Schuepbach added one big caveat, however, that affects how every fabricator is organized: "The market drives the projects."

The types and timing of those projects shapes daily life at a structural fabricator. The shop tries to land work where it excels—its sweet spot—but the market doesn’t just magically "give" a fab shop all the sweet-spot work it wants, when it wants it. So, a fabricator employs bidding strategies to build the best, most diverse portfolio of projects it possibly can, balancing fabrication capacity with myriad project timelines.

The local market, and the project mix it provides, also shapes the kind of people a structural fabricator employs and equipment it buys. It’s why some shops have huge beam lines while others get by with a band saw, mag drills, and a few expert welders.

On Developing People

One big reason why a fab shop acquires other fabricators has nothing to do with hitting a revenue goal. As Schuepbach put it, "Sometimes, it’s about attracting and developing leaders, good people with good character, and giving them opportunity to grow and lead."

Financial incentives are there, of course. For instance, some very large structural fab shops happen to be employee stock ownership plan companies (ESOPs). As Schuepbach described, a founding family might have transferred ownership to the ESOP, which in turn incentivizes every employee to help grow the business, making their retirement portfolio stock within the ESOP more valuable.

Regardless, immediate financial gain isn’t the goal here. "A few companies out there really want to do well and help people," Schuepbach said. "It’s not about meeting some overall growth goal within a certain period."

This really gets to the heart of structural fab: It’s a complicated, sometimes risky business, and there are easier ways to make a quick buck. But it’s also full of down-to-earth people eager to learn. Structural fabrication has never been a particularly easy business, but considering the good people it employs and the technological marvels it uses to improve productivity, it’s also not a bad way to spend a career.
