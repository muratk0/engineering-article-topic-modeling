"P

lease, take the job. Please. The organization needs you. It needs your leadership skills." That’s what Michael Helleson, information technology system manager at Special Products & Mfg. (SPM), told a member of the leadership team, Nora Harding, at the software conference they were attending in fall 2018. They had just gotten off the elevator together, and he sensed that Harding was thinking about something. He sensed correctly that Harding had been offered the position of director of operations by company President Ed Grand-Lienard.

This wasn’t foreign territory for Harding, who joined the metal fabricating company in Rockwall, Texas, in October 2011 as its human resources manager. She had grown with the company as it attempted to reinvigorate an organizational culture that would empower workers and grow leaders; a command-and-control structure had served the company well enough into the 2000s but wouldn’t allow it to scale as owners wanted. A consultant who visited the company in 2010 told CEO Rob Grand-Lienard (who was focused on the financial and human resource aspects of the business) and President Ed Grand-Lienard (who oversaw operations) that the organization lacked the flexibility to respond quickly to customer demands and industry trends. Decision-making was centralized, and the staff’s knowledge and expertise were being ignored. As a result, opportunities were being lost.

These two company leaders didn’t take the constructive criticism as an affront. Both had spent time in the late 1990s and early 2000s networking with others in local industry and joining executive coaching and peer mentorship organizations. They agreed that the third-generation company had to be different than the one their father had founded more than 60 years ago.

That’s why they wanted Harding, with her HR expertise and institutional knowledge, to take the reins, even if she might not have the technical chops of her predecessors.

"We went through three or four different iterations [of operations leaders], and all the while in my mind, I knew who was going to do it. And I knew that sacrificing the technical for the right skill set—her skill set—was going to be a benefit," Ed Grand-Lienard said.

When Harding heard the pitch again, she entertained the same thoughts: I’m not a metal fabricator and I’m not really an expert in the technology on the shop floor. However, Helleson’s pitch offered a new perspective: SPM needed her skills, not necessarily technical leadership.

She liked the people with whom she worked. The company believed in her contributions, having named her director of leadership development in 2014. And she had gained managerial and leadership experience, not to mention familiarity with continuous improvement methodologies, during previous positions in the water products industry.

This time the pitch made more sense. She would step forward for the organization that she valued, and which valued her.

"I originally took on the role with the mindset that it would be temporary," Harding said. "I always assumed I’d serve as director of operations until a permanent replacement could be found."

In those ensuing years, SPM has created an environment that is helping it to grow successfully with its customers and tackle jobs in new industries, all while having a workforce that is smaller than when Harding arrived in 2011.

SPM employed 230 employees when the company had those initial meetings with its business consultant. Several years later, revenue grew while the number of employees decreased.

"Today, we are doing more with less," Harding added.

SPM also has set up a separate machine shop in Rockwall and established an outpost in Round Rock, Texas, to be closer to customers.

Harding attributes that growth to the employees and the next generation of leaders at SPM. Over the years, documentation has improved, and automation has helped to overcome lost institutional knowledge. But people at SPM are contributing in ways they never have before. Is that the secret ingredient for success in metal fabricating?

"It is employee engagement for me. That’s what makes the wheels turn. You can only be as fast and good as what your employees are engaged in doing," Harding said.

Luckily, SPM’s culture-building is not top secret. It just takes a lot of time and dedication to make it work. Here’s how The Fabricator’s 2025 Industry Award winner has done it.

Over-Communicate

In the years following 2010, SPM management knew it wanted to create a new culture where decision-making wasn’t a product of the executive suite, but something that everyone in the company had a say in. But communicating such a change can be a challenge—even the basic aspect of trying to explain such a goal verbally.

"We had several conversations with our employees about changing the ‘culture.’ Our people immediately responded with ‘Culture? I love my heritage,’" Harding recalled. "We realized that words mattered, and we needed to explain the difference between a ‘company culture’ and an employee’s own birthplace heritage. Once that was clear, it opened the organization’s understanding and quickened the pace of the culture change."

The first step was to get people involved. SPM management assembled a cross-functional team to help with the original value stream mapping, looking at all the processes involved from bringing in work to accepting payment for that work.

From this exercise, SPM was able to document processes, and in some instances, root out wasteful steps. The process also opened the eyes of the cross-functional team members to other possibilities, such as offering assembly services.

Team members also began to define what attributes they would like to see as part of this developing culture and how they could spread the gospel to other SPM employees. This is where the use of "visual management enhancements," as Harding described them, comes into play.

For instance, SPM’s core values are summarized by the acronym BUILT:

Best people:

The company wants to attract and retain employes who are committed to working safely, challenging the status quo, striving for excellence, and celebrating successes.

Urgency:

Smart decisions need to be made confidently and quickly. A disciplined approach to processes ensures consistency, and collaboration creates transparency and clear expectations.

Integrity:

Communication is clear, consistent, and sincere. Employees take responsibility for mistakes and help to contribute to solutions.

Learning:

Everyone is encouraged to be coachable, show curiosity, accept feedback, embrace change, and think not just about the customers, but also team members, suppliers, and the community.

Teamwork:

Working together allows people to exceed expectations. It also fosters enthusiasm and drives positive cultural change.

These core values aren’t locked away on a sheet in a filing cabinet. They are painted on walls and shared on monitors that can be found all over the shop floor, in the front office, and even the break room.

This messaging also is part of SPM’s communication structure, from the management team’s annual strategic outlook meeting to monthly sales and operations meetings with department heads, weekly efficiency and schedule attainment meetings, and daily toolbox conversations and GEMBA meetings that provide an opportunity to address immediate issues. For special projects or hot jobs, project management meetings are held when necessary. Additionally, communication boards throughout the company are kept up to date, and messages are shared through the company’s Plex ERP system.

"This initiative keeps not just employees but also customers, suppliers, and visitors informed and aligned with our strategic initiative," Harding said.

Employees also have the opportunity to share their input through continuous improvement initiatives. SPM has a full-time continuous improvement team that assesses shop floor activities, making adjustments as new jobs are introduced and as customer demand changes.

The continuous improvement program also has an employee contribution component—anyone is encouraged to submit an idea for process improvement. All submissions are reviewed, and some contributors ultimately earn monetary rewards and receive companywide recognition—pictures of the quarterly winners are shared in the SPM break room.

"These strategic improvements have allowed us to enhance operational efficiency, reduce errors, and improve both equipment reliability and customer satisfaction, positioning us as a forward-thinking leader in process innovation," Harding said.

Develop Skills

Many metal fabricating companies in the U.S. realize that if they need an employee with a specific skill set, they are likely better off finding someone with a willingness to learn and training that person. Trying to find fabricating-related skills in the open marketplace, particularly in an area with a tight labor pool like Dallas-Fort Worth, can be challenging.

SPM recognized this several years ago and began an aggressive campaign to boost the skills of its employees. In 2023 alone, its employees received 1,115 hours of training.

To keep track of skills attainment and gaps, SPM leans on an add-on module that it purchased for its Plex ERP suite. Through the module, the supervisor can request training for a direct report. Human resources then can coordinate who is going to provide the training, when it’s going to happen, and how a final audit will be done once the training is completed. SPM then has a good handle on who can help out where when the shop floor work mix changes or when absences necessitate new assignments for the week.

"Our performance management process ensures the employees are consistently aware of how they are performing against key goals and expectations," Harding said. "This transparent feedback loop helps employees stay on track and work toward continuous improvement."

Special coaching and training initiatives also are part of what the company calls SPM University. This classroom time comes in handy when the shop brings in new fabricating technology or looks to implement a new process.

Harding said the company has extended this concept to work with customers too. SPMU-C is an educational initiative aimed at making customers aware of what the metal fabricator can bring to the table as a supplier of specialty services. Course topics have included "Metal Fabrication 101," "Design for Manufacturability," and "Design for Cost Reduction and Quality."

Harding said that as part of this large-scale training initiative, SPM wants to develop subject matter experts who can provide expertise and leadership in certain technology areas of the business. These folks have intimate knowledge of something like automated welding because they create the programs for welding cells and develop a familiarity with the process. They then are able to float among meetings with engineers and machine operators, providing perspective from real-life experience that improves the entire process because no one has to rely on educated guesses.

"Those are our experts. We utilize [them] to train our associates on the floor at operator level and look for efficiency gains in those areas," said Jacob Grand-Lienard, plant manager for welding, powder coating, assembly, and shipping. "Those are the guys who ask the questions like ‘How can we automate these parts? Where can we cut processing time out of these parts? How can we create a better flow for these parts?’

"They really know their departments. They know how it all works, the ins and outs," he added. "And they are going to use their expertise to make their areas better in whatever ways they can."

Lean on Technology

Automation has played an important role in SPM’s cultural transformation as well. It’s enabled the metal fabricator to take on more manufacturing capacity while simultaneously keeping costs down.

"New technology drives up efficiencies, sustains quality, and drives cost out," Harding said.

SPM has automated load/unload on all four of its AMADA punch/laser combination machines and its two AMADA fiber laser cutting machines. (For more on SPM’s combination machine expertise, check out "How to optimize the punch/laser combo," The Fabricator, June 2024, p. 56.) The company also has an automated tool change function on its newest punch/laser combination machine.

In the bending department, it has two AMADA EG AR robotic cells and three AMADA press brakes with automated tooling changers.

In the welding department, the company has four robotic welding cells with OTC welding power sources.

"Not only does the automation help us to stay cost-competitive, but it also makes us less dependent on tribal knowledge," Harding added.

Beyond the shop floor, one of the biggest changes for SPM came when it upgraded its ERP system to Plex in 2016. The company realized that its previous ERP software wasn’t going to be able to scale with its growth plans, so it found something that provided the functionality that could foster business expansion.

Harding said that the plan was to "systemize" everything possible within the new ERP. An early example was the creation of a customer "touch list," which could be used to ensure that SPM sales folks were contacting customers and prospects on a regular basis to understand their metal fabricating needs and to share company news.

SPM moved its sales personnel to offices that were right off the shop floor so that they could interact with the people in charge of fabricating parts.

SPM Executive Vice President Nora Harding points out how this visual aid in the break room reminds employees of every job that is active on the shop floor during a three-month period.

One of the reasons why SPM has been able to grow revenues in the midst of a very tight labor market is the use of automation, such as this robotic bending cell.

But perhaps the biggest change that new ERP software allowed was the automatic extraction of forecast data from customer portals. This gave SPM the ability to pull together capacity plans based on real sales orders and customer forecasts. It gave the company confidence in its planning and put it in a better position for making—and keeping—commitments down the road.

Now the company holds daily meetings to keep everyone updated on real-time changes. The long-range planning ensures that changes are not major fires but rather manageable adjustments.

SPM also has streamlined its quoting efforts as it moved to Paperless Parts. Having software that can generate quotes automatically in minutes instead of hours or even days promises to help the company be more responsive to customers, who increasingly need feedback as soon as possible.

Fight Stagnancy

Nothing lasts forever, and that applies to company initiatives as much as anything. New approaches ensure that employees pay attention, if only to find out what’s happening.

Harding said that SPM is always willing to change things up. It’s all part of continuous improvement efforts. If something can be done better, why not try it? No one said that continuous improvement had to be limited to shop floor processes.

One example is the addition of a safety component to the daily toolbox conversations before work begins for the day. These brief discussions keep the spotlight on employee safety. Conversation points might include potential safety hazards or good safety practices, but the focus is always the same: minimizing injury risk for SPM employees.

Harding said that these daily reminders, along with incentive programs, have created "a consistent and proactive approach to workplace safety." She points to this communication strategy as playing a key part in reducing the number of recordable incidents at SPM over the past several years.

Sometimes the change is not as subtle. For instance, Harding said she has always thought the engineering department should be split into those who ensure that repeat work flows smoothly to the shop floor and those who can see opportunities for design and production efficiencies when new work comes to the company. One set of engineers acts as the police, confirming that machine programming is correct and work instructions reflect company-approved practices. The other set of engineers sit in on the new product launch meetings, challenging the manufacturing status quo and pushing for innovation when it makes sense.

"It’s really two different mindsets. So we’ve broken those responsibilities up," Harding said.

Inspiration for change doesn’t just come from gut feelings or a suggestion box. Harding said that she regularly holds what she calls "listening posts," where she meets with people from different departments within the company and checks in with them. She can find out what’s working and what’s impeding progress straight from the folks in the thick of the action.

These listening posts also give Harding a chance to see if the company’s messaging is understood. Do employees know the company vision? Do they understand what the company values are?

"These conversations show me the deficits in the organization. Then I can go and attack those gaps," Harding said.

Keep Planning for the Future

Harding has been in the role of executive vice president for nearly five years, after serving as director of operations for two years. During that time, SPM has invested wisely in fabricating technology and has documented processes, helping to save institutional knowledge and make it accessible for everyone.

But more importantly, she’s prepped the new generation for success in leading the company—and not just the Grand-Lienard family.

"It’s great to see the development of all these leaders within our organization," Ed Grand-Lienard said. "There’s an attitude that they can do anything that they set their minds to. No task is too big. No hill is too tall.

"They’re ready and able to go and attack the future. There’s a lot going on within the organization right now to set them free."

The next generation of leaders believes that as well.

"I would put this team up against any team," said Jason Grand-Lienard, plant manager for punching, laser cutting, bending, and hardware insertion. "I’ve seen so many things that we’ve been able to accomplish together, and there’s still much more to do.

"It’s not just about celebrating. We recognize that there are areas where we’re not yet where we want to be," he continued. "Our expectations are high, and we have the confidence that we will continue to grow and be a successful organization."

Harding said she doesn’t know how much longer she’ll be leading SPM, but she is certain about one thing: The next generation of leaders will step forward and continue to push ahead into new markets and grow with existing customers.

Harding answered the call for leadership more than a decade ago. She’s certain others will follow successfully in her footsteps when the time comes.
