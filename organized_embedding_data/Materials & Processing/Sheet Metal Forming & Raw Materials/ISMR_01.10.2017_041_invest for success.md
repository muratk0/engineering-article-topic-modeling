# INVEST FOR SUCCESS

[TARİH: 01.10.2017 ISMR]

Voestalpine is investing over €100 million in the development and production of high-quality steels at its Donawitz site in Austria.

ISMR SAYS:

In line with its strategic focus on customer segments with high technology requirements, the voestalpine Group has decided to invest more than €100 million in two future-oriented projects at its Donawitz site in Austria.

One of the projects is to set up a new continuous casting line for producing extremely pure steels as a sophisticated pre-material for premium rails, quality rolled wire and hightech seamless tubes. The highly automated production line, with an annual capacity of up to 950,000 tons, is scheduled to start operation in 2019. The second project is to set up a new research centre on company grounds that is dedicated to developing even lighter, more corrosion-resistant and stronger steel grades.

"Two significant new projects are underway to develop new highperformance steels with higher strength, lower weight and better corrosion resistance

Voestalpine Group's Metal Engineering Division is headquartered at Donawitz, Austria. With its steel solutions, it is one of the world's leading providers of special rails for the railway infrastructure, rolled wire (especially for automobile manufacturers) and high-tech seamless tubes for the oil and gas industry.

## Steels of the future

"This major investment in a continuous casting line will enable us to produce even higher quality pre-material for our products and further expand our technology leadership in our key customer segments. This investment will also serve to secure the Group's Donawitz site," Franz Kainersdorfer, Member of the Management Board of voestalpine AG and Head of the Metal Engineering Division, explained.

voestalpine is setting up a 2800m² "Metallurgy Centre" research facility that will work on steels of the future, starting in 2018.

The new line, which will be set up parallel to the existing line and will replace it in the future, features fully digitised process control and has been designed with Industry 4.0 in mind.

On company grounds at Donawitz voestalpine is setting up a 2800m² "Metallurgy Centre" research facility that will work on steels of the future, starting in 2018. The centre will supplement its steel research centre at the flat steel site in Linz.

"Ongoing development of steel is the basis for ensuring that our final products meet the highest quality criteria. A small version of an entire steel plant will be built in the research centre, enabling us to transfer our findings directly to the large plants," Kainersdorfer told /SMR.

"The goal of the project is to develop new high-performance steels with even higher strength, lower weight and better corrosion resistance—especially for the mobility and energy sectors. The use of these future materials will allow, for example, stronger yet lighter drive rod components to be used in automotive construction, reducing fuel consumption," the Group told /SMR.

While the existing Metal Engineering Division operating facilities require a minimum production quantity of 68 tons of steel, development in the "Metallurgy Centre" will work with comparatively low weight units of around four tons. The characteristics of the new steel grades will be tested in normal machining processes for producing rolled wire, rail and seamless tubes. This should help to significantly shorten innovation times for high-performance steels and to supply customers with small quantities, something that has not been possible for the Group to date.

In business year 2016/17, voestalpine Group's Metal Engineering Division and its Steel, Rail Technology, Wire Technology, Tubulars, Turnout Systems and Welding Consumables business units generated revenue of around €2.7 billion, of which 40% was generated outside of Europe. The division, with over 13,000 employees worldwide, reported an operating result (EBITDA) of €361 million.

## About voestalpine

In its business segments, voestalpine is a globally leading technology and capital goods group with a combination of material and processing expertise. With its products and system solutions using steel and other metals, it is a partner to the automotive and consumer goods industries in Europe and to the aerospace, oil and gas industries worldwide. ■

## A SMOOTH FINISH

## By automating the surface finish process, Kaup increased process speed by roughly 50 per cent so that one cycle now lasts just eight minutes.

Kaup uses the slogan "Helping hands for your fork lift truck" to promote its products. The family-run company manufactures over 40,000 attachments for fork lift trucks annually for all types of fork lift suppliers and sells these around the world. A large portion of these is made up by the roughly 8,000 lift truck forks produced each year. Up to now, laborious manual dressing was needed after the welding process for the right finish. However, a fork welding robot from KUKA Industries now automates this job.

Everyone has seen the forks used on fork-lift trucks at some point. The manufacture of these and other attachments is done by Kaup – a family-run company in its fourth generation. In Aschaffenburg alone, the company produces a wide variety of "hands" for fork-lift trucks (for example, attachments such as multi-pallet handlers, slip-on arms, carton and drum clamps) across an area of 43,000m².

Approximately 8,000 standardised lift truck forks are produced using a fork welding robot in a system developed and installed by KUKA Industries. Automated welding of the two plates, with thicknesses of between 40- and 80mm, creates the L-shaped blank of the fork. In subsequent steps, the forks are dressed to size and the welds are ground to a smooth finish.

## Significant improvements

At Kaup in the past, dressing prepared fork blanks was a heavy-duty job primarily carried out manually by two employees in alternating shifts.

"In 2014, we realized that the day was soon approaching when both employees would leave us to go into their well-earned retirement," explained head of production, Ernesto Martin. "Today, it is nearly impossible to find workers who are prepared to perform such a physically strenuous task day after day. After all, we are talking about more than 40 forks per day here!

"This was one of the reasons prompting us to discuss an automation solution with our long-standing partner, KUKA Industries. A further motivation was that - as is the case with many other companies - the time required for surface finishing had never really been factored into our cycle time calculations. Every second is counted in the welding process, but manual dressing simply took as long as it did.

"As a result, these secondary tasks were ultimately a bottleneck in the production cycle. We had to change this before our employees' retirement in 2016."

In Autumn 2014, the stakeholders from Kaup and KUKA Industries began initial tests at the KUKA TechCenter in Obernburg. Specialists on both sides quickly realized that the supposedly simple task of automating the grinding processes depends upon an enormous number of parameters, sequences, measured values and tool wear analyses - while also requiring a great deal of experience. Only when all the factors interact in the right way can the desired result be reached, making any subsequent manual finishing work unnecessary. The new robot system now eliminates tolerances from the previous machining steps, as well as component tolerances, using pneumatic tools.

A practical example reveals how using a robot for grinding/dressing is anything but trivial. As Ernesto Martin explains: "We used the angle grinders and other grinding devices ourselves to understand exactly which sequence is required so that we could then develop the motion patterns for the robot. If, for example, the tilt angle is not constantly adjusted, pores clog up very quickly when using corundum polishers and flap wheels, drastically reducing their life."

## The automation of a logical sequence

KUKA Industries designed the system based on the expertise of the customer and its own automation specialists and installed it in Autumn 2015. The robot system is structured with the central element of the two-station system as the robot, with a payload capacity of 60kg. It can perform all the required surface finishing operations with a tool-changing system. An operator at each station loads two fork blanks, using a crane and clamps them onto two stationary clamping fixtures. Key data on the forks are entered into a parameter database and are assigned to a grinding program.

The first step is to measure the actual values of each individual fork since certain tolerances cannot be prevented in the pre-fabrication of workpieces with these dimensions (fork weight up to 250kg, fork thickness up to 80mm, fork length up to 3,000mm and fork height up to 1,100mm, as well as various inner radii).

The robot also fetches a radio transmission touch-probe from the tool-changing station for the relevant points. The measurement results are used as a basis for correcting the stored component program. A tool change then follows. The robot now uses an angle grinder with a flap wheel, which is operated pneumatically, as are the tools that are used subsequently. This allows them to work with greater flexibility on component tolerances. The program varies the contact pressure, according to the tool and workpiece.

Using the angle grinder, the robot dresses the outer front surfaces of the forks around the welded angle as well as the underside (onto which a wear plate has been welded). The outer radius of the fork is also rounded off using the angle grinder. A further tool change then takes place. The robot then picks up a grindstone, first moving it in front of a measuring device to determine the current diameter. This serves as a parameter for carrying out the grinding operation with the appropriate pressure and the correct motion patterns along the inside fillet weld of the fork. If the grindstone wears down below a minimum diameter, it is exchanged for a new one in the toolchanging station. The robot carries out the finishing touches along the inside fillet weld of the fork using a flap wheel which it picks up after returning the grindstone.

The working life of the angle grinder's abrasive paper and the flap wheel were determined through testing. Additionally, the operator monitors their state when they are set down in the changing station and can provide a new tool as needed.

## Setting the parameters

"The greatest challenge during programming was the flood of parameters we jointly collected with Kaup," explained Stefan Seiler, Sales and Projects Team Leader for Arc & Laser Solutions (EMEA), KUKA Industries. "Surprising as it may sound, there wasn't even a roughly comparable system for us to use as a reference."

The result was the creation of reference programs, which Kaup – using its in-house expertise – continues to successively expand and adapt to other tasks and workpieces. A wealth of information, such as the tilt angle pressure, feed rate, wear per grinding cycle, anticipated working life as well as adapted oscillating motions for grinding open the pores of grindstones and abrasive papers, is stored in the applications.

KUKA Industries and Kaup have jointly brought an area of manual production into the world of automation. As Ernesto Martin concluded: "With help from KUKA Industries, we have automated a process step which is very unpopular due to its physically strenuous nature as well as the noise and large quantities of dust that it produces. Humans will now only perform such dressing tasks when unusual special workpieces are involved. At the same time, we were able to increase the speed by roughly 50- per cent using the new system. A cycle now lasts just eight minutes."

Kaup is already planning further automation steps in deburring and chamfering.
