# Why metal fabricators can make good finishers

[TARİH: 01.03.2025 The Fabricator]

Editor’ Corner

Fab shops look to add more capabilities, like powder coating, because they understand the power of process improvement and employee engagement

DAN DAVIS

M

etal fabricating is complex enough. Can you imagine adding a powder coating line to the mix?

Many shops would reply with a quick "no" to that question. It’s a headache they don’t want. Also, a powder coating line, whether manual or automated, takes up significant room and calls for a sizable investment.

But more shops are showing an interest in finishing capabilities. It’s one more reason to keep current customers, offering them an additional service that other fab shops aren’t likely to have, and it might just be what sways a new customer to consider the shop for metal fabricating and finishing services.

Those aren’t the only reasons, however. Some metal fabricating companies simply want more control of lead times and quality, both of which are challenged when outside contractors, like custom coaters, operate on their own schedules. A contracting company might be considered a partner, but when it comes down to it, a fab shop is likely one of many customers that the contractor has to please. Schedules, as a result, are more flexible than a metal fabricator might like.

So the motivations to take on finishing responsibilities are clear. The choice to focus on powder coating is just as clear. The process doesn’t involve volatile organic compounds, produces relatively little waste, and is somewhat easy to apply.

That doesn’t mean that there isn’t complexity. Parts must be cleaned and prepped before powder is applied. Powder must be applied consistently, and it needs to be cured within a specific temperature and time range. Equipment requires cleaning. Pretreatment chemicals must be monitored. Dry-off and curing oven performance must be checked.

Fortunately, metal fabricators know all about attention to detail and proving out processes. That’s how they successfully introduce new jobs to the shop floor, leaning on thorough production part approval processes that ensure new work is ready to be fabricated without hiccups. That’s how quality assurance programs operate, confirming that parts meet customer specs at blanking, forming, welding, and finally finishing.

That’s why some fabricators aren’t intimidated by becoming powder coaters. They are in the business of figuring out how do to something and then working to improve.

Traditionally, the role of applying powder was for entry-level employees or even temps. That still might be the case in some shops, but high-performing operations know they need engaged employees to get the most out of any production process, including powder coating.

"The definition for quality parts is consistency," said Brian Korecky, district sales manager for Bulk Chemicals, in this month’s feature story "What’s behind the powder coating defect?" Korecky was speaking about the pretreatment process, but the idea applies to the entirety of the powder coating process. If a shop can dial down its maintenance schedules, quality checks, and production steps, it can make just about any process perform as expected.

Of course, a process is only as good as the people involved. Metal fabricators understand this as well. It’s one of the reasons they are always talking about finding the "right" people for the job.

Traditionally, the role of applying powder was for entry-level employees or even temps. That still might be the case in some shops, but high-performing operations know they need engaged employees to get the most out of any production process, including powder coating. Standing there and moving the spray gun in the same pattern and at the same speed for upwards of four hours is not something easily done. But those who can do it and are motivated to keep with it because of further opportunities are valuable.

"The good technicians out there, they can spray a part, and it becomes kind of a game," Mark Mortensen, president, All-Color Powder Coating, told The Fabricator for this month’s powder coating feature. "You look at a powder-coated part and say, ‘You got 4 mils on there.’ The good technician will say, ‘No. It’s 2.5 mils on there.’ Usually, the good ones are right."

That doesn’t sound like much, but a savings of 1.5 mils of powder over hundreds of parts is noteworthy. Extract that same attention to detail over a year’s time, and that veteran powder coating technician is saving his employer significant dollars.

Enlightened fab shops get that. They are committed to skills development for everyone in their shops, including those in the powder coating department. They aren’t going to let those finishing technicians languish there without some sort of discussion of what the future might hold. Training is good for the individual, the employer, and even the customer waiting for finished parts.

Fab shops don’t need to powder coat to be successful. But if they want to be that one-stop shop for the customer looking to cut one purchase order for parts, they have to consider the possibility. Powder coating is not for the faint of heart, but metal fabricators know all about that already. If they choose to pursue powder coating, they’ll figure it out.
