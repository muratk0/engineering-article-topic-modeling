# Expertise makes the most of machine technology

[TARİH: 01.10.2025 The Fabricator]

Plasma Cutting

ProFab finds a competitive edge with its combination plate processing machine

By

Dan Davis

ProFab uses its K5000xmc multiprocess machine for thermal cutting, as well as drilling, tapping, and part marking. But the company’s use of the machine as a horizontal mill is something that’s somewhat unique in the metal fabricating community.

Images: ProFab Manufacturing

T

he metal fabricating business is complex, but one of the keys to success is somewhat less so: Offer something that others can’t.

That’s how ProFab Manufacturing in Anderson, S.C., has approached the market in the years following its founding in 2016. It’s an offshoot of Lakeside Steel and Machine, which was a metal distributor that started to dabble in some value-added fabricating activities exiting the Great Recession in 2009. When Lakeside Steel’s founder, Jack Jenkins, retired, his son, Brad Jenkins, took over and pushed the ProFab side of the business to the forefront.

"We wanted a fresh name, a fresh look—the whole nine yards," Brad Jenkins recalled. "We were going to be doing more manufacturing at that point."

In the ensuing years, the company added more fabricating and machining equipment to bolster its capabilities. In 2019, it added a 12- by 12- by 20-ft. powder coating booth and a 10- by 10-by 30-ft. curing oven, something not too many others offered in the area.

When it came time to replace an old plasma cutting table, Jenkins said he didn’t want to just replace the equipment with something similar. He wanted something that could accommodate multiple processes, which would minimize material handling, possibly eliminate secondary processes, and separate ProFab from competitors still running simple burn tables.

He liked what he saw in a multiprocess machine from Kinetic USA, so his team attended the North American Steel Construction Conference in St. Louis in early 2019 to take a closer look at the machine. Jenkins verified his initial feelings about the Kinetic K5000xmc after a conversation with the machine tool company’s engineers, and he decided to make the purchase. He had big plans for the combination machine.

Something Different

ProFab took delivery of the machine from Southern States Machinery in early 2020, and it was up and running by the end of February. It immediately started paying for itself, according to Jenkins.

The company had won a big defense contract, and a nearby manufacturer with similar cutting capabilities was going through a reorganization and looked for a contractor to take over metal manufacturing for it. As the months went by, word got out about what ProFab could do.

"That machine has opened doors for us where we otherwise wouldn’t have been able to open," Jenkins said.

The K5000xmc has plasma and oxyfuel cutting options. The burn table is 10 by 27 ft. It can plasma-cut carbon steel up to 2 in. and oxy-fuel-cut even thicker materials. It also has the ability to bevel, which is something that ProFab technicians used a lot in the beginning as it was learning about the machine.

The machine also can drill holes up to 4 in., tap holes up to 2 in., mill, thread mill, mark parts with plasma etching, counterbore, and chamfer. Jenkins said ProFab works mostly with steel and stainless steel.

But what ProFab is doing differently with the equipment is that it’s using it as a horizontal mill. It’s not what the K5000xmc was originally designed to do, but Jenkins said he saw the potential—and the ability to do what no one else around him was doing.

"A lot of structural companies that have stuff like this, they’re not looking at tolerances like we look at tolerances," he said. "They’re working off of a 1/16 or 1/32. We work off thousandths."

On the back end of the machine, opposite of the burn table, is a 4-ft.-deep pit. This provides the shop with a 7-ft. window under the head.

"We’ve got some precision beams that we’ve machined down at the bottom of the pit," Jenkins said. "When we did that, we built our fixtures off the bottom of it.

"We can actually adjust the height of it when you’re setting up to do these things. We’ve used it all kinds of ways."

The machine’s operating window and the pit’s 40-sq.-ft. size provide ProFab with 10- by 40-ft.-long machining capability. A gantry or bridge mill of that size would be enormous and likely wouldn’t fit inside the 50,000-sq.-ft. shop.

"We put everything it takes to run a machine part on it, even probes. Positioning wise, we don’t have any problems," Jenkins said. "It’s the only machine like that that I know of in the world."

Jenkins said that he has some very skilled craftsman on his staff of about 30. From the very first day of delivery, they helped to prepare the machine for precision work. That started from installation and progressed into learning about what the machine could and could not do. It took about two years before ProFab started using the machine to its full potential, according to Jenkins.

Now his machinists are able to conduct some sophisticated maintenance on the multiprocess machine, such as changing a spindle. ProFab has a staff that benefits from the augmented reality glasses that machine tool vendors rely on for remote support because they know the inner workings of the machine.

Jenkins recalled the early days of running the machine and how his staff challenged the technical experts back in New Zealand, where the Kinetic machines are made. "They didn’t have a lot of techs that could answer the questions that we had at that point," he said.

The K5000xmc multiprocess machine’s automatic tool changer can hold 24 tools.

Changing the Game

ProFab’s Kinetic K5000xmc operates as a horizontal mill for approximately 30% of the day. When it’s being used as a burn table, machinists can work on setups on the milling side.

The horizontal milling activity actually requires different software to run. Kinetic’s Primecut software is used to run the thermal cutting side of the table, but ProFab is using Mastercam for its milling activity. This requires some give-andtake with Mastercam as the metal fabricator has to routinely engage with the vendor for new postprocessors—or posts—which sort of translates generic CAM software instructions into specific code that guides the toolpath.

This type of interaction isn’t unusual because ProFab is consistently being pushed to try new jobs.

"There’s not a machine around here that’s big enough to handle these things, so you’ve got to kind of look outside of the box when you’re looking at doing work," Jenkins said.

As an example, he described how a local company needed help with some holemaking on 30-ft. subway rails. The rails required a series of precisely placed holes, which proved to be a problem for other nearby fab shops.

An automated beam line sounds like a perfect fit for this type of work, but that’s not the type of job for which companies buy beam lines. They don’t have that equipment for limited-run work; they’re literally pushing tons of steel through those automated machines for larger projects.

Jenkins said ProFab took on the job without too much consternation. The shop built a fixture for the 40-lb. rails, set up the program for the Kinetic machine, and knocked out the holemaking.

"When you have a machine like this, you have to be willing to dive in and do things," he said.

Meanwhile, Jenkins added that they are still challenging the Kinetic technicians in the Southern Hemisphere.

"We were doing some tapping, and they told us that we had to use a tension compression system because the tapping heads had to have a clutch on them so they wouldn’t break. Well, we tried to form-tap these things, which convert that to a rigid setup and run your spindle faster, but you’re actually forming the thread instead of cutting it," Jenkins said. "So we ended up with a form tap.

"Kinetic said it couldn’t be done," he added. "I can now show them that we can do it all day. Now, we’re drilling and tapping a hole in about three seconds."

Not Worrying About Other Shops

As for other fab shops stepping up and trying to duplicate his company’s fabricating capabilities, Jenkins isn’t too worried. He believes Pro-Fab’s skilled team is hard to duplicate, and he wonders if other shops even have the desire to be as creative as it has been when it comes to maximizing capital equipment investments.

Other shops already have multiprocess machines—or "glorified burning tables that can do drilling," as Jenkins calls them—but these competitors are mainly interested in producing round holes, which simple plasma cutting struggles to do. Laser cutting can cut more precise holes than plasma, but that is going to take longer than drilling. Laser cutting capabilities also are a lot more expensive than more traditional thermal cutting alternatives.

Jenkins said ProFab wants to pursue more than just holemaking jobs. He knows the shop can do what others can’t.

"I understand why people haven’t figured it out. A lot of people don’t want to," he said.

For ProFab’s next act, Jenkins is looking at adding laser cutting capability. But he won’t be adding another machine. He wants to add a laser cutting head to another gantry that would be placed on the Kinetic. Laser cutting would not generate nearly as much dross as plasma cutting.

That’s a couple of years down the road, but Pro-Fab wants to be ready for the next key equipment upgrade. Staying in front of the competition is a good place to be.

ProFab’s burn table is 10 by 27 ft. and can plasma-cut carbon steel up to 2 in. and oxyfuel-cut even thicker materials.

ProFab added powder coating in 2019 in a bid to offer a highly sought-after value-added service to its customers.

ProFab believes in paying its skilled employees their worth. Brad Jenkins, the company’s president, said it helps to reduce turnover and rewards employees for being engaged and producing top-notch results.
