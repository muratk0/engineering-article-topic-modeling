Sheet metal is one of the most widely used products in manufacturing today. Long flat sheets of steel, aluminium, copper and other metals (pressed and rolled) have transcended the ages, enabling technological developments every step of the way. Today, sheet metal is part of some of the most exciting engineering projects and, with new methods of online manufacturing, its potential for innovation is exponentially expanding.

Online manufacturing – think 3D printing, CNC machining and injection molding (enabled by AI, and connected via a distributed network of partners) – has been growing in popularity amongst product engineers with its instant quotes, high turnaround and lack of minimum quantity orders.

## Prototyping, trial and launch

As the relatively new industry ages, it continues to add new capabilities. One such capability is the introduction of sheet metal fabrication by European B2B online manufacturing specialist, 3D Hubs, earlier this year. The process allows engineers to increase the speed of iteration, with the test cycle reduced to days or weeks rather than months.

The speed of iteration when prototyping has allowed for some unbelievable feats of engineering. Take the story of a start-up founded by four graduates from the University of Toronto. Kepler Communications' goal was to put a satellite into space, enabling the transfer of large amounts of data from remote parts of the globe, benefiting everything from the maritime to the mining industry.

Making anything that needs to be launched into space is extremely expensive.

However, the Kepler team managed to go from a sketch on a napkin to its first functional nanosatellite launched into space within 365 days, enabled by the power of online manufacturing. By 2021, Kepler expects to have 50 satellites in orbit.

This rapid process of prototyping, trialing and launching products has seen the manufacturing market open up to entrepreneurs and engineers. Sheet metal today is used in the production of everything from electric vehicles and personal drones to air conditioners and soda cans. But did you know that it was used by the ancient Egyptians and sketched by Leonardo da Vinci?

Sometimes the best way to see what the future holds is to look to the past. Here are four ways in which sheet metal has played a crucial role in developing our civilisation:

## 1. Leonardo da Vinci's rolling mill

One of the earliest mentions of sheet metal in the modern period is in a drawing by Leonardo da Vinci. The Renaissance master – who died 500 years ago this year – sketched a rolling water mill in 1485. The mill was to be turned by a running stream and would then, in turn, power a sheet metal rolling mill.

The sketch was the first time that press and roller technology (passing material between two cylindrical rollers to adjust its thickness) was raised in history. Whilst da Vinci's invention sat on the back-burner until around 100 years after his death, his thinking took mankind a step forwards.

Da Vinci also used a similar concept to move forward the standardisation of the coin press. Up until the 1700s, coins were made with painstaking precision by hand. Along with other artists and engineers in the Renaissance period, da Vinci made around 1,500 machines for the production of currency.

## 2. Nicolas Briot and the coin press

It wasn't until an innovative French coin engraver by the name of Nicolas Briot invented a way to press coins evenly that sheet metal became widely used in society. Although he produced the coronation medals for King Louis XIII, the French Government believed he was a fraud and refused to implement his technology on a large scale.

As a result, Briot fled to England, where he was welcomed with open arms. He began to work with Charles I of England to develop the technology on a large scale. The technique quickly spread to continental Europe, giving governments the ability to produce large quantities of currency with striking accuracy.

The invention of the coin press standardised the production of currency on an international level, subsequently putting sheet metal production in the hands of the masses. Just in time for the Industrial Revolution....

## 3. The Industrial Revolution

Fast forward a hundred or so years to the 18th century and demand for sheet metal dramatically increased due to the Industrial Revolution. Innovations including the assembly line, the press brake and steel fabrication drove the mass manufacturing of the material and, in turn, contributed to increased sheet metal production.

What was once powered by elbow grease, water or animal-labour power became cheaper, quicker and more robust thanks to an ample supply of energy. During this time, sheet metal was used in the transport industry to create innovations such as the automobile, the metal-winged aeroplane, railway cars and the marine steam engine. Entrepreneurs at the time used the new technology to invent products which, without sheet metal, wouldn't have been possible, let alone on a mass scale of production.

These inventions inevitably shaped the world as we know it today. Time and distance were separated like never before. Communication from one side of the world to the other became more efficient and travel no longer relied upon expendable energy.

## 4. The Vespa and Enrico Piaggio

At the end of the Second World War, Italian heavy vehicle manufacturer Piaggio's main factories in Tuscany lay in ruins. When Enrico, the son of Piaggio's founder, saw the dire straits of Italy's cities, its crippled economy and the country's potholed post-war roads, he decided to rebuild the factory and use Piaggio to address Italy's need for affordable transport.

By 1946, the first Vespa was born. Using a wealth of knowledge from aircraft assembly lines, including the lightweight sheet metal casting, he clad the body in sheet metal. The look became a defining factor of the innovative mode of transport, contributing to its name (Vespa means 'wasp' in Italian) and its inclusion in the patent.

The original Vespa's sheet metal exterior skeleton gave a new world view on the material's social and developmental potential. Vespa did not just influence Italian post-war culture, but the scooter also influenced the international attitude towards sheet metal's interaction with style. The creation of such an icon inevitably gave way to the exponential growth of the sector.

## Today's sheet metal industry

We often consider the use of new forms of materials as the next stage of our evolution and the periods of change that mark the beginning of new chapters in our history books. However, it's not their accessibility that is the exciting part; it is the innovation that it enables. The ability to design and manufacture modern life in a meaningful way.

As sheet metal continues to be a strong and inexpensive way to make parts, demand has grown steadily throughout the years. Today, its introduction to online manufacturing is the beginning of a new wave of accessibility.
