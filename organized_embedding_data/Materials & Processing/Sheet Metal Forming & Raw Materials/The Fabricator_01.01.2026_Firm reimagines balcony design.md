New approach meets structural demands while eliminating the need for welding

Dan Davis

ob shops usually can’t share the successful designs they create for customers, and OEMs don’t go out of their way to publicize their own product designs. So when you get the chance to chat with someone whose company has introduced a new product that differs greatly from what is commonly done, you jump at the opportunity.

Early in the fall, BŌK Modern, San Rafael, Calif., sent out a press release announcing the introduction of its latest architectural metal fabrication creation—the Bolt-On Balcony. What stood out visually about the new product was its sleek design; it was not dependent on cantilevered steel supporting the bottom of the balcony or hanging systems from above. It also required no welding.

"Architects love this. It’s a much cleaner look than other balcony designs," said Russ Naylor, BŌK’s co-founder. "It also can be provided in a kit of parts for ease of installation."

INSPIRATION FROM A PREVIOUS PROJECT

Key to the Bolt-On Balcony design is a patented folded plate arrangement that creates an integrated truss structure within the balcony sidewalls for added strength. The design, which makes it possible for the entire balcony to be supported by just two vertical attachment points at the façade, successfully handles any additional weight, such as furniture or people.

Naylor said the inspiration for the design came from another project where BŌK was tasked with providing panels to clad a fabricated truss system that cantilevered out to the street about 25 ft. and out to the corner about 40 ft. To save costs on the building project in San Francisco, BŌK reconsidered the overall design and suggested the use of folded plate to replicate the support offered by the original truss design. It didn’t hurt matters that the new folded plate plan provided a more interesting look than the traditional truss system.

"The idea of eliminating the steel trusses completely and have it be 100% bulk panels all sounded a bit outlandish at the time," Naylor said, "but we actually got somebody that could engineer it. We found that if we bumped up the gauge to ¼ in. that this approach would work."

BŌK Modern’s inspiration for its new balcony design came from work done on this canopy on a San Francisco building. Instead of a covering over a traditional steel truss system, the design firm suggested the use of folded plate. The new design provided the support needed for the structure and created a more visually appealing fabricated canopy.

The Bolt-On Balcony from BŌK Modern features an integrated truss structure in the sidewalls, eliminating the need for traditional balcony support systems, which can be unsightly and lead to water damage in some instances.

BETTER FOR WATERPROOFING, INSTALLATION

According to Naylor, this balcony doesn’t come with the same waterproofing worries associated with traditional cantilevered balcony systems. With those designs, moisture can work its way along the structural supports and into the actual structure, causing damage over time. This is especially a concern in wood-framed buildings.

The Bolt-On Balcony is mounted to the outside of the building, relying on moisture-resistant stainless steel or galvanized steel brackets that are affixed to the façade. This minimizes disruption to the building’s waterproofing treatments.

In addition, the balcony’s floor panels have folded edges that, when connected, can collect any moisture and direct it to the sides or even a downspout. This prevents water from collecting on the balcony and keeps downstairs neighbors dry if they happen to be out enjoying the weather.

Each balcony—made from powder-coated or anodized aluminum, as well as Cor-Ten steel—is shipped in a flat-packed kit to the jobsite. The kit contains 16 laser-cut parts, all from the same sheet, and the bolts to connect the pieces. The entire balcony can be assembled on the ground and lifted into place when complete.

"This is to avoid getting into a lot of welding," Naylor said. A single tradesman can tackle assembly, which helps to keep labor costs down and increase quality control on the site, he added.

In the future, BŌK is planning to enhance the balcony design with wood flooring, expanded sizes, and integrated lighting options built into railing panels.

For the record, Naylor and BŌK don’t fabricate all of these balcony pieces, relying instead on a local metal fabricator that the company has worked with for several years. The relationship works well, especially for a firm that started out doing architecture.

"It’s all because an architect would ask us, ‘Hey, if you can do that, can you do this?’ Naylor said. "We would reply, ‘Yeah, I don’t see why not,’ and we would get it done."
