abricators need to know what they’re working with. They have material certifications from their suppliers, of course, but that’s just a paper trail—not a physical test. Positive material identification (PMI) tests prove the material they received is indeed the material they asked for. Material identification and testing in the fab shop can include not only the base material chemistry, but also the chemistry of plating and conversion coatings.

Thing is, most fabricators who process a mix of carbon, stainless, and aluminum and other nonferrous grades have no all-in-one tool that works equally well for everything. Hand-held X-ray fluorescence (XRF) units work well, but they can’t detect precise levels of carbon. Optical emission spectroscopy (OES) excels at carbon detection, even picking up minute amounts, but the technology usually isn’t very portable.

Although laser-induced breakdown spectroscopy can detect carbon levels in steel, it requires a clean surface. The laser vaporizes the topmost layer, whether that’s a clean surface of carbon steel or a surface with oil, slag, or rust.

XRF is more forgiving of dirty material, and measurement is quick—basically point and shoot. The hand-held device can identify material usually within two or three seconds, depending on the application. Considering this, if a fabricator works with carbon steel, stainless, aluminum, and a variety of other grades, when does XRF make sense? To find out, The Fabricator spoke with Michael Hull, director of product management, analytical instruments, at Evident Scientific, Waltham, Mass. He explained that finding the answer starts with a simple question:

What do you need to accomplish?

THE POWERS OF DEDUCTION

"Today, OES remains the gold standard if you need to have a precise measurement of carbon content," Hull said. So, if an operation needs to know the exact carbon level in a material, or if it needs to differentiate between two very similar grades of carbon steel—1018 versus 1014, for example—" XRF really isn’t going to be the solution."

This has to do with the way XRF "deduces" material grades when working with carbon steel. Knowing how this works can reveal how the XRF can work in certain applications involving carbon steel—again, depending on what needs to be measured.

When X-rays hit iron or other heavy elements, they force the electrons to create secondary fluorescent X-rays, giving the XRF device the "signature" it needs to detect the element. Light elements like carbon don’t behave this way and, hence, can’t give the device the signature it needs.

That said, the XRF can detect other heavier elements within carbon steel, giving software the information to deduce what grade the steel is likely to be. If it detects iron and manganese but no chromium, nickel, or molybdenum, XRF knows it’s 10xx series carbon, and—based on the levels detected—can make a rough assumption on the specific grade.

As Hull explained, certain elements leave better "trace fingerprints" than others. Chrom-moly steel is a prime example. When the XRF device detects iron and manganese as well as chromium and molybdenum, the system software knows that plain carbon steel never contains those elements. So in this case, the software identifies the grade as AISI 4140.

As Hull explained, if the goal is to sort and prevent material mix-ups—and if precise measurement of carbon levels isn’t needed— XRF can work very well, especially considering how portable and quick the technology is.

XRF IN THE METAL FABRICATION VALUE CHAIN

For applications outside carbon steel, "we see fabricators creating their own [material] certifications. They test the material as they receive it. Then, at every stage in the value stream, they test to prevent any material mix-up.

"Then, at the shipping dock, they do a final quality check to confirm the material they’re loading matches what was ordered, as well as what the XRF measured previously earlier in the value stream," Hull said.

He added that although XRF can’t be used to certify a carbon steel’s composition, it can certainly help sort material through the value stream, he added.

Weld testing, especially for stainless applications, is another popular way to ensure, say, an ER308 filler metal is within spec—except for the carbon element.

So, if you’re trying to verify that a low-carbon filler metal variety, like ER308L, is truly low carbon, XRF isn’t the answer. But as Hull explained, XRF can help prevent wire mix-ups.

XRF is a surface measurement, so if material is plated, the technology will detect only the composition of that surface plating. But verifying that plating or other surface treatments happens to be a big application area for XRF.

"When you have [plate] coatings between 2 and 15 microns, that’s really the sweet spot," Hull said.

Conversion coatings represent another major application area. Chromate, phosphate, zirconium, and others all can be detected by XRF, which can measure thickness, coverage, and elemental composition. This can help when, say, troubleshooting a paint adhesion issue.

TABLE STAKES

XRF isn’t a panacea, especially for fabricators working with a full range of carbon steels. But in many end markets today, material traceability and verification represent table stakes.

"The classic example is aerospace," Hull said, adding that aerospace manufacturers are demanding (and are often required to have by regulation) 100% material verification for certain products.

"And it’s understandable, considering these products are mission-critical. If something goes awry when a rocket goes into space, things can go very wrong very fast."
