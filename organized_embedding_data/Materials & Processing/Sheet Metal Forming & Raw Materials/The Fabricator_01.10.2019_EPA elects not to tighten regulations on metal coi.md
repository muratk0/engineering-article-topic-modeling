fter a detailed technology review of air emission control technology at large metal can and metal coil facilities, the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) has decided not to tighten emission standards for hazardous air pollutants emitted by those facilities. The agency, however, has made some changes in the emission standards first adopted in 2002 for can and 2003 for coil.

The primary hazardous air pollutants (HAPs) emitted from both sectors are glycol ethers, xylenes, toluene, ethyl benzene, and a few other chemicals. The agency found that the initial standards provided an ample margin of safety for humans and the surrounding environment, and that’s why no changes were made to emissions standards. The minor changes the EPA wants to make involve new reporting to the agency related to manufacturing startups, shutdowns, and malfunctions. In those instances, emissions of regulated HAPs sometimes exceed, temporarily, the standard in each of the two NESHAPs (National Emission Standards for Hazardous Air Pollutants). As a result, the EPA is proposing a new requirement that the metal processing facilities estimate the quantity of each regulated pollutant emitted over any emission limit and provide a description of the method used to estimate the emissions. Examples of such methods would include product-loss calculations, mass balance calculations, measurements when available, or engineering judgment based on known process parameters (e.g., coating HAP content and application rates).

The EPA is proposing this requirement to ensure that the agency has adequate information to determine compliance, the severity of any failure to meet an applicable standard, and to document how the source minimized emissions during a failure.

Possible Changes to Silica Standard at OSHA

The Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA) is thinking about broadening its acceptable control methods for silica dust in the construction standard first issued in 2016. (Silica is a substance generated by metal grinding and other metal fabrication processes that can be harmful to humans who are exposed to it over long periods of time.) Although machine shops are covered by the separate 2016 general industry standard for silica, they may benefit from changes to the construction standard.

The minor changes the EPA wants to make involve new reporting to the agency related to manufacturing startups, shutdowns, and malfunctions. In those instances, emissions of regulated hazardous air pollutants sometimes exceed, temporarily, the standard in each of the two National Emission Standards for Hazardous Air Pollutants.

The construction standard includes two options for construction firms to stay on the right side of silica regulations: meeting the new permissible exposure limit (PEL) or incorporating the proper engineering and work practices that minimize exposure to workers. These engineering and work practices are associated with 18 common construction tasks listed in the regulations as "Table 1." Many of these activities are related to work done with materials such as asphalt, concrete, mortar, and rock.

Sectors like metal fabrication, which is covered by the general industry standard, must meet the PEL, except in some limited circumstances where they, too, can adopt Table 1 controls. The PEL for respirable crystalline silica adopted in 2016 for both construction and general industry is 50 mg/m

(1) The task performed is indistinguishable from a construction task listed on Table 1. (2) The task will not be performed regularly in the same environment and conditions.

The request for proposals that OSHA issued in August opens the door to expansion of that leeway on two separate grounds: a potential increase in the 18 tasks, which might cover some tasks done regularly in metalworking, and new flexibility for machine shops to access alternate control technologies when they are regularly performed in general industry or maritime settings in a relatively stable and predictable environment. Most of these control technologies are centered around using tools in accordance with manufacturers’ instructions to reduce dust emissions and dust control equipment.
