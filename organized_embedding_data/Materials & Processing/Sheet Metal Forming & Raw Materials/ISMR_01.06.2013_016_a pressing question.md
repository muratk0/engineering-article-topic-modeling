# A PRESSING QUESTION

[TARİH: 01.06.2013 ISMR]

ISMR SAYS:

“Pressing and forming technologies have evolved to meet the requirements of today’s metal stampers”

Press forming is a metal working process in which a flat blank, constrained between two surfaces, is forced by a punch to take a required three-dimensional shape such as cups and gas cylinder domes. The metal may be re-shaped by a combination of drawing, stretching, ironing and bending processes. Production of some panels may require a combination of annealing, re-drawing or reverse re-drawing after the initial forming operation.

Stamping, or pressing, includes a variety of sheet metal forming manufacturing processes such as blanking, embossing, drawing, trimming, piercing, flanging and coining. This could be a single stage operation where every stroke of the press produces the desired form on the sheet metal part, or could take place over a series of stages. Stamped components are made by forming, drawing, trimming, blanking or piercing metal, in sheet or coil form, between a die.

The flexibility provided by press forming in terms of product design and size enables the manufacture of a diverse range of products. Flat-screen TV body panels and built-in compressors for refrigerators, air-conditioners and washing machines are examples of products manufactured by press forming.

A wide array of products of different sizes can be formed using presses, ranging from simple shapes requiring a single process to more sophisticated products requiring multiple processes.

## Choosing your press

Sheet metal blanks are formed in either hydraulic or mechanical presses – each type has specific advantages and applications. Shallow pressings can be completed in single-acting presses using springs or die cushions to provide the blankholder pressure. Deeper pressings and irregular shapes are generally produced in double-acting presses where consistent drawing speeds, stroke adjustment and uniformity of clamping pressure are available. Factors such as capacity requirements, die space and length of stroke are important considerations in press selection.

Most pressings require a number of stages to complete a given part design. To achieve this, separate presses can be run sequentially or automated transfer systems can be used to move a part between stages, either within the bed of a single press or between separate presses. Various specialised press forming operations are available such as the hydroforming process and the stretch drawing technique where unidirectional strain is applied to the blank prior to drawing. These specialised operations have been developed for specific demands which cannot be met by traditional presses and tools.

Environmental, cost and safety considerations, in sectors such as automotive, are driving the evolution of new press forming processes and technologies.

Press drives can be mechanical, hydraulic, servo or pneumatic, with gap, straight-side or C-frame designs, and single – or double-slide (ram) connections. Mechanical presses can additionally be flywheel; single-geared; doublegeared; double-action; link motion (also called alternative slide motion) and eccentric-geared. Hydraulic presses are particularly suitable for deep-draw applications because they can apply full tonnage over the complete length of the stroke.

With increasingly stiff competition, the differentiating factor for automotive manufacturers seeking dominance is the creation of body designs that attract consumers, lighter vehicles that boost mileage and stronger vehicles that enhance crash safety. These trends are resulting in new challenges for the press industry. Increasingly sophisticated forming technologies are becoming necessary for both the forming of exterior panels for body designs and the forming of lighter and stronger frame members that reduce vehicle weight and improve crash safety.

Stamped components are made by forming, drawing, trimming, blanking or piercing metal, in sheet or coil form, between a die.

Achieving the three-dimensional outer body panel designs that automotive manufacturers seek requires unprecedentedly high-level drawforming technologies - servo presses, where the forming speeds could be precisely controlled, answered this requirement. A servo press can provide positional control that is not possible using a conventional press, and this has enabled controls that offer precise synchronization between the press and the transfer equipment. A servomotor gives a press slide motion flexibility in terms of accurate speed, motion and position control. This flexibility produces infinite slide motion variations that may improve part quality and operations such as painting and assembly during one press stroke.

Factors such as capacity requirements, die space and length of stroke are important considerations in press selection.

“Servo presses are being employed increasingly by manufacturers, a trend that is most prominent among European automobile manufacturers,” press manufacturer AIDA told ISMR. “Servo press manufacturers are currently locked in a fierce race to develop new products. Amidst this intense competition, we believe that strengthening our ability to create forming systems incorporating presses with peripheral equipment will be key to differentiating ourselves from the competition.”

The two major components of cold forming tooling are punches and dies. Punches transfer the required force from the machine to the material and provide close dimensional control on the internal part features. Dies confine and direct the material into the desired geometry and provide close dimensional control on the external part features.

## The evolution of pressing technology

In recent years, the automotive industry has been taking environmental concerns into consideration and has been promoting automotive production that uses light yet strong materials to improve fuel mileage and thereby reduce CO emissions. Since the automotive industry is now using more lightweight high-strength materials that are more difficult to form, even more advanced press forming technologies have become necessary.

It is now possible to work even difficult-to-form materials that could not be formed using conventional presses, such as titanium alloys used for watch cases and the magnesium alloys used to make the lightweight cell phones and computers that consumers crave. Another example is a motor core (electrode) used for the motor of a hybrid car. This needs a precise and delicate process technique to laminate multiple ultra-thin metal plates and only a press can be used to achieve this result.

The development of high-strength steels that can be formed at room temperatures is lagging in the United States and Europe, and thus “hot presses” that perform warm-forming processes which utilise heat treatment for hardening are widely used. This forming method consists of heating steel sheets to a high temperature – approximately 1,000ºC – then forming the sheet and quickly cooling it, and the resulting maximum strength exceeds that of high-strength steels.

Today, the earth is beset with various environmental problems such as global warming. The manufacturing industries bear some of the responsibility to resolving these issues and they are required to contribute to environmental protection as part of their business activities, regardless of their type of business. Some press manufacturers, such as AIDA, have formulated press forming production methods which conserve energy and resources (reduced energy consumption, recyclable materials etc.). Press forming is an environmentally friendly process as the levels of waste produced and energy used are relatively low.

These next generation press forming technologies support the enhanced performance and multifunctional abilities of products. ■

Oil and gas pipelines are an essential part of hydrocarbon transportation and distribution, required to ensure the smooth operation of the energy industry. The market is currently experiencing a number of important changes, with the factors driving spending differing considerably between each region. In particular, escalating oil and gas demand in Asia and the growth of the oil sands and tight oil markets in North America are creating opportunities for companies involved in the pipelines industry.

Market analyst Visiongain has determined that the value of the global oil and gas pipelines market will reach US$ 47.35bn in 2013. At present 103,378km of pipelines are under construction, planned or proposed throughout the world.

“Oil prices were relatively stable during 2012, held back by the economic slowdown in Europe and the sluggish nature of the US recovery, among other factors. Political uncertainty over the direction of tax and fiscal policy in Washington may have had a negative impact on the deal market during 2012. The total 2012 value of global energy deals reached US$ 321.5 billion, compared to US$ 300.6 billion in 2011, despite a drop in the number of deals from 698 to 576,” expert analyst Deloitte added.

## Trends and directions

An increase in offshore discoveries is prompting a surge in exploration activity across the Middle East and Africa and driving up the amount spent on drilling, states the latest report from business intelligence firm GBI Research. The company’s latest oil and gas report forecasts offshore drilling expenditure across the region to climb steadily from US$ 13.56 billion in 2012 to US$ 17.03 billion in 2016. Cumulatively, the total spend of this five-year period reached US$ 77.3 billion, which represents an increase of approximately 22 per cent over the 2007-2011 total of US$ 63.5 billion.

Drilling outlay is expected to grow across all major nations in the region, with those in West Africa leading in terms of exploration activity. Escalating activity in countries relatively new to the offshore drilling industry, such as Sierra Leone and Liberia, may prove to be future competition for the more established nations of West Africa.

“Ghana is expected to emerge as one of the most prominent countries in West Africa for the exploration of oil and gas, with 16 offshore discoveries made between 2008 and 2012 – second only to Angola, where 22 discoveries were made during the same period,” said GBI Research.

In terms of drilling expenditure, Angola is expected to remain the biggest spender in the region by some margin, over the next few years at least. GBI Research expects drilling expenditure in the Southern African country to continue climbing in the near future, hitting US$ 6.67 billion in 2016. Nigeria and Egypt are forecast to place second and third, with totals of US$ 2.26 billion and US$ 1.52 billion, respectively.

Qatar sits on the world's third-largest proven gas reserves which are concentrated in the offshore North Field. The country's robust efforts to commercialise these deposits have resulted in Qatar becoming the world's largest liquefied natural gas (LNG) exporter. BMI sees Qatari gas production growing to nearly 190bcm by 2022. Given the economy's robust growth outlook, Qatar's domestic gas consumption is also set to rise quickly, to as much as 72bcm. BMI expects a 12 per cent increase in oil production in the period 2013-22, with an associated small increase in net exports.

Escalating oil and gas demand in Asia and the growth of the oil sands and tight oil markets in North America are creating opportunities.

Shale gas is set to remain a largely regional resource over the next one to three years with an uncertain global impact due to the increased technical challenges and higher development costs of the resource. This is according to the Deloitte Touche Tohmatsu Limited (DTTL) 2013 oil and gas reality check report. The study focuses on the primary challenges facing the oil and gas industry including: shale gas, liquefied natural gas (LNG) pricing, resource nationalism, national oil company (NOC) expansion and market complexity.

## East Africa

According to Visiongain, East Africa is the world's newest oil and gas hotspot.

“After being neglected for decades the region is now among the fastest growing oil and gas markets in the world. Following the first oil discovery in Uganda in 2006 and gas discoveries along the Mozambique and Tanzanian coast, the region has attracted interest from an increasing number of international oil and gas companies. Offshore exploration has increased exponentially and, over the last five years, East Africa has developed from a frontier region to becoming a possible game changer in the global gas market,” explained Visiongain.

East Africa covers a territory roughly six times the size of the North Sea yet less than 500 wells have been drilled in the region to date. The reserve estimates in the region have increased rapidly over the past few years and yet only a small percentage of the region has been properly explored. The market has changed too, as oil majors increasingly drive out smaller companies indicating the industry's confidence in East Africa's immense potential.

“Deepwater gas for large-scale LNG export terminals has become the biggest story in East Africa and attracted worldwide attention. Onshore, commercial oil production will start within the next few years and several oil and gas infrastructure projects are under development bringing billions of dollars of investment to the region. With its wealth of oil and gas reserves and vast future potential, East Africa will be the industry's hotspot for the next decade,” continued Visiongain.

Visiongain has determined that the value of the East African oil and gas market in 2013 will reach US$ 3.9bn.

## Latin America

Latin America is also emerging as one of the world’s most rapidly growing energy markets. Despite the unparalleled opportunity and progress this region offers, Latin America faces several environmental, economic and social challenges that highlight the need for ongoing business reform and growth, according to a report released recently by Deloitte Touche Tohmatsu Limited (DTTL), “Getting to Par: Spotting the Sand Traps in Latin America Oil and Gas”.

The report identifies six specific areas – referred to as “sand traps” – where oil and gas companies, as well as individual countries within Latin America, will need to focus in order to maintain momentum and leverage their expanding presence on the world stage:

## ■ Fuelling the foreign investment dragon:

While foreign investment has initiated short-term benefits and trade relationships, governments should incorporate their FDI policies into a longterm, comprehensive development strategy, encouraging less reliance on raw materials and natural resources and, instead, enabling domestic firms to further develop themselves as competitive, self-sufficient exporters.

■ Untangling Latin American energy policy: Divergent regulatory, tax and financing regimes are identified as key deterrents for many foreign investors. In addition to political clashes and internal opposition, fuel specifications, industrial emission limits and caps on carbon production also pose challenges to potential investors, limiting foreign participation in the industry to service contracts.

■ Minding the productivity gap: Latin America’s businesses are not yet competitive with their counterparts due to inefficient operations and antiquated concepts around wage and performance.

■ Solving infrastructure barriers: Physical and social infrastructure constraints are seen as key limitations in Latin America’s growth potential. Without secure and predictable networks for transportation, finance and materials supply, these businesses face detrimental uncertainty in logistics and risk.

■ Relieving the talent crunch: Shortages of experienced talent and skilled labour are more acute in National Oil Companies (NOCs) versus private firms as the former are slower to develop differentiated technical expertise and employ fewer technical personnel. As a result, labour has escalated as a primary concern for 45 per cent of the region’s employers in 2012, compared to 28 per cent of employers in 2011.

■ Managing the realities of NOCs: With an estimated 230 billion barrels (bbl) in proven oil reserves in Latin America, resource nationalism is on the rise as governments consider these assets critically important to their energy security and socio-economics goals.

The report notes that, despite these impeding issues, in 2010 Latin America had the world’s most rapid growth in both outward and inward foreign direct investment (FDI), with inflows up by 40 per cent over the previous year, and outflows up fourfold to hit an all-time high of US $43 billion. ■
