Aviation supplier meets load capacity requirements with overhead material transport

SITUATION

Aviation industry supplier Premium Aerotec produces aluminum skin panels, which form the basis for the subsequent fuselage sections, at its plant in Nordenham, Germany. The firm uses a crane as an overhead handling device to lift the individual component carriers with the fuselage sections into special pressure-tight kilns called autoclaves, and then transport them to an ultrasonic test facility after the bonding process.

These tasks were performed by a double-girder overhead traveling crane with box-section girders and four 1.1-ton hoist units.

"Since the loads that this crane had to lift several times every day weighed around 4.1 tons, we were always working close to the limit," explained Andreas Voge, who is responsible for assembly system factory planning at Premium Aerotec.

Planning for a new investment became more specific when the double-girder overhead traveling crane, which dates back to 1961, came up for refurbishment. To make the production processes more flexible, Premium Aerotec planned to invest in two single-girder overhead traveling cranes that could operate separately as well as in tandem.

Because of the load-bearing capacity of the building structure, the two new cranes were not allowed to exceed the deadweight of the existing crane and, therefore, their wheel loads were not to be any higher.

RESOLUTION

The company acquired two Demag V-type cranes from Terex Material Handling. Each has a span of 24 m and is equipped with two Demag DR-Pro 1.5-ton rope hoists. The cranes satisfy all of the company’s conditions, and their reduced deadweight enabled the rated load capacity to be increased by a few hundred kilograms so the company is better prepared for future developments.

"Thanks to the V-type crane’s reduced oscillation characteristics, the jigs can be picked up and deposited in a more controlled way."

—Fred Mass, Premium Aerotec

"By using two cranes, we have gained more flexibility. The cranes can also perform various transport tasks along the 115-m-long crane runway," said Voge. "However, they still are mainly used to serve the autoclaves. To do this, both cranes work in tandem, and the four hoist units operate under synchronized control."

"Thanks to the V-type crane’s reduced oscillation characteristics, the jigs can be picked up and deposited in a more controlled way. Simple switchover to tandem operation, as well as the possibility to position components gently and carefully, has resulted in swift acceptance of the new cranes by our crane operators," said Fred Mass, Premium Aerotec’s coordinator of material handling equipment supplied by external vendors.

Safety during regular operation was a high priority for the company. According to Mass, "Thanks to the V-type structure and vertical struts, [the cranes’] weld seams can be checked at a glance. The regular visual inspections can be completed within a quarter of an hour."

North America’s largest parts leveler ends fabricator’s quest for flat metal parts

SITUATION

O’Neal Mfg. Services (OMS), Birmingham, Ala., is one of the largest manufacturers of complex metal fabrications for the heavy equipment industry. All of its 10 plants are ISO 9001: 2008-certified, offering 1.5 million sq. ft. of outsourcing support for OEMs in the agriculture, construction equipment, material handling, railroad, and power-generation industries.

In 2013 the company acquired an Arku FlatMaster 88 parts leveler for its Greensboro, N.C., plant, but the capacity wasn’t enough for a recent prospective project.

"Our Birmingham facility was quoting some business that required a tight flatness tolerance on parts larger and thicker than our machine in Greensboro could handle," said Gene Gadient, an application engineer at OMS. "We have a gantry flattening press that could do the work … but the gantry press is a trial-and-error method that takes an experienced operator. It may take several hits from both sides of a part to get it flat."

The contract for the Birmingham plant largely involves mild steel nearly 1 in. thick, with a minimum yield of 36 to 50 KSI. The parts, cut from plate by laser or plasma machine, vary widely in size and shape, some with openings. A typical flatness requirement is 0.07 in. across a 60-in. diameter.

Circles, triangles, and parts with openings are a particular challenge with a hammer press. "You can chase a high spot for quite a while," Gadient explained. "It’s a very inexact science."

RESOLUTION

The company decided to acquire a second Arku FlatMaster leveler, a model 120-200, at the Birmingham facility. The machine is unique in the U.S.: It is the largest FlatMaster parts leveler in North America, allowing OMS to level parts up to 1.675 in. thick and 78 in. wide.

Each part presents a changing cross section as the leading edge, center, and trailing edge enter the leveling rollers. These changing cross sections require varying levels of force to maintain the leveling gap. The leveler’s servo-hydraulic system can recognize any change in the required force in a fraction of a second and adjust to maintain a precise gap.

More than just leveling the parts, the machine also stress-relieves them, making them much easier to weld or bend. "We found in Greensboro there’s a definite advantage to using flat parts in our downstream operations," said Gadient. "It is a unique manufacturing asset."
