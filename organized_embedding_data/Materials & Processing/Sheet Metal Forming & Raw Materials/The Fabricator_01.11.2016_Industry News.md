DAVI hosts Thick Plate Fabrication Process Seminar

DAVI, a manufacturer of plate and angle rolling machines, rolled out the carpet to welcome fabricating customers to its Cesena, Italy, facility for a Thick Plate Fabrication Process Seminar, Oct. 6-7. More than 100 delegates from at least four continents attended the event.

"The color of our machines connects with the manufacturers of the area," said DAVI President Orazio Davi (see

Figure 1

), referring specifically to the red of the DAVI machines and the iconic

rosso

of nearby Ferrari, but also making the connection to the manufacturing and engineering heritage of northern Italy.

During the seminar, fabricators had the opportunity to learn about technology developments in the areas of plate rolling, weld preparation, and welding from experts at DAVI, PEMA Welding Automation, and Lincoln Electric, respectively.

Orazio Davi set the tone for the twoday event with a warm welcome to all the delegates and by expressing his gratitude to the guests for participating in the 50th anniversary celebration of the company. During his introduction, Davi recalled the company’s growth, which was founded by his father Vincenzo in 1966. He said that the Davi family is building the business based on the same passion and commitment that his father had and that the company will be in business for many more generations.

During the technical sessions, Stefano Santoni, DAVI heavy duty product and market manager, continued the machine focus with a presentation on the differences among traditional three-roll, pyramidal; four-roll; and the three-roll, variable-axis machines (see

Figure 2

). Santoni said that four-roll units are wellsuited for high-volume work involving the same thickness of material, for example pipes, offshore foundations, or wind towers, while the three-roll, variable-axis machines are a good fit for a shop just getting into or that does a limited amount of plate rolling.

Figure 2

The MAV series of plate rollers from DAVI are three-roll, variable-axis machines that provide the flexibility to take on large- and small-diameter jobs that a plate shop might see in a typical workday.

Santoni described the three-roll, variable-axis equipment as the "perfect" choice for the job shop that looks for versatility and flexibility to take care of a variety of jobs involving plates of different thicknesses and hardness. Pressure vessel, boiler, and heat exchanger fabrication typically requires those types of material.

The key is the independent movement of the rollers. The top roll can descend to the plate, like the ram of a press brake. The two bottom rolls can move independently along the bottom of the plate to offer support as the bend is being made. For example, as the plate is fed into the plate roll, one of the bottom rolls can be positioned very close to the descending top roll, leaving a minimal unbent flat at the beginning of the rolling process. (The other roll is located far enough away not to affect the pinching process taking place between the other rolls.)

Figure 3

Narrow grooves required for weld prep on large plate rolls can be made by mechanical machining, and the PEMA CM 55 milling machine for circular seams was designed to deliver that type of edge preparation.

A three-roll, variable-axis machine can deliver a minimized unbent area, like that delivered by a four-roll machine. In both cases, it depends on the pinching capacity between the top roll and the pinching bottom roll.

Santoni added that, because the rolls have a freedom of movement, a threeroll, variable-axis machine also has higher capacity in rolling both smalland large-diameter jobs.

This type of machine also performs rerolling/calibration better than other plate rolls, according to Santoni, because the bottom rolls can hold a higher load and the rolls can deliver a more favorable adjustable geometry. It should be noted that pressure vessel work doesn’t allow for those fabrications to be rerolled. Because of this, as far as the machine capacity allows it, the unbent area must be minimized to respect the geometrical tolerance. Otherwise the initial flat portion has to be cut before the can is completely closed.

Teemu Tolonen, application manager for welding and production automation company PEMA Welding Automation, headquartered in Finland, shared some of the advancements tied to intelligent programming, smart weld preparation, and efficient welding of these types of preparations.

Tolonen explained that with smart programming methods, fabricating heavy workpieces and preparing the most demanding groove shapes for weld prep with robots are now feasible for any sized shop. High-volume work is not the only way to recover the original investment on a robot. PEMA has developed special tools for the rapid creation of different product shapes, Tolonen said. These tools are able to create welding programs automatically to the shapes of products. In the most demanding applications, he said, sensors are used to verify the real shape of the welding groove, compare the groove to the product model, and adapt the welding to the original specification.

In the second part of the presentation, Tolonen explained the benefits of using a milling machine to deliver a narrow groove instead of relying on standard oxyfuel-cut edge preparation (see

Figure 3

). This type of approach can lead to tremendous welding consumables savings, particularly for those applications that require literally tons of wire for repeated, heavy-duty work, according to Tolonen.

Michael Flagg, Lincoln Electric’s manager of application engineering and technology, shared news about the electronic developments in the company’s power sources that are now giving them a level of control over the arc that the equipment was never able to deliver. For example, its Power WaveR AC/DC 1000R SD power source allows for the use of extended electrical stick-out for submerged arc applications. This translates into increased travel speeds for the welding head in these heavy-deposition jobs. In one instance involving a 5/16-in. horizontal fillet weld for which an AC balanced square wave was set on the control and a 5-in. stick-out was used (at 775 amps and 40-41 V), the travel speed was boosted by 80 percent.

ASHRAE releases new performance standard for dust collectors

Metal fabricators now have a consensus standard to use in evaluating dust collection systems. Dust collection is critical in facilities that generate hazardous substances during welding, laser and plasma cutting, and thermal spray applications.

ANSI/ASHRAE Standard 199-2016 is a voluntary test method for filtration manufacturers. It was developed by the Atlanta-based American Society of Heating, Refrigeration, and Air Conditioning Engineers (ASHRAE) as an alternative to Minimum Efficiency Reporting Values (MERV), a rating system developed for HVAC filters but applied to dust collectors in the absence of more relevant test methods.

Standard 199 measures the performance of a dust collector on three factors over 48 hours of pulse cleaning, variable dust loading, and failure and recovery that mimic real-world conditions. The entire system is evaluated—not just the filter element—and a test report includes:

• Operating pressure drop on the filters, which relates to operating cost and filter life.

• Particulate matter in the vented emissions, an indication of efficiency.

• Energy consumption for compressed air to clean the filters

On Sept. 21-22, Amada welcomed more than 200 customers to its Schaumburg, Ill., facility for Innovation Expo. The event highlighted advanced digital processing technologies, automated bending processes, and the design and processing of parts for laser welding. It highlighted the ways in which shops of all sizes need to consider how they will adapt to demands for higher-precision part specifications and finished-part quality.

All machines on the exhibition floor included the advanced digital processing capabilities of the company’s Virtual Prototype Simulation System (VPSS) 3i software. The software simulates not only individual production processes, such as cutting, bending, or welding, but also analyzes data from all Amada’s machines a fabricator owns. The software takes into account all steps in the production process and determines the best way to process a part before a piece of sheet metal even gets near a machine.

As an example of the adaptability of the software, attendees were given a demonstration of how it changes a part’s parameters if it is going to be laser welded (see

Figure 1

) rather than going through a GMAW or GTAW process. In this case, an electrical enclosure was processed, and the design was optimized using a mortise and tenon joint to reduce fixturing during the welding process.

This demonstration was followed by a walk-through of each process required to make the enclosure on Amada’s verification floor. During this time, customers saw the LC 2515 C1 AJ punch/fiber laser system at work, as well as the HG 8025 press brake and the FLW 4000 fiber laser welding system.

Rotating Lens Adds Capability to Laser Welding Machine

One of the more interesting innovations included on the FLW is a patented rotating lens. As the company explained it, this lens does not align the laser beam on a specific point along the target path. Instead, it circles a small area as it welds. This allows it to bridge larger and uneven gaps while producing a clean weld.

Daniel Belz, product manager, fiber welding, noted that a product such as the electrical enclosure could take as much as 10-20 minutes to weld by hand, plus postprocessing. Laser welding can reduce that time to just a few minutes, and postprocessing can be virtually eliminated. The key is ensuring that the job in question warrants a laser welding system.

Proposed ASTM standard to help engineers with stainless steel projects

ASTM Intl., W. Conshohocken, Pa., is creating a consolidated reference guide for engineers and consultants who want to use stainless steel in transportation and construction projects.

"The proposed standard will be a practical guide for engineers to use when plans and specifications are being put together for project approval," said ASTM member Christopher Hahin, an engineer of structural materials and bridge investigations for the Illinois Department of Transportation. "It will list all kinds of stainless alloys, including austenitic, martensitic, ferritic, and duplex, as well as tests for preselection."

The standard is being designed for structural engineering consultants; engineers at federal, state, and local transportation agencies; manufacturers of light pole, sign, and signal structures, as well as bridge fabricators; and other makers of equipment for bridges and transportation structures.

Hahin noted that an appendix to the standard will allow corrosion engineers, stainless steel manufacturers, and testing organizations to contribute new alloys to it. Recommendations for preselection of alloys before they are used for certain applications or locations also can be made.

MSCI calls for federal action to address trade law violations hurting U.S. aluminum industry

Rolling Meadows, Ill.-based Metals Service Center Institute (MSCI) has announced that R. Holman Head, vice chair of its board of directors and president/COO of O’Neal Industries, recently testified before the U.S. International Trade Commission (ITC), calling for tougher government action to halt unfair and illegal trade practices, particularly by China, that have harmed the U.S. aluminum industry.

Head urged "decisive action, and continued vigilant monitoring" in a program to address dumping, predatory pricing, and currency manipulation.

"MSCI data shows that U.S. shipments of aluminum in 2015 were down nearly 20 percent since the pre-recession peak in 2006. Unfair trading practices by certain foreign governments, particularly China, whose economies are free market in name only, are largely to blame for that," Head said. "Global demand for aluminum has stagnated and declined. Meanwhile, according to U.S. government and International Aluminum Association data, the Chinese share of the global aluminum market has exploded from approximately 10 percent in 2000 to nearly 55 percent last year. China’s government-subsidized aluminum production glut, currency manipulation, and other unfair trade practices have weakened the aluminum industry."

MSCI member service centers supply industrial metals to about 300,000 manufacturers and fabricators. Service centers purchased an estimated 2.1 billion lbs. of aluminum mill products in 2014, according to the Aluminum Association. On behalf of MSCI, Head recommended that federal policymakers:

1. Vigorously monitor compliance with free trade agreements and promptly take action against violators.

2. Negotiate with trading partners directly to reduce global excess capacity.

3. Make it a stated principal objective of U.S. trade policy to target excess capacity in countries, such as China, that increase production through marketdistorting policies.

4. Expand efforts to enforce antidumping and countervailing duties, which currently cover only aluminum extrusions.

5. Declare that the Chinese government is a currency manipulator and immediately initiate negotiations to address the causes of the undervaluation.

6. Resist efforts by China to be declared a market economy in the World Trade Organization, which would help insulate it from investigations and enforcement actions aimed at its unfair trade practices.

"China is harming the primary aluminum market in North America," said AEC President Jeff Henderson. "We’ve seen these tactics being used in China’s aluminum extrusion industry for years. China uses their production of semifabricated aluminum to flood the global market with unnecessary aluminum."
