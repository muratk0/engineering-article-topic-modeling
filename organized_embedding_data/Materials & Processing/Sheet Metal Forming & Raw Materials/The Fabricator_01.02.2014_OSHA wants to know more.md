Confidential injury and illness information at risk

Some metalworking companies might have to start reporting injury and illness information that is now considered to be confidential, if the Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA) gets its way.

OSHA’s proposed rule, issued late last year and aired at a public hearing in Washington on Jan. 9, would require companies with more than 20 employees in select sectors to submit their Form 300A

annually

in an electronic format. Form 300A is a summary of injuries and illnesses.

The annual 300A requirement would apply to companies in select Standard Industrial Classification (SIC) codes, including all subcodes in 31-33. These are primary manufacturing codes. For example, 3398 covers metal heat treating and 3399 covers primary metal products. But 34-level codes are not included, meaning companies that fall into the codes 3444 for sheet metal work and 3441 for fabricated structural metal would be exempt from the requirement.

However, if a company in any SIC code employs more than 250 workers, it would have to electronically submit Form 301 and Form 300 quarterly in an electronic format.

Currently, companies subject to OSHA’s injury and illness reporting rules—generally those employing more than 11 people—have to complete Form 301 (Injury and Illness Incident Report) for each injury and illness and Form 300 (Log of Work-Related Injuries and Illnesses), a compilation of all those injuries and illnesses. In addition, each year the employers must use the information from these forms to complete Form 300A.

“OSHA has not provided any reasons to show how making this previously confidential information public will advance safety.”

—Miles Free, Precision Machined Products Association

OSHA gets direct access to those forms only if the company is subject to an inspection. However, as part of the OSHA Data Initiative, all companies subject to reporting must submit select data from Form 300A in paper form once every three years, if they qualify as a high-injury workplace. That affects about 1 percent of all companies covered by the reporting requirement, or about 80,000 workplaces.

“OSHA has not provided any reasons to show how making this previously confidential information public will advance safety, and it removes the presumption of privacy rights from the employer and employees whose data and information would be reported,” said Miles Free, director, industry research and technology, Precision Machined Products Association.

He is particularly miffed that OSHA has pushed the proposal while claiming it adds no new requirements to keep records. OSHA apparently believes the new rule only modifies an employer’s obligation to transmit these records to OSHA.

He called that claim “disingenuous at best when the proposal in fact removes the privacy of the employers’ data and changes its status from confidential to publicly accessible.”

Import Charges on Imported Hot-rolled Steel Remain

The U.S. International Trade Commission decided in mid-December to keep in place existing antidumping and countervailing duty orders on hot-rolled steel products imported from China, India, Indonesia, Taiwan, Thailand, and Ukraine. The ITC found that revoking the orders would likely be harmful to U.S. companies and workers who produce steel products used in a variety of applications, including automobiles, railcars, construction materials, appliances, machinery, and oil country tubular goods.

These cases always pit U.S. producers of steel products against U.S. buyers of imported steel. The former press for import restrictions and for keeping them in place; the latter oppose them, wanting to retain access to cheaper steel. In this case, the producers won out, in large part because of strong congressional support for maintenance of the countervailing and antidumping duties. That support came from 19 senators from both parties and 50 members of the House Congressional Steel Caucus.
