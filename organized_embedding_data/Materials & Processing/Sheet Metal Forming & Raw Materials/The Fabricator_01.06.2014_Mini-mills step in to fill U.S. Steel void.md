# Mini-mills step in to fill U.S. Steel void

[TARİH: 01.06.2014 The Fabricator]

METALS NEWS

Editor’s Note: This information is provided by the staff at

MetalPrices.com

.

Ferrous scrap prices may take different paths in the coming months. Industrial steel scrap such as No. 1 busheling could be on the high road, with its price climbing. But obsolete grades like heavy melt and shredded scrap could be left behind, or even lose some value.

Steel sheet products, or lack of them, have become the driving force for finished steel markets. U.S. Steel’s production problems at two of its mills —a furnace outage in Detroit and shortage of iron ore at Gary, Ind.—provided an opportunity for mini-mills that make steel sheet to step in and fill the void. That could boost demand for No. 1 busheling, which has been overhanging the market in some industrial regions. But stronger demand won’t produce more busheling. Its output is a function of manufacturing activity; busheling comes to market regardless of the price. Thus, some flat-rolled mills may be outbidding each other for this grade or reaching into remote regions where more may be available. However, they often must pay higher prices to draw it away from local users and offset higher freight costs.

None of these mills are likely to run short of scrap. Higher prices will encourage dealers to sell all they have on hand and what they expected to receive in May. Also, some mills are still owed scrap from April because of the rail transport problems. That is easing now that warmer weather has arrived. Some dealers told us that they have finished shipping March orders and now are delivering scrap sold in May.

Most mills also expect to see an adequate supply of the obsolete scrap now that winter has ended. New demolition projects often begin in the spring, and those postponed by the cold weather resume operation. Collection of scrap by the smaller dealers and peddlers also rises as the weather warms. This year some expect a huge increase because snowstorms and cold temperatures were more severe and sharply reduced scrap flows into dealers’ yards during the winter.

At the same time, the ferrous export market has not rebounded as much as anticipated. During the past decade, U.S. scrap exporters have been siphoning off about 20 million tons per year from the nation’s obsolete scrap pool. But that has declined by one-third or more in the past two years. That could leave another 500,000 or 600,000 tons of heavy melt and shredded scrap looking for homes on U.S. shores. Combined with spring pickup in supply, this could help to drive down prices in May and June.

Many industry members said their outlook for both steel and scrap demand into the third quarter and beyond remains uncertain at this time. The iron ore shortage and operations problems at some integrated mills have created an acute and immediate need for more sheet steel at this writing. Many of the electric arc furnace sheet producers have indicated that they are more than happy to step into the breach.

Lead-times on orders have stretched out to June for some cold-rolled products, and new price increases announced at the start of this supply crisis are sticking. How long this boom will last for the mini-mills is undetermined. Most steel users expect the integrated mills to be back up to speed by the end of June at the latest. Whether scrap suppliers will get to share in the bounty is uncertain. The seasonal spike in obsolete scrap flows and weakness of export demand also could cool expectations that prices will rise.

Most expect the iron ore shortages to be resolved and the demand for prompt scrap to be lower, but June and July also are months when the automakers take vacations or shut down plants to retool for new models. That typically shrinks the flow of prompt scrap into the market. At the same time, the pent-up supply of heavy melt and shredded scrap will have subsided. The spring thaw, if it generates as much scrap as some expect, will drive those prices lower and reduce those flows. Unlike busheling and other industrial grades, heavy melt and shredded scrap are very price-sensitive.

Global Outlook

The worldwide recession drags on with the main manufacturing drivers—the U.S. and China—struggling to grow their economies. China has clearly disappointed expectations across the commodity complex. Driving these disappointments are credit concerns, anticorruption initiatives, and pollution policies. Most notable is the subtle shift from manufacturing to a service-oriented economy.

In Europe, car manufacturing is a bright spot, but other sectors remain tepid. The indecisiveness of both the European Union and individual governments is creating barriers to investment and prolonging the economic recovery. As a result, the investment community is sitting on the sidelines, unwilling to make commitments without political leadership. The impasse is preventing opportunities for growth and contributing to poor consumer sentiment.

Minor events will have a minimal impact on prices, but without a major event or policy changes, prices will show an agonizing range reflecting weak demand and less-than-impressive growth. Our nonferrous team anticipates slow to moderate growth, particularly as we head toward the traditionally sluggish summer months.

Aluminum

In the first quarter of 2014 the LME aluminum price was flat. Since the beginning of the second quarter, though, the LME aluminum price increased by $75 MT. Premiums, however have increased $90/MT, with duty-paid premiums increasing from $290 MT to $380 MT. The market is waiting for more information from the LME about new warehouse rules and how to deal with the Rusal lawsuit. The LME cash to three-month spreads have tightened as the result of the slight inventory deficit in Europe and the U.S. Conversely, inventories in China are building steadily, with our sources reporting CIF Shanghai premiums at $350-$355 MT.

European secondary smelters are running at capacity as high line car sales remain strong. India’s demand for secondary grades of scrap improved over the short term, particularly sheet and cast aluminum. With global physical demand sideways, the forecast for the next three months will be relatively flat.

Copper

High-grade copper scrap continues to be tight since the beginning of the year. However, with disappointing demand from China’s construction sector (which is copper-intensive), there has been a minimal effect on premiums. European demand is steady, but scrap shortage has some consumers substituting cathode for scrap, particularly in brass manufacturing. Copper premiums are currently $130-$140/MT, which is within the $5/MT range of the beginning of the year. We expect weak demand from China for this quarter, similar to the first.

Nickel

The increase in demand, in addition to the ongoing Indonesian ore export ban and possibly a Russian export ban (both Indonesia and Russia are large producers of nickel pig iron), has caused the LME cash price to rise from $13,900/MT in early January to $17,000/MT in May. Compounding the shortage are the LME warehouse stocks, which decreased by 7,000 MT in early May. With low levels of scrap available, processors are competing for the available stainless steel and nickel units and struggling for low-margin material to make blends for the steel mills. The next quarter will show if this is a sustained demand from mills.

Zinc

There has been a $10/MT drop in LME premiums since the beginning of the year to $150-$160/MT. Material is readily available in Europe, with premiums dropping depending on the size of the purchase. China again is slow and has no current demand for zinc units. Zinc seems to be somewhat in balance until China returns to the market.
