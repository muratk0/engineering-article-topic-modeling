# Sheet metal clinching 101

Joining

The basics behind an often overlooked joining method

By

Byron Selorme

W

hen you walk into a fab shop, you’re bound to see sparks flying from welding stations and hear the clatter of hardware insertion machines. These traditional joining methods are staples of metal fabrication. However, another method—sheet metal clinching—often flies under the radar, but it’s gaining traction for its unique advantages.

The joining method has roots that go back to the 1800s, but it didn’t gain widespread adoption until the mid-1970s, when the automotive industry started looking for faster and more efficient assembly methods. By the 1980s, both European and American car manufacturers had developed advanced punch-and-die clinching systems.

Today, clinching has become increasingly popular in the automotive industry and among manufacturers of appliances, HVAC systems, and electronics. Its fast operation, ability to maintain metal integrity, and installation versatility make it a good choice for creating strong, reliable joints in everything from car hoods and trunks to refrigerators, washing machines, HVAC components, electronic devices like printers and computers, and channel letter signs.

Sheet Metal Clinching Basics

Instead of adding hardware, clinching relies on reshaping the metal itself to form a durable bond (see

Figure 1

). It involves placing two or more metal sheets between a punch and a die. The punch forces the sheets into the die, deforming the material to create a localized interlock, or "button," that mechanically holds the sheets together. Completed in a single press stroke, the process is fast and suitable for high-volume production.

A step-by-step breakdown of the clinching process looks like this:

The punch initiates the clinching by pressing into the metal and shaping the joint. This is what determines the joint’s size and structure.

The stripper first clamps and then clears the punch after each press to ensure joint consistency.

The die anvil provides resistance and helps distribute pressure evenly across the joint.

Die blades guide the material as it deforms, allowing it to flow laterally during the clinching process.

The die collar shields the blades from damage to prevent them from getting pinched, and the die elastomer helps the blades return to their original position. The collar also aids in the material clamping to reduce or eliminate marking.

Characteristics of a Clinched Joint

The joint is created by an interlocking mechanism, though this also means clinching isn’t the best option if you need a flat or smooth surface in the finished product. The process preserves the integrity of the materials being joined. Unlike welding, which can alter material properties through heat and create a heat-affected zone (HAZ), clinching is a cold-forming process that avoids thermal distortion. Metal fasteners introduce the risk of stress concentrations that could lead to failures, which clinching also avoids.

Several factors determine a clinched joint’s strength, including the material type, thickness, and the specific tooling used. It’s often highly resistant to shear and pull-out forces. It can be tested nondestructively and destructively, so it’s easy to confirm that the joints are as strong as you need them to be. Typically, clinched joints aren’t as strong as welding.

Pros and Cons

Clinching can be applied to various materials, including different types of metals, nonmetal materials (like insulation) between metallic layers, as well as precoated metals. Thickness ranges from 32 ga. to 8 ga., and when working with thinner stock (32 ga.), some setups can clinch more than two material thicknesses together in one hit. The process reduces material waste since it requires only the metals to be joined—no fasteners or consumables. It doesn’t produce pollutants or emissions or require heat.

It is well suited for automation, so it’s a great choice for high-volume manufacturing, where automated clinching systems rapidly produce consistent joints with minimal human intervention.

Like any technology, clinching does have its limitations. Joints with extremely high strength requirements usually aren’t a good candidate. And unlike other methods like MIG welding or pop rivets, the clinching press’s die and punch need to access both sides of the workpiece (see

Figure 2

).

The other limitation here is that the deeper the joint is in the center of the workpiece, the larger the clinching machine frame will need to be. Joints need to be in an overlap configuration, and the workpiece surface needs to be large enough for the tooling to create the interlock. The larger the joint diameter and the higher the force required for the materials, the larger surface you will need.

Clinching two sheets of significantly different thicknesses also can be problematic. Typically, the metal on the punch side shouldn’t be thinner than half the thickness of the die side. You can always test with rectangular clinching tools (rather than circular, which is the standard) to see if it’s possible to join them anyway.

The sheet metal grade needs to have a certain level of ductility for clinching to work well. It’s hard to make a rule of thumb about which metals will work and which ones won’t. Our research points to a strain-hardening value of less than 0.22, elongation at failure of greater than 10%, and 0.2% proof stress less than 550 N/mm

2

. Very brittle metals like cast iron and certain grades and tempers of aluminum are often more likely to crack than bend, so clinching isn’t ideal. That said, clinching is quite versatile, and most commercial-quality galvanized steel can be clinched with no problems.

Assessing a Clinched Joint

Three key lab tests can help you evaluate how strong a clinched joint is. This is a great way to compare the strength of clinching to any other joining method you’re considering. For each test, you start with a material sample about the size of a business card, create a clinched joint, and then apply force to pull the joint apart.

The

lap shear test

pulls the metal sheets apart parallel to their surfaces, simulating how the joint would hold under sliding forces.

The

pure tensile test

positions the two pieces in an "X" shape and pulls them directly along the punch axis. This test is less common due to its complex setup.

The

coach peel test

bends the material at a 90-degree angle before pulling it apart, mimicking the stresses that might occur if the joint is pried open.

Note that these are all static tests. Other types of tests might involve seeing how the joint holds against vibration and the force it can absorb under movement.

You can change the clinched joint to make it stronger, provided the workpiece and materials allow for it. For example, you can increase the diameter of the clinching joint from 3 mm to 3.8 mm to increase its ability to withstand forces.

Inspection Techniques

After you’ve done some destructive tests to find the ideal strength and type of joint you need, you can then use nondestructive methods for ongoing quality control. Because clinching produces very consistent joints, it’s often enough for operators to run a visual inspection at the beginning of every production run to ensure everything is working properly.

To do this, they first check the surface of the material for any gaps or damage. They make sure the joint is even, with a consistent width and depth for a solid connection. And they look for any signs of deformation or cracks, especially in aluminum. Cracks in the button cap might point to an over-clinching or material problem.

A well-formed joint has a distinct appearance, with a visible interlock and no cracks or deformities in the surrounding material. It usually doesn’t take much experience to perform that type of quality check. Often, you can simply show operators a few badly joined pieces and compare them to a stable clinched joint.

Beyond visual inspection, you can measure the joint itself. To measure the button diameter, you can use a caliper or go/no-go gauge. The button diameter is the diameter of the clinch that the material bent into. It can be tricky because the joint isn’t always a perfect circle, so having visual instructions can help with consistency.

You also can use a clinch measuring tool to check clinch thickness (CT). For round dies, the CT should be roughly a third or even a fifth of the total thickness of the two sheets. For rectangular dies, aim for about half the total thickness.

Troubleshooting

Clinch joints can fail in two ways: pop failures and shear failures. A pop

failure

happens when the joint separates, leaving a dimple in the metal. This can be caused by insufficient clinching force. If the die is too shallow, it won’t penetrate deeply enough into the metal. Switching to a deeper die can resolve this. If the punch tip radius is too large, it won’t form the material properly, requiring a sharper punch tip to create a better clinch. If those adjustments don’t help, the material might be too hard. Switching to a square or rectangular die might correct the joint.

A

shear failure

occurs when the clinched joint leaves a hole in the punch-side material. This typically happens due to excessive squeeze. If the tooling squeezes the material too tightly, it becomes overly stretched and weak. Similarly, using a die that’s too deep can also cause shear failures; switching to a shallower die can help.

If the punch tip radius is too small and sharp, it might create holes in the material, so increasing the punch tip radius can mitigate this issue. Brittle or inflexible materials also can lead to shear failures, especially if the punch-side material is thinner than the die-side material.

Again, a pop failure often comes from insufficient clinching force while shear failure comes from excessive force. In between these two types is a situation where the material sometimes pops and sometimes shears, or partially shears but leaves a piece attached to the punch-side material. The idea is to find the "Goldilocks zone" in between, where the material neither pops nor shears. This often points to an optimal setup.

Is Clinching Worth Another Look?

Clinching can add a ton of efficiency and simplicity, and it’s one of the easiest joining methods to automate. It saves on labor costs and creates a much cleaner and safer work environment, free from hazards like sparks, smoke, and fumes. It also eliminates the need for fasteners and consumables.

The joining method handles different projects and materials—aluminum and steel, stainless and galvanized metals, or even dissimilar materials like copper to aluminum. With just a few dies and punches, you can manage everything from 14- to 30-ga. mild steel and most types of aluminum.

Again, like any other technology, clinching has its drawbacks. Some joints simply require the added strength of welding. Other joints don’t have the clearance or surface area that a clinching press requires. And some material grades just don’t have the ductility that clinching requires. That said, for a vast number of applications in the sheet metal arena, clinching is still a vastly underutilized method for what it can do.
