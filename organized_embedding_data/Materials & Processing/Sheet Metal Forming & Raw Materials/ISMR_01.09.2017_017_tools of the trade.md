By Jeff Kiernan, Commercial Director, Dawson Shanahan

Tooling plays a critical part in most manufacturing operations, but nowhere is it more evident than in metal forming and fabrication. The speed, accuracy and repeatability of operations such as cutting, punching and welding depend on the quality of the tooling employed.

Tooling essentially describes the process required to design and engineer the tools needed to manufacture parts or components.

For sheet metal working, it comprises manufacturing aids such as cutting tools, punching presses and extrusion machines, and includes dies, fixtures, gauges, jigs, moulds and patterns.

Good tooling design guarantees tools that are safe to use. It also ensures that finished parts are engineered consistently to the required quality and specifications, in the volumes and lead-times expected by the customer and at the most competitive cost.

## Expertise is everything

New methods of developing tooling have transformed the production of parts, including special tooling software. For example, advanced software products include in-process measuring systems for CNC machines. They monitor all processes while the parts are still

on the machine. This type of measurement in real-time makes it simple to identify weak spots quickly. Indeed, the introduction of powerful computer-aided design and modelling software has boosted the efficiency and effectiveness of modern-day tooling.

However, it still relies on the knowledge and experience of tool- and die-makers. In particular, effective tooling design requires a comprehensive understanding of the conditions that a tool will encounter while in operation (see box below). This knowledge is used to inform the design, type of materials and construction of the tooling, as well as potential modifications to processes.

## Tooling costs

Tooling is an essential element in the processing of sheet metal. However, there has always been confusion over the cost of tooling and the way in which it is charged to each customer. This is, however, no longer the case.

The cost of the tooling itself and how it is charged to each customer can, however, be confusing. As there were no standard definitions to guide cost calculations historically, suppliers tended to adopt a variety of practices to calculate and charge-out the cost of developing tooling. Few of these were consistent, many were opaque and almost all depended on the expertise of a small number of experienced toolmakers and estimators.

Thankfully, software has come to the rescue by making it quick and simple for engineers to accurately assess the time, labour and materials required to produce individual tool-sets. As a result, standardisation is growing across the sector.

A critical step in the development process is to consider how the cost of both tooling and the overall project can be reduced, while ensuring other criteria (such as part quality and delivery deadlines) remain unaffected.

Indeed, it is tempting to focus solely on the cost of tooling without appreciating that this may actually lead to higher part costs once the project enters the production phase. In other words, it might be better to invest slightly more in the initial tooling development if this leads to a significant reduction in part costs.

An equally important point at this planning stage is to consider the actual method of manufacture.

For example, despite the fact that the tooling for precision cold forming may be slightly more expensive that for conventional CNC machining, the actual production process is generally far more cost-effective; cold forming uses considerably less material, is faster and produces a higher quality component that, in many instances, requires little or no subsequent processing. As a result, part costs are far lower, with the added advantage that less waste is generated and less energy is used.

Methods of manufacture for sheet metal include:

- Punching (where a top tool traps the metal against an appropriately-shaped bottom tool and pushes through, creating a hole or other feature).

- Laser cutting (which requires no tooling).

- Bending (typically using a hydraulic press brake which can be fitted with a wide variety of tooling to produce different bends).

- Stamping, also known as pressing (using tools similar to injection moulds that are basically single-purpose).

- Chamfering (to remove the sharpest parts of sheet metal that has been worked upon).

The types of tooling required will therefore vary, depending upon the task.

Six tooling design considerations

Tooling designers must take account of a wide range of factors, including:

- Tolerances of the finished part – these will affect the configuration of the tooling.

- Mechanical strength and rigidity of the tool to ensure workpiece accuracy, repeatability and quality.

- Cutting tool strength which must be sufficient to withstand machining forces.

- Sacrificial or weak links which may need to be incorporated to accommodate wear and protect tools from damage.

- Machine tool speed, feed and size which determine the characteristics of each tool set.

- Disposal of swarf and other waste from the working area.

## Purchasing tips

Sheet metal machines are used predominantly for shaping, cutting, pressing and forming sheet metal. Before deciding which tools to buy, it makes solid business sense to review the market for tools available. Other buying criteria include:

- Select tools from a reputable supplier.

- Take advice from sheet metal machine manufacturers and others with knowledge and experience of the equipment that you are using.

- Identify the type of tools that you require and set a budget.

- As well as cost, compare the availability of the tools you wish to buy and delivery times.

- Remember, you get what you pay for so don’t be tempted by companies selling exceptionally low-cost tools.

- Consider the warranty period offered by the manufacturer and the after-sales service available.

- Discover how existing customers rate the products. Generally, it is better to pay a little more for superior quality tooling because it is likely to be more reliable and last longer than cheaper tools.

## Tooling costs

Most conventional tooling costs for techniques, such as CNC machining and precision cold forming, are relatively low. The exceptions tend to be where low volume parts are complex, or where specialised or difficult-to-engineer materials are required. Even here, however, cost can often be engineered out through intelligent design and careful control of production processes.

Cost and time delays often occurred in the past because the development of tooling was seen as an iterative process, with improvements to tooling designs being made through a succession of prototype stages.

Although this iterative methodology continues today, advanced engineering software tools (such as SolidWorks and DEFORM) enable tool design, analysis and testing to be carried out on-screen, rather than in the tool room. This effectively eliminates the traditional 'trial and error' elements commonly associated with toolmaking and allows the correctly dimensioned and quality approved tooling to be engineered first time.
