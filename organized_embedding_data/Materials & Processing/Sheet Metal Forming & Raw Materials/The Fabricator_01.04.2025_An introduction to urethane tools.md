am often amazed how few people in this industry have little or no knowledge of forming with urethane tools or, for that matter, even know that urethane is a thing. Not having a basic working knowledge of urethane in your toolbox is like trying to put a picture puzzle together without having all the pieces. Your shop’s puzzle may never require this piece, but you won’t know if you don’t know. This piece won’t fit everywhere, but where it does fit in, well, you get my point.

What Is Urethane?

Urethane is a versatile polymer that exists in multiple forms, including thermosetting and thermoplastic materials. The term "urethane" in our industry is often associated with polyurethane, a broader category of materials created by reacting different chemicals. This combination provides a balance of flexibility, toughness, and durability. Depending on the formulation, urethane can be rigid or flexible, making it suitable for a wide range of industrial applications.

One of urethane’s key characteristics is its ability to resist abrasion, impact, and chemical exposure, which makes it an ideal material for applications requiring durability. Urethane-based materials (elastomers) are particularly popular in manufacturing for their ability to absorb shock and reduce wear in components such as rollers, bumpers, gaskets, and bushings. Additionally, urethane’s flexibility allows it to be molded or extruded into custom shapes while maintaining high performance under mechanical stress. In softer durometers, it acts as a solid hydraulic; any force applied is transferred equally throughout the pad—more on this later.

Despite its many advantages, urethane has some limitations, including sensitivity to UV degradation and hydrolysis (water absorption) in certain environments. Overall, urethane remains a valuable material due to its combination of strength, flexibility, and resistance to harsh conditions, like bending metal, making it indispensable in modern manufacturing and product design.

Urethane Tooling Basics

Urethane is an excellent material for press brake forming due to its unique combination of elasticity, durability, and resistance to deformation. Soft and hard urethane offers distinct advantages depending on the bending operation being performed.

Unlike traditional metal dies, urethane tools reduce or eliminate witness marks on the sheet metal, allowing for high-quality finished parts without the need for secondary finishing processes. Its ability to absorb impact and distribute pressure (acting as a solid hydraulic) evenly helps prevent cracking, distortion, or scratching, making it ideal for bending delicate or polished materials such as aluminum, stainless steel, and prepainted metals.

Soft urethane is particularly useful in press brake applications requiring gentle forming. Its high elasticity allows it to conform to the workpiece while exerting uniform pressure, preventing metal thinning and marring. This makes it especially valuable for forming delicate or coated materials where maintaining surface integrity is crucial.

Forming in soft urethane pads is neither an air bending nor bottom bending operation; it is a unique method that produces results similar to bottom-bent or coined parts. This is again due to the urethane properties; under load, the material flows into and fills any cavities while forcing the material to conform to the punch shape to produce consistent bends with minimal tool wear. Additionally, soft urethane’s compressibility helps in applications where metal springback is a concern, allowing for better control over the final bend angle, as well as large- and profound-radius bends.

Hard urethane, while still flexible, offers greater resistance to deformation and is often used as a substitute for traditional metal dies in certain bending applications. It provides excellent workpiece support by maintaining a defined shape under pressure, reducing the risk of metal distortion. Note that hard or high-durometer urethane dies do not have the same strength as steel dies. They complement, but should never replace, the standard steel dies in your tooling library.

Hard urethane dies are particularly useful in creating sharp bends without excessive tonnage. Its ability to withstand repeated compression without permanent deformation extends tool life and reduces maintenance costs compared to traditional press brake tooling.

Overall, urethane’s ability to absorb impact, distribute force evenly, and resist damage from repeated use makes it an invaluable material in press brake forming. Whether soft or hard, urethane tooling provides increased flexibility and reduces tool witness marks, making it an ideal choice for many precision metal bending applications. Hard urethane dies will have a shorter working life than steel ones. However, they tend to be far less expensive than their steel counterparts.

The durometer is a measure of how hard or soft a material is, specifically how resistant it is to being indented or compressed. It is commonly used to describe the hardness of materials like rubber, urethane, and plastics (see

Figure 1

).

The durometer is measured using two scales: Shore A and Shore D. Shore A is for softer materials (like rubber bands), and Shore D is for harder materials (like hard plastics). The higher the durometer number, the harder the material. For example, a soft urethane pad might have a Shore A durometer of 40, while a harder urethane block could have a Shore D durometer of 80 or more. Note that there is no color-to-durometer standard between different manufacturers. Be sure you know the color of the durometer code for the tooling manufacturers’ products you are using.

Urethane’s Resilience

Urethane is almost indestructible: paint, solvents, and oils—almost nothing phases this material. Even if it has deep cuts or gouges, a urethane pad still works great for forming. This is again because urethane pads, those in the Shore A range, act as a solid hydraulic. When force is applied to the pad, it flows, first filling in any gougedout areas as it flows, then building equal force against all constraining surfaces of the retainer box.

The most common way urethane pads get destroyed is through hysteresis, the buildup of internal heat within the pad. This comes from overworking the pad, which causes it to break apart. To avoid this issue, be sure the pad volume is at least 10 times that of the penetrating punch and material; any pad larger than that is fine.

The Retainer Box

The retainer box is a container used to constrain the softer urethane pads. Having the finest retainer box money can buy is always preferred, but it may not be necessary or practical (see

Figure 2

). If you look at a manufactured retainer box, you will find that it will have a base with a machined air channel and four sides. Yes, they are nice, but they can be made easily from a common construction C channel. You also could weld up one of your own. The retainer box requires only a base and two sides; the end caps are nice but unnecessary. As for the required air channel underneath the pad, any two pieces of round stock can become your deflection bars. If a deeper air channel is required, increase the bar diameter.

The air channel allows for greater penetration of the punch and the material being formed into the pad while reducing the tonnage load. When forming into a urethane pad, the required tonnage is greater than air forming and increases with the durometer as it gets higher. That said, it’s not as high as bottom bending or coining.
