ISMR SAYS:

“The drive to produce more products in shorter timescales sometimes leads to slug pulling”

In the world of high-volume sheet metal cutting and production, challenges are often encountered. The drive to produce more and more products in shorter timescales by punching or blanking sometimes leads to slug pulling. Cutting, although a simple and trouble-free operation at first glance, can blind-side us. Slug pulling, a concomitant effect of sheet cutting, is one such unpleasant surprise. Slug pulling occurs if a slug sticks on the punch face, holds and remains there. For most companies involved in the forming of sheet metal, it can be a recurring, undesirable side-effect responsible for downtime, financial losses, press failure, punch breakage, poor geometry precision and low roughness quality etc. Slug pulling can be such a serious problem at many plants that it can stop all sheet metal cutting if no steps are taken to reduce its effect.

## Reasons for slug pulling

There are two main physical effects responsible for slug pulling: magnetisation of punches and the vacuum between the punch and the slug. See Table 1.

## How to stem slug pulling

Slug pulling is a problem which can almost totally be solved. First of all, it is important to avoid the factors outlined in Table 1. If this is not possible, then other precautions must be taken.

There are a surprisingly large number of products for this problem. Most of the solutions are based upon a change in punch or die design or eventually contain sensors for controlling the cutting process.

During punch sharpening by grinding, the punch lies on a magnetising plate and the punch becomes magnetised. Therefore, the first step to preclude slug pulling is de-magnetisation of the punch by a demagnetising device. After this, the punch remains de-magnetised and does not attract steel slugs.

One easy solution is an aeration punch (a). The aeration punch has a thin hole in the middle of punch, which air can flow through to aerate and decrease vacuum between the punch face and the slug. Parallel grooves to the punch face (b), made by grinding or slotting, have a similar but weaker effect. Air can flow through these grooves to aerate and decrease vacuum.

One favoured method is an ejector (c). The ejector consists of a pin and a spring in the body of the punch. Ejectors do not work well in rapid cutting operations but otherwise can be successfully used, mainly with bigger punches.

Another method is replacement of the ejector. A punch has, on its face, a small piece of urethane, placed in a drilled hole (d), or a small drop of a metal. These precautions do not allow the creation of a vacuum bubble between the punch face and the slug. A new kind of ejector, made from pyrathane or other plastic, brings more benefits than common steel ejectors; such as faster press speeds, greater reliability and a longer ejector life.

It is possible to reduce slug pulling by using punches with various faces. A stepped face, shear angle face (e), balled face, cone face etc. decreases the cutting force and leads to a smoother cutting process without shocks causing noise. One disadvantage is more complicated grinding.

Deposited hard metal on the die land (g) not only reduces slug pulling but protects the die from wear and thus increases die life too. The metal deposit is executed with a deposition device.

## Rapid cutting methods

However, if you cut sheet rapidly, these methods might not help you to stem slug pulling. You would then have to turn to more reliable methods.

Air ejection of the slug is one such solution. A continuous or timed blast or, even better, a timed blast of a mixture of air and oil, flowing through the punch (f), can blow the slug off.

This system is often used with another element such as a power vacuum die (h). The power vacuum die pulls the slug downward from the punch face. Moreover, the vacuum die increases tool life, reduces penetration and increases cutting speed.

One effective method is a die with protrusions (i). The die contains two or four interior protrusions below the cutting surface. The protrusions retain the slug in the die. The die minimises slug pulling and is suitable for cutting all materials. There are also dies containing, for example, two grooves (j) made under an angle, which trap slugs so firmly into the die that the punch does not pull the slug up from the die.

A die with a unique bore (k) uses another principle. It is based on the mechanical influence of the die on the slug, which slightly tumbles in the die bore. The utilisation of this die is limited for round holes and it is not possible to use sheared punches for this application. Another solution to reduce slug pulling is a tapered die with a constriction bore (l). The die works as a single-way filter, which does not allow the slug to return and preserves the cutting process from slug pulling.

Besides the solutions previously described, there are other systems that control the whole cutting process based on an acoustic emission or similar principle. These systems involve sensors which output a signal to an operator’s monitor. The sensors automatically stop the press as soon as the slug is stuck on the punch face or if the slug has fallen onto the sheet metal to preclude possible damage.

We can also use a combination of several methods. Each of these methods has advantages and disadvantages. You need to choose the solution that fits your needs. Slug pulling and precautions against it should always be a priority and the problem should never be underestimated. It is far more cost effective to pay for slug-pulling solutions than to suffer the far more costly slug-pulling consequences. ■
