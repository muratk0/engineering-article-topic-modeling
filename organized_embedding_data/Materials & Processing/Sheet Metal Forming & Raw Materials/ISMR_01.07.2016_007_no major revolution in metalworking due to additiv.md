# No major revolution in metalworking due to additive manufacturing

[TARİH: 01.08.2016 ISMR]

A VDW STUDY on the opportunities and risks of Additive Manufacturing (AM) gives the all-clear - only one per cent replacement of existing processes. This is at least true for the next five to seven years, it said.

"Additive Manufacturing (AM) supplements the manufacturing processes available in the metalworking sector. For the time being, there is not going to be any broadly based displacement of existing machining processes, nor the much-cited revolution in industrial production operations," said Dr. Myron Graw, a partner at KEX Knowledge Exchange AG in Aachen, speaking at METAV's opening press conference in Düsseldorf. He is responsible for the firm's AM operations and for the study entitled 'Additive Manufacturing– potential and risks from the viewpoint of the German machine tool industry', commissioned by the VDW (German Machine Tool Builders' Association).

"Additive manufacturing processes are linked to high expectations," commented Dr. Wilfried Schäfer, Executive Director of METAV's organiser, VDW. "In particular, the vision of entirely new value creation chains right down to customised production of parts or spares on-site is arousing keen interest," he explained. The VDW therefore commissioned an academic study of what this actually involves from the machine tool industry's viewpoint.

KEX AG, in conjunction with the Fraunhofer Institutes for Production Technology (IPT) and Laser Technology (ILT), conducted the study in five stages, with a major focus on metalworking operations. Assuming annual growth of 40 per cent for additive processes, less than one per cent of the existing technologies will be replaced by additive processes said the study. This relates to the production volume of the international machine tool industry.

Obstacles to greater market penetration are the costs involved and the machining time required. In small-series manufacturing and when producing complex customised and small components, the cost advantages of an additive process can be achieved using tool-less manufacturing. A special advantage ensues when substantial added values can be generated by additive manufacturing, such as lightweight structures in the aircraft industry, cooling ducts and undercuts.

When it comes to manufacturing large components, additive processes often have cost-related disadvantages. These result not least from the comparatively low build-up rates. Other relevant factors include the expensive machinery required and the high material prices for metal powder.

"These cost-drivers will, in the years ahead, be changed by technological advances and the upsizing of capacities," admitted Dr. Graw. This, he added, will speed up the dissemination of AM.

The development of hybrid systems is an exciting topic. These integrate functional quality for additive manufacturing, e.g. laser deposition welding, into conventional machinery concepts. This creates potential for repeatedly performing targeted machining tasks during the build-up process.
