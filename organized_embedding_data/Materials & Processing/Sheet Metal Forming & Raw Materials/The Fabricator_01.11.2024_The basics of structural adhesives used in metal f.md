T

he use of structural adhesives in metal fabricating applications is on the rise because they are an attractive alternative to welding and mechanical fastening in many instances.

Manufacturers that don’t have enough welders can alter part designs to use adhesives to join metal parts together, allowing less-skilled employees to accomplish the joining task. Other manufacturers are looking for more expedient joining methods to quicken the production cycle. In some instances, adhesives are the answer to lightening an end product, whether it’s a helicopter or a box truck. The reasons to consider structural adhesives are many.

Even with their increased usage, many in manufacturing don’t have a good understanding of structural adhesives. This article aims to educate metal fabricators on the terminology associated with adhesives and technical data sheets, to provide an overview of some of the structural adhesive chemistries on the market, and to discuss some applications where the adhesives are being used.

Structural Adhesives Terminology

To understand something, a person needs to speak the language. Knowing these terms used by adhesives manufacturers and on technical data sheets can help a manufacturer become smarter about structural adhesives and what they might be able to offer.

To begin, we have to understand the difference between an

adhesive

and a

sealant

. Adhesives are materials that are designed to bond two surfaces or two materials together. A sealant is a material that may or may not bond to the surface well, but it’s designed instead to seal out moisture and air from a seam or an assembly. It can be rigid, or it can be elastomeric.

A

structural adhesive

is a little bit different than a regular adhesive in that it’s an adhesive that can achieve 1,000 pounds per square inch (PSI) or 6.9 megapascals (MPa) in shear. This is a little bit different than a simple glue or contact cement. Those are designed to join materials together, but the structural adhesive is much stronger.

Two other terms used in the industry are two component (2K) and one component (1K). Adhesive material that is considered 2K comprises two different materials, but they are kept separately until application. When they’re brought together according to a specific mix ratio, they cure into a solid adhesive bond. Adhesive material that is considered 1K is a single part and is dispensed onto a product. Once it’s exposed to the environment, be it moisture, heat, or even an energy source such as UV light, it will then begin to cure. Caulk or sealant is a good example of a 1K material.

Open time, pot life

, and

working time

essentially mean the same thing. This is the time allowable between when the two parts of 2K adhesive are first mixed and when the parts must be mated. The open time listed on the data sheet also is dependent on time, temperature, and mass of the adhesive.

Handling strength, green strength

, and

fixturing time

refer to the time required for the adhesive to cure and attain sufficient strength to hold the parts together without additional fixturing. Typically, that’s between 50 and 100 PSI of shear strength, depending on the size of the part. Once handling strength has been achieved, the part can be moved downstream to other manufacturing processes without the need for fixtures. This also is dependent on time, temperature, adhesive mass, and the joint design.

Processing time

is the time required to reach that structural strength of 1,000 PSI or 6.9 MPa. Once the adhesive reaches that strength, the next step in the manufacturing operation can readily be performed without degrading the bond line or fracturing the bond and causing quality issues. Downstream processing activities might include cutting metal to length, deflashing, or adding screws.

Density

is simply the mass per unit volume that is commonly expressed as pounds per gallon or grams per cubic centimeter. In basic parlance, it’s how much the material weighs.

Color

often relates to the material in an uncured state, a cured state, or even a mixed state. When two different materials come together, they might be different colors to create a third color when they’re mixed. The third color acts as a visual confirmation of mixing.

Flashpoint

is the lowest temperature at which vapors from a volatile component ignite when exposed to a small flame under controlled conditions. Oftentimes this is listed on a technical data sheet and the safety data sheet. This is important for manufacturers as they look to bring an adhesive into a facility and plan to possibly utilize the material near welding activity.

Mixed ratio

applies to 2K materials. It spells out the ratio required so that both components can create the adhesive. It can be expressed by volume, by weight, or oftentimes both.

When working with structural adhesives, it’s also important for manufacturers to know about mechanical properties that might be referenced on a data sheet. For example,

strength

refers to the maximum load or force per unit area that can be applied to the adhesive. It’s often expressed in PSI or MPa.

Elongation

is simply the distance the material can stretch until it begins to fail. It’s usually expressed as a percentage. This measurement applies to the material itself, not as a bonded part. However, elongation is extremely dependent on the test method, so care should be exercised to understand how to use this value.

Modulus

is often used from an engineering standpoint as a starting point to design a part. It’s simply the relationship of stress versus the elongation. It’s expressed in a force per unit area, and typically a higher modulus will lower the elongation.

Hardness

is a measurement of how strong a material is or, more specifically, how well it resists indentation. A Shore hardness tester is typically used to deliver this measurement. It can either be on an A or D scale, with the A scale being a softer scale and the D scale being a harder scale.

Coefficient of thermal expansion

is the relationship of the volume change and temperature for the adhesive. Like most materials, as an adhesive gets warmer, it tends to expand. This is helpful when it comes to designing joints, particularly when using different materials such as metal bonded to a plastic.

Glass transition temperature

is the point at which the adhesive changes from a rigid or glassy state to more of a rubbery state. This is a range of temperatures, not just an absolute value. It’s often expressed as a single temperature value, but the softening occurs over a range as a gradual process.

Working With the Structural Adhesives

With a bit of terminology covered, a manufacturer can now begin to understand how adhesives work and what it’s like to work with the materials in a production setting.

Different packaging options are typically available for manufacturers. Bulk packaging, such as gallon kits, pails, and drums, is good for high-volume applications that use dispensing equipment. For low-volume applications or assembly activities that require portability, convenience packaging in the form of cartridges is available.

Structural adhesives with multiple cure speeds are offered. A faster adhesive might have an open time of three to five minutes, while a slower one might be 60 minutes.

Glass bead technology helps to maintain the optimum bond line thickness and prevents too much adhesive from being squeezed out when a joint is compressed.

Structural adhesives are available in different chemistries: acrylics, epoxies, and urethanes.

Acrylics (which also covers methyl methacrylate adhesives) are most often 2K material. They’re designed really for bonding unprepared metals and also cross bonding to other materials, such as plastics and ceramics. They typically require minimal surface preparation, so a dry rag wipe usually is all that’s necessary to prep a surface for the adhesive. Acrylics are a room temperature curing product.

Acrylics are workhorse products in metal bonding. They have a wide range of open times and demonstrate high shear and high peel strengths. They often are resistant to high-temperature bakes, making them suitable for some applications in which powder coating follows joining.

One of the things that makes acrylics unique is that they cure very fast once the working time or the open time has expired. So they develop strength quickly, and they reach that structural strength or processing strength where a fabricator can move on to the next stage of its manufacturing operations.

Urethanes also are 2K material, often designed to bond composites, plastics, foams, and primed or painted metals. They tend to be quite flexible, have a lower odor than the acrylic products, and have a room-temperature cure. Urethanes usually have a broad range of open times. They are a really good choice for engineering thermoplastics and composites. They have a very low odor, and they are good for cross bonding these different materials together.

Epoxies offer the ability to bond a wide variety of material, such as metals, painted metals, foam, fiberglass composites, wood, and ceramics. They have some of the highest shear strength and typically some of the lowest elongation. They also offer the best environmental resistance when compared to the other adhesives. They are room-temperature curing products but might require some heating to achieve full performance.

Some epoxies are specialty adhesive products. For example, cryogenic epoxies are designed to operate at very low temperatures, -100 degrees C to -200 degrees C.

MS polymers are somewhat of a new category of adhesive chemistry and are mostly sold as a 1K adhesive. They are designed to bond unprepared metals, painted surfaces, and composites. They have a high elongation, typically greater than 100%. They often are used as a sealant or a sealant/adhesive. These products can achieve over 1,000 PSI in shear strength in some cases depending on the joint design, but typically they’re used as sealant.

Common Bonding Applications

Structural adhesives have been used for metal bonding for several years now. In that time, adhesives have replaced conventional joining methods, such as welding or mechanical fastening, in numerous applications.

These applications have involved metals such as aluminum, steel, stainless steel, galvanized steel, and prepainted metal. While not as common, adhesives also have been used with metals like magnesium, titanium, cast iron, bronze, brass, and copper. Metal bonding adhesives are designed to be exposed to typical postapplication processes, such as powder coating, e-coating, or even conversion coatings.

So where are these structural adhesives being used? One of the best examples is the trailer, transit, and utility bodies sector (see

Figure 1

), where roofs, doors, walls, floor panels, underbody boxes, truck beds, lift gates, ramps, racks, trim, and latches and brackets are often held in place by these adhesives.

Structural adhesives also are used in fabricating containers and enclosures, HVAC systems, aircraft, missile systems, agricultural machinery, antenna dishes, speaker assemblies, windows, doors, and other architectural products.

To get a clearer picture of how the adhesives are used, let’s look at a bus (see

Figure 2

). The roof, which is either made of composites or sheet metal, is likely bonding to the metal frame. The metal panel that is the door is likely bonded to the metal frame with an adhesive. Adhesives are used for the side panel bonding with luggage doors, where a metal sheet is bonded onto a frame. The same occurs at the front and back ends, which are often made from composites.

High-performance 2K adhesives are typically used for these types of applications. They provide the adhesion and durability required for the job, and they cater to the long service life of products like a bus.

Another example is a typical box truck. Here, the composite sidewalls are bonded to a metal post, as is the roof to rails. The adhesives also are used on the doors.

The trucking industry uses painted steel/compositecored material, and the material is becoming a lot more ubiquitous across the board. In these instances, adhesives can replace joining methods like rivets and other mechanical fastening in construction of sidewalls.

Common Adhesive Testing

Adhesives aren’t tested using digital simulation. Real world tests (see

Figure 3

) are done to ensure the adhesives perform as they are intended. These are some of the tests that structural adhesive manufacturers perform on their products.

A lap shear test checks the shear strength of an adhesive. Two coupons are bonded together with a certain overlap size and then pulled apart in shear. The load at failure is measured.

The T peel test is named for the shape in which the materials are joined. The two sides of the top of the T are pulled until they peel apart. The test provides a measurement of how well the adhesive adheres to the substrate.

A tensile pull-off test typically involves pulling a workpiece that is adhered to a fixed base.

The cold impact test involves a single lap shear sample that is impacted with a weighted pendulum from one side. Often used in the automotive industry, it’s typically performed at -40 degrees F.

Strength of Structural Adhesives Compared to Spot Welds

These tests are useful because they validate the strength of structural adhesives. For instance, a lap shear test of 6061 aluminum (see

Figure 4

) reveals that the structural adhesive bond performs very well to about 5,000 lbs. of force, when the 4-in.-wide workpieces were pulled apart. When that is compared to spot welds on either 1- or 2-in. centers, the spot welds don’t perform as well. In fact, the spot welds with 1-in. centers are pulled apart at roughly 3,500 lbs. of force. It’s really only when a manufacturer performs MIG welding on 1-in. centers that a similar result as with the structural adhesives is seen.

Of course, part design can greatly affect the way a structural adhesive performs. A tensile/peel test on two aluminum workpieces that are joined by two narrow flanges (see

Figure 5

) reveals that spot welding with 1-in. centers is likely to slightly outperform an adhesive. Having said that, a structural adhesive still shows better strength than spot welding with 2- and 3-in. centers on those same tests.

In summary, structural adhesives are proven alternatives to welding and mechanical fasteners in the metal fabricating marketplace. Manufacturers interested in learning if an application might be a good candidate for conversion to this type of joining method should reach out to a structural adhesives supplier to learn what might be possible.
