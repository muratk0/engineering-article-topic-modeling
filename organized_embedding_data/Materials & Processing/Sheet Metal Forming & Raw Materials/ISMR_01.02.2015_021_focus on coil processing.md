## What is the best location for an edge trimmer?

MISCONCEPTION: It is fairly common for some manufacturers to locate the edge trimmer before the leveller while other manufacturers locate the unit directly after. Is there a reason for doing so?

CLARIFICATION: With any slitting/shearing process, there is stress induced into the material as it is cut. Typically, the heavier the material and the narrower the strip, the greater the potential stress. By locating the edge trimmer before the leveller, the strip is levelled after being trimmed thereby reducing this stress.

The leveller also has a tendency to knock down the burr which may have resulted from the slitting process. Although locating the edge trim before the leveller has benefits, it also has potential limitations. Because the strip is first trimmed and then levelled, shape irregularities in the strip can affect tolerances. In addition, the levelling process itself can also cause dimensional changes to occur.

Edge trimmers positioned behind the leveller trim the material after it has been levelled. Consequently, shape irregularities are removed before trimming and optimum tolerances can be achieved.

SUMMARY: Typically, either configuration will work equally well. While both approaches have theoretical reasons for doing so, the benefits of one over the other are minimal.

The stress induced into a strip, especially a wide strip which is normally the case when you are edge trimming, is inconsequential. The leveller can cause dimensional changes to occur. However, these changes are insignificant as well.

## How is a leveller’s configuration determined?

MISCONCEPTION: While leveller designs may vary from manufacturer to manufacturer, there are basic fundamentals that ultimately determine a leveller’s configuration for a specific application.

CLARIFICATION: The most important consideration when selecting a leveller is workroll diameter and roll spacing. The diameter of these rolls will determine the gauge range that the machine can effectively level.

For example, a 44mm (1.75″) diameter roll would typically have an effective range of 0.610mm (0.024″) through to 3.4mm (0.134″) mild steel, while a 56mm (2.187″) diameter roll would be limited to 0.914mm (0.036″) through to 4.17mm (0.164″) mild steel. Normally, these ranges will vary only slightly between manufacturers.

Levellers are often referred to as 4, 5, or 6 HI. A 4 HI machine consists of upper and lower work rolls and a group of upper and lower backups. A 5 HI unit refers to a machine that also incorporates a full width intermediate roll normally between the top work rolls and the adjustable backups.

A 6 HI machine utilises intermediate rolls on the top and bottom. This extra set of rolls eliminates back-up roll wear on the work rolls which might otherwise mark soft or surface critical material. Marks resulting from worn work rolls are often referred to as skunk or zebra strips.

SUMMARY: When purchasing a new leveller, the manufacturers will select an appropriate unit based upon your specifications. If you’re purchasing a unit on the used market, do not be misled into purchasing a leveller with 76mm (3″) rolls if your primary application is 1.22mm (0.048″).

Consequently, although a 4 HI leveller with re-ground work rolls can adequately level aluminium, marking will occur once the rolls wear slightly. For consistent long term results, a 6 HI machine should be employed.

## What is camber?

MISCONCEPTION: While virtually everyone in manufacturing has been exposed to the problems caused by camber, uncertainty remains regarding how camber is defined and to what degree it influences part tolerances.

CLARIFICATION: Camber is the deviation of a side edge of a strip from a straight edge. It is caused by one side of the strip being longer than the other. Camber is measured by placing a straight edge on the concave side of the strip and measuring the distance between the straight edge and the sheet at the centremost portion of the arc. When possible, this measurement should be taken over a span of 6096mm (20′‑0″). This increment serves as a good benchmark since this is the length that mills refer to when referencing camber.

The standard mill specification for camber is 25mm in 6096mm (1″ in 20′‑0″). Most mills normally offer a quarter of this tolerance of 6.35mm (0.25″) in 6096mm (20′‑0″). However, the majority of commercial grade coil produced today generally has less than this amount. It is also important to be aware of the fact that, because camber is a continuous curve or radius of the strip, for a given amount of camber in a specific distance, if you double this distance, the camber will increase by four times that amount.

For example, a camber statistic of 1.59mm (0.0625″) in 1829mm (6′‑0″) appears to be relatively small when mathematically projected over 6096mm (20′‑0″). This figure is actually 17.45mm (0.6875″) which is considered excessive.

SUMMARY: Camber is the most prominent factor influencing part tolerances. To produce close tolerance parts without re-shearing, camber must be minimal. The tighter the tolerances required, the less camber can be tolerated.

## What is the best location for a leveller?

MISCONCEPTION: It was once common practice for manufacturers to locate the leveller after the shear. However, today most manufacturers position the leveller before the shear. What is the reason for doing so and how does leveller location affect the finished parts? CLARIFICATION: When levellers first came into widespread use several decades ago, they were typically located after the shear. While this position offers the simplest integration into the line, it also imposed certain limitations. Because parts were measured and cut-to-length before being levelled, shape irregularities could affect part tolerances. The levelling process could also change the parts dimensionally.

As a result, as part tolerances became more critical, manufacturers began locating the leveller before the shear. This allowed the leveller to operate continuously while permitting the strip to be levelled before being measured and cut‑to‑length.

Locating the leveller before the shear also eliminates the tendency of the entry leveller rolls to mark the leading edge of each sheet. SUMMARY: To simplify installation, the practice of installing the leveller after the shear is still occasionally used today when adding a leveller to an existing line. However, because of the benefits, most new systems manufactured today place the leveller before the shear.

## Do I need a straightener and a leveller?

MISCONCEPTION: It is relatively common to see some lines incorporating both a straightener and a leveller while other systems use only a leveller. What’s the difference? CLARIFICATION: The practice of using both a straightener and a leveller in the same line varies from manufacturer to manufacturer. Typically, if both machines are used, the manufacturer will contend that, by using a straightener to remove coil set before levelling, better flatness can be achieved.

Manufacturers that use only the leveller maintain that, when the strip is levelled, coil set is also removed as a consequence of the process and prior removal has negligible benefits.

Because the coil set in the material constantly changes throughout a coil, as with a straightener, the first group of work rolls in a leveller are actually used to induce reverse coil set into the strip. This reverse set is constant, which the remaining rolls can flatten without constant roll adjustment. This basic principle of operation eliminates the necessity to pre-condition the strip.

SUMMARY: As regards effectiveness, either line configuration will produce satisfactory results. However, most equipment manufacturers that actually produce a leveller state that pre-straightening is not required.

## What is the difference between a straightener and a leveller?

MISCONCEPTION: Although there has been widespread use of straighteners and levellers for decades, there remains a great deal of confusion regarding the proper application and capabilities of each machine.

CLARIFICATION: Conventional straighteners, sometimes referred to as flatteners, incorporate a series of large diameter work rolls. Typically, between five and eleven rolls are used. As the material passes through the rolls, it is alternately bent from the tangent of one roll to the tangent of the next. As a result of this bending process, coil set and crossbow is removed.

However, because this bending motion is restricted to one axis, i.e. up and down in the same horizontal plane, straighteners cannot correct side-to-side strip length variations.

A similar machine used to remove coil set is the precision straightener. The precision straightener incorporates a series of relatively small diameter work rolls. Back-ups are used to maintain parallelism between the top and bottom rolls.

Because of their similarities, a precision straightener can be mistaken for a roller-type corrective leveller. However, unlike the leveller, the straightener’s back-ups are fixed. As a result, a precision straightener is also limited to the removal of coil set and crossbow.

Similar to the precision straightener, a corrective leveller incorporates a series of closely spaced, relatively small work rolls. Along each roll are back-ups. However, the leveller’s back-ups can be adjusted to intentionally deflect a portion of the corresponding roll. Thus, if the material contains an edge wave, the rolls are deflected at their centres and the opposing edge. This stretches the centre and opposing edge of the strip while leaving the wavy edge its original length. This stretching equalises the strip dimensionally, resulting in flat material. SUMMARY: While there are similarities between machines, there are evident differences between straighteners and levellers. Although a precision straightener can work material more than a conventional straightener due to the size and number of work rolls, the benefits of a precision straightener over a conventional unit are marginal.

Both machines are limited to removing coil set and crossbow. In applications where the removal of coil set and crossbow is sufficient, either configuration will work satisfactorily. Applications that necessitate optimum flatness require a corrective leveller.

## Is cut-to-length speed the same as yield (output)?

MISCONCEPTION: Line speed is a specification often used to help determine the production capability of a line. Line speed refers to the speed of the material during normal line operation. However, line speed is often misinterpreted as system output or yield which actually refers to the amount of material processed in a given minute.

Line speed and yield would be identical if you were evaluating a continuously operating processing line such as a slitter. However, it is frequently misapplied to intermittently operating or variable speed cut-to-length lines. CLARIFICATION: For a start/stop cut-to-length line, line speed refers to the speed of the material while it is moving. However, because this type of system operates intermittently, the material is stopped momentarily while it is being sheared. As a result, yield differs from actual line speed.

A dual-speed hump table cut-to-length line operates with two speed modes – in one mode, line speed is relatively high. However, while the strip is being sheared, the line is in a creep mode and the material is actually moving considerably slower. Consequently, the yield is an average between the two speeds and the duration of time spent in each mode.

The feed system in a looping-type line operates intermittently. An accumulation loop between the feed and leveller allows the material to be levelled continuously while the feed stops the strip momentarily for shearing.

While feed speed may easily exceed 183 MPM (600 FPM), because the material is stopped for shearing, yield again varies from actual line speed. To determine yield, you must again average the feed speed, dwell time, deceleration and acceleration rates. SUMMARY: Although line speed and yield can be identical, there is a distinct difference between the two. The most meaningful statistic is true yield. The easiest way to determine yield on any cut-to-length line is to simply count the number of parts produced in a given minute and multiply that number by the part length. This will be the yield for that particular part.

In addition to part length, it is also important to consider the circumstances under which this speed was achieved i.e. which type of material was being processed, how wide, what gauge and the part tolerance attained.

These variables will ultimately determine the true yield that a system is capable of producing.
