arts flow from blanking to bending with minimal work-in-process (WIP). It all seems seamless until you walk to your inline powder coating system. You see WIP everywhere. Parts are hung haphazardly, widely spaced. Even worse, you see someone wielding a Dremel to frantically remove paint from hooks. Evidently, a lack of grounding was creating some adhesion difficulties.

You can trace these and other powder coating problems back to one issue:

parts hanging

. Getting it wrong can make powder coating an excessive, unpredictable constraint. Getting it right can relieve the bottleneck, keep parts flowing, and increase throughput for the entire operation.

Jeremy Davitz talked about these issues at length while presenting at last year’s FABTECH show. The president of Ashville, Ohio-based Production Plus Corp., which develops racking systems (dubbed Magic Rack), he described how to release the powder coating bottleneck. As with so much else in manufacturing, the path to improvement starts with the fundamentals.

HANGING BASICS

Part presentation in powder coating involves choosing the proper hook and (if needed) a fixture that secures the piece. Complete coating coverage demands an electrostatic ground that’s strong, consistent, and uninterrupted. It also requires that parts emerge from the dryoff oven completely dry, with no leftover pools of water from the washing station.

FIGURE 2 This framing system has movable crossbars that help secure various workpieces while maintaining high line density.

The contact between the hook, fixture, and metal part creates the electrostatic ground that the powder needs to adhere before the piece arrives in the cure oven. The weaker that connection, the weaker the current flow, and the worse your part coverage.

Imagine a setup in which the operator hangs lightweight sheet metal parts on standard hooks. Parts travel through pretreatment and washing stations, then a dry-off oven. All the while, the pieces jostle about like wind chimes.

The movement creates two uncertainties. First, the contact points between the hooks and parts start to shift. Instead of hanging on bare metal, the part now might be touching a portion of the hook that—unless it was just cleaned—is at least partially covered in paint from a previous cycle. That used hook hinders the all-important electrostatic ground. Second, the unintended movement could cause a part to hang in a new position that causes water from the washing station to pool in a trapped area. Alternatively, the new part orientation could create a nook where powder accumulates.

FIGURE 3 These crossbars have interior contact points for the part hooks. The arrangement helps maintain the electrical ground after multiple powder coating cycles.

Adding a drain hole to the part could help ensure it arrives at the powder coating station completely dry, and a quick burst of compressed air can remove powder buildup from a cranny. But as Davitz explained, if the root of these issues can be traced back to that unexpected jostling, then perfecting the part hanging and fixturing strategy could eliminate the issue.

Ideally, parts should be hung and fixtured in a way that eliminates unexpected (and unpredictable) movement, ensures proper drainage and drying, and maintains a secure connection for electrostatic grounding.

FIGURE 4 C and S hooks (on the left) have a curve known as the bowl, the depth of which determines how deep a part’s hooking hole can be within its geometry. A large bowl, however, also opens the possibility of part movement during washing, drying, and coating. A hook with a sharp bend at the bottom (on the right), perhaps combined with additional fixturing, might help keep the part from moving.

"This is why daisy-chaining can create problems," Davitz said, referring to the practice of hooking parts vertically in ladder-like fashion, which aims to increase part density. "Paint gets on those exposed hooks, which disrupts the electrostatic current flow for those parts on the bottom."

THREE PRINCIPLES OF PARTS HANGING

Considering this, what would be an effective way to increase powder coating throughput, balancing density with process reliability? To answer this, Davitz described three parts-hanging principles that act in concert.

First is

density

. Pack more parts together and you increase throughput.

Second is

standardization

. "You need to focus on a part or a part family’s common features," Davitz explained, "with minimal tooling and fixture changes."

Third is

adaptability

. "You need to accommodate a variety of parts and be ready for new ones. Your racking system needs to be flexible. Yes, some changeover will be necessary, but you need to minimize it."

To illustrate, Davitz pointed to the racking systems in

Otherwise, the system would have the same issue as with daisy-chaining: Bits of powder land between the hook and crossbar, breaking the contact point and hindering the electrostatic grounding. Instead, the hook is hung

inside

the crossbar on two contact points. The crossbar itself looks like an upside-down V (see

).

FIGURE 5 A spring hook grasps a washer’s inside diameter. Notches on the hook make the washer snap in place and keep it secure and stable throughout the powder coating line.

Second, the rack is modular. Elements can be moved as needed to accept different parts within a product group or family. Third, the hooks and frame together help secure components, meaning that the contact points don’t move through pretreatment, washing, drying, powder coating, and curing. The result: Water runs off as intended and, thanks to consistent grounding, powder coverage is complete.

HOOK GEOMETRIES

"You need to choose a hook that allows the part to hang in the right orientation, allowing water to drain completely and the air from the dry-off oven to circulate sufficiently," Davitz said.

C- and S-shaped hooks (see

Figure 4

) can work well for certain parts, especially those with hanging points (holes or other features) that are deeper into the part interior. The deeper the curve in the hook (what’s known as the

bowl

), the farther away from the edge the hanging point can be within the part geometry. "These are designed so the part can find its center of gravity hanging on the hook, at the bottom of the bowl," Davitz said.

These hook shapes do, however, create the possibility for part movement during washing, drying, or the coating process itself. This might shift the contact point toward a painted part of the hook and, hence, cause grounding issues.

FIGURE 6 Staging jobs on a cart, especially when batching numerous small pieces, can help keep the powder line fed, without part hanging becoming the constraint in the system.

In these cases, other hook styles might work better. If the part geometry doesn’t allow a hook with a tight V to be used, then a spring hook might suit. These grasp the ID of a part’s hanging hole. Specific divots in the wire form force the piece to snap into place (see

Figure 5

) securely enough to stay put throughout the powder coating process.

In still other cases, strategically formed hooks can hang parts from two sides, effectively doubling throughput. "These double-ended hooks work well for small, lightweight parts, especially if you have operators spraying from both sides, and only one side of the part needs to be coated," Davitz said. "And if two sides do need to be coated, you can stagger the placement by changing the hanging points on each side."

Hook designs are endless, of course, and how well each works depends on the part geometry and coating requirements. "When you’re looking for hanging points on a part," Davitz said, "a hole is just a starting point. It makes part hanging easy, but if you have no holes in the part, you can still hang them, as long as you can find a rim or lip. Similarly, if a small part has a channel shape, you can use spring hooks to engage the inside of that channel. CNC wire forming opens up a host of possibilities.

"Know, however, that anywhere that wire [from a hook] contacts the part, you won’t have powder there. So, you want to minimize those touch points. It all revolves around the application requirements."

CLEANING THE HOOKS AND RACKS

How often should hooks and racks be cleaned? "It’s usually not every cycle," Davitz said. "It really depends on the operation and how thick the coating being applied is. If they’re trying to build a certain mil thickness on a part, that same paint is going to build up on the hooks."

Rack frames and crossbars with interior contact points can be left on the line for longer periods while still maintaining the electrical ground. "These crossbars can be staged in a location where someone can insert new hooks onto them, without cleaning the crossbar or entire frame."

Even so, cleaning regimens depend on the needs of an operation and the part mix. If a rack is specifically designed for a part family, the entire rack and all hooks might be cleaned as a unit. Other times, frames and crossbars can be cleaned in less frequent intervals than the hooks themselves. Regardless, all elements— even the crossbars with the interior hook contact points—must be cleaned at some point.

As Davitz described, some operations send out their racks and hooks for cleaning, while others perform cleaning in-house. Using a burnoff oven is the oldest technique, though the resulting ash and smoke can create some environmental concerns. Other methods include using a fluidized bed, in which heated abrasive media like sand is "fluidized" by air to strip paint. Chemical stripping—racks are laid flat in a basket that’s lowered into a chemical bath that strips off the paint—also works.

KANBAN MEETS COATING

Davitz described how some manufacturers with in-house powder coating use frame-hanging carts. These allow individual parts to be hung offline, after which the parts-filled frames can be loaded onto the powder coat line. He added that these carts needn’t remain in the finishing department. Some operations place such racks throughout the value stream.

Imagine the cart in

Figure 6

staged in the press brake area. As operators form each piece, they (or others on the team) hang them on the cart. When a certain number of frames are filled, they’re wheeled downstream to powder coating. When more are needed, a cart is staged by the brakes, ready to accept more formed pieces. It’s pull-type kanban replenishment at its finest.

Such replenishment requires a steady manufacturing rhythm, which means that every process between the final metal manufacturing step (forming at the press brake, for instance) and powder coating needs to be accounted for.

For some applications, this includes masking, and some types of masking can be streamlined, especially if they simply involve plugs for holes. Davitz added that "certain types of masking, like a silicone plug, can be built into the racks themselves." A separate hook—one that provides a metal-on-metal contact point— needs to be there, since silicone plugs don’t allow for electrostatic grounding. Even so, a rack-mounted masking plug can serve a dual purpose: It streamlines masking and further secures the workpiece for powder coating.

FIND A COATING RHYTHM

Parts hanging involves a lot more than operators hooking sheet metal pieces onto racks. It remains just one element of a flexible coating operation.

Regardless, a well-tuned racking and hanging system can become a key improvement that keeps work flowing and, ultimately, helps free the powder coating constraint.
