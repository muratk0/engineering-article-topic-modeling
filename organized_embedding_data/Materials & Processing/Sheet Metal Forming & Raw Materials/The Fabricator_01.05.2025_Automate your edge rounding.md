Quality and consistency upstream pay dividends downstream

chieving a smooth, safe edge is an essential but often overlooked step in metal fabrication. While deburring removes sharp burrs left after cutting, it does not inherently create a rounded edge. Edge rounding, however, goes a step further by eliminating burrs and softening sharp edges (see

Figure 1

).

Why does this matter? The No. 1 reason is safety; sharp edges can harm both workers and customers. Beyond safety, a consistent cut edge makes downstream processes more consistent as well. Consider the rework costs when coating doesn’t adhere to a sharp edge—or, even worse, that sharp edge causes safety issues on your shop floor or elsewhere in the supply chain, like in your customer’s assembly plant.

Whatever cutting method you employ—plasma, laser, punching, or waterjet—the process really isn’t "complete" until you have an edge ready for downstream handling and processing. Often, this involves creating a consistent, safe-to-handle rounded edge, ready for forming, painting, assembly, and beyond.

Burr-Free vs. Rounded Edge

Modern sheet and plate cutting processes can create a small heat-affected zone that, after cooling, can become hard and brittle—especially on the top and bottom corners of that cut edge. A blank might appear to be burr free, but without rounded edges, it still can cause problems downstream.

A rounded edge establishes a foundation that promotes better safety and efficiency in downstream manufacturing processes. And in welding applications, well-prepared edges ensure better fusion between metal parts.

The Need for Consistency

If you visit a shop that relies on manual deburring, you might see workers using angle grinders, sanding blocks, and even hand files to create a rounded edge on a stack of blanks.

Scaling up such an operation, however, usually isn’t practical. Manual edge preparation, including deburring and edge rounding, is highly labor intensive, inconsistent, and time-consuming. Workers must physically inspect each part and adjust their grinding and filing as necessary. Repetitive motion causes ergonomic strain, and safety concerns arise as workers make direct contact with sharp metal edges.

Achieving repeatable results becomes difficult, especially when workers must manually handle large volumes of parts or complex geometries. Creating a smooth edge within a tight interior cutout can be particularly challenging. Process inconsistency can hinder throughput anywhere in the value stream or job routing. But in sheet metal fabrication, inconsistency immediately after cutting can be especially detrimental, essentially making your primary cutting operation less productive.

Blanks without good edge preparation can make their way to welding or painting, at which point the rework ensues. If parts need to be cut again, the ripple effects on the entire production schedule can be profound. Dial in the edge preparation process, however, and the situation changes. This is where process automation comes into play.

Edge and Surface Prep Analysis

Again, your cutting process really isn’t "complete" until those blanks can be sent to the next primary manufacturing operation. As a first step, analyze the edge and surface characteristics of the part profiles your cutting operations are producing. What materials are you cutting? What size, and at what thicknesses? The cutting process might leave some combination of slag, burrs, and sharp edges. Identifying exactly what you need to remove guides your machine investment.

Imagine you have a plasma cutting operation that produces slag. One possible solution would involve first placing parts in a deslagging machine that uses belts with metal pins (see

Figure 2

) that contact the part surface from above and below.

From here, parts are conveyed to another machine that deburrs and rounds the edges. Accomplishing this is a two-step process. First, a set of belt abrasives, above and below the part, grind and deburr the edges.

Next, the part passes between two belts with edge-rounding blocks comprising of nonwoven material and coated abrasive in a brush configuration (see

Figure 3

). The belts can be adjusted to position the brushes so they round the edge (see

Figure 4

). The edge-rounding setting depends on the metal thickness and rounding requirements. Moreover, as the media wears, the edge-rounding blocks adjust automatically to ensure they maintain consistent contact and provide a uniformly rounded edge throughout production.

The deeper the brushes reach, the more significant the rounded corner is on the top and bottom of the edge. The machine’s "zero" calibration point is where the edge-rounding brush tips barely touch the surface of the part. As the brushes move toward the material, from above and below, they gradually produce a more rounded edge. At a certain point, adjusting the brushes produces diminishing returns (and will just lead to excessive brush wear). To round more, the operator slows the conveyor belt speed.

Note that this two-machine setup is designed for deslagging, deburring, and edge rounding, but not graining. The machine’s media does create a light horizontal grain across the part, but the system itself isn’t designed for surface finishing—though other machines are, of course.

Regardless, the example shows how edge rounding occurs within the larger context of surface and edge prep. Manual operations for deslagging, deburring, and edge rounding can create a host of inconsistencies and, not least, create a less-than-ideal, even dangerous working environment. By reducing reliance on manual labor, automated systems help you reallocate workers to more value-adding tasks such as quality control, machine operation, and process optimization.

About Safety

The mixture of metals you’re deburring and edge rounding factor in when considering dust collection and safety. Dust collection requirements, wet or dry, change depending on the mixture of grades you’re processing. Aluminums usually call for wet dust collection, and many operations separate their nonferrous work—for obvious safety reasons.

Regardless, a well-designed, automated edge-rounding system is far safer than the manual alternative. Manual edge rounding requires operators to work in close contact with sharp metal parts, increasing the risk of injuries such as cuts and abrasions. Additionally, exposure to metal dust and debris from grinding operations can pose respiratory hazards.

Automated machines mitigate these risks by minimizing direct handling of sharp edges and enclosing abrasive operations within a controlled environment. With minimal operator intervention required, workers are likely to experience fewer injuries. The result: Overall workplace safety improves.

Material Type and Geometry Factors

When you automate edge rounding, first group your material by thicknesses. Machines can process a range of thicknesses, but most are designed to handle either sheet metal or plate material best.

Different grades and hardnesses will require different machine settings. If you’re processing, say, thick aluminum with a high saw-tooth burr on the edge, you’ll slow the feed and apply high grinding pressure and aggressive edge rounding. Thin galvanized material is on the other end of the spectrum, calling for light pressure and a higher feed rate.

Most other grades sit in between these two extremes. If a fiber laser cuts thin-gauge stainless steel with a relatively burr-free (though still sharp) edge, your operator can use a higher feed rate (though probably not as high as galvanized material) and light pressure. If you’re processing plasma-cut carbon steel with a significant burr, you’ll slow the feed and raise the pressure.

Edge geometry isn’t a limitation. Machines can round straight or contoured edges around the part perimeter and within cutouts of any shape, curved or angular. Operators might create certain edge-rounding conditions for certain geometries. For instance, fasteners might require holes to be somewhat angular, to ensure a solid "friction" fit between the hardware and parent material. In this case, the operator would adjust the system to keep that angular (though still not totally sharp) edge.

On the other hand, some holes that will be painted benefit from having a very rounded, smooth edge, promoting optimal paint adhesion. Similarly, other holes for electrical work will have wires going through them; a very smooth edge ensures no wire is inadvertently frayed by a sharp corner or stray burr. In these cases, the operator would adjust the machine to round these parts more aggressively.

Surface Condition Factors

Galvanized material in particular shows how even automated systems can deburr and edge-round using a careful touch, with little to no effect on the material’s zinc coating. In fact, if you etch a part number (or anything else) on the surface of a cut blank and send it through the deburring and edge-rounding system, you’ll notice the writing is barely changed—a sign that the galvanized material’s zinc coating is still intact.

What if you’re cutting hot-rolled pickled and oiled or another grade with an oily surface? You can send these through the deburring and edge-rounding machine; just know that the system will require more frequent cleaning.

Parts also should be flat. These machines are typically not designed for deburring features such as louvers or up-formed tabs.

Optimize Your Workflow

Whether as a stand-alone unit or part of a larger fabrication process, edge-rounding systems can be configured to work in conjunction with automated material handling solutions, robotic arms, and smart factory environments.

Again, you can configure edge rounding within a multistage process that includes deburring, edge rounding, oxide removal, and surface finishing (graining)—all of which create a blank that’s prepped for smooth downstream processing.

Be sure to think of the big picture here. Customers in numerous sectors—medical devices, food and beverage, steel service centers, general retail—demand slag- and burr-free rounded edges.

Can you imagine worrying about cutting your finger every time you go shopping and reach for something on the shelf? Metal surgical trays hold delicate instruments that must be accessed quickly during operations. Any sharp edge on a tray could puncture surgical gloves, increasing the risk of contamination and potential exposure to pathogens.

Shops might see deburring and edge rounding as a secondary process to avoid. So, they inspect each part and send only those needing deburring and edge rounding to the finishing department, where a team of workers grind away with manual tools.

Imagine the same cutting department, only now with automated deslagging, deburring, and edge rounding. Cut blanks have rounded, smooth edges: repeatable surfaces for press brake backgauges, optimal joint fitup in welding, and good coating adhesion.

Again, edge-rounding systems can be coupled with automated material handling. But even if operators feed blanks into an edge-rounding system, these individuals add significant value. Why? Because they produce quality blanks optimized for easy downstream processing, and they do it in a quick, consistent, and—most important of all—

predictable

way.
