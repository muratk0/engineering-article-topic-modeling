T

he Department of Energy (DOE) just finished holding four hearings around the country getting input on its proposal to revise its 1999 Chronic Beryllium Disease Prevention Program (CBDPP). Those were in conjunction with an upcoming rule that will apply to DOE contractors.

The Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA) issued a separate proposed beryllium rule last December that applies to manufacturers and fabricators more broadly. Beryllium is primarily used to stiffen copper in specialty alloys. These alloys are cast and machined, for example, into nonsparking tooling for applications where fire and explosion are a concern and into bushings for bearings found in commercial and military aircraft landing gear.

The proposed amendments to the CBDPP would do many of the same things as the OSHA-proposed rule, such as requiring engineering and work practice controls and medical surveillance of workers. A key change would be lowering the OSHA-established action level to 0.05 μg/m

(micrograms of gaseous pollutant per cubic meter of ambient air), down from 0.2 μg/m

. Exposures above an action level trigger a number of controls and protective measures.

Medical surveillance requirements are likely to be a sticking point for DOE contractors as they have been for manufacturers covered by the OSHA-proposed rule. The issue has been whether low-dose computed tomography (CT) scans for lung cancer detection are effective and whether employers should have to pay for them.

The proposed amendments to the U.S. Department of Energy’s 1999 Chronic Beryllium Disease Prevention Program would do many of the same things as the OSHAproposed rule, such as requiring engineering and work practice controls and medical surveillance of workers.

Charles Johnson, vice president of policy, The Aluminum Association, said low-dose CT should not be required until concerns about overdiagnosis, false positive findings, follow-up invasive procedures, and other questions are clarified.

Whether either the OSHA- or DOE-proposed rules are finalized by the end of the Obama administration is open to question.

FAA May Make Changes in Small Plane Fabrication Rules

Changes in the fabrication process for small airplanes are a part of a proposed rule from the Federal Aviation Administration (FAA), which is acting on congressional directives to make it easier for manufacturers of normal, utility, acrobatic, and commuter category airplanes to get FAA certification for their designs.

The current system for small plane certification is based on prescriptive standards adopted decades ago. Manufacturers of those planes complain they cannot get certification under the current standards for modern, innovative designs—for example, for hybrid and electric propulsion. This leaves manufacturers with an aging stock of planes that prospective pilots turn their nose up at.

Congress passed the Small Airplane Revitalization Act of 2013, which requires the FAA to issue a final rule revising the certification requirements for small airplanes by adopting performance-based regulations based on consensus standards. The proposed rule has a section on design and fabrication standards. Manufacturers would have to use methods and processes of fabrication and assembly that produce consistently sound structures. If a fabrication process requires close control to reach that objective, the applicant would have to perform the process under an approved process specification.

Design values would have to ensure material strength. Fabricators of small planes would have to attest to strength of the structural elements of the plane and account for material variability used in those different subassemblies.
