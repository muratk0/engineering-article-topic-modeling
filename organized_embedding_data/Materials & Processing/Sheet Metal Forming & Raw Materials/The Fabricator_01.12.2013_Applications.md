Magnetic drills, customized cutters solve drilling challenge for pressure vessel-maker

Situation

Mueller Environmental Designs, located outside of Houston, manufactures a patented helical separator system that eliminates almost all of the contaminants from natural gas.

The pressure vessels, which measure in diameter from 4 to 8 ft., are made from 0.5- to 3.5-in.-thick 5617 rolled steel plate. Inside the vessels is a gas-cleaning separator and an area to hold the accumulated solids and liquids that are removed.

To measure the amount of waste material inside the tank, liquid level indicators are placed 1 to 2 in. off the bottom of the tank, and a high-level indicator is located in the middle of the tank. Holes from 1 to 2 in. dia. must be drilled into the vessels for these level indicators.

Drilling a hole into the center of a large pressure vessel can present its own set of challenges, but nothing like the challenges of drilling the radically angled hole at the bottom of the vessel.

For years the company’s workers used an old magnetic drill with a drill chuck and twist drills to make the lower-level holes. Starting with a 0.375-in. twist drill, the operator drilled a pilot hole, then enlarged it with a 0.625-in. twist drill, and enlarged it more to 0.75 in. This process continued until the proper hole diameter was achieved. Depending on the hole angle and location, sometimes the operator had to use arc gouging and grinding to finish the hole. The process took eight to 12 hours per hole, and each vessel requires two holes.

Resolution

The company contacted Hougen Mfg., which recommended its standard HMD915 magnetic drill and a couple of Rotabroach® cutters. On a thinner-walled vessel, operators welded a platform that was perpendicular to the angle that needed to be drilled. The platform allowed the magnetic drill to magnetize, and the plate acted as a guide bushing for the cutter to drill into the edge of the vessel.

The machine drilled through the wall of the vessel, on the proper angle, without a problem. However, Mueller noted that future projects would require a depth of cut on larger vessels of up to 8 in., and the standard cutters were available only for up to a 6-in. depth of cut.

Hougen engineers increased the stroke on the magnetic drill from a standard 3 in. to 6 in. to accommodate a larger Rotabroach cutter. With this extra stroke, Mueller could use a 6-in.-depth cutter to drill straight through the vessels requiring a 1-in. hole. On the 2-in.-dia. holes and through the 3-in.-thick side-walled vessels, the operator could use a step-up method of drilling. This involves drilling down with a tool for the first depth of cut, then using a longer tool to drill the rest of the hole. For deep holes, this lessens the horsepower needed and aids chip extraction. Custom 2-in.-dia. by 8-in.-deep cutters were made to drill through the longest areas with the most extreme angles.

Mueller operators tested the magnetic drill and customized cutters on a vessel that required 2-in.-dia. holes drilled through a 3-in. side wall with a total drilling depth of 8 in. After setting up the guide plate, they drilled through the guide plate and down 3 in. Next, they inserted a 6-in. cutter in the hole and continued drilling until the cutter bottomed out. Finally, they used the custom-made 8-in. cutter to complete the hole. Total drilling time was 45 min., saving 12 hrs. per hole.

Band saw brings turbulator production back in-house

Situation

Turbulator Co., located in Oklahoma City, Okla., makes turbulators for the cementing phase of the oil well drilling process. To produce these metal cylinders with blades welded to the outer surface, the company makes about 700,000 high-tolerance, precision cuts per year.

Until recently the company was using two automated band saws to process all the cutting jobs, but it often had to farm out work to job shops to keep up with production demands. As customer delivery schedules grew tighter and more new orders were entered, McHenry decided that approach wasn’t enough to remain competitive while maintaining or improving quality.

Resolution

The company acquired a Behringer Eisele HCS 160 carbide circular cold saw for high-speed cutting of metal barstock. The fully automatic saw is designed for cutting high-alloy, heat-resistant steels.

Configured for workpiece diameters up to 160 mm, the saw delivers speeds of 20 to 250 RPM to accommodate wet and dry sawing applications. Its rigid frame helps dampen vibrations, and the blade guide absorbs blade vibration.

This saw uses multifluid cooling technology to prevent heat transfer to product during mass cutting applications. Its flood coolant system enables rapid cutting over long periods of time with few interruptions. Operators don’t need to keep checking the work for heat buildup, so unattended operation is increased. Additionally, the saw has a vortex air blowing system to help improve cutting temperature control and to ensure material stays free of liquid when required.

The single saw has replaced the original two band saws, and the company no longer relies on outside machine shops for cutting.

“The saw helped us shorten our supply chain,” said McHenry, “and in the oil drilling business, that is an important step toward being successful.”
