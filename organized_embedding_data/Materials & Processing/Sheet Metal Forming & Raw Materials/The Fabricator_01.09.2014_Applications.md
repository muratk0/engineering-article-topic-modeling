Manufacturing network cooperates to hold tight tolerance on complex stamped part

Situation

Haag Education, a division of Haag Eng., recently had to find a supplier for the new Haag Roofing and Siding Gauge™.

Unlike other parts the company sourced in the past, this was a complex metal fabrication; it required holding a tolerance of 0.0005 in. after being heat-treated and plated.

Resolution

Erica Lee, product and communications manager, put an RFQ on

Fabricating.com

and was soon contacted by Jeremy Hahn, sales manager at Chirch Global

®

Mfg., Cary, Ill. Hahn explained that while the stamped part seemed like it would be straightforward, he anticipated a lot of potential problems caused by the tight tolerance requirements.

“Holding 0.0005-in. tolerance is like slitting an average piece of hair in half,” said Hahn. “You’re literally splitting hairs. It’s not something most stampers are comfortable with.”

Hahn had the support of the Chirch Global Manufacturing Network, a group of 14 companies that serve as a single source to their global customers. On this project, both Induction Heat Treating Corp. and Reliable Plating Corp. were able to help.

They chose the induction heat-treating process over furnace heat treating, because heat treating the entire part can cause the metal to distort or bend. The distortion is miniscule, but it makes a difference at such tight tolerances.

The material was plated with mid-phosphorus electroless nickel measuring 0.0002 to 0.0003 in. thick, followed by a three-hour bake at 375 degrees F for hydrogen embrittlement relief. They chose electroless nickel plating for the uniformity of the deposit, as a little change would make a big difference at the tight tolerances. The bake was required because of the material’s hardness, but it had to be done just right so that it would not fracture any of the heat-treated areas that were critical to part function.

After going through other, secondary operations, Lee was able to get the product she was looking for.

Laser scanners replace tactile probing for body-in-white inspection

Situation

Based in Istanbul, Turkey, TOFA (Türk Otomobil Fabrikas A..) manufactures cars, taxis, and vans and sells them internationally. Employing more than 6,000 people, the company is one of the top two automotive manufacturers in the region, with a production capacity of 400,000 units per year at its factory in Bursa. Products are offered under several brands including Fiat (which jointly owns the company), Peugeot, Citroen, and Opel.

The company was using touch probes for making diagnostic measurements of sheet metal components and body-in-white (BIW) assemblies.

Resolution

The company installed Nikon Metrology XC65Dx-LS cross scanners and CAMIO multisensor metrology software, which are retrofitted on existing Hexagon DEA Bravo double-arm coordinate measuring machines (CMMs) at Bursa. Inspection is now twice as fast compared to touch probes and provides better insight, reducing the time needed to diagnose problems.

Incorporating three lasers in a cross pattern, the cross scanners digitize complex features from three sides and acquire the complete 3-D geometry of the features. Smart laser intensity adaptation allows any surface, such as those with varying color or high reflectivity, to be scanned without the use of a matte spray or other user interaction. This is enabled by automatic, real-time adjustment of sensor settings between successive laser stripes and for each individual point along the laser stripe.

The cross scanners capture geometry from up to 6.70 in. away, gaining optimum access to BIW structures and scanning over the clamps that hold components in position.

During vehicle development, the scanners are used instead of touch probes for inspecting both individual car panels and for complete diagnosis of the BIW after the car’s sheet metal panels have been welded together but before the hood, doors, and trunk lid have been added. In this way, the vehicle’s entire sheet metal structure is inspected to very close tolerances, showing the interaction between the panels and allowing parts issues to be separated from process issues. Completed vehicles also are inspected, mainly for gap and flush spacing between different car panels.

“The savings are significant when using scanners instead of tactile probes for feature and surface inspection of BIW on our CMM,” said Özgür Ogur in the quality department at Bursa. “Laser scanning fulfills our accuracy requirements, and there are no significant differences in accuracy between tactile and scanning. We require 0.2-mm uncertainty on a 5.5-m diagonal, and as the inspection device has to resolve to one-tenth of the tolerance band, the scanners need to measure down to 20 microns. Measurements have proven that the scanners achieve this requirement in line with their listed specifications. Inspection is now twice as fast as when we used touch probes, reducing lead-time between design iterations and raising efficiency.”
