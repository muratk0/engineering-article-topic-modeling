# Truck parts maker eliminates pallets with reusable storage cartridges

[TARİH: 01.07.2026 The Fabricator]

Material Handling

Daimler uses Lean Manufacturing Products system to vastly curtail waste, time spent looking for material

By

Lincoln Brunner

Wisconsin Metal Parts started making the cartridge system now sold by Lean Manufacturing Products as a way to create a high-density material storage system that would eliminate wooden pallets while improving safety and material flow.

Lincoln Brunner

A

s Zachary Bingham and his team looked across their sprawling warehouse three and a half years ago, they knew they were painting, as it were, from the wrong palette.

Actually, make that pallet.

Part of Bingham’s job as materials supervisor for Daimler Truck North America Parts and Logistics in Gastonia, N.C., is to ensure that material—in this case, sheet metal—moves efficiently through the warehouse. The division makes millions of parts per year for Freightliner and Western Star trucks, Thomas Built buses, and Freightliner custom chassis on about 10 laser cutting stations served by multiple material handling towers.

The issue at hand: Wooden pallets littered the warehouse, representing both a potential safety hazard and an environmentally unfriendly storage option, as many pallets routinely splintered and had to be scrapped. Unfortunately, that was the M.O.: The facility used innumerable wooden pallets and a massive collection of cantilever racks to hold thousands of sheets of steel and aluminum that range from 1/16 to 1 in. thick.

Not only were the pallets a safety hazard, but constantly transferring material from pallets to the racks with side-loading lifts meant an additional step, repeated heaven knows how many times per day, every day.

So, when Bingham found out about Lean Manufacturing Products’ returnable cartridge system for storing sheet metal in uniformly spaced racks, he was duly intrigued. The cassettes have a 5,000-lb. capacity and can be customized to hold material from 48 by 96 in. to 72 by 144 in. or other custom sizes.

DTNA uses 4- by 10-ft. and 5- by 10-ft. cartridges for its sheets—690 in all, staged on 37 different racks located throughout the facility. The salient difference between then and now is that instead of delivering material to DTNA on wooden pallets, the many steel service centers supplying the facility deliver the sheets already loaded into the cartridges, which DTNA cycles endlessly through its array of cutting machines.

Because of the space between the steel cartridge on the racks and the wider-than-normal 3- by 10-in. fork holes in the cassettes, material handlers can move the cassettes in and out of racks with ease. In addition, the weight of the material is borne by the racks, not the material itself, as it would be on wooden pallets.

Lean Manufacturing installed the first racks at DTNA in June 2023. "We went with the Lean cassette-style system to increase the amount of material we can store per square foot and decrease our carbon footprint by eliminating wooden skids, on top of safety issues," Bingham said. "We did this by using Lean’s returnable cartridges. We sent those to and from our metal suppliers in place of wooden pallets. This also helped us phase out aging lifts and create a safer and more standard way of moving material with a forklift.

"This process is not only faster, it’s safer—and it’s able to be done with one employee versus two, which saves on indirect cost."

—Zachary Bingham, Daimler Truck North America Parts and Logistics

"We send them empty cartridges in return to keep a consistent cycle, like a milk run. This eliminates pallets and helps with quality. It also saves us the step of needing to transfer the sheet metal onto Lean cartridges."

Lean Manufacturing Products was launched about eight years ago by Wisconsin Metal Parts, a contract manufacturer in Waukesha, Wis., as a way to better organize the raw materials in its shop. Wisconsin Metal Parts built some racks for itself, one of its large OEM customers saw them in action, and the orders to customize additional systems started rolling in.

"They were looking for a high-density storage system," Lean Manufacturing Products Director of Sales Steve Drida said. "There were one or two [systems] on the market at that point, and they explored those and asked if they could do things differently or add different things and get more out of it.

"It’s not the cheapest solution. You can get pallet racking for a few thousand bucks per footprint, but it’s totally different in comparison. So, from exploring that, that’s how we went down this rabbit hole and spun off into Lean."

Back at the DTNA parts facility, once cartridges are unloaded at the plant, they’re stacked up, each one with a unique container ID to match against the pick tickets that employees use to quickly locate materials before they drive them to the appropriate material handling tower or another holding spot before fabrication. Material then goes to a depalletizer, which removes the metal from the cartridge and takes it directly to the laser to get loaded.

The cartridges have their own polished steel positioning pins—roughly 2 by 6 in.—that are inserted into holes on the perimeter of the frame. The pins secure the material within the cartridge until unloading and also prevent material from being damaged when material handlers occasionally bump cassettes into each another or into the storage rack on which they’re placed. These pins are also what allow the cartridges to have the direct stacking function while avoiding any accumulative weight on the sheet metal itself.

"This process is not only faster, it’s safer—and it’s able to be done with one employee versus two, which saves on indirect cost," Bingham said.

And, with material prices ever susceptible to the latest tariffs and trade disputes, ensuring material quality every way possible has become all the more important the past few years, especially for a facility that churns through the volume that DTNA does at the plant. The returnable cartridges keep sheets stacked and protected like a deck of cards in a box during shipment so they can’t slide off the top of a bundle or get damaged by too much material loaded on top of them on a pallet.

"It pretty much eliminates the opportunity for … material to be oversized or be wavy," Drida said. "If it’s misaligned on a truck and it’s thin gauge so that vibration and weight from the truck make it wavy, you might not hit tolerance on your press brake if you don’t catch it with your naked eye."

The change at DTNA Parts and Logistics has been nothing short of remarkable. Over the past three years, there’s been a "decrease in service time—time material is ordered to time being delivered—and our accidents stemming around moving material on wooden skids have dropped to zero since we have switched to metal cassettes," Bingham said. "Indirect labor costs [have] decreased due to the time savings. Also, morale is up, and this took little to no effort to get floor buy-in. All shifts have loved it since it was installed."
