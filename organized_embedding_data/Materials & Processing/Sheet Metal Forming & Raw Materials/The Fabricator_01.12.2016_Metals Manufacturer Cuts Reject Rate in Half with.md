SITUATION

When considering the many steps involved in producing polished medical equipment, finished office equipment, and metal components and parts—from design, manufacture, and finishing to assembly, inspection, and delivery—and considering the time, effort, and costs involved in each step, packaging and shipping would seem to be the easiest parts of the process.

But for Mark Schoenborn, COO at metal fabricator DeWys Mfg. in Marne, Mich., these final steps posed a challenge. He manages a staff of 160 employees at DeWys, which serves customers throughout the U.S. with precision sheet metal fabrication, parts machining, welding, contract manufacturing, powder coating, assembly, and direct shipping.

Running a company dedicated to lean manufacturing principles, including delivering high-quality products with zero defects, Schoenborn struggled with an order return rate he considered unacceptable. A single order that gets rejected upon arrival and returned could cost DeWys several thousand dollars. For a company that ships nearly 150 pallet loads each day, these costs stack on top of the costs in time, materials, and labor to repair or completely reproduce the product or part and then reship.

Defects like this create a cascading effect that saddles customers and, in turn, their customers, with costly delays and potentially lost sales. Schoenborn was growing increasingly concerned not only about the costs, but damage to the company’s reputation as well.

Orders were either wrapped on a semiautomatic horizontal stretch wrapper or by hand. Though workers paid close attention to the plastic wrapping, the wrapping system was coming up short.

From a review of data on returned orders, Schoenborn noted that most of the returns were because of scratches, scuffs, and other defects that occurred during transport to the customer. The plastic film surrounding the parts was not always strong or wrapped well enough to keep the parts and products secured in place.

The high center of gravity of the finished furniture and equipment, the mass of many heavy metal parts, and the parts’ sometimes long or unwieldy shapes caused products to shift inside trucks and trailers. All too often, a large bump, short stop at a traffic light, or tight turn caused large pieces of metal to slide off of the pallet and arrive scarred from the trip and unacceptable upon inspection.

RESOLUTION

While attending the FABTECH

®

tradeshow, Schoenborn saw the TAB Wrapper Tornado, which wraps products 360 degrees around and under the pallet to join the products to the pallet and create a single, secure, unitized load.

The orbital wrapping machine, offered in three standard sizes to suit 60- by 60-in., 48- by 48-in., and 24- by 24-in. pallets and in custom sizes, encases the entire palletized load in multiple layers of plastic film to keep everything intact and in one place, helping to ensure safe arrivals while also guarding against the elements. Instead of requiring two workers to wrap each pallet, the wrapper allows a single forklift driver to center the load within the wrapping ring and wrap the load while it is raised on the forklift. With automated, wireless remote operation and automated cut and wrap, the forklift driver can operate the wrapper without leaving his or her seat.

"I immediately saw how the [wrapper] fastens the load to the skid and knew it would prevent product damage during transit and make our whole system run faster and more efficiently," said Schoenborn. "I bought one on the spot."

The machine replaced the horizontal turntable wrapper and the manual wrapping method at DeWys and currently is used to wrap more than 150 skids per day. All four lift truck drivers use it almost nonstop. Though the 80-in. model was designed to accommodate standard, 48-in. pallets, the machine handles a variety of products, regardless of shape or size, according to Schoenborn.

"With the [wrapper], we’ve been able to eliminate several steps in the packaging process, we’ve eliminated ergonomic and safety issues with manual wrapping, and we’re saving so much time," said Schoenborn. The horizontal turntable wrapper required two people and a full minute to wrap a pallet, while the orbital wrapper requires one person and 15 seconds. At 150 pallets per day, DeWys saves four labor-hours each day by using the new machine. This translates to 1,040 labor-hours saved annually, a direct savings of $15,600 and a payback time on the machine of less than a year.

DeWys also has been able to meet on-time delivery targets and increase sales without hiring additional full-time employees to work in the packing and shipping area. "The savings that come from not having to hire more people aren’t as visible as cutting the cost of material or health care, but they impact the bottom line just the same, or more," explained Schoenborn.

Most important, DeWys Mfg. has cut its order return rate by at least 50 percent since installing the machine. This translates to far fewer customer complaints and thousands of dollars in savings. It has also enhanced the company’s reputation as a quality manufacturer while meeting the lean manufacturing demands for efficiency and continuous improvement.
