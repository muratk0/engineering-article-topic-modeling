# How SparkForce camps spark engagement

Biz Talk

And why it matters for the industry’s future

TIM HESTON

A SparkForce camper, working with SolidWorks, discusses a project with Augusta Tech instructor Warren Overton.

W

arren Overton, an instructor at Augusta Technical College in Augusta, Ga., walked over to the front of the manufacturing lab to talk to a student in her early teens, drawing a metal caster in SolidWorks. This was the final day of STEM Ignited Summer Camp, held in early June at the college and supported by SparkForce, The FMA Foundation.

Overton answered the student’s question by asking questions, to let her discover the answer on her own. After she found the answer, Overton again chimed in.

"See? You first did the upper quadrant of the wheel. You mirrored it, then you revolved it. And look at this here. You can choose different material. Right click, and look, you can make it out of 1060 aluminum, or mild steel, or anything else, as long as it makes sense for the design and overall manufacturing." The student then looked up aluminum 1060—

turns out that alloy has low mechanical strength compared to other materials. Is this the best choice? That depends on the application.

So much talk in manufacturing revolves around either the latest technologies or (this year at least) the effects of tariffs and trade wars. Technologies advance or become obsolete. Elected officials in Washington come and go with the political winds. What doesn’t change, though, is the need for good people.

Everyone knows manufacturing has a skilled labor shortage, but when you get to the root of it all, what drives people to choose manufacturing boils down to two things: exposure and human interaction. Exposure will happen next month as student groups tour the FABTECH show floor in Chicago.

But it’s also happening at SparkForce camps throughout the country—including the one at Augusta Tech.

In another classroom, Kim Gaines, department head and instructor, asked the kids about their trip to Plant Vogtle, a nuclear power plant in nearby Waynesboro, Ga.

"Even though it was focused on nuclear engineering, I saw a lot of influences of mechanical engineering, software engineering, materials. The variety was really great to see."

That was Ashwin Gurumoorthy, a 13-year-old camper who in June got exposure that he and his fellow campers likely wouldn’t have otherwise. Any plant—be it in manufacturing, processing, or a nuclear generator—has an array of career opportunities, especially for those who like to solve problems.

During a discussion later that day, Gaines asked her room of campers a question that was all about solving problems, though not of a technical kind. "Remember? We talked about the SWOT analysis? How about the segmentation of your market for your product?"

The students were preparing PowerPoints showing the products they developed—not just on the computer screen but actual cardboard mockups. One team had a tilted conveyor that they tested with a marble that didn’t move quite the way they expected. They iterated the design, moved some cardboard components, and the marble rolled as expected, free of barriers. Gaines observed, asked how they solved the issue, then made a few suggestions herself. High-fives all around.

Later in the day, Gaines asked a question: "So, what’s the difference between an experimental design and an engineering design?" They discussed the answer. The first is grounded in the scientific method, while engineering design focuses on an iterative process of creating practical solutions.

Listening to the campers, I could tell most of them knew their way around software—no surprise there, considering the iPhone was released years before they were born. Touchscreens are just a part of life. Even working with 3D CAD seems natural.

"Since I was 10 years old, I’ve been looking into different CAD systems like CAD Sketcher and Blender. I just thought the design process was so cool." That was 15-year-old camper Kayla Tiller, who in the years since has designed a variety of games and clothing items, rendering it all online. "For me, it all just clicked."

More than anything else, manufacturing offers people the chance to solve problems—big questions like what will power tomorrow’s grid, along with into-the-weeds questions, like designing around the characteristics of 1060 aluminum. Being curious and engaged is what makes any job worth doing. And in manufacturing, a curious and engaged person can go far.
