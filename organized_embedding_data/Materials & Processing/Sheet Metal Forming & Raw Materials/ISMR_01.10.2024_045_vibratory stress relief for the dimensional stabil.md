# Vibratory stress relief for the dimensional stabilisation of welded components before, during or after mechanical processing is becoming increasingly important.

"Vibratory stress relief applies targeted vibrations to component areas with existing residual stresses. The yield point of the material is exceeded at localised points, resulting in localised relief and the reduction of residual stresses."

ISMR SAYS:

By | Jebens GmbH Jebens GmbH is a specialist in large, heavy, flame-cut parts; complex assemblies, mechanical processing and welded structures (with weights of up to 160 tonnes per item). It is based at Korntal-Münchingen and Nördlingen in Germany.

In components made from metallic materials, residual stresses occur during manufacture, processing and deformation which are unevenly expressed and distributed. These can lead to distortion and cracking of the component during further processing and, with the operating load, can even lead to its failure.

Traditionally, stress relief annealing is the method of choice for relieving internal stresses in metallic structures. Equally tried and tested for decades, yet significantly less well known and used, is vibratory stress relief. This process is considerably faster, cheaper and more environmentally friendly. In view of increasing demands on environmental protection, resource conservation and cost structures, vibratory stress relief for the dimensional stabilisation of welded components before, during or after mechanical processing is, therefore, becoming increasingly important from the perspective of sustainability and costeffectiveness.

Vibratory stress relief, which was introduced in the USA around 100 years ago and marketed and significantly improved by VSR Industrietechnik GmbH in Germany over the last 50 years, works by applying targeted vibrations to component areas with existing residual stresses. Due to the targeted load effect of the imbalance motor in areas with high residual stress, the yield point of the material is exceeded at localised points. This results in localised relief and the reduction of residual stresses.

## The stress relief process

At the beginning of the process, it is important to consider the correct positioning of the workpiece, in addition to the material and weight of the component. The minimum acceleration required in the component of ten metres per second squared, and the maximum speed of up to 6,000 revolutions per minute, are then determined and set at the level of the natural frequencies. The modal hammer generates multiple force pulses by striking the component briefly and forcefully.

During vibratory stress relief, two vibration transducers measure and monitor the acceleration triggered in the component by the impulse applied. A triaxial accelerometer measures the acceleration of the workpiece in the X, Y and Z axes. A monoaxial sensor measures how much acceleration is being applied at the point on the plate furthest away from the motor.

The stress relief process takes place in five cycles of up to ten frequencies each, in which the modal hammer determines the natural frequencies of the component. Once the frequency of the component changes, the automatic control system uses modal analysis to adjust the imbalance and rotational speed accordingly. A frequency band is used to determine the respective stress relief status of the component. A final measurement is taken once the five cycles have been completed. All the frequencies are recorded in a log and the stress relief status is depicted in a diagram.

For large components, which are the order of the day at Jebens, the motor must be moved several times to ensure that the entire component can be relieved of stress.

All the processing frequencies are determined, processed and documented automatically (alphanumerically and graphically) in the vibration log.

According to VSR Industrietechnik, modal analysis has provided scientifically valid evidence that the residual stresses in the component have been reduced to such an extent that distortion in the component can be ruled out. Crack formation in components is also eliminated due to the low vibration amplitude. The Duisburg-based company has sold around 150 vibratory stress relief systems to date.

Industrial companies such as ThyssenKrupp, AUDI, Ford, Caterpillar, Liebherr and Sennebogen have been using this process in their own production for decades or have prescribed its use to their suppliers. At Jebens, many demanding industrial customers also rely on vibratory stress relief for components for press brakes with part weights of up to 100 tonnes, for forging manipulators with part weights of up to 40 tonnes or for presses with part weights of up to 80 tonnes.
