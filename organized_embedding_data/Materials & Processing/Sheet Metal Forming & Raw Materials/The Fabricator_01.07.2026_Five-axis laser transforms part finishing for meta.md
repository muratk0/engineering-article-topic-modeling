SITUATION

Founded in 1982, Wenzel Metal Spinning operates multiple facilities in Indiana and Alabama. With roughly 250,000 sq. ft. of manufacturing space and about 150 employees, the company maintains a diverse market portfolio.

"We avoid being limited to a single niche," said Ryan Funkhouser, general manager. "Our capabilities span from high-volume industrial components, like trash can lids, to high-precision, specialized parts made with exotic materials for the Mars Rover."

The Metal Spinning part of the company’s name refers to a cold-work process that forms a flat metal disk or tube over a rotating mandrel, creating a round, axially symmetric part. Shapes typically constructed with metal spinning include conical, parabolic, and toroidal. The process, which can be performed manually or by a CNC lathe, serves as an alternative to low-volume or short-run metal stamping.

"It’s as much an art form as it is a science," Funkhouser explained. "In many ways, it’s akin to blacksmithing. You are moving metal, flowing it, changing its grain structure. It requires a feel for the material that you can’t just program into a computer."

Because the metal spinning process is so labor-intensive, it is essential to avoid bottlenecks in both upstream and downstream steps that disrupt production and reduce efficiency. For years, Wenzel faced a critical postprocessing challenge: Trimming spun parts was a slow and often dangerous manual task that lacked the consistency needed to meet increasingly complex customer demands.

"We were hitting a wall," Funkhouser explained. "We had the capacity to spin parts faster than we could finish them. We saw opportunities to bid on contracts that required complex hole patterns or intricate cutouts, but we declined them because we couldn’t do the finishing cost-effectively. We needed a solution that could handle the 3D nature of our parts."

RESOLUTION

To address the bottleneck issues, the company began a search for a 5-axis laser system.

"We looked at everything—speed, footprint, software, and support," said Ned Kaiser, operations manager. "Ultimately, Prima Power best fit our overall needs, including the size of the cabin, which accommodated the larger parts we make. We chose them because they understood our business. They didn’t just try to sell us a laser; they looked at our workflow. … Plus, the support network was there. When you’re running production, you can’t wait three weeks for a technician."

The integration of the Prima Power Laser Next 2131 into Wenzel’s workflow transformed how the company finishes complex parts. Specifically, the machine’s split-cabin design allows the laser to machine a part on one table while an operator loads or unloads the other, eliminating downtime and allowing the laser to run continuously for a seamless flow.

Operations that used to take hours of manual labor or required moving parts between multiple machines are now completed in minutes.

"What previously required multiple jigs, steps, and several workers is now down to a single operation," said Production Manager Lance Angus. "We’ve cut tasks that took over an hour down to just a few minutes."

Beyond speed, 5-axis capability has enabled the company to achieve tolerances previously impossible with manual trimming, added Kaiser. "We are doing aerospace parts where the tolerance is ±0.005 in," which has drastically reduced scrap. And eliminating manual trimming has removed the risk of injury caused by sharp metal edges and the spinning blades of routers, band saws, and circle shears.

With a user-friendly design, the machine is easy for new operators to learn. And experienced operators like Kaiser can combine decades of hands-on forming knowledge with 5-axis programming, allowing the company to bring complex, one-off jobs online in minutes instead of hours.

"We are no longer just a spinning house that does a little fabrication on the side," Funkhouser reflected. "We are a full-service metal forming solution. This machine has given us the confidence to take on more complex jobs where spinning is just the starting point."
