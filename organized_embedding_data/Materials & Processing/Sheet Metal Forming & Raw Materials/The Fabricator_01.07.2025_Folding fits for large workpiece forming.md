# Folding fits for large workpiece forming

Forming

The right folding machine setup can deliver improvements in part accuracy and production speed

By

David Prokop

I

f asked to name the most demanding process in fabricating sheet metal components, nearly all metal fabricating professionals would emphatically agree on bending.

The challenges of blank creation are all but solved. Many options exist to make huge varieties and volumes of blanks with very little labor. But here’s the catch: An estimated 70% to 90% of these sheet metal parts undergo some form of bending. This is where the challenges begin.

Despite advancements in traditional bending methods over the years, the process still demands skilled operators, involves significant labor, and remains one of the more hazardous tasks in a workshop. The difficulty in finding and retaining qualified employees drives up labor costs even further. These rising expenses ripple through the entire process chain, as downstream operations also grapple with labor challenges and escalating costs.

Modern servo-electric press brakes have emerged to help with small, intricate profiles. A single operator can process those small parts quickly and efficiently with such technology.

For higher production volumes, robots or cobots can help in the bending department. They run continuously, cutting labor costs, reducing the chance of operator injury, and increasing part quality.

Panel benders are ideal for larger, thin-gauge parts, especially those needed in high production volumes.

But what about mid- to large-scale sheet metal parts? These parts represent the greatest challenge for forming, as their size consumes labor, cripples production speed, and destroys part accuracy.

Challenges When Forming Large Parts on a Press Brake

Material handling remains the biggest issue with press brakes. More than 90% of the part being formed is outside the machine, placing stress on those that need to support the part during the bending cycle. As part size grows, productivity drops, labor demands increase, safety concerns escalate, and accuracy plummets.

Parts too large for one operator require two, more than doubling labor costs as output per unit of labor quickly declines. Parts too large for two operators need equipment like jib cranes, forklifts, or other lifting and support devices, escalating costs even further and slowing productivity to a standstill. Sheet lifters help support the sheet during the bend, but they can’t flip or lift the part up and over the die for reverse bends.

These material handling challenges not only drive up costs in the forming department, they also affect downstream operations. Variations in production speed based on part size create inconsistencies in downstream workflow, disrupting line balancing and further reducing overall efficiency. And the more humans are involved in an operation, especially manually maneuvering large workpieces, the more difficult it becomes to maintain consistent accuracy from one part to the next.

Poor consistency leads to poor fitment, which results in extra time and effort for assembly when bolt holes don’t align, for example.

If the parts are destined for robotic welding cells, costly fixtures might be needed to force parts into position so robots can track seams. With an estimated 50% to 80% of sheet metal parts requiring welding, proper fitment is essential for maximizing welding efficiency.

Modern folding technology can help metal fabricators overcome these difficulties, delivering accuracy, efficiency, and labor savings, regardless of part size.

Folding Fits the Bill for Large-Format Forming

One of folding’s greatest advantages is its ability to eliminate size-related issues while maintaining consistent processing times. Folding machines keep more than 90% of the part under control, moving only the flange during bends. Parts never need flipping, supporting, or wrestling. A single operator easily manages even the largest, heaviest parts on the folder’s ball transfer support table. The semiautomated gauge holds the part, progressing it from bend to bend, while the folder seamlessly transitions between up and down bends without operator involvement.

The ease of part manipulation on a folder helps ensure high, consistent part accuracy. This eliminates fitment issues in downstream operations, and as accuracy becomes standard, downstream costs drop significantly.

But a folder’s capabilities extend beyond ergonomics. The machine also addresses one of the biggest challenges in large-scale part forming: material thickness variation.

Both surfaces of the material come into play when bending with a punch and die. As material thickness varies, part penetration into the die varies, and angular accuracies are compromised. Thickness variation is responsible for about 70% of the bend errors on a press brake. A folder is unaffected by material thickness, bending in the same way a protractor measures. Using only the outside surface, the protractor accurately measures the angle, ignoring the material’s thickness. Similarly, a folder bends the material by pivoting the outside surface to the desired angle, eliminating thickness variation errors.

Forming large parts on a folding machine is efficient when compared to the same part being formed on a press brake, and the entire process can be done quickly with one operator.

A folding machine’s folding beam maneuvers the outside surface of the sheet metal to the desired angle.

Folding machines can use extremely tall tooling, making the technology suitable for forming large box-type fabrications.

Even 20-in.-tall tools can be managed with an automatic tool changing system. With these systems, tool setup times average less than 60 seconds.

Offsets, radii, and other special forms are done on a folding machine using the same tools as all other bends.

A fully automated folding cell has a robot that handles blank placement, part removal, and part stacking.

Metal fabricators might be surprised to learn about the type of parts that can be produced on a folding machine.

The remaining 30% of angular errors on press brakes stem from tensile and grain variation. As the punch tip presses into the material, it creates a compressed zone at the bend center, effectively dividing the resulting radius into two distinct halves. In contrast, folders form a single air-formed radius outside the tool tip, cutting tensile variation error by roughly half.

Angular accuracy is critical for maintaining dimensional precision, as each bend depends on the accuracy of the previous one. A 0.5-degree error in a bend results in a 0.008-in. flange error at a 1-in. vertical gauge point, compounding to a 0.024-in. error across three bends and nearly 0.050-in. when both sides are bent—enough to disrupt robotic welding and reduce manual welding efficiency. Larger parts amplify these challenges, causing errors to cascade with each bend.

Folders eliminate the issue by gauging to a single datum point per part side, from which all subsequent bends are made. The gauge securely grips the part, automatically feeding it from bend to bend, eliminating cumulative errors that compromise part fitment.

Additionally, the part rests on a ball transfer table, eliminating gauging issues related to wrestling with large parts.

Fewer Tooling Changeover Concerns

Further production gains are achieved with a folder’s ability to use a single toolset for bending the full range of material thicknesses and types within the machine’s capacity. This reduces setup time, minimizes tooling costs, and maximizes overall efficiency. Different part radii, material thickness offsets, and hems are simply programmed, and all are achieved with the same single set of tools.

When setting up the tools for the required part width, Automatic Tool Change can add further production gains and reduce tool setups to less than a minute on average, during which time the operator can remove the finished part and bring in the next flat. The rapid part-to-part time makes the equipment suitable for kit production, feeding to assembly or welding, and maintains a high production velocity regardless of part or run size.

Automatic Tool Change further enhances the folder’s capabilities by accommodating tall tools that are too heavy for manual changes. Standard tools measure 15¾ in. tall, with options up to 23½ in. for specialized applications. A 1-to-1 ratio between tool height and four-sided box depth allows for an exceptionally large part envelope.

Automated Material Handling

Automated part handling on a folder is simple and efficient. The part remains in a horizontal plane throughout the bending cycle, never flipped or regripped by the robot, which functions solely as a pick-and-place device. The robot picks a blank from a stack or conveyor and positions it on the alignment plate for precise orientation. The robot then transfers it to the folder’s gauge table, aligning it against gauge pins. The Advanced Handling System securely grips the part, controls the entire bending cycle, and returns the finished piece to the robot for removal and stacking. Meanwhile, the next part is prestaged to help ensure uninterrupted workflow. Designed to handle parts up to 159 in. by 72 in. by 0.196 in., the automatic handling system operates consistently, regardless of part size or weight.

During the forming process, the machine controls the bending process, delivering consistent part accuracy, no matter how large or heavy the part. While a 500-lb. part takes slightly longer to rotate than a 100-lb. part, the difference is measured in seconds, not minutes. And because robotic part handling occurs outside the bending cycle, it does not negatively impact part production time, unlike other automated bending technologies where handling is part of the cycle. This helps to ensure that a part’s size or weight never slows down production speed.

Unfolding Possibilities

It is said that an operator can be put in charge of bending good parts but has little control over it. This is particularly true when bending large, heavy profiles.

Poor-quality parts lead to rapidly escalating costs and plummeting productivity, forcing metal fabricators to seek better solutions. In these instances, lean manufacturing principles suggest simplifying processes and eliminating wasteful activities to optimize production activities. Folding machines can help companies achieve those continuous improvement goals in the bending department. And as part size increases, folding machines prove their worth even more.

Fabricators face unprecedented challenges in today’s demanding landscape. Success depends on efficiency, precision, and innovation. Advanced folding technology offers advantages over other methods of bending large profiles. Those able to leverage this technology can gain a decisive advantage over competitors.
