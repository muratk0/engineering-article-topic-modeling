# New coverage in powder coating

[TARİH: 01.05.2014 The Fabricator]

TECHNOLOGY SPOTLIGHT

A different take on powder coating results in a much smaller footprint than a typical line

By Dan Davis, Editor-in-Chief

Larry Savage has been involved in designing and selling powder coating lines for 34 years. It wasn’t until three years ago, however, that a customer really forced him to think of a better way to powder-coat large fabrications.

The customer came to Savage with a request to design a system to finish large frames for large computer servers. The company could fabricate the racks, but it didn’t have enough room in its facility to accommodate a traditional powder coating line, which can easily take up 9,000 square feet with its pretreatment system, dryoff oven, powder coating booth, and curing oven. Savage was tasked with thinking of something that could work in the small footprint provided and for the large fabrications, which typically would require large booths and ovens.

Well, the project fell through, but Savage kept thinking about the design. Surely, other fabricators were faced with the same powder coating dilemma.

“The more I thought about it, the more sense it made to me for a niche market,” he said.

That’s where the idea came up for the Torrent powder coating system.

Small System, Large Parts

This new powder coating line requires only about half the space of a traditional system with a five-station pretreatment line and a monorail material handling system. Yet, occupying just 5,000 sq. ft., the Torrent System (see Figure 1) has two hexagon-shaped booths—one for pretreatment and one for powder coating—that can accommodate large parts or several smaller parts.

Figure 1 This new powder coating system design accommodates large parts in a much smaller footprint than a traditional powder coating line. Torrent Systems’ largest powder coating line configuration can handle parts that fit into a 7-foot-tall by 9-foot 6-inch-diameter cylinder.

“The benefit is that it’s a system for someone doing a machine frame or large fabrication, like blower housings,” Savage said. “To this point, they’ve only been able to put in a very large and expensive powder system with poor utilization, or they’ve had to batch-coat them and deal with poor pretreatment and powder quality.”

To understand how the system works, let’s follow a part through it.

The first stop is the pretreatment center (see Figure 2). Conveyors move and transfer parts into the center of the washer enclosure, which is made of stainless steel walls and components. The parts are hung from a 6-in. shaft-and-wheel carousel assembly. When the doors close, the drive system starts to rotate at 1.5 RPM.

Figure 2 The pretreatment system can accommodate an oversized part or dozens of smaller parts. The nozzles spread all around the booth’s walls to provide complete coverage as the parts rotate. The application time for each pretreatment stage can be tailored to the product design. In more traditional monorail systems, parts are hit from just two sides and the application and dwell times are fixed.

Five pumps are attached to the washer. Each individual pump handles a separate pretreatment task: alkaline spray, city water rinse, conversion coating spray, and two water rinses. The system has variable programming options for application times of each pretreatment stage, up to 10 minutes long.

One of the unique parts of the washer design is that each pump is attached to its own set of spray nozzles and risers, the vertical pipe to which the nozzles are attached. Also, during draining, each pretreatment stage is fed into its own tank. An indexing floor drain helps to keep the fluids separate and aids in recycling of the fluids by preventing cross-contamination.

Once the first stage begins, the nozzles that are connected to the five risers around the perimeter of the booth hit the parts from all angles. Unlike in a typical pretreatment application where the parts are hit from 180 degrees, in this line the parts are hit from 290 degrees as they slowly rotate.

“That’s how we got the word torrent—because inside that enclosure, it’s really busy with spray,” Savage said.

He added that because the part is being hit all over, the cleaning is likely to be much more thorough than in a typical monorail system.

After 90 seconds or so, the pump shuts down, indicating the end of the first pretreatment stage. The parts hanging from the carousel continue to rotate, as the alkaline solution drains back into the tank being serviced by the firststage pump. After about 30 seconds, a stainless steel distribution pan rotates and hits a proximity switch that signals for the stage-two pump to turn on. Again, this pump feeds its own set of risers and spray nozzles. This same process repeats itself until the fifth-stage rinse is complete.

“We have a sealing system at the bottom of that tank, which is an integral part of the system—preventing solution from getting into the wrong tank. There’s no cross-contamination,” Savage said. “Monorail systems have to overflow their rinse stages because they become contaminated with the alkaline cleaner or the iron phosphate.”

Once the pretreatment has been fully applied, the carousel with the parts are pulled out manually or automatically and sent into a dryoff oven, where they stay for about 10 minutes. From there they enter a cool-down tunnel for another short stretch. The goal is for the parts to exit the cooling process at no more than 120 degrees because parts with a higher surface temperature tend to attract and build up powder film.

Powder Application

The parts then move on to the powder coating booth, which is similar in size to the pretreatment booth and also features stainless steel construction. Inside there, the heavy-duty hanger system rotates at 1.5 RPM, and two manual operators or an automatic setup of powder coating guns or both can finish the parts. Like traditional powder booths, color changes can be accommodated with simple changeout systems on the market, or the operators can shoot to waste. Dedicated booths for specific colors also can be added to the system to enable “dust on dust” primer/topcoat processing, Savage said.

“The important thing in the powder booth is that in a monorail system using automatic guns, the components are in front of those guns for maybe 60 to 90 seconds because that monorail is carrying those parts through the target zone. The chain is always moving. The powder cloud, particularly where the powder is dense, is only about 1 to 2 feet wide along the direction of travel,” Savage said.

“Because our process is timed at 10-minute cycles, assuming one minute to index parts into the powder booth and one minute for the parts to come out, you’re left with eight minutes during which a manual operator can get at those part areas that are difficult to reach,” he added.

Once coated, the parts head to a curing oven.

Savage estimated that two to three people can finish more than two truckloads of parts per shift with this type of powder coating setup.

In the end, even though a metal fabricator might find the cost of running a compact powder coating system favorable, Savage said he believes his new powder coating system will gain favor in the market because it can coat those parts that fabricators find difficult—if not impossible—to powder-coat themselves.

“This is not suitable for doing plain parts. A sheet metal part is a flat part that’s easy to coat,” he said. “This is for complex, heavy shapes.”
