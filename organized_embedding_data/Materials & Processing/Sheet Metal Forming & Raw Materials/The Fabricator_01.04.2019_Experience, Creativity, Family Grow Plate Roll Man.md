Waldemar Design & Machine and Athens Machine Builders build for standard and special applications

By Sue Roberts

A

husband and wife team tucked their children into bed then began their work on engineering drawings for plate rolling equipment. At daylight that equipment was built in their Liberty, Ky., shop that had been erected using scrap material. That was in the 1980s.

Today heavy-duty equipment in three product categories is produced by the Mennonite company that grew from those late-night sessions. Angle iron and section rolls, small plate rolls typically installed in job shops, and large plate rolls used by manufacturers of products like propane and beverage tanks are manufactured in a 15,000-sq.-ft. facility in Spencer, Tenn., a small Cumberland Plateau community known for the beauty of its state park.

Ammon Weaver Jr. and his wife, Eva, began Waldemar Design & Machine LLC (WDM) in the small Kentucky shop. Affordable land drew them to Tennessee to grow the business. As the family and company expanded, Weaver identified new areas to apply his engineering skills. Now the majority of his time is directed at designing custom plate rolling equipment for another Weaver company, Athens Machine Builders. Three of the seven sons, Henry, Neal, and Joel, work with him on the specials.

The custom equipment is manufactured by Wildcat Gear LLC, a Weaver company established as part of WDM to produce gears for the original line of roll bending machines, short-line agriculture equipment, and horse-powered treadmills used by the family businesses. The treadmills are manufactured and marketed by Athens Enterprise, another family company.

Sidney, another son, handles the manufacturing of WDM’s standard product lines.

Starting Early

A self-proclaimed tinkerer, Ammon Weaver honed his metalworking skills as a young man while building equipment in the 24- by 28-ft. workshop on the family’s Pennsylvania farm. "I started out building farm machines and wagons for our own use. I built a large concrete mixer for the farm using a lot of miscellaneous parts. Then I started building front end loaders for tractors." His principal machine tool was a 180-amp stick welder.

At 22 he approached a local farm equipment dealership about selling his front end loaders. Although he did not get the response he expected, it caused him to consider other possibilities.

"The owner said something that really stuck with me. He said that he expected that I would end up building something entirely different. He saw a mismatch between the product I had chosen and the skills I was developing. So, I started building sawmill machinery and my company was very successful until the interest rates rose, causing it to crash."

The impact of losing that company and the challenges of the early 1970’s OPEC oil embargo made Weaver question establishing another company of his own and sent him to work in a distant cousin’s shop, where he built a variety of products, including RV frames, woodstoves, and solid-waste containers. The company also fabricated large ductwork for industrial processes. A plate bending roll was needed, so they built one for in-house use. On the side, however, Weaver continued designing and building plate rolls for other shops.

"My wife and I decided to start WDM after building eight or 10 plate roll machines. We started with a low budget and a goal of selling five machines in the first year. We sold 22.

"What attracted me to roll building is that it requires many different disciplines. There’s the structural discipline that deals with the deflection of the roll and so forth, the bearings, the drive system, the frame, the hydraulic and electric systems. Putting this all together so it works is the fun part."

The sawtooth design of the Spencer, Tenn., facility brings daylight into both stories of the office and engineering areas and the manufacturing plant. Low-emissivity glazing and natural ventilation help keep the plant cool during summer months. Tubular steel columns throughout double as air receivers.

A 1 ½-in. by 12-ft., four-roll plate bender with side and overhead supports is loaded and ready for shipment. The machine weighed in at 52 tons.

A 1-ft. rolling machine is used to form expendable helicopter parts.

Both the control and the base of this special machine were designed to interact with a grain bin manufacturing system. The fourroll machine forms both 2.667- and 4.0-in.-pitch staves from corrugated sheets of material.

Large-diameter staves for stainless steel storage tanks are rolled on an Athens Machine Builders’ ½-in. by 6-ft. special machine.

Keeping with the Mennonite tradition, the couple’s 13 children were taught to assist with the family business as their abilities allowed. As adults, some chose other paths for their careers, but several children and grandchildren continue to contribute to the success of the family companies.

"We try to keep the businesses family-centered and provide a strong family setting," said Weaver. "This allows the family to stay together and cooperate within our society." Family members comprise the majority of the 20 people who are key to production.

Balancing Capabilities With Economics

The Weaver family companies have the capabilities and processes needed to build their products from scratch, but components and services are outsourced when it makes sense. Nearby Mennonite-owned companies are used whenever possible.

"Anything we can economically buy outside, we do. For instance, components that need to be flame-cut. Yes, we can produce our own, and we do flame-cut some, but the decision comes down to cost. There are so many people competing to provide that service that they keep the price down.

"In most cases, our runs are very short so we have our own conventional equipment for machining large parts. We also have local, family-run shops within a 200-mile radius providing components that come together for assembly and QC in our main shop," Weaver said.

Having full capabilities in-house gives the companies flexibility to react to the market. "The nature of the business is to go in spurts. Sometimes we are selling lots of basic machines, and sometimes we are selling automated cells with material handling that will pick up a piece, roll it, and then eject it onto a conveyor.

"Having all the manufacturing processes at our main plant gives us capacity when we need it and saves us money when we don’t. It helps balance out any mismatch of our workmen with the jobs going through."

Designing Specials

Customers for both standard and special equipment come from a large variety of industries, including aerospace, agriculture, structural and decorative architecture, shipbuilding, utilities, and medical. Creating Athens Machine Builders as a separate entity was a response to the growing number of requests for special equipment—some smaller and some larger than the established models.

"In the specials category we were asked to build a machine that would bend the titanium pins used in hip replacement hardware. We built a machine that was smaller than our standard models and that had different characteristics."

On the large side of specials, the company re-engineered a 28-ft., four-roll, double-pinch plate bending machine to accommodate the low ceilings of a tank truck manufacturing facility.

Weaver said, "The space between the floor and the crane hook disappears really fast when you make big rolls and the ceilings were relatively low for manufacturing such a big product. The company was also spending a lot of time with material handling because of the height restrictions. And they were working with polished stainless that couldn’t be scratched.

"We took drastic design measures to keep the machine passline height down to 397/8 in. Then we developed an offloading process that babies the finished rolls. A completed part is supported overhead while a row of wheels comes up for offloading. Pushers move the part off the wheels without dragging it across the roll and causing cosmetic issues. A ball transfer lets the wheels roll both ways, and an overhead support handles the heavy load."

Cone-shaped cantilevered rolls on this special machine create skid-free cones for the bottoms of beverage tanks.

"I BELIEVE THAT LITTLE BIT OF INSTRUMENTATION IMPROVES PRODUCTION DRASTICALLY. BEYOND THAT POINT THERE IS VERY LITTLE IMPROVEMENT. A GOOD ROLL OPERATOR IS AN ARTISTIC PERSON AND, LIKE A BLACKSMITH, HE KNOWS HOW METAL BEHAVES AND CAN WORK WITH THE MACHINE FOR THE BEST RESULTS."

—AMMON WEAVER, JR.

Another special-order machine that incorporates cone-shaped cantilevered rolls was delivered to a beverage tank company. The cone shape allows for rolling a variety of steep- or flat-diameter parts.

There are two customer requirements involved when the company agrees to develop a machine with unique specifications. One is a down payment. The other is an agreement that the customer will provide test material. A contract, for example, may guarantee that a plate roll will produce a certain number of parts in a specific amount of time. If the resulting machine doesn’t meet the specifications, it goes into rework or the company starts over and builds another machine with a new design. If expectations still aren’t satisfied, the down payment is returned and the only things the customer loses are time and test material. Weaver said, "This practice usually closes the deal."

Finding the Art

"I have observed that there is a trend in the industry to computerize everything. There are definitely places where this works well, but in others, like plate rolling, there is an art involved that gets left behind with too much automation. I believe that a little bit of instrumentation improves production drastically. Beyond that point there is very little improvement. A good roll operator is an artistic person and, like a blacksmith, he knows how metal behaves and can work with the machine for the best results," Weaver said.

Machines from WDM and Athens Machine Builders use PLCs to achieve production speed. Standard features include dial pointer indicators; digital readouts are available as an option. Paired with a good operator and uniform plates, Weaver said standard and special machines roll workpieces to within ½ of 1 percent of perfect concentricity.

Enjoying the Challenge

The comment from the farm machine distributor still holds true for Weaver. Rather than building the same piece of equipment over and over, he enjoys designing unique machines to improve manufacturing processes.

"When I started building specials, I would be asked if I had built a machine like the one being requested before. I’d say no, but I think I can. It can get boring when a design gets to the point where everything works. The challenge is getting something new and unexpected to work. It’s the smile on a customer’s face when he gets more than expected that motivates me."

Equipment from the Weaver family companies is sold directly and through a select group of distributors. Although some distributors feature the equipment on their websites, the companies themselves do not have their own websites. Members of the Mennonite society prefer personal interactions for doing business and forgo electronic communications.
