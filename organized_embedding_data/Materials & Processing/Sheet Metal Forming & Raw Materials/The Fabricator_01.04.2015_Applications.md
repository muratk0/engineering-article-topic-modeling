Metal Panels Curved On-Site for Fire Station Installation

SITUATION

The Tyler Fire Station 5 in Tyler, Texas, is a 8,865-sq.-ft. building designed as part of a plan to provide better spacing and coverage for a large portion of the west part of the city and reduce response times.

The roof of the $1.7 million facility was in need of replacement. The building is in a high-visibility location, so appearance was an important consideration. The city considered installing asphalt shingles and single-ply roofing, but decided on metal for reasons of aesthetics and durability.

RESOLUTION

McElroy Metal manufactured and delivered more than 14,000 sq. ft. of its Maxima ADV 2-in.-tall standing seam panels in PVDF medium bronze—chosen because of its similarities to the traditional brass that adorned historic fire engines. The striated 16-in.-wide panels were manufactured from 24-ga. Galvalume

®

.

The panels were delivered strapped in crates. They were unpacked and separated so each panel could be curved at the job site by a McElroy Metal roll forming technician. All seven sections of barrel roofing were the same radius.

A crew of eight from Hefner Roofing of Pittsburg, Texas, installed the roofing panels in less than four weeks. "Working with curved panels is a little more labor-intensive all the way around," said Matt Jones, project manager at Hefner Roofing. "You have to handle every panel individually a couple times before you get it installed. It’s not difficult, it just takes a little longer."

Jones said a special hoist, built in the shape of the curved panels, was constructed to get the panels up to the roof. Hoisting curved panels a few at a time takes more time than hoisting a crate of panels, and the uncrated panels are more likely to be affected by winds, so extra care was taken during that process. Once the panels were safely on the roof, they were attached to the structure in a similar manner to a noncurved roof, using the same clips and seamer.

Dock, safety equipment-maker improves productivity with orbital stretch wrapper

SITUATION

Bluff Mfg., Fort Worth, Texas, is a fabricator and designer of dock, warehouse, and safety equipment. The company adheres to the ANSI MH30.2 standard, a rigorous testing process that ensures the highest-quality products.

While safety and quality are a top concern, the firm takes extra care to get its products to customers intact and on time. The company loads and wraps 15 to 20 pallets of safety products each day.

Those pallets previously were secured with hand wrap and steel banding, a process that required three to four employees each time. Employees were continuously lifting 30- to 50-lb. rolls of film and bending in awkward positions, leading to back strain and pulled muscles.

Using straps or metal bands to secure loads also came with risks. Not only was the process very tedious, but bands could destroy the pallets and the loads. With too much tension on the strap, the bands could pop back when cut and injure employees.

Aside from the labor savings, Bluff Mfg. was able to cut film usage by about 25 percent per year and reduced its steel banding width from 1.25 in. to 0.75in.

RESOLUTION

In a chance meeting at a tradeshow, Bluff Mfg. was introduced to the Yellow Jacket 87M orbital stretch wrapper from Muller. "After seeing the Yellow Jacket orbital stretch wrapper, we knew we could do better at wrapping our skids," said Chris Pinyan, plant manager.

The horizontally positioned stretch wrap dispenser moves around and under a load as it is manually advanced across the length of the load. Loads also remain on the forklift while being wrapped. Loads that previously required two workers about 10 minutes to wrap by hand now can be wrapped with one operator in one minute.

"The labor savings created by incorporating the Yellow Jacket allows us to use our human capital in a better and more efficient way than simply wrapping the product," said Pinyan. "The machine does the work of three to four employees in less time, with greater load containment and improved safety."

The system has eliminated the need for most steel banding as well. "The fact that the Yellow Jacket could eliminate some risk to our employees was a main selling point for us," said Pinyan. To ensure employee safety, the company does continue to use some banding to ensure loads don’t topple over during transport within the warehouse.

Aside from the labor savings, Bluff Mfg. was able to cut film usage by about 25 percent per year and reduced its steel banding width from 1.25 in. to 0.75 in. The company also has reduced its freight claims by 12 percent.

"The Yellow Jacket creates a consistent, tight, and stable load every time," said Pinyan. "We don’t have to worry about product shifting or damaging during transport."
