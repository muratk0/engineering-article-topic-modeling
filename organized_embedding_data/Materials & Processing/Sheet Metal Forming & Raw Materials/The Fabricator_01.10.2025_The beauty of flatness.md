ouis Industries, Paynesville, Minn., has worked hard the last three years to achieve balance in its operations.

"We’ve been chasing capacity," said Lance Louis, CEO of the company his grandfather founded in 1940 as Louis Blacksmith Shop.

The imbalance came from a bottleneck in blanking. The company’s laser cutting machines were overwhelmed with work, and some of that hole-intensive work was a better fit for the already-busy punching machines. Louis Industries needed technology upgrades in both areas.

Because of the bottleneck, downstream departments got behind schedule. Almost everything became a rush job as people in the forming and welding departments tried to make up for time because of the delays in blanking.

Capital Equipment Upgrade Plan

This past spring, Louis Industries wrapped up an aggressive three-year capital equipment investment plan. The new additions included two 15-kW fiber laser cutting machines, two laser/punch combination machines, and another robotic press brake cell.

"Once all the equipment came online, our plant came into complete balance with capacity," Louis said. "The staff is happy because they’re not chasing anything."

The focus is back on metal fabrication. With the new machine tools and newfound ability to concentrate on improving processes, instead of just scrambling to get parts out the door, Louis Industries had time to take a closer look at its blanking operations. Two high-powered lasers offered up incredible performance, but they weren’t necessarily the right equipment choice for some of the parts being sent to the shop floor.

"We were actually cutting a bunch of stuff that we should have had on those laser/punch machines," Louis said.

Laser cutting technology continues to redefine how metal fabricating is done. Louis Industries experienced that back in the 1990s, as it shifted from being a fabricator of agricultural products to more of a traditional job shop. It purchased its first CO

laser cutting machine in 1995 just as the industry was in the midst of fully embracing toolless blanking.

But over the years, Louis said he and others on his team have come to realize that while laser cutting might make sense for 80% of the parts produced in a job shop, that other 20% is probably more suited for punching machines. Of course, modern punching machines and tooling can provide various forms that save parts from having to undergo secondary processing, but that’s not all that turret punches can offer. Simply put, they are holemaking marvels.

"I’m not ripping any of the laser manufacturers, but the reality is that the motion of the cutting head, the actual cutting, and possible tip-ups are all factors when you are cutting 1,000 holes or even 100 holes in a piece. There are drawbacks," Louis said. "If you want to run your laser 100% effectively, you need to take the bad jobs off the laser."

Punching Isn’t Easy

The arrival of the last laser/punch combination machine really made the reassignment of hole-intensive work easy for Louis Industries schedulers. They finally had the capacity that they had craved for years.

The company also is fortunate in that it has a team that understands the nature of turret punching. Thanks to advancements in laser cutting and press brake technology, most shops don’t want to be bothered with becoming experts in punching.

"Programing is tougher. Training operators is tougher. Maintenance on them is more involved than [it is] for lasers," Louis said. "People want to get rid of those machines."

Committed to turret punch technology, Louis Industries sought out more work for its new machines. This led the company to take on architectural panel work.

The laser/punch combination machines could handle the extensive holemaking in these parts, and Louis Industries could handle large enough parts that it could alleviate some welding on behalf of customers that once had to weld smaller perforated panel sections together to create larger sections. But as the company took on more of this work, it discovered a recurring issue: keeping the parts flat after processing.

"Punching sheet metal sometimes leads it to looking like a potato chip," Louis said.

For instance, some of the 10-ga. perforated sheet metal parts after turret punching might have had as much as 0.375- to 0.5-in. curl. Even parts that weren’t as severely malformed were an issue because the customer expected flat parts for its downstream processes. Out-of-tolerance perforated parts slowed down their own production.

Louis said that his team began to look for fabricating technology that could help solve this issue and found a couple of companies that offered machine tools that could help. Luckily, FABTECH 2023 was in Chicago, a relatively short trip from Minnesota.

"We decided to literally bring a suitcase full of parts to FABTECH that year," Louis said.

What Leveling Offered

Louis Industries met with ARKU at the show and ran the parts on a FlatMaster 55 leveling machine. About two hours later, the machine had processed the various parts and delivered consistent flatness that had escaped the Louis Industries team.

The ARKU machines stress-relieved the metal parts, which likely were prone to not being level because of prior processing. Most sheet metal leaves the mill as part of a coil. As curvature is introduced to the material, it begins to change. For instance, in narrow strips of sheet metal, a fabricator might see crossbow, or in broad strips, wavy edges or central buckles. Once the sheet is in a coil, it’s never as flat as it needs to be for precise processing.

But that’s not all that affects the sheet metal. Thermal processes, such as laser cutting, can create further stresses. Even mechanical processes like shearing or punching can do the same.

The ARKU FlatMaster 55 machine relies on roller leveling to remove the stresses from the metal and return it to a flat state. The process is similar to roll forming in theory, where the material, likely from a coil, passes through multiple sets of rolls, each designed to perform a specific bend until the final shape is achieved. With a leveling machine, a blank is subjected to bending and counter bending by staggered level rolls, alternately moving upward and downward.

Louis Industries installed a deburring and edge-rounding machine to improve the quality of its laser-cut parts. The part on the left has been processed by the Edgebreaker 6000.

Brandon Wester, a machine operator for Louis Industries, checks on a part that was sent through the FlatMaster 55 leveling machine.

That alternating bending introduces counter forms that help the material work its way back to a flat state. As the material is fed into the machine, a curvature is generated that is greater than the strongest curvature in the nonlevel blank. This begins the process of eliminating the stresses in the material. By the time the material is close to exiting the roller levelers, the alternating bending is minimized, and the uneven sections have sprung back to a flat state.

Following the Leveled Path

After FABTECH, Louis said that his team started to look for people in the upper Midwest that had a FlatMaster and could offer the leveling services. In the end, they found companies with the leveling capabilities, but they weren’t interested in selling service time on their machines.

That left Louis Industries back where it started, but it also provided the company with another perspective that helped to make the case for buying a leveling machine. With no one offering this service to other metal fabricators and OEMs, Louis Industries could fill a manufacturing niche in the surrounding market.

"We could attack a market where there is demand, and we can do it with a very high confidence," Louis said.

In early 2024, Louis Industries installed its Flat-Master. The machine not only helped to answer internal production issues related to the nonlevel parts, but it also opened doors to new business opportunities, just as company leaders anticipated.

"We kind of think of ourselves more as consultants than just a fabricating company," Louis said. "We go in and want to know what issues people are dealing with.

"Now, if one of the issues is flatness, where they can’t punch parts or they want to stop with secondary operations, we can help them because we put the FlatMaster in."

He added that he views the equipment as a sort of insurance policy for Louis Industries’ own fabricating activities as well. Raw material stock can be run through before the sheet is put on the laser cutting machine, and machine operators know that the material is less likely to distort when the laser cutting head is engaged. Laser-cut blanks sent through the leveling machine likely won’t be as troublesome on the press brake, and the brake technician can more consistently and efficiently produce parts that meet customer specifications. It also helps automation investments, like the shop’s robotic press brakes, maintain uptime because the blanks are not out of tolerance.

"To solve problems that other fabricators can’t solve, there’s value for that—for us and for our customers," Louis said.

Surface Changes

Realizing that the leveling machine was helping to boost its reputation for quality parts with its customers, Louis Industries felt it made sense to add to its insurance policy. That’s when it decided to add an ARKU EdgeBreaker 6000 surface finishing machine.

The metal fabricator had motivators for adding the equipment at the end of 2024. First, it would be helpful for slag cleanup on some of its laser-cut parts. The shop enjoys the power and speed in which its 15-kW laser cutting machines process parts, but the cutting dynamics sometimes result in slag on the bottom of the parts. Second, it was going to help break down very sharp edges, something that is an obstacle to proper powder coating adhesion. Again, quality is a major concern for customers.

The EdgeBreaker deslags and rounds sharp edges through its three-stage process. When parts, which can be as thick as 1 in., enter the finishing chamber, they initially encounter a wide belt that handles deburring. As parts progress on the conveyor belt, they are exposed to rotary brushes with flexible abrasive media, which round all of the exposed edges. (ARKU officials report that the equipment can deliver a radius up to 2 mm.) The final stage includes a fleece belt that cleans up the surface.

Louis said the EdgeBreaker further bolsters the company’s reputation with its customers.

"We have to perform at a higher level because we can’t have them questioning our quality," he said.

The deburring and edge-rounding machine also has helped Louis Industries clean up its grinding efforts. They are no longer dealing with inconsistent results, which always seems to be the case when people are manually trying to deslag laser-cut parts. Simultaneously, the dust associated with manual grinding has been eliminated, leading to a much cleaner finishing area.

The step to improve surface finishing of the laser-cut parts might have been the last official act that allowed Louis Industries to find its manufacturing balance and prepare for the fabricating opportunities that await.
