Worthington Steel to acquire Kloeckner & Co.

Worthington Steel has announced that it has entered into a business combination agreement with Kloeckner & Co., a service center and metal processing company with approximately 110 locations across North America and Europe.

Kloeckner has broad product capabilities including carbon flat-roll steel (sheet and plate), electrical steel, aluminum, stainless steel, and long products. Over the past few years, the company has been transitioning toward high-value-added processing and fabrication via M&A and strategic growth initiatives.

The proposed acquisition advances Worthington Steel’s growth strategy by strengthening its position in the North American metal processing sector. It will create a larger and more diversified metals processing company with an enhanced product offering and broader geographic reach. Once completed, the transaction will position Worthington Steel as the second largest steel service center company in North America by revenue, the company reports.

Bystronic acquires Coherent’s Tools for Materials Processing business unit

Bystronic has announced it has completed the acquisition of Coherent Corp.’s Tools for Materials Processing business unit, gaining access to new markets including medical technology, semiconductors, and general manufacturing.

In addition, Bystronic’s portfolio will be expanded to include new laser applications such as micro material processing, marking, engraving, and drilling.

With this acquisition, the company acquires the rights to the Rofin brand, which will continue as part of the newly created Bystronic Rofin business unit. Employing about 400 people, the business unit is headquartered in Gilching, Germany.

Coe Press Equipment marks 50 years in business

Coe Press Equipment is celebrating its 50th anniversary in 2026. John C. Coe founded the company in 1976 as a garage-based operation focused on rebuilds, used equipment, and accessories. When a customer requested a new coil feed line, Coe hired two employees and designed the company’s first original machine.

In 1980, the firm relocated to its current Sterling Heights, Mich., facility and continued to expand its capabilities through research and development and strategic acquisitions, including key assets from Sesco Inc.

The company’s industry-first advancements include a Kevlar nonstretch timing belt drive (1982), a programmable servo roll feed (1985), and PLCs with automated setup and simulation (2020).

John Deere to add two new U.S. facilities

John Deere has announced plans to open two new U.S.-based facilities: a distribution center near Hebron, Ind., and an excavator factory in Kernersville, N.C., both set to open in the next year.

The distribution center, located to help enhance the company’s supply chain capabilities nationwide, will be designed to streamline operations and help ensure timely delivery of equipment and parts. The project is expected to create 150 jobs. John Deere will continue to maintain its primary North American parts distribution center in Milan, Ill.

Machitech announces strategic laser partnership with Lantek

Quebec-based Machitech has announced a strategic partnership with Lantek Sheetmetal Solutions, Mason, Ohio, offering Lantek Expert Cut nesting software on all Machitech Fiber PRIME S high-power laser cutting machines, and Lantek Flex3D nesting software on all Machitech Fiber TUBE XL tube laser cutting machines.

The software partnership applies to Machitech laser plate and tube cutting machines and press brakes.

Endurance Technologies chooses New Mexico for first U.S. manufacturing plant

Endurance Technologies Ltd., a Calgary, Alberta-based steel pipe manufacturer, has selected an existing 45,000-sq.-ft. building in Las Cruces, N.M., for its first manufacturing operation in the U.S.

The company produces steel pipe designed for environments subject to extreme wear, erosion, and corrosion. It serves critical industries including oil and gas, agriculture, forestry, and mining.

Endurance Technologies is preparing to begin construction improvements to the existing facility and anticipates opening its New Mexico operation before the end of 2026.

Heil Trailer Intl. to expand manufacturing operations in Tennessee

Heil Trailer Intl. has announced plans to expand its manufacturing operations in Athens, Tenn. The project will create 18 jobs and invest $9.8 million following the securement of a 10-year U.S. Department of the Army contract for the company’s Tactical Fuel Distribution System.

The project comprises the construction of new warehousing space, automated paint and welding systems, a new testing area, and an expanded welding and training school.

The company designs and builds tank and specialty trailers.

Association of Metals Processors and Distributors names Barry Schneider, Steel Dynamics Inc., its Steel Executive of the Year

Barry Schneider has spent a lifetime in the steel industry—from working maintenance at LTV in Cleveland to helping to start and grow Steel Dynamics.

In his role as president/COO of Steel Dynamics, he’s at the helm of an organization that is helping to supply the resurging domestic manufacturing industry. That’s a great vantage point considering how far the company has come since it opened a flat-roll mill in Butler, Ind., in 1996. Today, Steel Dynamics is one of the largest producers of carbon steel products in the U.S.

Recognizing his contributions both to Steel Dynamics’ success and to the modernization of steel production in the U.S., the Association of Metals Processors and Distributors, an affiliate of the Fabricators and Manufacturers Association, has awarded its 2025 Steel Executive of the Year honor to Schneider.

The Fabricator had a chance to speak with this steel industry leader earlier this year. Even after 36 years in the industry, he still enjoys the process of finding new ways to make steel products more efficiently.

Can you share the story of how you first came to work for Steel Dynamics Inc.?

I came to SDI and started working at the end of May in 1995.

I sent a note to SDI when they located the mill [Butler, Ind.] in October 1994. I was working at LTV Steel at the time. There were great people that I worked with. They were super helpful to me and very gracious with their teaching me things.

I had started up a big caster at LTV, and I sent a note to Keith Buss [at SDI] that said I’d love to be considered and that I’m an engineer with startup experience.

Barry Schneider, president/COO, Steel Dynamics

I got a call in January, did an interview in February, and didn’t hear back for a little bit. Then they called me in April. I started at the end of May.

It was literally a farm field when I got there. There was like an old farmer house on the one side and the beginnings of rail. There was a hole in the ground where water treatment was going to go, a bunch of trailers, and a lot of mud. I was originally the mechanical engineer in melting and casting.

My wife and I had our son, our first kid, in October of 1994. So he was six months old when I took the job. I had a real sense that I have to take care of my family. LTV wasn’t going to do that, so I decided to follow the technology.

You started working for SDI when it was literally a startup. Now that the company has grown to become an industry leader in steel production, what do you recall about those early days?

We were a new company startup. So it’s not just a mill startup. We had no money as a company. I was buying tools for the guys with my Sears credit card, and I was broke as a joke. At one point, I had like $12,000 on my Sears card.

We also knew the private equity behind us had expectations that we were going to get that thing running. They brought together a team of a lot of new core talent but also big steel. Maintenance people and metallurgists were big steel. Having some of that big steel experience for quality and applications was good.

It also was a good time because mini-mill technology was just being born. That’s why I thought I have to follow this.

Crawfordsville was a disruption technology. [In 1989, Nucor opened the first mini-mill in the world to produce flat-rolled steel using thinslab technology in Crawfordsville, Ind.] Nobody knew. And that’s what the founders of SDI did. They were part of that Nucor team that did it.

Did you have experience with the metals industry before going to work for LTV?

I graduated from Rose-Hulman and I went right to LTV. It was funny because the guy that hired me was a Rose grad.

He came back recruiting, and I was the only guy that went to his pizza party. I came in after football practice. He had like 20 pizzas sitting there. He thought there’d be a lot of people, but I told him that everybody wants to interview with Eli Lilly, Caterpillar, and all of these blue chip companies. He asked, " Why are you here? I said, "Well, my brother-in-law works in steel in Cleveland with LTV. He’s a chemical engineer, and he’s three years older than me." So I had a chance to go through the mill on like a family day, and I thought this was going to be great. My wife had another year of school, so I said that I’ll move back to Cleveland.

I’m not a millwright or anything, but I understood that craft. I definitely learned a lot about listening. The youngest guy on my crew was 58.

What has made you stay with SDI all of these years?

Every job I’ve taken at SDI was typically with either [company co-founders] Mark Millett, Dick Teets, or even Keith Busse at one point just saying, "Hey, this is the next role we got for you. What do you think?"

I was like, "Sure, let’s do it." The camaraderie that we have with our culture. You figure out how to get things done.

So originally I’m a maintenance person, and we don’t have maintenance departments. We have operating departments where the supervisors have maintenance people and quality people working for them. I had to learn pretty quick about how do I support what they need, how do I train the folks they have, and how do I prioritize what they should be doing. So I was very intimate with how the plant ran, which again was super interesting for me.

You almost have to be at a mini-mill to understand because it’s small. We’re very lean, particularly then. So I got involved in a lot of things, but we also were a growing company.

You’ve had a lot of positions within SDI during your career. What was it like to take a position with the knowledge that this might not be what you wanted to do down the road?

I took it really not knowing. I think that too often young people will think, "I’ll do it for six months." That doesn’t teach you a lot. Again, I spent five years doing it at LTV, and I was more of a maintenance and startup engineer kind of person. But I was exposed to casting and hot rolling, and the experience was great.

In a little over seven years I spent on shift, you learn a lot about leadership, especially when it’s the middle of the night on a Saturday. There’s nobody coming out to help you. How do you maintain expectations? How do you keep the safety focus? How do you understand what your people need?

When the jobs came up, I was at a fork in the road and I took the hard fork.

What’s been the greatest challenge during your career at SDI?

My greatest challenge is when I went down to our engineered bar mill [in Pittsboro, Ind.] in 2007. I went down there as a general manager. In our culture, the general manager has top line and the bottom line responsibility. And fortunately it was a small mill. There were only 450 people when I went down there. It was still starting up. We had bought that out of bankruptcy, and we retooled it a little bit. It had been running right at three years when I went down there.

What would you say to a young person considering entering the steel industry?

Well, steel is a fundamental part of our economy. In almost every case, it’s the best building material. The fact that we predominantly do it from recycled content is important because it’s important to young people these days.

When you bring young people to SDI, Nucor, or any mini-mill, we’re not just new; our technology’s different. We don’t have coke plants. We don’t have massive blast furnaces.

If you visit our Mississippi plant, that’s a steel mill. We bought that from Severstal 10 years ago. Since then, we’ve put a lot of money into the plant.
