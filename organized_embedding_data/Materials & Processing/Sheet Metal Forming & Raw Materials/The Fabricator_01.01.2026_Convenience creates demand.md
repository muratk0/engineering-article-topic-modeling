# Convenience creates demand

[TARİH: 01.01.2026 The Fabricator]

Tech Talk

Why democratized manufacturing lifts the whole market

By

Caleb Chamberlain

Y

ears ago, I wrote that "sheet metal has an image problem." Back then, engineers avoided designing for it because it was hard to buy and often hard to design. I called it the "red-headed stepchild" of manufacturing—an ironic label, considering just how much is made out of cut and formed sheet metal.

Since then, sheet metal’s image has improved, driven in large part by democratized, fast, economical access to the technology. Today, multiple companies can provide instant prices, detailed manufacturability checks, and rapid turnaround times for sheet metal parts of all kinds. This kind of online-first access to manufacturing is a new phenomenon. It’s been possible for less than 20 years, and it’s only recently taken off for sheet metal.

Far from stealing demand from existing fabricators, I believe that online-first, high-mix shops are creating net new demand. Students, hobbyists, and engineers are suddenly able to buy sheet metal in any quantity, for cheap. And not only is it easy, it’s fast.

Once people learn what sheet metal can do, they start singing a different tune: "We make everything we can out of sheet metal, it’s just so fast, easy, and economical." That’s a boon for the high-mix online shops, but it also lifts the entire fab industry. More people are designing for sheet metal than ever before, and that means production-focused shops will also see a net increase in demand as jobs transition from low to high volume.

This makes me think of a common marketing saying: Convenience creates demand. If it takes a week to quote a one-off sheet metal prototype and a month to have it on my desk, I’m going to avoid making things out of sheet metal. But if I can get an instant price and have a part on my desk tomorrow, sheet metal will be my go-to material every time. Multiply that out across thousands of people, and suddenly you have an upswell of sheet metal demand across the board. A rising tide floats all boats.

It’s an exciting time to be in manufacturing, because this trend isn’t limited to just simple sheet metal parts. CNC machining represents an even larger market, and dozens of online services make it accessible, if generally costly. Injection molding—usually characterized by long lead times and expensive hard tooling—is now becoming faster and more economical. Some shops are now even offering instant prices on weldments and assemblies, with self-serve configuration tools online. And laser tube cutting is now gaining traction as a fast, accessible service just like sheet metal. Now, CNC tube bending is getting the same kind of treatment.

Once users upload a 3D file of a part requiring tube bending, they witness a simulation of the rotary draw process, along with any manufacturability issues.

OSH Cut

My company, OSH Cut, launched tube laser cutting a few years ago, and when we did, we found that our customers desperately wanted tube bending as well. Tube laser cutting pairs well with bending, just like sheet metal cutting and bending. But we’ve found that CNC tube bending is hard to get in small volumes, much like sheet metal was not too long ago.

There’s a good reason for the friction: Bending metal tube is tricky. People might think it’s "just another CNC process," but springback, wall thinning, ovality, elongation, tool wear, and fixturing can make the process like black magic. Repeatability is key, but it’s a lot more difficult than bending sheet metal. We were surprised to learn that aluminum tubes behave differently as they age. What a thing to have to track!

And it’s not just bend accuracy that makes tube bending complicated. Designing for manufacturability is a real challenge. If I reach out to a production tube bending shop to price a one-off job, I’m going to have a hard time. There will be days or weeks of back and forth while we figure out how to design a part that can actually be made. Can it bend without collision? Are there cutouts in formed regions, or that are positioned in a way that causes excess wear on mandrels? Is the material stocked? What centerline radii do they support? What tool stackups are available to create multiple radii in a single part?

The back and forth would be frustrating for me, a customer, because I just want a part in hand, preferably yesterday. And it’ll be even more frustrating for the tube bending shop. The shop won’t have time to teach me how to design around its capabilities and tools unless I’m likely to convert to high-volume production. Triage is the name of the game, and in this reality, customers often will have a poor experience. It’s nobody’s fault; it’s just complicated.

For these reasons, tube bending remains a niche manufacturing technology. It’s commonly used to make certain classes of products, but it doesn’t lend itself to economical, fast prototyping. Since the experience is inconvenient and slow, fewer people will design around it.

Many in our industry hope to change that, of course, and this includes our own shop. After months of working through numerous tube bending challenges, we’ve designed customer-facing software that simulates rotary draw tube bending tailored for specific bend dies, mandrels, and other tooling we have available. And as I mentioned in a recent FMA podcast, we’re even thinking of expanding our machining capabilities so we can maintain a robust tooling library.

Through our platform at

oshcut.com

, customers can now upload 3D models of bent tubes and get instant prices. Our software detects the tube profile, unfolds the bends to create a straight part with cutouts for the tube laser, and high-lights any design for manufacturing (DFM) issues. Then, customers watch an animation of their parts bending on our machines, right in the web browser. If the part can’t be made, customers can immediately see why and iterate.

If users don’t start with a 3D model, they can also design a part in-browser by specifying bends and angles, or tube lengths with connecting bends. They can design, watch it bend directly in the browser, and then download the 3D model so that modifications can be made in CAD if needed.

THE QUANTITY CONUNDRUM

Just like with automated quoting for sheet metal parts, immediate simulation, DFM feedback, and quoting of tube cutting and bending help solve the low-quantity conundrum. When a customer sends in a print for a part requiring tube cutting and bending, estimators need to submit a bid and engineers need to clean the drawing up, perhaps offering DFM feedback that requires time-consuming back-and-forth emails or calls. For this reason, a shop might choose to quote only the low-quantity work that has volume potential. It’s betting a money-losing, disruptive-to-production prototype will pay dividends down the road.

The crux of the issue is the office workload. On a shop floor with modern, flexible equipment, producing one piece doesn’t present major issues, besides disrupting bread-and-butter production work. But in the office, all the effort doesn’t make that one-off job worth pursuing.

GREATER ACCESS, GREATER DEMAND

Today, the industry has what could prove to be a better solution to the quantity conundrum: automate the office with instant quoting and software-driven order processing. It’s working for sheet metal cutting and bending, it’s working for tube cutting, and it’s now just starting to work for tube bending.

Technology like this aims to create clarity and democratize access to CNC tube bending. As more people discover the new convenience, demand will increase for shops everywhere. A niche service will become mainstream.

In this column, I’ve recently highlighted a variety of companies that are leveraging software to create amazing new manufacturing experiences. Everything is getting faster and easier, from custom sheds to wheelchairs to cable assemblies—and, of course, sheet metal and tube. We are watching dozens of industries transform before our eyes. That’s invigorating, but here’s the exciting thing: there are far more products and services that haven’t yet experienced the digital transformation. The list is basically infinite.

For those looking at a way to build an impactful, profitable manufacturing business, this is the key insight of our time: Find something that’s slow and painful— and fix it. It’s still early. Imagine what we can build together!
