ISMR: How has the current economic climate affected metalforming research operations (particularly in the automotive sector) and what is your strategy this year? When do you predict an end to the downturn?

TA: North America was hit very strongly by the global economic downturn. I see a slow recovery in 2010 but it will be a long and hard, and probably characterised by high unemployment. I fear that we will never go back to the “good old days” because essential levels of engineering training and education are not being kept up – the ‘golden days’ are gone.

ISMR: What trends do you see developing in the metal forming industry in the US, Europe and Asia?

TA: In the metal forming industry, Europe is holding its own, mainly thanks to considerable industry-related R&D, conducted at German and Swedish universities. In the US, with few exceptions, there are hardly any metal forming innovations. In Asia, Japanese and Korean companies are continuing to adapt cost effective technologies, despite the rather adverse economic conditions, especially in Japan. It is remarkable how quickly Chinese companies, especially automotive companies, have acquired advanced technology very quickly to compete with Korea and Japan, in some instances. It’s not a matter of cheap labour, as some people still think, but simply of better engineering. Chinese engineers are now helping us design our projects… the world has changed and we have become too complacent in the west.

North America does not produce as much research as Europe – there is no comparison to the output from German and European universities. Germany research, in particular, is instantly adapted and used by industry - this is because their academic institutions have excellent contacts with industry and work with industrial partners at an early stage. Unfortunately, much of the research (I do not include software development in this) in Anglo-Saxon countries (US, UK etc.) and some South East Asian countries (such as Taiwan, Singapore etc.) is done in universities who have little contact with industry and tend to publish their results in peer-reviewed magazines.

I would urge universities and research institutions to adopt the German model and work closely with industry so that the results of their research is instantly applied – this will have an important effect on the economy and keep moving the industry forward.

ISMR: How do you view the future development of hydroforming and its costs?

TA: Hydroforming is still not very cost effective for high volume production, while it is widely used. In automotive applications, it will continue to be applied to selected cases such as engine cradles and exhaust components.

However, hydroforming technology will not have the growth it experienced during the 1990s and early 2000s. I see no significant new investments on the horizon for hydroforming whereas, in hot stamping, there are still investments out there.

Users have realized that, by using alternative techniques, they can achieve the same levels of productivity. Hydroforming has also struggled to cope with high strength steels, which are not easy to form.

ISMR: Which materials offer the most potential for the future, in terms of flexibility, formability etc., and why?

TA: Deep draw quality steels are easiest to form but, to save weight and increase crash safety, we need to use AHSS, hot stamped parts and Al alloys, where cost can be justified (high end vehicles and products).

ISMR: Where do you see future forming challenges for higher strength materials such as high strength steel? How can these be met?

TA: The major challenges in forming AHSS are:

■ to account for variability in materials, from lot to lot (a simple test, such as the bulge test, is needed to characterise each coil or batch)
■ springback (no easy solution here, hopefully research will help)
■ formability (the forming process has to be designed to take into account the low formability of the material - the use of CNC controlled multi-point cushions may help)
■ die wear and galling (this can be handled with the appropriate lubrication, die insert materials and coatings).
