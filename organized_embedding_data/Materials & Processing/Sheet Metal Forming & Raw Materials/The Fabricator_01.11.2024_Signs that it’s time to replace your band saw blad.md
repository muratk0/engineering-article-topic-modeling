# Signs that it’s time to replace your band saw blade

[TARİH: 01.11.2024 The Fabricator]

Sawing

Tips for the longest blade life

By

Charlie Starrett

The interrupted nature of sawing structural materials causes additional challenges that can increase blade wear.

Checking blade tension with a tension gauge can prevent cracking in the back of the blade caused by low blade tension.

I

n cutting operations, increasing blade life is an obvious quest, and it starts by choosing the most appropriate band saw blade for the application at hand. Understanding the material characteristics before starting to cut gives you the best opportunity to achieve the longest band saw blade life and production efficiency. Blade speed, feed pressure, and feed rate must all be in balance for the material being cut.

However, it is counterproductive to continue using a saw blade once blade condition and cut quality begin to deteriorate. Fortunately, these situations offer outward indications to alert you that it’s time to change the blade. One main indicator of blade degradation is excessive noise, such as a loud whining or grinding sound from the band saw. Several visible signs on the saw blade and in the cut quality also can indicate blade deterioration.

Blade Wear Indications

Breaks and Fractures.

Signs of wear can appear as microcracks and fractures on the blade backing material. One reason for these fractures is an excessive backup guide preload. Properly adjusted backup guides that are in good condition are crucial to blade life. If positioned to push the blades out of the guides, the backup guides will wear out quickly and damage the back of the blades. Backup guides should be smooth (without grooves or cracks) and adjusted properly.

Also, low blade tension will bow the blade as it travels between the guide arms, causing cracking in the back of the blade. You can easily check this with a tension gauge. Reduced feed rate or pressure can help prevent wear on the back edge of the blades and other issues.

Improper blade tracking also can cause a blade to wear out prematurely and ultimately break. If the saw blade has a sharp fracture, check that the back of the blade is not riding on the band wheel flange. The blade back should be adjusted as close as possible to the flange without hitting it.

A straight break indicates fatigue and the need for blade replacement. You can prevent this type of break by using proper band tension, a wheel diameter that supports the blade well, and an unworn and undamaged guide. Make sure the blade is not rubbing the wheel flange and that the side guides are not too tight.

An irregular break may be due to an indexing sequence issue. If the machine is not indexing correctly, it can push the material forward while the blade is down or not high enough, breaking the teeth.

Tooth Wear.

Over time, the blade’s teeth will begin to wear, strip, or chip. Once teeth are damaged, their clearances and angles change, and that leads to poor cut quality. If you try to compensate for damaged teeth by decreasing the cut time, you might generate excessive heat, which will cause further damage to the blade and the work.

Establish the blade speed based on the type of material. If you set the speed too low, the teeth can overpenetrate, which will put excessive stress on them and eventually cause them to strip. In general, use slower speeds for harder materials and faster speeds for softer materials.

Feed pressure also needs to be adjusted appropriately to prevent overpenetration and tooth strippage. Refer to cutting recommendations from the saw machine manufacturer, and be sure to break in the saw blade properly to maximize blade life.

Fractures on the back of the teeth indicate that the work is moving in the vise and calls for an inspection and adjustment. Nesting clamps can help hold the material and prevent it from spinning or vibrating.

Wavy, Rough, and Uneven Cuts.

As a saw blade degrades, the quality of the cut deteriorates. For example, you may notice a washboard cut on the surface of the material as clearance and angle change due to teeth damage.

Proper speeds and feed rates are necessary not only for blade life and sawing production, but also for the surface quality of the piece being cut. Running a blade at too slow a speed will result in teeth with loaded chips, allowing the teeth to "walk." Running a blade at too fast a feed rate also will cause the teeth to load, producing a similar outcome.

Low blade tension also can allow the blade to "walk" in the cut. Make sure the blade is supported properly by adjusting and tightening the guide arms when installing a new blade. Set the tension to the machine manufacturer’s recommendations using a blade tension gauge. And be sure you are using the correct tooth pitch. As with incorrect feed rate and speed, an incorrect tooth pitch can cause chip packing in the gullets, which will lead to stripped teeth.

Blade Scoring.

When coolant flow is insufficient, heat and friction increase, allowing the guides to score and damage the blade.

Work Hardening.

If each tooth does not penetrate properly, work hardening can occur and the blade will only rub across the material, causing premature dulling. If this occurs, you might need to replace the blade and increase feed pressure.

Once blade teeth are damaged, their clearances and angles will be different.

Material Nuances

In general, the harder the material, the slower the blade speed and feed rate. Harder material simply will not cut as fast as softer material, no matter its shape or size. Therefore, the same type of band saw blade will wear faster when cutting harder material versus softer.

Also, the signs of wear may differ depending on the material that you cut. For example, if you are cutting softer materials such as aluminum, you may see increased backer wear, including cracks and fractures, before you see tooth wear appear. Conversely, if you are cutting harder materials such as titanium, you may witness tooth wear first before a noticeable decrease in the cut quality.

Unlike solid rounds and billets, the interrupted nature of sawing structural materials causes additional challenges that can increase blade wear. In almost all structural cutting, the blade is engaged in the cut for a shorter time compared to the overall size of the material. This can subject the blade to increased force and vibration as the teeth move in and out of the workpiece material.

Also, when structural materials are bundled, the possibility of the material moving during cutting increases dramatically depending on the material shape. For instance, round material tends to spin, which leads to premature blade dulling and increased potential for teeth stripping. Be sure to adjust vises and nesting clamps to hold the material tightly, because any movement can cause tooth breakage.

Chip welding, which occurs when heat buildup bonds the chip to the blade tooth face, can be caused by a worn or missing chip brush, improper coolant, and excessive feed rate or speed.

Pay Attention to the Variables

Here are some other sawing situations in which it’s best to proceed with caution.

Tooth Jamming in the Cut.

When tooth jamming occurs, do not put a new blade in that cut. There’s a good chance that remnants of the teeth are still in the cut; if you install and feed a new blade back into that same cut, those remnants will likely cause the new blade to strip teeth as well. It’s always best to start a new cut, or if you must cut at the same position, turn the material over and approach the cut from the opposite side.

Chip Welding.

Chips loading up on the saw blade gullets can negatively affect the cut quality. When this happens, look for a worn, misadjusted, or missing chip brush. If a brush is not installed correctly or is inoperative, chips can load up in the gullets, increasing heat and leading to chip welding and stripping. Also check for improper coolant, lack of coolant, and excessive feed rate or speed, as all of these can cause heat buildup and subsequent chip welding.
