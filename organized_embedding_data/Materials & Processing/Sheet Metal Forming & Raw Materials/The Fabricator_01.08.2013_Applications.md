Quick turnaround of surface finishing tools puts helicopter-maker back into production

Situation

Sikorsky Aircraft Corp., Stratford, Conn., designs and builds advanced helicopters for commercial, industrial, and military use. The manufacturer was confronted with a problem in cleaning scale from a small outsourced part that was preventing it from proceeding with production of its helicopters worldwide.

The problem was a carburized bearing surface delivered by a supplier. “We attempted to remove this scale with a carbide reamer and a diamond lap, to no avail,” said Tony Lawrence, tooling services manager for Sikorsky Product Center Core Services.

“The material in question was 9310 [alloy steel] and very hard, around 66 RC,” Lawrence explained, which required a tool of equal toughness to do the job properly.

Resolution

The group had used diamond Flex-Hones from Brush Research Mfg. in a limited set of applications previously, but not in the type of high-volume situation the company now faced. The ball-style tool has a shaft with small, abrasive globules that are permanently mounted to flexible filaments. Mounted in a hand drill motor or machine tool chuck, the flexible hone comes in many sizes with a selection of grits to finish materials from hard, heat-treated alloys to softer, nonferrous metals.

For the helicopter manufacturer, the flexible hone needed to be constructed with diamond crystal grit to finish hard materials from 0.1 to 0.15 µm roughness. Sikorsky also needed the tools in a very small size and large volume, as quickly as possible.

Grant Fowlie, Brush Research customer service manager, said that custom orders such as this one require close coordination with the customer. If the customer knows the honing grit required, the company sends a few standard hones for the customer to test. In other cases, the company requests samples of the material to be honed and performs on-site surface finish analysis.

“Surface finish analysis will give you roughness and a variety of other measurements, including peak height, valley depth, bearing areas, and other specifications that have their own importance in different applications,” said Fowlie.

“We were going to need to produce thousands of these [flexible hones], since we were at a production stop,” Lawrence said. “We contacted Brush Research and explained our dilemma. Two days later we had the first production run of brushes, and we were able to ship our product again to customers all over the world.”

Cost-effective CNC plasma cutting machine moves cutting in-house for metal fabricator

Situation

Industrial Revolution is a custom metal fabrication company located in Brantford, Ont. A key part of its business is supplying custom-fabricated components for wind turbines, which are produced to meet stringent specifications.

As a new, growing business with limited resources, the company did not own a profile cutting machine to cut steel plate into CAD-drawn parts, so it subcontracted plasma cutting services. According to Ted Cowan, company owner, the process was restrictive and expensive, because the delivery schedules from local cutting service companies were not reliable, and the cut steel parts delivered were not always the parts required to support immediate production requirements.

As a result of this problem, the manufacturer had to quickly revise its production schedules to use what had been delivered by the steel cutting supplier. This system was hampering the manufacturer in following a production schedule and affecting on-time delivery of customer orders.

Resolution

In his search for solutions to these problems, Cowan was introduced to the NAM portable CNC plasma cutting system, produced by WestingCUT Industrial Technology and available from Profile Cutting Technologies Ltd. With its cost-effective purchase price, the machine was an affordable option for Industrial Revolution to begin cutting parts in-house.

Since the company already owned a Thermal Dynamics CutMaster hand plasma system, it bought a machine torch for this plasma cutter, making it suitable for use with the new cutting machine. The manufacturer also purchased a cutting table to cut 4- by 8-ft. steel plate and was able to create nested cutting programs to cut full sheets into the parts required.

The new system includes a CNC with menu-driven color graphic display; a motorized torch positioner; and an integrated arc voltage torch height control system, which maintains the correct cutting height to deliver good cut surface quality and prolong the life of plasma torch consumables.

The manufacturer also uses the system to cut holes in structural tubing and—since the machine supports a flame-cutting torch—to cut parts from thick plate steel.

Cowan explained his decision to buy the machine was based on his calculation of cost savings from plasma cutting parts in-house rather than paying for outside cutting services. Eight months after buying the first machine, the company had recovered its investment and purchased a second cutting system.
