Any comparison between steel and aluminium as structural materials requires numerous technical and economic criteria to be taken into account. Amongst these are weight, strength, rigidity, yield strength or workability as well as resource availability, raw material costs, production costs and follow-on costs. For lightweight construction, it is often desirable to select an optimum combination of both materials: solid steel for heavy-duty constructional zones combined with lightweight aluminium for less highly-stressed areas.

Until recently, however, arc joining was not feasible for such joins, despite its many technical and economic merits. Fronius has now eliminated this particular bottleneck.

The move to lightweight construction Lightweight construction brings substantial benefits in automotive manufacturing, but also for designer pieces, on factory roofs or in wind-power installations.

In cars, lightweight construction has an especially crucial role to play. At around 40 per cent, bodywork accounts for the largest single share of a vehicle’s overall weight. If the transport sector’s CO2 emissions are to be reduced, weight savings – and new ideas in autobody construction – are one of the first places to start. 100kg less weight can trim up to 0.3 l/100km off fuel consumption, meaning 700g/100km fewer CO2 emissions. The 130g/km CO2 emission limit prescribed by EC Regulations from 2012 makes this especially relevant.

Given the need for acceptable battery weight and range, body weight is a critically important factor for electric vehicles. Substantial weight reductions continue to be a central goal of modern-day autobody construction.

Materials-joining technology therefore plays a role in lightweight construction, particularly in terms of joining steel to aluminium. Fronius partnered with Austrian steelmaker voestalpine to address this challenge. The aim of their project was to develop a semi-finished product in the guise of a high-formability hybrid sheet made of both steel and aluminium, with these two metals joined using a thermal joining process.

## Issues and boundary conditions

Steel and aluminium were long held to be impossible to join to each other permanently by thermal means. The main reasons are their widely different melting points, of over 1,500 and approx. 660°C respectively and, in particular, the formation of intermetallic phases (IMP). This is caused by the limited mutual solubility of iron and aluminium at room temperature. An IMP is formed in the process of diffusion and is generally characterised by great hardness and extremely low toughness.

To give some examples, Fe2Al5 has a Vickers hardness of around 1050 HV, and FeAl3 of around 900 HV. The greater the amount of heat inputted into the seam, the thicker the IMP – and the poorer the mechanical and engineering properties of the joint. The IMP layer should be as thin as possible and no more than 10 µm. Further challenges result from the widely differing thermal expansion coefficients of around 1.2mm/100°C for steel, and of 2.34mm/100°C for aluminium. A further factor is the abrupt change in the electrochemical potential, of approx. 1.22 V for steel/aluminium and of approx. 0.9 V for zinc/aluminium.

In the light of these physical factors, metallurgists and welding experts have defined the following prerequisites for joining steel to aluminium:

- The process used should generate the lowest possible thermal input
- The surface of the steel sheet must have been coated with a zinc layer
- The thickness of the IMP must be minimised and its properties must be optimally exploited.

The choice of thermal joining process went to the CMT (Cold Metal Transfer) arc-process from Fronius, in existence for over ten years.

## The journey to the innovative join

Three main factors influence the formation of a flawless join: the two work pieces to be joined, the joining process and the filler metal.

As well as the zinc layer, which must be at least 10 µm thick, another factor influencing the quality and resilience of the join is the edge preparation of the steel sheet. The edge-geometries developed by voestalpine for the sheets to be joined are patented. Satisfactory results have been obtained with normal grades of deep-drawing steel. The aluminium sheets are made from materials of the AW5xxx or 6xxx series.

In the same way as with conventional metal inert-gas welding, the surface of the aluminium merely needs to be ‘clean’. Because the IMP between two materials that have been joined behaves in a similar way to ceramic, it is sensitive to perpendicular stresses but less so to tangential ones. Allowances must be made for these properties in the course of constructional design.

The special CMT gas-shielded arc process fulfils the crucial requirements of the joining-process used here: low thermal input and good controllability. This ‘cold’ weld process is protected by several patents filed in Fronius’ name.

When steel is joined to aluminium, the filler metal and the aluminium wet the galvanised steel sheet, and the filler metal fuses with the aluminium. On the steel side, a brazed join is obtained against which the aluminium is then welded, hence the term ‘braze-welded joint’ to describe the resulting join. For the hybrid sheets, it has been found best to use welding systems which braze-weld ‘in sync’ with the CMT process on both sides.

The third main factor is the filler metal. Fronius also has a patent for the special aluminium alloy used here in braze-welding. To obtain an optimum seam, it is important for the filler metal to be correctly positioned during braze-welding.

## The steel-aluminium hybrid sheet

To explore and document the product’s suitability for actual practice, both the partner companies and external institutions performed extensive testing on braze-welded steel-aluminium hybrid sheets.

## FOCUS ON JOINING

The CMT braze-welding process is characterised by a combination of welding on the aluminium side and brazing on the steel side.

Hybrid sheets also fulfil the practical requirements for subsequent re-shapeability.

The ‘cupping tests’ clearly demonstrate that even in the boundary zone, this process still leads to positive results.

Components made from hybrid sheets can systematically absorb, for example, the impact energy occurring in an accident.

The braze-welded seam survives without damage even when the component is crash-tested.

Tensile strength: Two different samples simulate the appropriate material combination for car roofs and window frames. A key result of the tensile test was that final rupture of the sample occurs in the aluminium sheet, with the braze-welded joint surviving the tests intact. Corrosion and SST: The corrosion behaviour of the unprotected steel-aluminium hybrid sheet has been tested with the SST (salt spray test). Even after 300 hours exposure, neither crevice/stress corrosion nor intercrystalline corrosion had occurred. The only slight traces of corrosion were on the surface.

Deformability behaviour: The deformability behaviour of the sheets plays a critical role in autobody construction. The steel-aluminium hybrid sheets are only suitable for reproducible sheet metal working if they can fulfil this requirement satisfactorily. Crash test: When they are designed from the outset as safety-critical components, hybrid sheets can systematically absorb accident impact energy. On the hybrid impact absorber, the energy absorption takes place almost entirely in the aluminium part of the component. Both the braze-welded seam and the steel part of the component are undamaged. In contrast to this, a steel-only component absorbing the same amount of energy sustains much more serious damage. Suitably designed hybrid components meet the requirements regarding energy absorption when needed, whilst also complying with defined geometries and stand-off distances.
