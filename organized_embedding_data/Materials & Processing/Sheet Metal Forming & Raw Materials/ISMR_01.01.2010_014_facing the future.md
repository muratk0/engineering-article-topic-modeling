## Professor Taylan Altan, head of the Center for Precision Forming in the US, outlines current and future metal forming challenges and solutions

ISMR: Where are you focusing the research efforts of the ERC/NSM this year - can you outline any current initiatives or plans?

TA: Our current focus continues to be (a) forming of AHSS, (b) sheet hydroforming for low volume sheet metal products, (c) warm forming of Mg and Al alloys, (d) hot stamping, (e) application of servo-motor drive presses and (f) precision blanking of thin sheets (thickness of 0.4mm or less).

We really are market-driven and our research work is sponsored by companies. We are about to start a new project on applications for servo-driven presses. Toyota already uses large servo presses from Komatsu – automotive applications call for extremely large presses (1000 tonnes and upwards) so there is great potential here.

Everyone wants to optimize the hot stamping process using FEA – we are currently putting together a detailed methodology to simulate the hot stamping process. Our work on aspects of the forming of advanced high strength steels is also continuing.

Our project work is generally geared towards reducing costs (energy, material scrap rates etc.) and increasing productivity and safety. The more we increase productivity, the more we can reduce material usage. In the US, where energy is relatively cheap, the energy issues are not prioritized as much as in countries like Japan where there are several projects in hand on this issue. We have also noted significant interest in the warm forming of aluminium and magnesium alloys but there is a significant cost involved in this, at the moment.

## DR. TAYLAN ALTAN

Dr. Altan received his Diploma Engineering degree from the Technical University, Hannover, Germany and his MS and PhD from the University of California, Berkeley. After working two years at Dupont and 18 years at Battelle, he joined OSU in 1986. He is a Fellow of SME, ASME, ASM International and CIRP, and Director of the Engineering Research Center for Net Shape Manufacturing (ERC/NSM) and the Center for Precision Forming (CPF) funded by NSF and Industry. He received the Frederick W. Taylor Research Medal from SME, the Gold Medal of the Japanese Society of Technology and Plasticity, and the William T. Ennor Manufacturing Technology award of ASME. He has co-authored more than 500 technical papers and 4 books on topics related to metal forming and machining.

2.2 In forming of AHSS we are focusing on a) determining “best” lubricants, b) selecting cost effective long lasting die materials and coatings, c) practical and inexpensive methods of measuring formability using a modified hydraulic bulge test, d) stretch bendability and e) stretch flanging capability.

ISMR: What do you foresee as the greatest challenges for metal formers and metal forming in the short and long-range future? How should metal formers respond?

TA: The greatest challenges in metal forming will continue to be a) variation of material properties from batch to batch, especially in AHSS, b) prediction of springback and control of part precision, c) design of tooling such to reduce scrap material left in the flange and binder area, maybe by eliminating the use of draw beads and d) rapid tool and process design to reduce lead times, especially in the production of small to moderate batch sizes.

Metal formers could respond to these challenges by (a) using more reliable tests for determining material properties (e.g. bulge test in addition to tensile test), (b) incorporating reliable Young modulus data to FEA simulations, (c) considering novel binder (blank holder) designs, possibly by using hydraulic cushions with multiple point force control and (d) developing an FEA-based technique to transfer the die design, achieved successfully in try-out presses, into the production press and operation, with little press time loss and trial and error in production.
