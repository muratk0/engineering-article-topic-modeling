# EPA targets two more chemicals used in metalworking

[TARİH: 01.10.2024 The Fabricator]

Around Washington

Shops heavily involved in surface cleaning and plating/anodizing could be affected

Stephen Barlas

T

he Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) is increasingly focused on chemicals used in manufacturing settings, and recent actions on two will have ramifications for a wide range of metalworking facilities.

The EPA has issued very recent initiatives under its Toxic Substances Control Act of 1976. One is aimed at n-methylpyrrolidone (NMP), and the other at 1-bromopropane (1-BP), also known as n-propyl bromide. In both instances the agency notes a long list of metal fabrication sectors that use those chemicals.

Both proposed rules set regulatory controls of varying strictness at any of three levels for different categories of use for either chemical. The least restrictive level is a Workplace Chemical Protection Program (WCPP). With NMP, the focus is on reducing dermal exposure. With 1-BP, the main concern is reducing inhalation dangers. More stringent are prescriptive controls, possibly with both dermal and inhalation controls. In certain conditions, a total ban of both chemicals is possible.

That is most likely in the case of NMP, where the EPA’s remedy of choice is a prohibition of use in metal finishing applications, such as cleaning, coating, etching, and invasive quality testing of metals, and the elimination of its use as an additive in machine lubrication.

Among the industry segments expected to be affected, according to the EPA, are machine shops, turned product manufacturers, and screw, nut, and bolt manufacturers (NAICS Code 332700); metal coaters and engravers (NAICS Code 332812); motor vehicle parts manufacturers (NAICS Code 336300); and household appliance manufacturers (NAICS Code 335200).

As is usually the case with EPA-proposed rules, any final outcome will come with compliance deadlines staggered depending on use of the chemical. Companies with a WCPP would generally have 12 months to comply with those facing a prohibition having 24 months.

At a June meeting to explain the proposed rule, Anthony Rufka, a risk manager with the EPA Office of Pollution Prevention and Toxics, said the agency "fully intends to consider all comments and, if appropriate, modify the proposal so that the final rule is as protective and practicable as possible."

The EPA definitely can expect comments—some of them critical of the whole effort.

"EPA’s rationale is based on flawed data that peer-reviewed scientific experts have demonstrated are incorrect," said Heather Blankinship, manager of the NMP Producers Group and a senior regulatory consultant with Bergeson & Campbell, at the June meeting.

The EPA also considers 1-BP to pose an unreasonable risk of injury to health. Any changes in the chemical’s regulation are likely to affect machine shops (NAICS code 332710); precision turned product manufacturers (NAICS code 332721); electroplaters, polishers, and anodizers (NAICS code 332813); pipe fabricators and pipe fittings manufacturers (NAICS code 332996); general metal fabricating companies (NAICS code 332999); and industrial machinery manufacturers (NAICS code 333249).

As with NMP, the EPA sets out the three same potential regulatory levels. In both industrial and commercial use as solvent for cold cleaning, the first and second choice is a WCPP and the third choice is total ban. For industrial and commercial use of a 1-BP-containing solvent in a closedloop or open-top batch cleaning process or in an inline vapor degreasing process, the preferred alternative is a WCPP. In a similar environment, the second choice is prescriptive controls. Lastly, a ban marks the final control recommendation. That WCPP/prescriptive control/prohibition sequence is typical of how EPA approaches the manufacturing industry.

A 1-BP WCPP would establish an existing chemical exposure limit to reduce inhalation risks. That exposure would have to be below 0.05 parts per million (PPM) (0.25 mg/m 3) for inhalation exposures as an eight-hour time-weighted average in workplace settings. A prescriptive requirement would dictate use of gloves to decrease dermal exposure.
