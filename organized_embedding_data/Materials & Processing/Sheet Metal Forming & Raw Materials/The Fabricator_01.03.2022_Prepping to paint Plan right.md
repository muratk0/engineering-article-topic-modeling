hen a coating meets a substrate as part of a fabricating or finishing process, that bond has to last. There are more pitfalls in that process than you might imagine. To get it right the first time—which is the only time—fabricators should approach surface preparation for painting not as one decision, but as a cascade of choices to obtain optimal adhesion.

Getting that first base coat to stick is critical. Where the coating meets the substrate, the pedal meets the metal. But not all paint adhesion challenges are created equal. For example, what is the initial surface finish? Are you working on a blank, or are you doing more intensive work like grinding a weld? Do you need to finish the entire part, or just certain areas? In broad terms, abrasive setups are either manual operations with hand tools, wide-belt fixed automation, or fully robotic installations.

Ultimately, there’s a specific approach that’s unique to each user, from the initial conception to the tool selection and deployment, right down to the settings and work paths that create the best results. You need to understand thoroughly your purpose, part, process, paint chemistry, and abrasive before you begin. Here are some of the key questions and insights that can guide your decision-making.

1. Know Your Purpose

This may seem simplistic at first:

My purpose is sanding because I’m going to paint

. But clarity here can influence everything that happens later. There’s a reason you plan to paint this part.

Is it functional, for example, to prevent corrosion or for color coding? If you’re painting just to color-code, a few visible grit lines and imperfections don’t matter. Is your purpose to be aesthetic, meaning that the part has to take the color in a way that pleases the eye? Whatever standard of performance the finished piece needs to meet, your prep work should make that possible.

No one sets out to do a bad job. But to do a good job, you need to start out with a defined standard of quality. When you abrade metal, you create scratches. The paint you apply must cover those scratches. A 1- to 3-mil paint coating might not cover 36-grit scratches, but a 4- to 6-mil coating would fill in the scratches so they are not visible.

2. Know Your Part

The nature of the piece to be painted determines many of the steps ahead.

Size

is one of the most fundamental considerations. There’s a difference between a part you will prepare on a workbench and one you will prep on a full-scale production line. The scale of the piece determines the scale of your workspace and the workflow as well. For example, it may be easier to fully automate the entire workflow when you’re finishing small- to-medium-sized parts. For large parts that require conveyors or robots moving parts from cell to cell, a large stand-alone system with more human interaction might be needed to get the parts into the cell.

Shape

is also a key factor. A flat workpiece is more likely to be suitable for a fixed automation setup in which abrasives can be applied on a regular path, usually belt-driven, at a comparatively high rate. Regular shapes such as cylindrical pieces or rectangular cross sections are also likely to work well with simpler abrasive setups.

Irregularities such as a weld or cut edge may involve more complicated tooling and abrasive selections. In other cases in which sanding has to conform to a more convoluted shape or work on only one part of a complex surface while avoiding another, a robotic surface preparation installation may be better suited to achieve reliable results at scale.

Material type

—carbon steel, stainless steel, or aluminum—affects the hardness and sharpness of the sanding media you choose, the pressure that should be applied, and the timing to apply the paint or coating. For example, carbon steel parts that will begin to oxidize quickly need to be coated right away.

Selecting the correct media and pressures to cut fast and efficiently is essential in reducing time to coat.

About 90% of paint jobs are on carbon steel, something that rusts. Similar considerations apply when preparing an aluminum piece to be anodized. Aluminum doesn’t rust, but it does corrode.

Heat

is a factor as well. Some materials get hotter than others during sanding. If the workpiece material is thin, it might even warp. In addition to adjusting the abrasive type and grade and the tool pressure, a common approach to managing heat and dust is to work in a wet sanding environment. However, that choice carries trade-offs. A lot of heat is generated on a dry machine. When you’re abrading carbon steel on a wet machine, you need to coat it immediately afterward before it can oxidize. Grade for grade, you’ll get a finer finish with a wet machine.

Cylindrical pieces are likely to work well with simpler belt abrasive setups such as this backstand finishing application.

3. Know Your Process

You need to determine whether a manual process using traditional tools in human hands will get the job done or if the finishing should be automated. This is a fundamental difference, not an incremental one. A production space set up for manual sanding bears very little resemblance to one set up for automated or robotic sanding. Media selection, tooling, finish consistency, and part throughput per cell for an automated setup can all be different from a manual approach because robotics and automation provide more options than a manual operator can handle.

If it’s a job for automation, what kind? The investments, tooling, training, and daily routine that go along with each type of automation vary just as widely.

Robotics and automation

are terms that frequently are referenced together, but robotics is just one type of automation. Fixed automation machines, also known as wide-belt machines, are adept at handling flat pieces and other simple shapes at a high rate. Complex shapes or operations can benefit from a robotic operation, which takes more time per piece but results in consistent quality.

One common situation involves finishing a flat piece cut from a steel plate. The cut edge often has a burr from the saw, torch, or laser, and that influences the prep stages necessary for painting. A fixed automation machine can remove the burr, abrade the surface, and put a slight radius on that edge. A small radius of about 2 mm can help foster paint adhesion; powder coat doesn’t stick well to those 90-degree edges.

Creating your production setup is one set of decisions. Knowing how you’ll use it is another. If your parts require only light finishing just to provide adhesion for a light coat of paint for appearance’ sake, you can approach it with simple tooling and a single abrasive step. But heavier work, such as removal of heavy scale from plate steel, weld and cut marks removal, or even shape alterations, calls for complex tooling and the need to sequence multiple abrasive steps for final finish requirements. Those will generate more heat, dust, and possibly sparks than light finishing.

4. Know Your Paint Chemistry

It’s vital to match the paint chemistry with what you’ll put it on, because if you don’t, no amount of surface prep will give you a good result. Once you have the right paint lined up, your sanding plan should match it.

Traditional liquid paint, typically spray-applied, may not conceal minor surface imperfections such as welds or grit lines as well as electrostatically applied powder coatings. That may lead you to step up the sanding approach, perhaps with a finer grit or a wet sanding method.

There’s a similar distinction related to color: Anyone with a dark-colored car knows that defects are more visible on it. Similarly, a glossy finish can reveal more faults than a matte finish. And, of course, you can conceal more surface features with a thick coat of paint than with a thin one. You can account for all of this by changing the paint plan, or you can account for it by sanding to match the paint you want.

5. Know Your Abrasives

There’s a right abrasive for every job, and plenty of wrong ones if they are not chosen under the proper criteria. Do you need an 80 or 180 grit? That’s certainly one of the decisions to tackle. But you also face complex questions beyond the grit count, such as which sanding head types to use, for which stages of work, and in which order. Belt, disc, nonwoven, and brush abrasives all do different jobs—sometimes on the same piece.

The grade of abrasive you choose influences not only how fine or rough the surface will be, but also the cut speed and heat and dust production. All of these become paramount when you’re performing the same steps all shift long. Matching them with a wet or dry setup can help you combine the cut you want with the working environment that delivers the best results. Aluminum parts tend to load up an abrasive head with dust more quickly than many other surface types.

Don’t forget the time factor. Every cutting tool has a lifespan. Abrasive materials begin at peak performance and eventually need replacement. If the abrasive performance isn’t perfectly consistent over time but you want your results to be consistent, you need to be aware of the rate of change and account for it in your work. That can affect everything from the frequency of disc changes to scheduled breaks for fatigued workers to the settings on a complex robot.

Sanding, grinding, and finishing are very labor-intensive. Fatigue can change the results from the first 10 minutes of a shift to the last 10 minutes.

When you’re faced with all these decisions, what can help make sense of it all? Keep the end goal in mind. You create the part for a purpose. You paint it so it will carry out that purpose. You sand it in a way that corresponds to that paint. That leads to a different set of specs for every operation. Fortunately, options are available that allow any fabrication shop to assemble the methods, tooling, and approaches that will answer its needs—and experts are available who can help answer questions.
