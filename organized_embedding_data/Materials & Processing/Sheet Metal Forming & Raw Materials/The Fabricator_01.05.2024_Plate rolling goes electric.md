# Plate rolling goes electric

[TARİH: 01.05.2024 The Fabricator]

Machine Design

New tech offers shops a new way to roll

By

Steve Bonnay

An all-electric plate roll streamlines the bending cycle with faster rolling and simultaneous roll movements, especially helpful during prebending and optimizing movements for complex, multiradius workpieces.

F

or more than a century, rolls have been shaping sheet metal and plate. Roll configurations have changed as have their drive systems, but the basic premise behind the technology hasn’t: the use of leverage to form workpieces into various shapes, from the cylindrical and conical to the complex, with multiple radii expertly formed to precision shapes, some of which really couldn’t be produced any other way.

Most recently, electric drive systems have entered the plate rolling arena. The new technology stands poised to boost quality and productivity in a significant way. To understand why requires putting the technology in context. It helps to understand where the electric roll fits within the technology’s long history—going back to the earliest systems in the middle of the 19th century.

Old-School Rolling

Mechanically driven, the earliest rolls were arranged in a

fixed pyramid.

They all turned, and the top roll moved up and down. Other than that, the rolls couldn’t reposition themselves in any way. The entire arrangement was very much like a press brake with a fixed punch tip radius and die opening (see

Figure 1

).

You can still find homemade machines here and there, and off-the-shelf pyramid rolls remain in some catalogs geared toward small shops and hobbyists. But by and large, the fixed-pyramid technology has gone the way of the dodo.

That’s because, by today’s standards, the technology has serious limitations, the most obvious being the significant, and unavoidable, unbent flat section at the plate edge. Governing its width is the distance between the centerline of the top roll and the centerline of one of the side rolls. During the early days, most roll shops simply trimmed off that flat section. Today, an operator (or, more likely, a home workshop hobbyist) would just bump-bend the plate edges on the press brake, then take it to their homemade fixed-pyramid roll to form the rest.

Initial Pinch

This next design iteration took a different path with the

initial-pinch three-roll machine.

The top roll remains fixed; one bottom roll sits not quite directly underneath the top roll, and a third roll sits off to the side. This design minimized that large, and often sacrificial, flat section on the workpiece. You placed the plate between the top roll and the bottom roll. At that point, the third roll moves upward, effectively "forming" (rather than rolling) the first few inches of the plate edge, performing a prebend—a huge advantage over the fixed-pyramid style (see

Figure 2

).

The big disadvantage is that operators need to remove and rotate the plate 180 degrees to perform the second prebend on the opposite edge. This is no trouble for smaller sheet metal workpieces. But as you move into bending larger and larger plate, rotating the workpiece for the second prebend can consume considerable time while tying up an overhead crane.

Introduced more than a century ago, the initial-pinch machine remained the industry standard for decades, and they’re still in use today, especially for small-scale work. If you’re working with thin, soft material several feet wide and long, it doesn’t take much for you to manually manipulate the blank to prebend both edges. But if the work is, say, 0.75 in. thick, 10 ft. wide, and 44 ft. long, manual manipulation for prebending will take serious time and resources.

Double-Pinch

After several decades of "initial-pinch" dominance in the plate roll market, engineers in the middle of the 20th century had an idea for improving efficiency.

What if an operator could prebend both plate edges without removing the workpiece from the machine?

From this came the

double-pinch three-roll

machine. The top roll remains stationary; the two bottom rolls start in a symmetrical pyramid arrangement and can both move upward toward the top roll (see

Figure 3

).

This machine solves one problem but then creates new problems. Because both bottom rolls move upward to prebend, operators can feed plate all the way through and back to pinch the material on the leading and trailing edge (hence the term "double-pinch") before the actual rolling cycle—all without removing and rotating the plate.

The design solved the problem of inefficient prebending—but it created other problems. First, compared with the initial-pinch machine, the bending points of contact between the top and bottom roll are farther apart, so material is more susceptible to twisting, especially when controlled by an inexperienced operator. Second, if you’re rolling large workpieces, the prebending method—feeding the flat plate all the way through and back again—requires significant floor space. A 40-ft. plate on an initial-pinch machine requires just 40 ft. of space in front of the machine; a double-pinch machine requires 40 ft. in front of and behind the rolls, or 80 ft. in total.

That ‘70s Machine: Hydraulics and the Four-Roll

When double-pinch machines came on the scene in the middle of the 20th century, plate rolling was still a mechanically driven process, and it remained that way for years—until the 1970s, which, for plate roll manufacturers, was a decade of experimentation. Some machines had hydraulic drive motors for the center roll, but side rolls were still mechanical. Other machines had hydraulic cylinders on the side rolls, but the center drive roll was still mechanical. By the end of the decade, nearly the entire plate roll market—both single- and double-pinch—had converted over entirely to hydraulics.

At this point, the

four-roll machine

emerged. This essentially represents the marriage of both the single- and double-pinch three-roll machine. Adding a fourth roll directly below the top roll provides a constant pinch, so the plate is always secure. The distance between the bend points remains close, to prevent slippage. And because you have forming rolls on both sides, you needn’t pass material through the machine or rotate the plate. You insert the plate between the top and bottom roll. The far side roll forms the first prebend. You then roll the workpiece, and, at the end, the near side roll moves up to bend the trailing edge (see

Figure 4

).

CNC Rolling

Properly sized, a four-roll can be a single-pass machine, and it’s the kind most commonly found with a CNC. The first step of a four-roll machine is to pinch the material—and it never lets go until the workpiece is rolled. From that first pinch, the CNC knows what material it has and how much material has been rolled through the machine.

In an initial-pinch machine, the operator must remove and rotate the material for the second prebend. In a double-pinch, the operator drops the two side rolls to pass the material through for the second prebend. In either case, the machines have lost their home position. Once they release the plate from pressure, they don’t know exactly where that plate is.

Today, when established rolling operations look for a new plate roll, eight times out of 10, they will choose a four-roll system. If not, they might try a three-roll system, as described previously. Or, if they need to roll thick plate, they might purchase a

variable-geometry plate roll.

On these, the top roll moves up and down and the side rolls move in and out, roughly mirroring the effects of an adjustable-width V die (see

Figure 5

).

Enter the Electric Roll

Up until recently, all new rolling machines, be they three- or four-roll, have been hydraulically driven. This, however, is just starting to change. Certain plate rolling systems have gone all electric.

The sheet and plate forming equipment arena has seen a shift toward electric drives for decades. Today’s electric press brakes, folders, and now plate rolls share similar advantages, at least at a top level. They have greater torque that’s instantly available, higher speeds, better accuracy, and less maintenance than their hydraulic cousins.

All-electric four-roll machines have now hit the market, and at first glance, you can see how quickly the rolls rotate. Typical hydraulic rolls turn at 20 ft. per minute (FPM), while an all-electric machine can turn at up to 32 FPM. That said, rotational speed really isn’t the primary benefit. The real gains come from what rolling steps can happen when.

Electric rolls have an external electrical enclosure far larger than what’s typical on a hydraulic plate roll (see

Figure 6

). These house all the independent inverters for each control axis. Some of the latest systems have nine control axes: two on each end of the side rolls (first through fourth axes); one on each end of the pinch, or bottom, roll (fifth and sixth axes); rotation of the top and bottom roll (seventh and eighth axes); and, finally, the yoke (ninth axis).

Hydraulic rolls can have numerous pumps, but they all have only a certain amount of flow and pressure. In most cases, they open one valve to have enough flow and pressure to perform the needed action. This means roll movement must happen sequentially, not simultaneously. In some cases, machines can open two valves and perform two actions, like having the rolls rotate as you reposition your set of side rolls (more on this later). Open more valves, though, and you lose tonnage, speed, or both. The machine just runs out of juice.

Not so with electric plate rolls. Because each inverter is its own power source, using one doesn’t take away power from another. It needn’t rob Peter to pay Paul. The result: Multiple axes can run simultaneously without sacrificing speed or tonnage. The same applies for additions like feed tables or overhead support. Each is electrically driven, and each has its own power source. The inverter feeds power to rotational, planetary, and linear actuators. Of course, when the yoke moves, nothing else can. (You wouldn’t want to open the yoke while you’re rolling. Bad things will happen.) Outside this, though, the electric design opens a host of simultaneous-operation possibilities that can streamline the rolling cycle.

Contrast this with the standard way to start a job on a four-roll hydraulic machine. You first bring the rear roll up and insert the material to square it against that raised rear roll. The system then counter-rotates the material to the pinch point, then pushes up the side roll to form that leading edge. On an electric machine, because every axis is independent and has a dedicated inverter, certain steps in this sequence can happen simultaneously. After squaring the material against the raised side roll, the top and bottom rolls can counter-rotate

while

the side roll moves into position and performs the prebend. That prebend occurs while the squared material moves back to the initial pinch point.

Having so many independent axes able to move simultaneously has broad-reaching implications. Consider the rolling of excavator buckets. These have multiple radii, and to roll them on a traditional hydraulic system, the operator rolls one radius and then needs to back off the side roll to form that new, larger radius. This disengages the plate, producing a small flat section between the two radii. Operators have tried backing off the roll in increments, performing what’s roughly equivalent to a bump bend between the two radii—but that still just gives you a series of narrow flats instead of one wider flat section.

Alternatively, hydraulic machines can be purchased with side roll interpolation, which allows the side rolls to move while they rotate, blending from one radius to the next. Electric machines, however, have no interpolation option because, well, they don’t need it. Again, every roll is independently powered and controlled. This, in effect, gives you

eight axes

of interpolation. Such complex motion can make the capabilities of a modern CNC really shine. Operators can control the motion of an electric plate roll manually, but the CNC can take efficiency to the next level. Like a puppet master pulling the strings of a complex marionette, the computer can move various elements of the machine at once to really dial in and accelerate the rolling process.

More Axes, More Possibilities

The state of the art in plate rolling can accommodate a certain level of material variability. That is, if a batch of plates vary from what was expected in a uniform manner, the CNC can detect that variation, make the adjustments, and dial in the parameters to run that batch error free.

The state of the art in electric machines also are pushing rolling accuracy into uncharted territory. Electrics’ tight positioning tolerances are making them most popular with plate that’s 1 in. thick and less, especially considering the tighter specs available in thinner material (less variation in material thickness, for example).

Of course, if job tolerances are tight and material variability is high, an experienced operator needs to read the rolling "tea leaves" to make the appropriate adjustments. The same holds true for specialty material with a narrow window between yield and tensile, making the workpiece extra sensitive to cracking. This is where operator skill takes center stage.

Physics are what they are, and rolling challenges remain. Today’s latest rolling technology simply gives operators better, more productive tools to overcome them. All that said, one reality isn’t up for debate: We’ve come an extraordinarily long way from the manual fixed-pyramid mechanical roll.
