P

art marking isn’t flashy, but it’s essential when tackling some of the greatest costs in metal fabrication, especially high-product-mix operations. Lost or mislabeled parts can cause ripple effects of wasteful effort. The technology varies widely, from laser marking on the laser cutting bed to bar code printing to automated labeling systems—and all of it can be designed for some seriously harsh environments, including galvanizing and blasting.

For a rundown of the basics, The Fabricator spoke with Brian Schlatterbeck, director of printer and tag sales, and Jennifer Mikus, automation sales engineer, at Chillicothe, Ohio-based InfoSight, about several key aspects to consider when developing a parts labeling strategy—especially in environments where the part label will take a beating.

Analyze the Current State

"Never forget how you labeled parts before," Schlatterbeck said. "If something were to happen, you can easily revert to the system you had previously, or at least some version of it."

For instance, a fabricator might choose to augment its existing part identification, such as keeping (and perhaps refining) a direct part marking method and incorporating a tag, perhaps one with multiple bar codes with the same information, just in case one becomes unreadable. In this context, everyone realizes that no labeling solution is perfect, and having multiple "backup" identification methods just makes sense.

That said, the exact marking strategy depends on the application requirements. The legacy method provides the baseline to be improved upon. This can include establishing how many parts need to be labeled. That depends first on external traceability requirements, of course. But beyond this, what level of labeling makes for optimal efficiency, and what’s overkill?

Schlatterbeck suggested fabricators start by asking some basic questions. "Is the part unique? Is it serialized? If there’s a batch of 50, you might not need to label every one. But if there’s only one or two, and, say, it’s a beam with a hole in a slightly different spot than another beam, but otherwise looks very similar to other beams that are sent down different routings through the plant, that’s when it becomes important to have a unique serialized part that’s properly labeled."

Next, fabricators need to determine what to put on the label, beyond the minimum traceability information a customer might require. "I think the busiest tag I’ve designed had 15 different pieces of information," Schlatterbeck said. "The simplest have just a bar code with a line of human-readable text underneath. The trick is to find a balance. At first, a shop might want to squeeze as much as they can on a label, but what do you really need to better manage your process? Also know you can easily change the label layout down the road, should your needs change."

An easy way to limit information at first is to consider cost. No matter what material the tag happens to be, it has a cost. "Consumables cost you money, and naturally, the smaller the tag, the smaller the consumable," Schlatterbeck said.

A fabricator can either mark the part directly or use a separate label. Direct marking has obvious advantages: There’s no label to lose. But a part label only has value if it’s readable. Powder coating and other processes can make direct parts marking a less attractive option. But even if a part won’t be coated, a mark directly on a part can still become unreadable.

"For instance, if you’re laser marking or applying part identification with a peening process, you need to make sure the mark is deep enough so it’s readable through all subsequent manufacturing steps," Schlatterbeck said. Specialized bar code labeling is designed to withstand various processes, from blasting to powder coating. "Think about a dot-peened label directly on the part. If it goes through painting, powder coating, or galvanizing, the peen can get covered up, and it’s unreadable. Laminated tags designed to endure these processes can overcome this problem."

Designing a Robust Tagging System

As a first step, training needs to emphasize the value of parts identification in general, even if it’s not directly useful at a particular work center. Imagine a steel workpiece with a label designed to withstand harsh conditions, even blasting. The job’s traceability requirements demand the label be visible throughout the value chain. Blasting operators might not be aware of part traceability requirements, though, and besides, the labels aren’t much use to them. So, they ignore them, and maybe even aim their blasting gun right at them. "They might see a white tag and, not knowing its importance, try to destroy it," Schlatterbeck said. "Whatever tag you use, if you try to destroy it, you can."

People need to know about part labeling importance, and the labels themselves also need to be placed so workers don’t have to go out of their way to avoid damaging them—for instance, in a corner that spends less time fully immersed in an anodizing or galvanizing tank. Or it might include using a robust annealed wire (which doesn’t melt), perhaps between a 9 and 12 grade, to attach a label, so a worker wielding a blasting gun can prep a surface while not hitting the label with blasting media directly.

A parts marking strategy also needs to consider the need to hide or remove identification. "Again, some operations only need to identify parts within their own plant floors," Schlatterbeck said. "Some operations, like those in architecturally exposed steel, don’t want you to deliver a part mark that’s visible."

If hiding a permanent label isn’t an option, tags will need to be removed at some point. "In these cases, a wire-tie could attach a label, which then could be easily cut off without damaging the final finish of the piece," Schlatterbeck said, adding that—ideally, at least—part traceability should be considered together with the process as a whole. This again includes thinking about how a label will be removed. Instead of leaving a bit of melted wire welded to the surface, why not have a small hole or other attachment feature in the part to wire the part tag in place?

What to include on a tag as well as the attachment method depends on the operational requirements and downstream processes—what the tag must endure and what workers along the value stream need to know to do their jobs well. Note the wire configuration. It’s secured far enough so the tag can swing freely as it endures certain processes downstream.

Attaching and Removing Labels

"Know that you have a spectrum of label attachment methods to consider," Schlatterbeck added. "You can weld a label so it’s on there permanently, but you can also weld a label so it can be easily removed."

He described a tag designed for the galvanizing process. The tag can’t be flush to the surface, since it would prevent the zinc coating from forming correctly. To overcome this, the tags are mounted on brackets that maintain a gap between the material surface and the tag itself.

"You also want the tag to get through the process and remain readable, which is another reason why you don’t want it flush against the steel. The tag is only 0.010-in. thick, so it’s easy enough to cut it off."

Attaching these tag brackets does take practice, especially when using a conventional MIG process. "Welders might be welding heavy steel, then weld this 0.010-in.-thick tag, so they need to adjust their equipment to avoid blowing out the tag." Some experimentation with alternative methods can help. There’s the stud welding option, while others fabricate tag-holder clips that can be quickly spot-welded to the surface.

The tags themselves have coatings that make it more difficult to weld directly onto the steel surface. "The coating acts as an insulator and makes it harder to weld," Schlatterbeck said, adding that this feature benefits applications in which the label might need to be removed at some point. If a tag will never need to be removed (it could, say, be hidden in the final product)—and having that tag permanently on the part is critical for the overall process—a fabricator might choose a tag with bare, uncoated edges, allowing them to be welded securely to the part.

If tags need to be removed, about 95% of fabricators attach them with a wire. Here again, the exact attachment and removal method needs to consider what the part will endure. "If you’re attaching a tag with wire, we recommend fabricators use annealed wire for everything, whether you’re galvanizing, blasting, painting, or doing anything else to the workpiece. You use a 12-in. wire, wrap 4 in. around one end. When you attach the wire to the tag, you need to ensure the tag can move and flex. So, if it gets hit with paint or shot, it’s free to move and get out of the way. If it’s tied or attached solidly to the part surface, it will take the brunt of the media and have less chance to survive."

Automation Options

As processes become more repeatable and volumes rise, operations might be able to make the jump to automation—especially if they’re marking the same part in the same way over and over. InfoSight’s Mikus described how certain dot-peen and even laser markers can be integrated into existing automated systems.

"Some automated dot-peen systems mark pieces deep enough so it’s able to be covered by paint and still remained readable," she said.

Certain marking technologies apply ceramic-based white coatings that can be etched with a bar code and remain readable even in harsh environments.

"Coatings on tags get darkened by the laser," Mikus said, adding that this is what makes the laser’s marks so durable to physical contact and varying temperatures, like in heat treating ovens, where even deep laser marks directly on the metal (without the contrast coating) can become difficult to read. "That coating is what allows the white to stay white and the black to stay black."

Mikus explained that such direct part marking, with contrast coating, usually doesn’t make sense for cosmetically critical parts. "But if the surface will be hidden, or the look of the surface isn’t critical, and you need that contrast to maintain traceability through the plant, this laser marking method can work very well."

She added that a variety of labeling systems have entered the market, including systems that automatically apply marks on the metal, sticky labels, and tags. They usually involve direct integration with a company’s ERP. The aim usually is to eliminate the repetitive work of applying labels, especially in high-volume settings. Regardless of volumes, though, she recommended fabricators start with basic questions: "What’s the space available for the equipment and workpieces to be marked? What are the repeatability requirements? What kind of shapes are you marking? How will it be presented for marking? And what processes does it need to survive downstream, whether it’s in-house or at an outside service provider?"

About Culture

Whether automation or manual marking makes sense or not really depends on the application. Regardless, whatever labeling approach a fabricator takes, sources emphasized that the best, most sustainable labeling and job tracking strategies start with a progressive shop culture focused on transparency, honesty, and overall improvement—not finger-pointing and playing the blame game.

An operation can have the most robust parts marking system in the world, but if front-line workers don’t buy in to the process, parts marking alone won’t solve the problem. "Ultimately, parts marking needs to be rolled out in a positive way," Schlatterbeck said. "It’s about improving the process, improving traceability, and improving quality to provide better products for customers."
