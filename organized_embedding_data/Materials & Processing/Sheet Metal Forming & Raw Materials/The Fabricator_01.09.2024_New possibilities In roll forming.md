Roll Forming

A tried and true method of metal forming continues to evolve

By

Dan Davis

and

Edwin Pfisterer

Roll forming still has a place in the modern world. Parts found in the transportation, elevator, construction, and agricultural industries are commonly formed using the process, and new roll forming advances are opening the door to additional applications as well.

B

efore the age of mass customization, manufacturing was dominated by high-volume work that required the creation of tooling and powerful machinery to cut and form metal. Roll forming was one of the processes that helped to build the modern world in the 20th century.

Times have changed, and manufacturing has been redefined, with high volumes not being as high as they once were and the emergence of laser cutting and press brake bending as an efficient means of fabricating metal parts. But roll forming still remains a viable method of continuously forming cross sections of metal, even if the jobs of today are measured in thousands of feet, instead of tens of thousands of feet.

Today, roll forming, also referred to as cold rolling or roll profiling, is producing shapes that industry veterans might find hard to believe. The process delivers much more than simple C- and U-shaped profiles. In fact, some of these cross sections look like extrusions or even like two pieces of metal that have been welded together. Roll forming is delivering plenty of unexpected possibilities for the metal fabricating community.

An Overview of Roll Forming

New blood is continuously entering the domestic manufacturing industry, particularly as baby boomers exit the workforce. With that knowledge, a new generation of fabricators might not have any idea just what roll forming is. This explanation hopes to paint them a vivid picture of the operation.

Like most high-volume metal process applications, a coil of metal, slit to any desired width, is fed into the roll forming line. On the way, features such as holes, embosses, and slots can be punched into the length of the material. When the material reaches the rollers, which are positioned on individual stations within the roll forming line, it is formed a bit at a time. Like any metal forming process, a roll forming line must contend with springback, where the metal wants to revert back to its original form, and that’s why multiple roll forming stations are needed, with each helping to reduce the stresses that prevent the material from reaching the desired shape.

In some instances, the final shape can be brought together to be welded closed. This is typically accomplished by laser or high-frequency induction welding. In cases such as thin wall tubing, this type of welding can reach speeds of up to several hundred feet per minute.

The materials that can be processed in a roll forming line are varied. Cold- and hot-rolled steel; chrome and chrome-nickel steel; hot-dipped galvanized steel; and aluminum are just some of the materials regularly formed on these types of lines. Even prepainted metal can be processed, if the coated coil hasn’t been sitting in inventory too long and the coating still exhibits elasticity.

Roll forming also is good for high-strength and ultrahigh-strength materials. These materials can be difficult to form because of their very nature. For example, on a press brake, multiple hits or adjustments in pressure during the forming process might be needed to get a desired shape. Taking into account the material’s characteristics, the roll forming process forms and relieves the metal strip between each station until the form is reached.

In terms of material thickness, roll forming lines at Welser Profile typically process strip from 0.011 in. (0.3 mm) to 0.323 in. (8.2 mm). The strip width can reach up to 39.37 in. (1,000 mm), profile lengths 88.5 ft. (27,000 mm), and profile height up to 12.6 in. (320 mm).

The New Realities

Roll forming has always been a suitable choice for high-volume metal forming, but today high volume takes on a totally new meaning than it did 20 years ago. Advances in roll forming technology help to make the process affordable and flexible.

For instance, a customer doesn’t require tens of thousands of feet of formed strip to be cost-effective for a job. Today, a minimum quantity of 15,000 ft. might turn out to be a good candidate for roll forming, depending on the material and complexity of the forms, of course.

A couple of things make this possible:

• The roll forming company has a set of standard rollers in stock, and they can be used for different applications. In this scenario, the customer doesn’t have to purchase all-new roll forming stations.

• The roll forming company manufactures and maintains its own tooling. There’s no need to outsource the creation of new tooling if it’s needed for the job. Also, any tweaks in tooling design can be done quickly.

• The roll forming application can be simulated in the digital realm to see how the cross section is derived. The simulation allows for the usage of standard and custom tool stations in the forming process, which minimizes tooling costs.

Additionally, if a roll forming company can participate in the part development process, it can help to develop a tailor-made design that meets the end objectives and maximizes affordability. This type of expertise comes with having thousands of cross sections and possessing indepth knowledge of what can be accomplished in the roll forming process.

New Possibilities

Roll forming might be looked upon as a traditional forming process because it’s been around for years, but that doesn’t mean advancements haven’t occurred. Just like any other manufacturing process, roll forming has to evolve to meet the needs of a changing marketplace.

For instance, the automotive industry has been focused on light-weighting for years, looking to trim weight from vehicles in the hopes of maximizing fuel efficiency. That type of focus results in scrutiny of every part and assembly.

Roll forming has been a traditional method of producing high-strength and complex profiles for vehicles. The one catch, however, was that the profile typically had the same material thickness through the part design.

That’s not the case anymore as a way of producing cross sections with various dimensions is now possible (see

). Designs deliver a profile where the load is optimized, allowing for certain sections of the strip to be thinned out, through a groove near or on the strip’s edge perhaps.

This thickness optimization is accomplished with specialized tooling and in additional passes through the roll forming line. Production speed is not sacrificed to introduce such a part feature.

The end result is a part with increased surface hardness, which helps it to stand up in applications where the surface is routinely exposed to harsh interactions, like in telescopic profile tubes. The parts also display strong characteristics even if they might not be as heavy as a similarly sized parts of one consistent material thickness.

Roll forming also is able to deliver a split bend design (see

Figure 2

). (Think about how a gymnast looks as a split is done on the floor.) This design is created by thinning out a section of the strip and then having the roll tooling basically form what becomes a flange.

This shape with the split bend design results in a hard and smooth profile, capable of withstanding a lot of wear and tear. Again, it’s one of the reasons why the automotive industry has gravitated to using this design in vehicle designs.

In particular, engineers no longer have to weld an L profile to a roll formed profile. Through the split bend design, the newly roll formed cross section has the flange created during the forming process. The flange then can be used for spot welding of other parts to the profile or to place indentations that can be used for bonding material.

New Applications

Roll forming has played an important role not only in the automotive industry in recent years, but also in markets such as construction, power generation, and storage systems. The process provides rigid profiles that can be produced quickly and cost-effectively.

New applications abound as well, particularly as more engineers learn what is possible with roll forming. Solar energy is one example. Roll forming can be used to fabricate posts, supports, and crossbars that can be used to hold photovoltaic solar panels in place. Also, because many of these types of projects are linked to decarbonization efforts, using recycled metal for these solar panel components proves to be especially attractive.

Roll forming presents a world of possibilities for manufacturers. Thousands of different profiles made from different materials for hundreds of different applications are possible. It just takes an engineer with an inquiring mind to figure out where roll forming makes sense.

Roll forming might be looked upon as a traditional forming process because it’s been around for years, but that doesn’t mean advancements haven’t occurred. Just like any other manufacturing process, roll forming has to evolve to meet the needs of a changing marketplace.
