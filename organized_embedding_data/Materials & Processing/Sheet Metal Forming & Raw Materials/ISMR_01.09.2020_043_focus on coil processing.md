# FOCUS ON COIL PROCESSING

[TARİH: 01.09.2020 ISMR]

The book aims to illustrate the benefits of being open to innovation and increase productivity; reduce waste and material handling; optimise raw material usage and save time on machine tool programming whilst increasing quality. These are the principles of in-line production.

I have been working at DALLAN now for twenty years, so this book celebrates my anniversary this year! It has now been published and the Italian version has been available on Amazon since mid-July. The translated version will be available shortly.

I started by writing a collection of articles on coil-fed technology and then, after my children asked me about the history of the company, I decided to turn these into a book. I contacted customers, asking them to contribute, and requested interviews with them to describe their experiences. Practical experience is very relatable!

In February, it was almost ready and then lockdown happened, so I spent time revising and adding to the book during lockdown. I added QR codes so that readers could watch videos of machinery in operation, and we could make the experience more interactive for customers.

ISMR: Your book outlines the benefits of coil-fed technology (such as automation, productivity, flexibility and material savings) and highlights the in-line production method. Please list the benefits of in-line coil-fed production for today’s manufacturers?

AD: It all comes down to efficiency. If a customer has a production challenge, or cannot find skilled personnel to operate machines or needs to manufacture more quickly or work in a changing production environment, this opens the door to in-line production, which is our speciality.

For example, rolling shutters are produced generally from pre-formed bars and then are cut and assembled. However, cutting bars creates scrap, and the bars have to be stored before final assembly. These are a lot of work processes which can be saved by using in-line production methods. So, why not cut the bars directly from the coil, to exact length (saving on scrap, which can take up to 15% of costs)? Scrap savings can total up to 100,000 euros per year. The bars are assembled automatically so that just one operator is needed to handle the production of 450 square metres of windows in one shift. The material, energy and storage savings, plus personnel/labour savings for the logistics alone, are significant when you use in-line technology. Automation is crucial in this process.

So, inline (or coil-to-window) production allows us to produce Just in Time (reducing storage, material and labour costs). Automation improves productivity, reducing coil set-up time.

ISMR: How have you seen coil-fed technology develop since you first started selling it?

AD: My father started the company in 1978 – producing roll forming machines and tooling for all material thicknesses was its main focus at that time.

He then made the decision to just focus on thin sheet metal, which was a huge change for the company (which worked with pre-painted materials etc.). When I started at DALLAN, Just in Time production and flexibility concepts had just come into use, making it possible for just one line to produce complete door frames, with different dimensions, one after the other (what we call Zero standard production). Continuous information and material flow was key.

Punching and laser cutting are a key part of our business. The laser increased the flexibility and the productivity of the coil-fed punching lines.

I have also seen increasing levels of automation in the assembly of finished profiles (for example in the coil-to-window systems) so lean production lines have become essential.

Since we specialise in sheet metal machines and roll formers, automation and flexibility have been the most important trends that I saw since I started at the company.

ISMR: How does coil-fed technology contribute to sustainable manufacturing and efficient processes?

AD: There are three examples I could cite of more sustainable production for customers. Firstly, rolling shutters made from aluminium and polyurethane - a composite profile. Producing these in a traditional way renders 10-15% scrap levels and is more costly. Our coil-to-window technology allows customers to operate more sustainably whilst producing a high-quality product. We do not have to save on material usage by reducing material thickness, but we get the same quality of product with at least 10% less scrap – a saving of hundreds of thousands of euros per year.

Another example concerns the savings generated from coil-fed laser cutting. Compared to sheet cutting, significant space savings are made on the shop floor. We adjust the size of the coil feed to the amount of parts needed to avoid high scrap levels. If we produce long parts (i.e. 5m long kitchen hoods), then flexible laser cutting for up to 5m parts is needed. The payback calculation on the machine (made on stainless steel material savings for one customer) justified the purchase of five coil-to-laser systems. This is a significant return on investment.

The last example is energy saving. Servo-electric punching machines are common now, and they use one fourth of the energy of hydraulic machines. All machines are controlled digitally, saving 50% of energy. They use less hydraulic oil (less waste and pollution) so this is a constant focus and brings better benefits for customers.

ISMR: How do you see COVID-19 impacting manufacturing markets in Italy and, more widely, in Europe? What do you predict for manufacturing markets in 2021?

AD: Around 90% of our customers are based abroad, primarily in central Europe and the U.S. Italy was the only country in Europe to shut down production facilities between March and April. In that period, most companies in the sheet metal industry were closed so now there is a rush to gain back the production that was lost in those weeks.

As far as our main markets are concerned, our customers’ projects are typically part of mid- and long-term strategies and these are being less affected by the present situation. Deliveries of new equipment are now set for the third quarter of 2021.

Some sectors, such as construction (in which we are particularly active), are doing better than others but we need to stay alert to change and always bear in mind that the quality of products, and the increasing levels of machine automation, will be essential in the near future.

I remember, when the pandemic and lockdown first started, that our first reaction was to try to keep the same strategies and production levels, but the pandemic made this impossible. We learned quickly to ‘roll with the wave’ and stay true to our customers’ requirements. Our machines process a lot of products for the interior finish market: our customers, in that industry, are reporting higher demand now. Perhaps this is a result of lockdowns and the rise of home DIY?

The financial crisis in 2007/8 meant that customers had, since then, prepared for a threat of that kind. However, the pandemic threat was very different to this. Flexibility and innovation are key now for companies to help them stay profitable and prepared. Our market, and our customers’ markets, are going to change and we need to be smart to follow this evolution.

Safety and automation go hand in hand. We will always need to make products and bend or cut metal (in our case). Doing that in a more automated way (including information systems) is in line with today’s social distancing and increasing security/ safety levels in companies. Efficiency does not just mean saving cost and making

profits - it also means moving companies in a more sustainable direction. Certain production systems use less raw material and less energy, which also feed into today’s environmental concerns.

ISMR: How has DALLAN adapted to the challenges of COVID-19 (i.e. its organisation, staff, production etc.)?

AD: Our first priority, during the COVID-19 outbreak, was to guarantee the safety of our workers, customers and our business (including our supply chains). We needed to pull together and help each other out. We had to change the structure of our offices, enlarging them, introducing shields, masks and 1m distancing, measuring temperatures etc. We already have a large production area and are completing a further production area of 5000 square metres so we can maintain and respect social distancing in the factory. We closed our cantine at the beginning, but it has now reopened with new measures in place.

Our contact with customers is also changing. We stayed in constant contact during the lockdown with our customers. We now do machine tests via video and company presentations on Zoom or Microsoft Teams. We are also arranging a lot more customer meetings online although we have started travelling again now to see customers. Different customers have introduced their own safety measures in their factories – and some are more cautious than others.

We have also changed the way that we communicate, especially as many exhibitions have been cancelled or postponed. We started making more detailed videos last year, to explain our machinery concepts and operation. The COVID-19 outbreak meant that we had to think more creatively so we have also been creating webinars on topics such as rolling shutters, metal ceilings etc. We are doing these every four weeks now for our customers.

ISMR: You mention, in your book, that new Industry 4.0-inspired systems include functions and technologies, such as remote assistance and office control, which require curiosity, elasticity and openness. These comes naturally to younger people. What are your views on Industry 4.0 and how has DALLAN embraced this principle in its operations?

AD: In 2002, I started working on the first parametric machines for our customers, i.e. connecting ERPs with the machine, collating machine data/feedback and exerting more control over production. Our machines have a high level of customisation. However, Industry 4.0 also connects different parts of the company. As far as DALLAN’s internal production is concerned, in 2012 we connected our design department (by 3D modelling) to our purchase department (issuing orders to suppliers). Now we have introduced automatic storage system data for internal logistics and young people have adapted to these new procedures much more quickly. We have also introduced visual management systems on our machines in one process area of lean production.

On the other hand, we have to keep in mind that, in sheet metal manufacturing, there is a huge bank of experience that our older and more skilled technicians (aged between 45-60) carry with them. This expertise is in technical problem-solving and communicating in the right way with customers. So, there is a massive range of technical expertise and experience that counts for a huge amount – it doesn’t just boil down to software solutions… Developing those sorts of skills takes time and cannot just be learned quickly.

ISMR: Please explain the term ‘Zero Standard’ production and what it can bring to manufacturers?

AD: ‘Zero standard’ production is a term that one of my customers invented and he applies it to his own production of special panel assembly for the construction industry. He coined this term because it is an extension of the Just in Time concept.

It is basically understanding that having three lines to produce what we need, when we need it, is more effective than having just one line, when we are changing lots of products all the time. Zero standard means the flexibility to meet customer requirements without having to store semi-finished products. We learn a lot from our customers so, once we understood the advantages of parametric production and flexible rollforming, we applied it to many of our own line products such as safety doorframes, ventilation, air conditioning, lighting etc.

AD: Some patents are under development and we cannot discuss them at the moment. Our plan is to launch a new fibre laser cutting system from coil at the EuroBLECH exhibition next March in Hanover. This is innovative technology with our vision camera system that enables production of very long products.

We have not put on hold any of our plans for new machine developments, despite the pandemic, and we are receiving customer requests for machinery next year which is keeping us busy.

We are also enlarging our new facility in Dayton, Ohio (USA), to provide service to our customers there (DALLAN America Corp).
