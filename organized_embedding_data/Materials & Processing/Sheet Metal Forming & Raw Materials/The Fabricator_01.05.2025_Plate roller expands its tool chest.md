# Plate roller expands its tool chest

[TARİH: 01.05.2025 The Fabricator]

Cover Story

Albina Co., Tualatin, Ore., finds CNC adds a new level of production efficiency that it didn’t have before

By

Dan Davis

Brian Smith and Ethan Kyle, Albina Co. Inc., check the diameter of a cylinder that’s fresh off one of the company’s plate rolling machines.

Two ends of a cylinder are joined before being removed from the plate roller.

M

odern metal fabrication often is defined by the advanced technology that dominates high-performance shops. Skills that were once locked inside the heads of craftsmen now have been programmed into control systems. An operator can hit a button and watch as parts are cut or formed on a CNC machine. The only other activity the operator might be involved with is removing parts from and then trashing the skeleton.

That type of CNC is commonplace in many shops, but not all. Some sectors of metal fabricating are still reliant on the wisdom of experienced machine operators. Advanced control technology still has trouble accounting for all the variabilities that come with these jobs.

One of those areas is in plate rolling. Workpieces are large. Material is often inconsistent. Not all projects are simple cylinder rolls. Without the skilled input of someone who has done this type of work, the chances of rework—or even worse, scrapping—greatly increase.

Albina Co. Inc., Tualatin, Ore., has provided curved steel to customers since 1939. With that history, the company has developed the skills necessary to deliver the most challenging of steel shapes on time and within specifications. A great example of that is one of its metal art projects called "Phoenix."

Designed by sculptor Damon Hyldreth, the piece consists of A572 plate rolled into five lengths of 20 ft. by 15 in. by 1 in. and one length of 7 ft. by 84 in. by 1.5 in. No drawings were available to create plans for the fabrication. The Albina team had to take a scale model, about 1/20th the size of the final project, and use that to derive the appropriate radii, arcs, and twists.

Once the design was complete, the manufacturing began. Because the parts had ever-changing radii and pitch, only 2- to 3-ft. sections could be rolled at a time before the part had to be adjusted to hit a new pitch.

Complicating the operation further was the awkward size of the plate sections. The riggings for the large workpieces were challenging enough, but technicians also had to continually remove the parts in and out of the machine to ensure the right- and left-hand parts matched.

Today, "Phoenix" resides at a luxury apartment complex in California. It represents one more successful project for the Albina team.

Even with a robust resume of curved metal projects in buildings, stadiums, infrastructure developments, and art installations, Albina is not relying solely on the experience of its skilled craftspeople to meet customer demands. Like most any other modern organization, it realizes that it can’t ignore the availability of advanced technology.

"As one of North America’s experts when it comes to bending steel, we view it as our obligation to our customers to stay in front of technology and improvements—to continue to learn and improve," said Brian Smith, Albina’s president and the third generation to lead the company.

A New Plate Rolling Path

Prior to late 2019, Albina had never really done any heavy-duty rolling of very thick plate. However, the chance to win a significant job emerged around this time, and Albina wasn’t going to be able to do the job without the right equipment.

Smith said he followed the wisdom of the cornfield in the movie "Field of Dreams," where ghostly whispers suggested that Iowa farmer Ray Kinsella build a baseball diamond in the middle of nowhere to attract the baseball ghosts of the past. Instead of landscaping, Albina purchased a MAV K30 plate rolling machine from Davi. (Albina had previously purchased a custom-designed MCP30 pyramid rolling machine from Davi in late 2017. The equipment, which had its power unit moved to outside the main machine frame to allow it to meet Albina’s height requirements without having to dig a pit, fit the company’s need to provide curving services for mid-sized structural members, pipe, and hollow structural sections.)

Unlike the movie, where ghosts and carloads of people came to see the newly built baseball diamond in the Iowa cornfield, the job didn’t materialize for Albina. Despite the setback, Smith said the company marketed its newfound plate rolling capabilities and eventually started to win jobs. Today, the machine is a workhorse for the company.

The MAV K30, nicknamed "Maverick" by the shop floor crew, can accommodate plate up to 10 ft. wide and can roll up to 4-in. material. Albina has a crane capacity that accommodates material weighing up to 20,000 lbs.

The top roll measures 23.6 in. in diameter, which means that it can bend approximately 26-in.-dia. plate. (Albina’s rule of thumb is that it can roll a plate to a diameter that is 1.1 times the diameter of the roll.) This type of consistency between rolls performed with the three-roll machine is challenged by variables such as the material’s grade, tensile strength, and yield.

Smith said the ability to form very thick plate changed the trajectory of the business in the ensuing years.

"We went from almost 100% helical- and spiral-bent parts off our plate rolls to more cylinders, sheets, and cones. It flipped," he said. "Today, we probably do 70% normal plate rolling and 30% helical and spiral bending." The MAV K30 was even used on the "Phoenix" project.

The company’s plate rolling journey took another interesting turn in 2021 after a very fortuitous "accident," as Smith called it.

Albina’s experience with Davi’s angle rolling machines paved the way for the company to investigate Davi’s plate rolling technology.

Albina has been providing curved steel to customers since 1939.

Enter the Era of Four-Roll Forming

In the market for a mid-sized plate roller after an older three-roll machine broke down, Albina reached out to Davi once again. It had found both Maverick and Iceman, the MCP30 pyramid roll, to be reliable with plate rolling assignments, so it felt comfortable ordering another machine from the same source.

But when Albina officials committed to purchasing an MCO 3034 three-roll machine, they did so with the knowledge that it was with a longer-than-expected lead time. Smith said that the company was able to live with the arrangement because Davi planned to loan it a four-roll MCB E30 at no charge until the MCO 3034 was ready. This enabled Albina to get to work on the jobs that were waiting on the shop floor.

When the four-roll CNC machine was installed in early 2021, the shop floor looked at it with a bit of skepticism. Reality, however, suggested that they needed to get on with the work. That’s when something unexpected happened.

"My operators fell in love with the MCB E30," Smith said.

The advanced controls on the machine allowed plate rolling jobs to be done repeatedly without "recreating the wheel," according to Smith. Operators were able to provide quantity runs for customers much more efficiently than if they were doing similar production runs on a traditional three-roll machine.

"We had a lot of cylinders that we needed to make all at the same diameter," Smith recalled. "We realized pretty quickly that once we set the machine up, roll the form, and get it out that we could start bending these similar shapes quickly because of the CNC function."

In a four-roll machine, the rolls pinch the material and never let it go until the workpiece is rolled. The CNC stays on top of bending and tracks the material as it is rolled through the machine. It delivers repeatable results, as long as the material is somewhat similar.

Smith said that the Albina plate rolling experts liked that the MCB E30 delivers better rotation speeds than older machines and welcomed the control’s programmable options and recommended settings. Even the bright and user- friendly control interface is appreciated.

The more traditional three-roll plate rollers can deliver high-quality parts like the four-roll machines, but a bit more care is required. For example, when a three-roll machine releases the plate for an adjustment, the home position is lost. The machine is not going to remember where it began. Also, quality checks with these machines are often manual. Radius templates are commonly the quality tool of choice. When a template is not available, operators have to figure out the chord (the straight distance between two points on a curve) and the rise (the distance, perpendicular to the chord, between the midpoint of a chord and an arc) to determine if the form is meeting expectations.

"All of our operators are cross-trained throughout the roll section and are experts in the field," Smith said. "Some of our operators are also cross-trained in other bending disciplines, such as induction bending, increment bending, or rotary draw bending."

That expertise is evident when experienced operators have to interact with the newer plate rolling technology. They recognize slight delays that come with using software. Typically, when these operators want to make an adjustment in a roll, it’s release and go.

Despite the familiarity with the three-roll technology, the operators recognized how the four-roll machine could help out with high-volume jobs, those with at least 100 similar cylindrical shapes. That’s why Albina ended up buying the machine that originally was just a standby. It also bought the MCO 3034 plate roller it had originally ordered but had to wait for. Goose (MCB E30) and Jester (MCO 3034) joined the Albina family. An MCP 11 pyramid roll machine for smaller structural members and hollow structural sections rounded out the company’s "Top Gun" family when it arrived and was given the name Viper.

Not Stopping With Advanced Technology

Albina’s 40 shop floor workers have demonstrated a comfort in taking on new technology, so it shouldn’t be a surprise that they would gravitate to a new way of plate rolling. All a visitor has to do is take a stroll through one of the buildings and notice everyone working with an iPad. It’s a communication tool that’s at the heart of making the shop floor work.

With a custom application developed just for Albina that connects to the production ERP system, an employee can call up production notes, drawings, and work instructions. The devices are even used for messaging, such as requesting attendance at a meeting.

Workers are using the iPads to provide information for internal systems. They log their hours and document the status of jobs.

That information is then used to provide information for customers, who access a portal to find out more about their specific jobs. Not only will they find the status of the work, they’ll also find pictures that have been taken by machine operators on the Albina shop floor.

Meanwhile, those same iPads provide workers with access to production metrics, which influence profit-sharing. It’s the full access that deskbound workers normally get via a personal computer, but in the form of a much more mobile tablet computing device that can be brought anywhere in the plant.

Something that might look a little more futuristic on the Albina shop floor is an employee wearing a set of augmented reality goggles. But there is no gaming taking place. It’s the company’s latest quality tool.

Wearing a Microsoft HoloLens, an employee can walk over to a workpiece and see if it meets the job specifications. The FabStation software is able to take the 3D model and overlay it on the actual workpiece. The person wearing the goggles is able to see if the workpiece falls within the highlighted 3D model’s parameters.

Smith said the company first started using the quality tool in March, and it made an immediate impact. A workpiece that had come straight out of the roller was identified as being way out of spec once it was given a review by a quality inspector using the augmented reality tool. When the job parameters were compared to the original work order, the team found out that incorrect information was plugged into the machine. Albina avoided the hassle of having a bad piece being shipped to a customer and then dealing with the aftermath.

In terms of future investments, Smith added that Albina would be looking for automation to free up skilled employees from redundant tasks. He wants them to do what they do best: create forms in metal plate that bring satisfaction to artists and architects alike.
