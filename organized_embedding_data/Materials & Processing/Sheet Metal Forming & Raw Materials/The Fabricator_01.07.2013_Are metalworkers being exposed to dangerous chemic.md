Another prominent chemical ingredient in metalworking fluids is up for review by a federal agency. This time it is diethanolamine (DEA). DEA is an ingredient that is a surfactant and corrosion inhibitor found in metalworking fluids. It also is a potential skin irritant for workers who have been sensitized by exposure to water-based metalworking fluids.

The National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health (NIOSH) plans to scour the latest data to determine whether workers who

inhale

DEA are at high risk for cancer. An affirmative finding could lead to a permissible exposure limit (PEL) imposed by the Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA). Currently there is no PEL, but the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) already has some limits on ground, water, and air contamination by DEA.

NIOSH has a 35-year-old recommended exposure limit (REL) for DEA at 3 parts per million (PPM) as a time-weighted average (TWA) concentration for up to a 10-hour work shift during a 40-hour workweek. When it was first established in 1988, it was hard to detect concentrations below 3 PPM. Analytical methods have improved vastly since then, however.

The American Conference of Governmental Industrial Hygienists’ threshold limit value is 1 mg/m

3

, with a skin notation (indicating danger of cutaneous absorption) and an A3 carcinogenicity classification, which means that DEA is a confirmed animal carcinogen with unknown relevance to humans.

But NIOSH has no current data on the number of metalworking employees breathing in DEA. Its most recent figures are from 1981-83 and are based on a National Occupational Exposure Survey. That data found about 800,000 workers—many of them metalworkers—were inhaling DEA in the workplace.

Of course, metalworking fluids that contain DEA also contain other ingredients, such as biocides, chlorinated compounds, metals, sulfur compounds, and nitrites. As a result, it can be tough to estimate the health effects from breathing in DEA based on exposure to metalworking fluids.

The National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health has older data that suggests 800,000 workers—many of them metalworkers—were inhaling diethanolamine in the workplace.

Chemical Safety Reform Likely to Pass

The recent death of Sen. Frank Lautenberg, D-N.J., who had a keen interest in protecting the public from toxic chemicals, actually might lead to updated legislation that he had been trying to enact since 2005.

Lautenberg had long been interested in updating requirements under the Toxic Substances Control Act (TSCA). He and others believe TSCA, passed in 1976, does not allow the EPA to do enough to check the safety of new chemicals coming on the market, or restrict the use of chemicals already on the market. Amazingly, that law has never been amended, which is to say modernized.

At the end of May, Lautenberg, just weeks before his death, announced a bipartisan agreement in the Senate on behalf of his Chemical Safety Improvement Act. Ten Democrats, including Lautenberg, and nine Republicans co-sponsored the bill.

Environmentalists considered the bill to be a somewhat watered-down version of the legislation that Lautenberg introduced in the last Congress. That bill was called the Safe Chemicals Act.

In addition to bipartisan support, the new legislation has the support from the main chemical industry lobby, the American Chemistry Council, and from at least one influential environmental group, the Environmental Defense Fund.

Lautenberg’s passing makes it all but certain this bill will pass the Senate, and it is a good bet that the Republican-controlled House will follow suit.
