# Trump talking tariffs

[TARİH: 01.01.2025 The Fabricator]

Around Washington

Mexico appears to be a major target for the trade penalties

STEPHEN BARLAS

"Imports of Mexican steel are now surging far in excess of historical levels of trade. Imports of Mexican steel conduit have soared to around 500% over the 2015-2017 baseline and may rise to 700% this year."

— Letter from U.S. senators to President Joe Biden in September 2024

P

resident-elect Donald Trump has promised to go on a tariff binge upon arriving at the White House on Jan. 20. Trump has threatened to impose tariffs of 25% on both Canada and Mexico, who happen to be the No. 1 and No. 3 exporters of steel to the U.S., based on statistics from the U.S. International Trade Administration.

Canada exported 5.9 million metric tons of steel to the U.S. in 2024 through November, followed by Brazil at 4.1 million metric tons and Mexico at 3.2 million metric tons. More than half of all aluminum imports in 2024 came from Canada, 2.8 million metric tons out of 5 million metric tons. Mexico was a small aluminum player, ranking way behind countries such as India, Korea, and China.

In almost all instances, Canada and Mexico have no tariffs applied to steel and aluminum sent to the U.S. This is the result of the free trade agreement Trump signed with those two countries in 2020. But the new president apparently has a bee in his bonnet about illegal immigration and fentanyl reaching the U.S. from those two countries. In addition, he also is unhappy about Chinese steel slipping into the U.S. untariffed via Mexico, a complaint that the previous administration had as well.

The U.S.-Mexico-Canada Agreement (USMCA) contains a provision for a joint review starting July 1, 2026. During his presidential campaign, Trump pledged he would renegotiate some aspects of the USMCA: "Upon taking office, I will formally notify Mexico and Canada of my intention to invoke the six-year renegotiation provision of the USMCA that I put in."

But Trump won’t necessarily have to wait until July 1, 2026, before slapping new tariffs on either Canada or Mexico. Generally, Congress has to approve new tariffs. But it has delegated authority for the president to impose tariffs in three types of scenarios: threats to national security, threats to domestic industry, and during national emergencies.

Trump theoretically could declare that imported steel poses a threat to the domestic steelmaking industry and impose a 25% tariff on Mexican steel, despite the USMCA being in force. In early December, the Steel Manufacturers Association (SMA) published an agenda it hopes Trump will adopt. The SMA guidance includes not only 25% tariffs on Mexico but eliminating the tariff rate quotas President Joe Biden signed with the European Union, Britain, and Japan, allowing them to get out from under the Section 232 national security tariffs Trump imposed in 2018. With regard to Canada, the SMA said the Trump administration should "closely monitor imports." In addition, the SMA wants the administration to adopt a melted-and-poured requirement for all North American steel, including steel from Canada and Mexico, to benefit from duty-free treatment.

The likelihood of Trump leveling a 25% tariff on Canadian steel and aluminum seems remote. Even before Trump won election, Canada had made a move to clamp down on Chinese transshipment of steel. On Oct. 22, Canada imposed a 25% surtax on certain aluminum and steel products, specifically iron and non-alloy steel and stainless steel, produced in China and imported into Canada.

Mexican steel imports are a much bigger political irritant. Last September, a bipartisan group of U.S. senators, including incoming Secretary of State Marco Rubio, wrote to President Biden urging "… additional administrative action to stop Mexico’s unfair steel surge in violation of the 2019 Joint Statement on Section 232 Duties on Steel and Aluminum."

That 2019 statement, completed before the USMCA went into effect, eliminated Mexico from the 25% tariffs on steel Trump imposed on all countries in 2018.

But that agreement said Mexico had to keep its steel exports to the U.S. at historical levels. The September letter from the senators to Biden said: "Imports of Mexican steel are now surging far in excess of historical levels of trade. Imports of Mexican steel conduit have soared to around 500% over the 2015-2017 baseline and may rise to 700% this year."

Last July, the Biden administration did re-impose a 25% tariff on Mexican steel melted or poured outside of North America.

"While this was an important step, it is insufficient because it did not apply tariffs to any steel melted and poured in Mexico itself and subsequently affected only 13% of total steel imports from Mexico," the senators wrote.
