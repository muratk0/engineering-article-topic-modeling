# Metals Prices Continue to Lag

[TARİH: 01.06.2015 The Fabricator]

Metals News

Steelmakers look for more international demand to boost business

By

MetalPrices.com

staff

The better part of a year of weaker prices in steel appears to be reversing course as the second quarter wraps up.

After falling by almost a third since late last summer, hot-rolled steel prices partially reversed direction this spring. High steel service center inventory levels have started thinning; steel production levels have started to creep up; and higher oil prices have stimulated some return of tubular production. High steel product import levels remain an unresolved issue, however.

Pricing stability, albeit at a lower level, could become the theme for the ferrous scrap market in the third quarter. In May, domestic flat-rolled steelmakers said their order books were stronger and raised steel prices by about $20/net ton. Some believe mills also are hoping to end the steel price erosion that dragged hot-rolled coil prices down to lows of around $440/net ton from more than $600/net ton at the end of last year.

Iron ore and other steel feedstock prices have yet to show much recovery, however, with prices for seaborne iron ore in Asia trending below $50/ton in early April after trading above $100/ton in the past year.

Stability on the pricing front will depend on scrap availability. Electric-arc furnace sheet steelmakers breathed new life into the moribund industrial steel scrap market in May by reaching out to dealers throughout the Midwest and Northeast, buying substantial tonnage for their mills in the South. This was driven by the recent uptick in orders for sheet products. No. 1 busheling, which is normally priced at a premium over shredded scrap, sold at the same price or lower throughout the second quarter. Rebar producers and other long-products mills began using busheling as a substitute for scarce supplies of shredded scrap.

If sheet demand continues at the current pace, industrial scrap prices could reestablish their price superiority over obsolete scrap grades though both heavy melt and shredded remain in tight supply. When scrap prices plunged in February, auto wreckers, demolition contractors, and other key suppliers of scrap held material from the market. Since then they have sold only what they generated each month, with nothing sold from their inventories. This last-in, first-out sales approach has helped them avoid losses on the material purchased earlier when scrap prices were higher.

Most mill buyers and brokers assumed that obsolete scrap supplies would rise when warmer weather arrived, but the spring scrap spurt has not produced much volume and has created regional imbalances in scrap supplies. The domestic scrap price decline also has sparked stronger offshore demand. Turkish mills have bought steadily from U.S. exporters who, in turn, have raised their buying prices for heavy melt and other obsolete grades. U.S. mills along the East and Gulf Coasts face stiffer competition for that scrap now, and this is expected to possibly continue in the third quarter.

Some believe mills also are hoping to end the steel price erosion that dragged hot-rolled coil prices down to lows of around $440/net ton from more than $600/net ton at the end of last year.

The steep scrap price drops in the first quarter also awoke the dormant containerized scrap export market on the U.S.’s East and Gulf Coasts. Buyers from India, Malaysia, and several other scrap-poor Asian steel-making countries had to endure lengthy delays in shipments from the U.S. West Coast ports because of a labor dispute there. But with lower prices in other U.S. regions, offshore buyers discovered they could buy from other coasts at competitive prices. These containerized scrap export sales siphoned off any excess supply of shredded along the East and Gulf Coasts.

The Nonferrous Side of Metal

China’s failure to stimulate demand continues to be a major problem for metals and particularly for aluminum as Chinese government actions to encourage economic growth often conflict with other economic plans.

The government reduced bank minimum cash deposit requirements to release money into the economy, but instead of sending excess funds to capital expenditures, the money went into the Chinese stock market. Simultaneously the government reduced taxes on metal exports, which will put more aluminum into an already well-supplied market.

China has announced shutdowns of some aluminum production, mostly at older, nonproductive facilities, but the country is also expanding more modern facilities. There is no indication that a significant stimulus to increase China’s demand for metals has emerged at this time or will be seen into at least the early part of the third quarter.

Other aluminum business also has been slow with P1020 ingot, T-bars, and sows flooding the market as worldwide premiums fall each week. Merchants believe this will continue, probably into the third quarter. China has been exporting P1020 in semi-fabricated form throughout the world, and until that production is consumed, the market will remain in oversupply and prices are likely to get weaker.

The Middle East also continues to expand aluminum production in the face of a worldwide slowdown in business activity with a new megasmelter being built in the United Arab Emirates and a new rolling mill coming on line in Oman. The Al Taweelah facility of Emirates Global Aluminum and Oman Aluminum Rolling Company will be well-positioned when markets rebound.

In North America, aluminum markets continue limited development, but there is still no sign of aggressive demand from any sector. Aluminum suppliers saw a steep decline in the second quarter in Midwest aluminum premiums, and there is no end in sight for further slippage as the market remains oversupplied with metal. Prime suppliers have been selling at discounts to the Midwest premium, and this may continue until the market finds a bottom.

Scrap suppliers also are faced with an oversupply issue. While consumer buying is consistent, suppliers remain stuck with excess volumes after monthly contracts have been fulfilled. Mills continue to buy at their leisure in the spot market without being overly aggressive. Several mills have commented that they see order books remaining flat and therefore do not need to change any of their current purchasing directives.

European markets recorded mixed business levels in aluminum and other metals during the first quarter, and the going should remain slow through the late second quarter and into the third quarter. A couple of months after Europe initiated quantitative easing, the euro has only just started to reduce the U.S. dollar’s steady strength. Europe’s commercial building sector has increased demand for extrusions, and the auto sector is strong. Car sales have been stronger for high-value models rather than smaller, fuel-efficient vehicles.

Copper’s small price gain in April seems to have stalled as the dollar met resistance from the euro. European copper business continues at decent levels with smelters and refineries finding a full pipeline of raw material and are currently purchasing for September delivery. Financial results for Germany’s Aurubis AG have been good as consumers increase margins. Premiums at Rotterdam remain in the $30-$50/ton range, with Russian material readily available to supplement homegrown cathodes.

China remains slow in buying copper scrap while cathodes are selling in China at the $80/ton premium level. There is no indication that copper business levels will change in the coming weeks, and prices will be influenced mostly by the movement of the dollar.

Prospects for China’s imports of copper and brass scrap have been bleak. Labor costs have increased, and Chinese employers are not willing to pay more workers to hand-sort imported scrap. Theft from sealed containers is also on the rise, and the police are said to have turned their heads instead of taking on criminals and organized crime rings.

In North America, many believe that trading will continue to hover above the floor but below the ceiling for some time in copper and brass. Most in the industry predict that Comex copper will be in the $2.80-$3/lb. range for the short term. With the weaker dollar, lower oil prices, and China’s recent third interest rate cut, many are just crossing their fingers that business will pick up.

Nickel had a gain of $0.89/lb. in April, again primarily because of the dollar’s strength. European mills have started to buy available scrap to fill some new orders, and no European scrap is currently moving to the U.S. However, China seems to be nowhere close to the challenge of increasing demand, and the outlook calls for flat supply and demand. Cutbacks in mine production also may affect nickel supply late in the second quarter or early third quarter.

Overall, the dollar dictated the first quarter, and a stronger euro at the end of April may impact prices. A demand increase in China and a stronger U.S. economy are needed to have a solid positive metals move.
