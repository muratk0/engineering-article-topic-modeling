# U.S. sees momentum in trade deals

Around Washington

EU, India, and Taiwan seek to have tariffs reduced

By

Omar S. Nashashibi

E

uropean Parliament political groups have reached a compromise agreement to move forward with legislation implementing the EUU. S. trade framework, including possibly mandating that the U.S. reduce tariffs on steel and aluminum to 15% or face a return of tariffs on U.S. industrial goods.

The agreement among the European parties clears the way for a series of votes and negotiations expected to unfold through 2026. Lawmakers added new safeguards to the deal, including a March 2028 sunset clause for the broader U.S.-EU agreement and the steel and aluminum reduction.

Under the compromise, the European Parliament’s trade committee was scheduled to vote on Feb. 24, followed by a plenary vote in March. If approved, negotiations with EU member states would begin in the spring, with the agreement potentially entering into force by late spring or summer 2026. The deal provides the U.S. with six months after the implementation of this European demand to reduce tariffs on covered steeland aluminum-related goods to a 15% level or risk EU countermeasures.

U.S., INDIA RELEASE INTERIM TRADE FRAMEWORK

The U.S. and India released an interim trade framework on Feb. 6 outlining a path toward easing bilateral trade barriers while leaving several key tariff changes subject to future implementation. The framework, described by both governments as a step toward a broader bilateral trade agreement, sets out sector-specific commitments and signals areas where tariff relief may eventually be provided. Under the framework, the U.S. has agreed in principle to apply a lower reciprocal tariff rate of 18% on Indian imports, down from the current 25% level.

Also on Feb. 6, President Donald Trump issued an executive order suspending the additional 25% ad valorem tariff that had been imposed on most Indian goods in August 2025. That surcharge, adopted under national emergency authorities in response to U.S. concerns over India’s purchases of Russian oil, was applied on top of existing duties. The executive order removes that Russia-related surcharge effective Feb. 7, but does not implement the lower reciprocal tariff rate contemplated in the trade framework.

The joint framework identifies several sectors—including textiles, apparel, footwear, chemicals, plastics, select machinery, and artisanal goods—expected to benefit from improved market access as negotiations continue. It also indicates that future negotiations could result in zero-tariff treatment for certain products, such as generic pharmaceuticals, aircraft parts, and gems and diamonds.

The framework and reports from administration officials also suggest that targeted tariff relief may be forthcoming for some Indian steel and copper products currently subject to U.S. national-security-related tariffs. However, specific details have not yet been released.

In exchange, India has committed to reduce or eliminate tariffs on a range of U.S. industrial and agricultural exports and to address selected nontariff barriers. The two governments also highlighted cooperation on energy trade, supply chains, and defense and technology ties as part of the broader relationship.

U.S. and Indian officials said additional guidance is expected in the coming weeks as implementation steps are developed, including clarification on when and how the lower reciprocal tariff rate and any sector-specific tariff relief would be applied.

U.S., TAIWAN CONCLUDE TRADE DEAL

The U.S. and Taiwan formally signed a new trade and investment agreement on Jan. 15 that pairs targeted tariff reductions with a major push to expand advanced manufacturing capacity in the U.S.

On the trade side, the agreement lowers the U.S. reciprocal tariff on most Taiwanese imports from 20% to 15%, aligning Taiwan with tariff levels applied to several other Asia-Pacific trading partners. It also establishes zero-duty treatment for a defined list of products considered strategically important or not produced domestically, including generic pharmaceuticals and active ingredients, aerospace and aircraft components, and certain raw materials.

The centerpiece of the agreement, however, is a large-scale investment framework aimed at strengthening U.S. technology supply chains. Taiwanese semiconductor and technology companies have committed to invest at least $250 billion in U.S.-based facilities. Taiwan’s government will back these investments in chip fabrication, advanced manufacturing, energy technologies, and artificial intelligence with up to $250 billion in credit guarantees, creating a combined financing capacity of as much as $500 billion.

According to U.S. officials, the agreement builds on broader efforts to restore domestic semiconductor manufacturing leadership by encouraging onshore production, supporting workforce development, and reinforcing research and development ecosystems tied to next-generation technologies. The framework also reflects growing coordination on supply chain security and technology policy as semiconductors and related inputs play a larger role in economic and national security planning.

Taken together, the tariff adjustments and investment commitments position the agreement as both a trade deal and an industrial policy instrument, aimed at deepening economic ties between the U.S. and Taiwan while accelerating the expansion of advanced manufacturing capacity inside the U.S.
