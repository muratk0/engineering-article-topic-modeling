We hear all manner of assertions regarding the status of the economy and a wide range of opinions regarding whether the news is good or not so good. As with most things in economics, much depends on one’s perspective. Given that an election year is looming, there is even more pressure to spin the news to fit whatever narrative one wants to pursue. However, there is one consistent story: The U.S. is experiencing one of the longest periods of economic growth in modern history. This period of expansion has thus far lasted nearly 10 years (the longest period was from March 1991 to March 2001).

There have been two sets of reactions to this development. The optimists assert that this expansion is a sign that old patterns no longer apply and there is no reason to think that this cycle will end anytime soon. The pessimists assert that cycles die of old age, and this one is about due. Others assert that past patterns matter very little as every cycle of boom and bust is different. This cycle was in its eighth year when Trump took office and has obviously continued.

A couple of factors have played a rather unique role in this decade-long period of growth. The first is that most of this growth period has been anemic. Coming out of a recession is usually a rapid process — the economy goes down fast and rebounds fast. That is the "V-shaped" recession. A delay in recovery is represented by a "U-shaped" graph as the economy lingers in the doldrums for a while. This recovery has been described as a "check mark," as there was a swift decline followed by an excruciatingly long recovery.

THE U.S. IS EXPERIENCING ONE OF THE LONGEST PERIODS OF ECONOMIC GROWTH IN MODERN HISTORY.

The second aspect of this boom to note is that progress was not all that well distributed. Many sectors of the economy have struggled and never really participated in the growth. The agricultural sector was a net loser, and many retail sectors fell on hard times. Job growth has been solid, but the majority of these jobs were in the lower-paid service categories. Despite 10 years of a boom cycle, wage gains were few, and most people experienced income stagnation. The point is that a boom cycle is subjective — some see real benefits and some don’t.
