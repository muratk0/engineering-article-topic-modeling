That’s the $64,000 question, or, adjusted for inflation and COVID-19, the multitrillion-dollar question. In his mid-May

Fabrinomics

e-newsletter, Chris Kuehl, economic analyst for the Fabricators & Manufacturers Association, described three potential scenarios that could play out over the next two years.

The worst case predicts a second wave that wreaks havoc on the global economy all over again. The best case has COVID-19 evolving into a "slow burn" disease with minimal economic impact (though "minimal" is a very relative term). The third scenario is a balance of the two: The next two years will reveal a series of localized, intermittent outbreaks that lead to peaks and valleys of economic activity, depending on the supply chain and geographic region. Kuehl calls this the most likely scenario.

Fortunately, the metal fabrication business knows a thing or two about peaks and valleys. In custom and contract fabrication in particular, being flexible in the face of demand variability is just part of the game.

"As we’ve proven time and again over the years, our agility and adaptability, coupled with our commitment to succeed by providing service, quality, and reliability to our customers, along with a strong balance sheet, are what will help us weather this storm, grow our market position, and come out stronger on the other side over the long term."

So said Bob Kamphuis, president and CEO of Mayville, Wis.-based MEC, during its first-quarter earnings call in early May. The $500-million-plus contract fabricator has been No. 1 on

The FABRICATOR

’s FAB 40 list (published this month) for many years.

MEC’s customer base touches many parts of the economy, from defense to commercial trucking, agriculture, packaging, and more. During the May earnings call, company leaders said that customer forecasts were changing and order volumes were dropping. Still, not one MEC customer had canceled its contract.

"While we lack the usual level of certainty from our customers, we still have visibility," Kamphuis told investors, "and in the near term our orders are following production schedules. No contracts or customer relationships have been lost. That means that when our customer volumes come back, our volumes will come back with them, and we are ready to service their needs. … Overall, we see growing outsourcing opportunity as a result of reshoring efforts by our OEM customers to manufacture and source products and components where they are sold, and avoid supply chain disruption. The pandemic has placed preexisting supply chain disruptions directly in the spotlight, and we will benefit from that."

The more U.S. fabricators can help their customers reduce the risk of supply chain disruption, the larger the market pie could grow.

Concerns about supply chain disruptions have reached the halls of Congress, made evident by a provision buried deep in the U.S. Coronavirus Aid, Relief, and Economic Security (CARES) Act. Specifically, the act directs the National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine to report on the U.S. supply chain to judge the nation’s dependence on health care devices and technologies sourced outside the U.S.

"Throughout our history we’ve always attempted to ensure that a core industrial base exists within certain industries," said Curt Hearn, a partner at New Orleans-based Jones Walker who has studied the CARES Act at length. "Think aircraft manufacturing or shipbuilding. Many are now coming to a conclusion that we should be doing the same type of analysis from a pharmaceutical, medical device, and PPE perspective … If you own a company that is capable of performing highly sophisticated fabrication, you might have opportunities now that in the past were sent overseas."

The verbiage in the CARES Act serves a nationalistic and moral purpose. No one wants people to die because a supply chain can’t deliver. But it also has an underlying motive that all businesses share: risk mitigation. A global supply chain might give a product the lowest total landed costs, but how risky is it? Can suppliers deliver as needed at the peak? Do they have revenue diversity and a healthy balance sheet to weather a deep valley?

This might be why many fabricators have rosy long-term outlooks, despite the uncertainty of 2020. They’re set to manage the demand peaks and valleys that FMA’s Kuehl predicts as the U.S. economy stumbles out of the current mess. The best fabricators help customers mitigate supply chain risks, smoothing out those peaks and valleys into manageable terrain.

The U.S. might be headed toward a "jagged V" economic recovery as different market sectors react to varying customer demands and, especially, changing policies and restrictions from state and local governments. The recovery could take a different shape—like a U, W, or L—but whatever shape it eventually takes, it’s likely to be jagged.

When will the jaggedness stop? When will demand trends smooth? When can we all go back to normal? It’ll probably be awhile—maybe years, and for some business sectors, maybe never. Thing is, fabricators are used to the jaggedness. The best operations—equipped with the latest cutting, bending, and welding technology and automation—have moved in sync with those jagged lines of variable demand for years. The variability now has just been turned up by a notch (or many notches).

What will the recovery look like for fabricators? Some will gain market share, others will lose it, and some won’t survive. But the fabricators who ride the peaks and valleys might even see the market pie get bigger. The more U.S. fabricators can help their customers reduce the risk of supply chain disruption, the larger that market pie could grow.
