# 10 trends metal fabricators should watch

[TARİH: 01.01.2026 The Fabricator]

Fabrinomics | Economic Insights

New Year brings new opportunities, uncertainties

Dr. Chris Kuehl

As I write this in early December, we are still gasping our way through the data desert, but there seems to be an oasis on the horizon. Much of the economic observation will be off for a while yet. In the meantime, I’ve included 10 trends FMA members can monitor as they plan a path forward into 2026.

1. GDP Growth Will Remain Steady—Probably.

First, I’ll give my usual disclaimer. The U.S. is big—really big. The U.S. GDP is larger than the GDPs of China, Japan, Germany, and the U.K. combined, and that means every state compares to a country in terms of the economy. When national numbers are quoted on GDP growth or employment, they will often differ significantly from state and local numbers.

That said, the U.S. has been growing much faster than expected— close to 4.0% in Q3 and close to that in Q4. There are always a range of predictions regarding growth, as there are always changes in the variables. Most forecasts call for growth between 3.0% and 3.5% for the first two quarters of 2026, but some skeptics assert that the impact of tariffs will manifest and drag growth down to as low as 1.0%.

2. The Construction Market Has Uncertainty With Pockets of Opportunity.

Manufacturing construction is expected to increase 2.0% in 2025 and another 2.6% in 2026 as the semiconductor and advanced manufacturing boom continues. Even so, the industry faces some moderation, since construction activity is already near historic record highs—four times the volume seen before the pandemic.

The office sector continues struggling with 20% vacancy rates, though spending is projected to have increased 1% in 2025 with a 1.8% CAGR over the next five years. These new buildouts will not be the offices of old; they will be ripe with AV automation, be ready for robotics, and need infrastructure and security to handle the significant AI transformation.

Higher-ed and institutional facilities are the bright spot, with projected gains of 6.1% in 2025 and 3.8% in 2026. This sector is driven heavily by higher-ed’s need to innovate and change its teaching approach.

Health care construction is showing resilience, with 4.3% growth projected for 2026. Handling more than 22 million boomers who will hit retirement age in the next five years will create a long runway for growth in the sector. Meanwhile, the hotel and hospitality sector is expected to grow 3.0% in 2025 and 6.0% in 2026.

3. The Data Center Construction Boom Is Creating Unprecedented Demand.

Data center construction remains the highest-growth segment, with AI infrastructure driving explosive demand. The global data center market shows record-level construction activity, with AI-specific capacity expected to drive momentum throughout 2026.

Major projects in motion include the Stargate AI Project, which requires 7-gigawatt (GW) power capacity across multiple states and a $400 billion investment; Anthropic, a $50 billion investment in Texas and New York facilities, creating 800 permanent jobs and 2,400 construction jobs through 2026; Microsoft Wisconsin, a $7 billion total investment with a second facility coming online by 2026; Meta, with its $1.5 billion GW-scale facility in Texas launching in 2028; and Amazon, investing $20 billion in its Pennsylvania AI campuses and another $10 billion in its North Carolina facilities.

These facilities demand the specialized AV integration, network infrastructure, security systems, access control, surveillance, and operational technology that systems integrators provide. Power demand from AI data centers could grow.

The sector requires sophisticated integration capabilities; security expertise; and the ability to handle complex, large-scale deployments.

4. Material Cost Pressures and Tariff Volatility Are Mounting.

The current tariff regime is creating significant uncertainty: 25% tariffs on steel and aluminum from all sources and 25% tariffs on Canadian goods including lumber (up from 8.6%). Tariffs are estimated to affect about 7% of homebuilding materials, potentially raising average construction costs by $6,400 per house. Structural steel, aluminum, glazing systems, and processed materials all face elevated costs.

Meanwhile, supply chain disruptions are leading to port delays and longer lead times for specialty components. Supplier diversification efforts are creating scheduling challenges. To accommodate, companies are implementing robust escalation clauses in contracts, diversifying supplier relationships, and building longer lead times into project schedules.

5. Supply Chain Fragility Persists.

While general-purpose components show stability, key segments tied to AI infrastructure and memory remain tight. The electronics supply chain faces persistent challenges heading into 2026. Lead times for certain chips are extending to between 18 and 30 weeks. And the geopolitical factors abound, with U.S.-China tensions, export controls, and potential semiconductor tariffs creating additional uncertainty.

6. Labor Shortages Are Intensifying Across the Technical Trades.

The worker shortage continues unabated. Fabricators compete for labor from other trades, of course, and in certain trades, wages are climbing dramatically.

Average hourly earnings in construction have risen 4.4% over the past year, significantly outpacing other industries. Electricians now command $40.41/hour on average, with a 5% increase in Q3 2025 alone, driven by data center and electrification demand.

Immigration issues have exacerbated the situation. Nearly one-third of companies report that immigration enforcement efforts have impacted their workforce availability, and workforce shortages are cited as the leading cause of project delays.

The current unemployment rate is still very low—4.3% at the U-3 level. The U-6 rate has moved up a bit, from 7.9% to 8.1%. This rate, which includes the so-called "discouraged worker" and the involuntary part-timer, generally is seen as a more accurate assessment of the job situation.

There have been more layoff announcements of late, but the reality is that there is still a serious labor shortage in many industries, and that has inhibited companies that might otherwise think about reducing their workforce numbers.

For fabricators, labor constraints will limit project capacity, increase subcontractor costs, and extend project timelines. Shops must invest in training, apprenticeship programs, competitive compensation, and retention strategies.

7. Interest Rates Fall but Remain Elevated.

The Fed cut rates several times in 2025. Still, Fed Chairman Powell mentioned that rate cuts were not "a foregone conclusion," considering the lack of data after the government shutdown and concerns about inflation. Despite this, Goldman Sachs still expects additional cuts in March and June of this year, perhaps reaching 3.25% or even less.

While rates are declining from peaks, they remain high enough to constrain commercial real estate investment and project starts. Interest- rate-sensitive sectors like office and multifamily homes will continue facing financing challenges. However, infrastructure and institutional projects are less affected.

8. Inflation Remains Elevated Despite Moderation.

U.S. inflation the Federal Reserve’s 2% target, though down from pandemic-era peaks. Alternative estimates suggest headline CPI remains steady at 3% for October and November. Unfortunately, approximately 55% of the CPI basket items are rising faster than 3%, indicating broad-based inflation pressure despite headline moderation.

9. Health Care and Education Infrastructure Investments Are Accelerating.

Health care remains one of the strongest construction sectors, with multiple mega-projects underway. Acute-care hospital construction remains the top project type, with renewed momentum after pandemic delays.

Meanwhile, technology spending in education is on track to exceed $404 billion globally by 2025, expanding at 16% CAGR. K-12 education technology spending reached $30 billion in 2024 and is projected to nearly double by 2033.

10. Recession Risk and Economic Uncertainty Cloud the 2026 Outlook.

There are conflicting perspectives on the risk of a mild recession. Many data points suggest that the U.S. is headed for one; these include the recession probability index, 10/2 bond inversion trend, and the Conference Board Leading Index.

Economic forecasts show elevated recession risk. For instance, UBS cited a 93% recession probability based on hard data indicators; J.P. Morgan cited a 40% probability of a U.S. and global recession. Meanwhile, the Bankrate Survey pegged the chance of recession at only 39% by September 2026. The UCLA Anderson Forecast said that recovery is not expected until mid-2026, with a "stagflation-lite" scenario possible.

Kali9/iStock/Getty Images Plus

Warning signs abound. Job gains slowed significantly in 2025, unemployment is expected to climb to 4.6% by September 2026, and average monthly job growth is projected at just 49,000 (less than half the current pace).

Economic hurdles also abound. We have tariff impacts on imports and manufacturing, fall 2025’s government shutdown disrupting data releases and creating uncertainty, policy uncertainty affecting business investment decisions, and slowing global growth.

On the positive side, though, we have a deregulation-supporting business environment, additional fiscal stimulus (though inflationary), and the potential for Fed rate cuts providing monetary support. Also, infrastructure spending remains resilient.

Economic uncertainty requires defensive positioning: strengthening balance sheets, maintaining credit availability, diversifying customer base across sectors, and focusing on sectors with funding support (infrastructure, health care, education). However, a full recession is not the consensus view. Most expect slow growth rather than collapse.
