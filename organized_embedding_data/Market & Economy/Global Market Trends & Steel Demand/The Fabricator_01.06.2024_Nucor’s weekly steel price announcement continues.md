# Nucor’s weekly steel price announcement continues to rattle markets

[TARİH: 01.06.2024 The Fabricator]

Steel News

Could this transparency in pricing lead to more stability in the domestic steel market?

Michael Cowden

W

hen I attended the Boy Scouts Metals Industry Dinner in Chicago in April, one of the big topics of discussion was Nucor’s new weekly spot hot-rolled coil (HRC) price announcements. It was big news then and remains so more than a month later.

Unless you’ve been under a rock, you know by now that Nucor’s published HRC price for the week of May 6 was $760/ton, down $65/ton from the company’s $825/ton a week earlier. I could use more colorful words, but I think it’s safe to say that most of the market was not expecting a decline of that magnitude at the time. It’s been a bit of a shock.

That’s not to say that the market hasn’t seen its share of shocks. We all got used to the idea that mills could announce price increases of as much as $50/ton to $100/ton on an almost weekly basis. February 2023 comes to mind. And it’s worth recalling, again, that U.S. sheet mills almost never announce price decreases. Increases, in contrast, are almost always public affairs. So a sheet price decrease still has the power to shock.

How disruptive was a big, published HRC decrease? Look at CME Group HRC futures. If you compare where things stood on Friday, May 3, with where they ended up on Monday, May 6, you would have seen steel futures drop across the board for the rest of 2024.

We saw something similar on April 8, the day when Nucor announced its first published price of $830/ton—or $70/ton below Cleveland-Cliffs’ list price at the time.

It wasn’t just the futures market that registered an event that was not "priced in." We’ve continued to see reverberations in the physical market too.

In late March (before Nucor’s first price announcement on April 8), approximately 36% of service centers were raising prices, 55% were holding them steady, and 9% were cutting prices. It looked like we were in for another upcycle in domestic HRC prices.

By mid-May, the script had flipped. Following Nucor’s two surprise price cuts, only 4% of service centers were still trying to raise prices. About 35% were trying to hold the line, and 61% were cutting prices along with mills. You’d have to go back to the UAW strike in 2023 to find an external shock on par with that.

Weekly Numbers That Adjust Monthly?

But let’s step back a minute and look at this from another perspective: Nucor’s weekly spot HRC price has, to date, functioned almost like a monthly price announcement.

For example, in early April, Nucor rolled out a $830/ton price, which was a lot lower than some had expected, especially with Cliffs at the time being at $900/ton. The price then stayed within $5/ton of that initial $830/ton announcement for the rest of the month—ending at $825/ton on April 29.

Then, on May 6, we saw another unexpected cut, down $65/ton to $760/ton. A week later, on May 13, the price was unchanged.

Is that what we should expect from Nucor going forward—a sharp move (up or down) on the first Monday of the month, followed by minor adjustments after that? And if that’s the case, what’s driving that pattern? Could it be scrap prices, which settle monthly, or Cliffs’ spot HRC price, which also updates once a month? Perhaps the target is contract discounts, also known as CRU-minus deals? (A CRU-minus contract is an agreement between the steel mill and end user or service center to use the CRU indices—which calculate the current spot index or average on various products—as the basis of their price negotiations. The two sides, mill and customer, then agree to an appropriate discount off of the CRU spot price.)

It’s early to draw any sweeping conclusions, but we can speculate.

If the goal was transparency, Nucor isn’t just paying lip service. The company seems to be delivering it with some street cred. It gets that credibility in part by very publicly acknowledging that steel prices not only rise but also fall. It also gets that by acknowledging not only where smaller spot tons are transacting, but also where bigger volumes and contracts are priced.

But if the goal was to reduce volatility, I’m not sure that’s being achieved, at least not in the short term. Nucor’s HRC price was arguably the primary driver of volatility in the first weeks of April and again in early May.

Why risk pushing uncertain customers to the sidelines and courting frustration over inventory devaluation? Perhaps it’s to better compete, over the longer term, with other domestic mills as well as with imports.

As

Figure 2

shows, even with U.S. prices dropping, domestic mills are hardly cheap compared to world prices. Also, given where ferrous scrap prices are, most mills are still making solid money with every ton sold.

There is also a case to be made that Nucor is trying to push more buyers back into spot buying in a domestic market that has arguably become tilted too far toward the contract side. How might you force the issues? Well, folks with CRU-minus contracts might have been paying less than spot buyers on May 3, but on May 6, some of those contract buyers might have found themselves paying more than a smaller spot buyer. At the very least, their contract discount was worth a lot less than it had been just a few days earlier.

What does that mean for the market? It will be interesting to see how this plays not only in the physical spot market, but also on the futures market and in contract negotiations. Also, will a lower price encourage more buying? I’m not sure. Some consumers bought heavy in March and are seeing slightly weaker demand. Are they going to jump back into the market for a cheaper price if they’ve got enough tons to tide them over for a month or two?

I wish I knew. Like many of you, right now I have more questions than answers.
