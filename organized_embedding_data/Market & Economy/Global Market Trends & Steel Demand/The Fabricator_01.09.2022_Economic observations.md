emember the mood ring? Those of a certain vintage will recollect this fad and the supposed ability of the ring to gauge one’s attitude. The reality is the thing shifted from gloom to elation all day. This is not a bad metaphor for the economic data we have been seeing of late. Those with a pessimistic outlook have plenty to worry about but those with a more upbeat outlook are finding ammunition as well.

There is no real need to review the negative elements at this stage. We have been inundated with news regarding record levels of inflation, the potential for much higher interest rates, the chronic labor shortage that keeps driving wages higher, and the ongoing energy crisis due to excluding Russia from the global oil market. We know full well why there are predictions of a recession in 2023 — over half the economists polled think one is due by the second quarter (third at the latest).

The bigger question is why there would be any optimism at all. The first indication can be found in the data collected by the Strategic Intelligence System itself. The overall manufacturing numbers are definitely slumping and are expected to be trending well below levels set prior to the pandemic but they are also expected to show a sharp rebound starting in the middle of 2023. A closer look at some of the sub-sectors indicates that much of that rebound will be led by automotive, aerospace, machinery, electronics, and electrical. The majority of the damage has been seen in the input categories such as primary and fabricated metals. That commodity crash can be attributed to issues such as supply chain and energy.

THE OUTLOOK FOR 2023 REMAINS IN FLUX DUE TO THE IMPACT OF THE SUPPLY CHAIN BREAKDOWN AND THE ONGOING ENERGY SECTOR CRISIS.

It is not just the Armada Strategic Intelligence System (ASIS) that shows some positive trends. Even though the latest Purchasing Managers’ Index is down from its levels last month, the numbers are at 53.0, and that still signals expansion. The level of capacity utilization was getting very close to what has been considered normal (between 80% and 85%) for months and has now hit that level with a reading of 80.8. The industrial production numbers dipped very slightly (by 0.1) but they had been up for six straight months prior to that. Corporate investment levels (cap-ex) remain near record highs.

Much of what has been driving the manufacturing sector stems from some of the big economic issues that have affected the global environment. The supply chain chaos in China has stimulated more reshoring and onshoring. Inventory issues have triggered companies to buy more and store more than was the norm before. The labor shortage issue is chronic and unlikely to be solved soon. That has encouraged companies to invest heavily in automation, technology, and robotics. Even the service sector is moving aggressively toward technology.

The outlook for 2023 remains in flux due to the impact of the supply chain breakdown and the ongoing energy sector crisis. Both of these issues have the potential to fade rapidly if China resumes normal production and if Russia is allowed back into the oil and gas world. These developments are not imminent but the possibility is there. The demand for manufactured output remains strong but input costs are hampering the sector as a whole. A shift here would result in lower levels of inflation and reduced risk of higher interest rates that could trigger a recession. If these input challenges are not addressed the potential for a downturn is greater.
