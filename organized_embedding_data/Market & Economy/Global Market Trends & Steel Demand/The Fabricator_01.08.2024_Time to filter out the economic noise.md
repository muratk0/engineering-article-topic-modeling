# Time to filter out the economic noise

[TARİH: 01.08.2024 The Fabricator]

Fabrinomics | Economic Insights

And focus on what really matters

Dr. Chris Kuehl

There is always a certain amount of "noise" when the subject is the economy. The definition of noise is a little loose, but basically it is hyperbolic information that is blown out of proportion to some degree.

There are lots of reasons for this behavior. The media likes drama and manufactures it, economists like to argue and scare people to some degree, and then there are the political motivations. These intensify in an election year as the incumbents seek to paint a rosy picture while their opponents portray the end of the world. How does anyone wade through all this to figure out what is actually going on? There are three techniques.

1. Trust the data.

This is a lot harder than it seems because data often tells contradictory stories. This forces you to understand what the data is actually saying. What is being measured, and is it being measured accurately? Look at data like the Consumer Price Index or the consumer confidence surveys. The CPI gets quoted a lot as it is released early each month, but it is based on assumptions. It is supposedly the buying patterns of an average family of four, and there are not that many of these around anymore. It is based on a mythical basket of goods this family buys, but if one gets the basket of goods and services wrong, it is not accurate. The Fed uses the Personal Consumption Expenditures (PCE) index because it shows what we actually bought, but that data often lags by a month or two. The consumer confidence survey asks people in a phone interview—a random sample asked five questions. Do these people have any real idea what is going on? Are they even being honest?

What is the motivation of the person or institution providing the information? The politician assures us that all is well if they are the ones in power while the opposition asserts everything is wrong. An economist working for a firm that does well when people are worried about the future will paint a bleak picture. Economists differ strenuously over values and outcomes. An economist oriented toward the worker will welcome higher wages and expanded job protections while the economist who favors investors and business owners will worry about wages that get too high and protections that hamper productivity. Everyone has a point of view, and that affects the conclusions they reach even when there is basic agreement on the data itself. Is low unemployment a good thing or bad? To the employee it is, but not to the employer.

3. Determine what actually matters to your business and community.

If there is a crisis in an industry or region that you have nothing to do with, you can basically ignore the problems. If you don’t import goods affected by a tariff, you need not lose sleep. If your region is booming, it really doesn’t affect you if another region isn’t. Some industries are little affected by recession, and some do not feel the pinch of inflation as much as others.

OK, So What Really Matters?

What’s important usually doesn’t grab headlines. A copper price report; news of the unprecedented demand for data centers and the electricity they require; shifting trends in automotive: These and other data-point breadcrumbs can lead to what’s truly important, especially to those running a manufacturing business.

What Is Going on with Copper Prices?

Last year, the markets looked at proposed production plans in the industry and realized they did not match up very well with expected demand. There has been an expected surge in the need for copper for EVs, the AI revolution, and everything else related to technology. This prompted the assumption that demand would greatly exceed supply, and this would drive up copper prices.

What happens when prices climb like this? Producers seize the opportunity to bring more copper to market in response. This rapid development reverses the expectation of a shortage, and suddenly there is an anticipation of excess supply. That convinces speculators to start dumping copper as they now think that prices have peaked and will only head lower.

These patterns are common in the metal market, and the impact drives users of these commodities crazy. Investors treat the metal markets the way they treat the equities or crypto markets. They try to outthink demand and supply. If they think a shortage is looming, they buy now so they can sell at a premium later. That triggers producers to boost output, and this reduces the chances of a shortage. The price then collapses as those same investors try to dump their holdings. The actual users of the commodity get caught by all this manipulation.

The EV and the Automotive Sector.

The major push toward electric vehicles (EVs) has met far more resistance than had been expected. This is due in part to some predicted challenges, but also to unexpected developments. It was always going to be a matter of convincing consumers to go electric, and that was going to depend on factors such as availability of charging stations. But one of the core assumptions has not proved to be accurate. It was predicted that gas prices would be in excess of $8 to $10 a gallon, and that would propel people to alternatives. Prices between $3.00 and $3.50 have dominated. Now the industry is reconsidering the hybrid. This was supposed to be a "transition" vehicle as people adopted the EV, but now consumers want the flexibility of a vehicle that can access both gasoline and electric power.

There has been a dip in the forecast for assemblies, although the numbers remain relatively healthy and stable. There was a fairly stable recovery after the slump during the pandemic recession, but that growth has started to fade. Thus far the numbers are still above the 20-year trend line, but the stall has many analysts digging into what exactly has changed.

Data Centers, Manufacturing, and Energy Production.

One area is booming and has only started to see radical expansion: Data centers are needed everywhere and in every industry. At a recent meeting of a company heavily engaged in the tech world, there were some astonishing and somewhat sobering predictions. To meet the proposed global need for computer capacity would require the entire GDP of the world. Accommodating the energy needs would require the construction of over 2,000 new power plants in the U.S. alone. The development would require the contents of Lake Superior. Obviously, this resource drain is not possible, but it suggests there will be a mad rush to be the developer that meets the needs first. AI development alone will drive over half the demand for data centers. The focus of many companies has been developing chips that hold more data than entire computers, and they’re doing this with less energy.

Some predict there will be a new data center built every single day by 2025. Many will be small and privately held by a specific company, but others will be vast complexes. The U.S. is deeply concerned about its chip vulnerability, and that has prompted the CHIPS Act to encourage more domestic development. The demand is truly exponential, so the issue is how to supply that demand and at what cost.

Data Drives Construction.

The data center is at the heart of just about every sector these days, and that has been reflected by the stunning demand—including construction. Residential construction has struggled to keep pace with changes in mortgage rates and home prices, but nonresidential activity has been encouraged by two areas: (1) medical and (2) the overall transportation and logistics area.

Nonresidential activity has exploded and is only now slowing a bit. It is still far above the 20-year trend line, however. The transportation sector has never been more technology-dependent, and it is no accident that data center development has coincided with the development of logistics centers. The other fast growing construction sector has been medical, and the data demand here is stunning.

Manufacturers have been on the cutting edge of technology, robotics, and automation for years. Not only has manufacturing contributed to the demand for data centers (and everything else that accompanies tech), but they have been instrumental in providing the support needed to construct these facilities. The most robust sector for construction has been demand from manufacturing—a 73% gain in 2023 alone.

Follow the Data

These points, and others like them, might help manufacturers filter out the noise and find new ways to compete in a complicated world—one in which nations that once provided cheap consumer goods are now capable of producing high-level equipment as well as high-level service activity.

How do nations maintain their economic systems in the wake of these competitive realities? The simplest strategy has been to impose tariffs, quotas, and other trade restrictions, but these often backfire. The long-term solution will involve, as it always has, training a globally competitive, skilled workforce—one that can filter out the noise and focus on what’s important.
