# Ah, Volatility, We Missed You So

[TARİH: 01.03.2018 The Fabricator]

By Dr. Chris Kuehl

In fact, we really haven’t. It has been so nice to see the markets so placid, so reliable. It was an assurance that all was well. So much for that fantasy. The last few weeks have reacquainted us with the fact that market volatility is the norm. What triggered it this time?

Concern regarding inflation has been increasing steadily – even though there has been very little to suggest there was anything to be concerned about. Commodity prices have generally been down, and upward wage pressure has been minimal. There was concern nevertheless, as it just seemed that there should be inflation by this point. It’s always been this way in the past, so why not now? That is the starting point for this latest round of volatility – it has been expected to take place anyday now.

In the last couple of weeks it seemed we finally had a r eason to actually worry. The factor that appeared to trigger the biggest response was the jobs report on Feb. 2. Finally the evidence showed that wages were going up in response to the low rate of unemployment, and that missing piece of the inflation puzzle may be enough for the Fed to start hiking rates more aggressively than had been planned. Remember, the Fed has been touting three hikes this year even before the evidence of wage inflation.

In addition to the wage issue is the commodity pressure, with higher prices for oil and metals and a variety of other inputs. Already the airlines have made statements suggesting that fares will rise, and other transportation companies are pointing toward fuel surcharge fees again. The level of capacity utilization is getting close to normal, and if it gets above 85 percent, shortages and bottlenecks will be likely, and this is inflationary as well.

Inflation spooks investors and the financial community as it means higher rates by the Fed as it tries to keep the economy from overheating. The tax cuts came at a not-very-helpful moment as they goosed an already growing economy, and that sets up a counter-reaction to slow the economy as a way to minimize overheating.
