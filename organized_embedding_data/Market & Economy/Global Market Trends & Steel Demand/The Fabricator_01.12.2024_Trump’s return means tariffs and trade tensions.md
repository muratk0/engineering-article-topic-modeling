# Trump’s return means tariffs and trade tensions

[TARİH: 01.12.2024 The Fabricator]

Steel News

New administration results in a lot of unanswered questions

Michael Cowden

P

resident-elect Donald Trump will officially retake the White House on Jan. 20. The question now is how his administration’s policies might reshape the steel industry and domestic manufacturing.

I covered Trump’s nontraditional first term: Section 232, Section 301, and USMCA. That was just on the trade policy side of things.

It’s safe to say that we’ll have no shortage of news in 2025 when it comes to trade and tariffs. So buckle up, it’s no doubt going to be another unprecedented year as Trump 2.0 gets underway.

Tee Up the Tariffs

Trump repeatedly professed his love for tariffs on the campaign trail. He has floated tariffs of 60% on imports of Chinese goods and of 10% to 20% on imports from other nations. Whatever the actual figure and scope is, I wouldn’t dismiss Trump’s comments as bluster.

The president has broad powers, especially when it comes to trade issues. Trump imposed Section 232 tariffs on March 8, 2018. I don’t think many people—even those in the room when it happened—expected a blanket 25% tariff on steel, yet that’s what went down.

I was doing a tour of what was then ArcelorMittal USA’s Burns Harbor Works in northwest Indiana at the time. I still recall how the move caught everyone, including a company media representative, by surprise.

It was a similar situation when President Trump on Aug. 10, 2018, doubled Turkey’s Section 232 to 50% via tweet. It was a Friday. I had planned to take the day off. I instead spent the time learning that, yes, he could do that. What’s different now? As best as I can tell, only that Twitter is X.

Here’s another point of continuity when it comes to Trump and trade: Robert Lighthizer could have a big role in shaping trade policy in Trump’s second term. Lighthizer served as U.S. Trade Representative (USTR) in the first Trump administration and was one of the engineers of policies such as Section 232 and Section 301.

Before that, he was a trade attorney at Skadden, where he represented U.S. Steel. During this time, he spearheaded the landmark trade petition in 2009 targeting rampant imports of Chinese oil country tubular goods (OCTG). (Fun fact: I covered some of those proceedings as a relatively new reporter at what was then American Metal Market.)

With that background, Lighthizer is unlikely to have changed his position on trade with China. Why would he change his tune?

What’s Next for USMCA?

The president always has broad powers to make trade deals. What caught many by surprise in Trump’s first term was that some of those same powers could be used to break trade deals as well. Another lesson from Trump 1.0 was that "national security" could be invoked to slap tariffs on U.S. foes and allies alike—including Japan, the European Union, and (initially) both Canada and Mexico.

Speaking of our neighbors, USMCA could be overhauled in 2026. Lighthizer helped negotiate it in Trump’s first term after Trump promised in his 2016 campaign to get rid of NAFTA. It went into effect in July 2020 and is up for review every six years.

Cleveland-Cliffs Chairman, President, and CEO Lourenco Goncalves has made a talking point of removing the "M" from USMCA. Could that become a talking point for Trump as well?

It’s worth considering whether Canada might be in the crosshairs too. Nucor Chair, President, and CEO Leon Topalian has vented frustrations about alleged "unfair" trade practices from both Canada and Mexico. He said that China had been transhipping steel through both countries.

Assuming USMCA remains in place, it will almost certainly have more teeth in 2026 than it does now, especially when it comes to allegations of transshipment.

Will the IRA stay?

Questions have swirled around whether the Inflation Reduction Act (IRA), a key legislative accomplishment of the Biden administration, might be repealed. Executive orders from one presidential administration can quickly be undone by a succeeding administration. But the IRA is law, which means it would be more difficult to reverse or amend.

And then there is the question of whether it would be politically desirable to remove it. Billions in IRA funding went not only to swing states that Trump ultimately won but also to solidly red states such as Texas. Why remove a source of demand in what remains a difficult steel market?

Demand Still Lackluster

Potential trade policies and key cabinet appointments are sure to grab headlines as the transition to Trump 2.0 gains steam. One thing that might not, but that is arguably more important to steel, is the state of demand.

SMU asks service centers in our market surveys whether they are releasing more or less steel than a year ago. If you look at

, the orange line means that centers are releasing less steel, the light blue lines that they are releasing the same amount of steel, and the dark-blue lines that they are releasing more steel. You can see that since about the middle of Q2, most service centers (and 70% to 80% more recently) have been releasing less steel than they were in 2023. Can a new presidential administration somehow change that trend?

As for steel prices, we surveyed buyers the week of the election. U.S. politics was turned upside down on Nov. 5. The steel market was not.

In fact, steel buyers’ expectations for steel prices barely budged. As shown in

Figure 2

, most (65%) thought that hot-rolled coil (HRC) prices would be in the $700s/ton come early January—or slightly above the $680/ton they were at when the poll was taken.

You could make the case that the market was being too cautious. U.S. buyers tend to hunker down in wait-and-see mode ahead of an election. Also, remember the "Trump bump" in 2016? HRC prices went from $480/ton just before election day in early November 2016 to $630/ton shortly after inauguration day in late January 2017—a gain of 31%.

That said, you could also make the case that tariffs work, so there is less reason to expect a jump in steel prices this time. What do I mean by that? Section 232 tariffs, as well as waves of petitions for antidumping and countervailing duties over the last decade, have resulted in a far more protected U.S. flat-rolled steel market. Additionally, U.S. mills have responded to those protections by investing billions in new, more efficient capacity.

For example, Big River 2 will double production at the Osceola, Ark., steel mill from 3 million tons per year to 6 million tons per year. BR2 made its first coil in November. Qualifications and trials are expected to continue through the end of the year. The mill is slated to have a bigger commercial presence as it ramps up into Q1.

Those tons will come into the market as SDI Sinton in Texas runs more reliably, and as North Star BlueScope continues to make progress with a capacity expansion in Ohio. And that’s just a short list of new capacity.

In short, there is a lot more new capacity now than there was when Trump first took office in 2016. Duties and tariffs might squeeze out imports. But will it be enough to offset that new capacity, or will older (mostly integrated) capacity be forced to idle?

The Big Picture: Inflation and Deficits

Let’s loop back to the logic behind Trump’s tariffs strategy. The tariffs are designed to bring manufacturing back to the U.S. How long does it take to rebuild factories, perhaps entire industries, that have been offshored? And how is there not at least some pain in the form of inflation when those tariffs are introduced?

Trump backers might note that the U.S. has a lot of leverage over export-oriented economies such as Germany and South Korea. They might say that such countries will have no choice but to "eat" the tariffs. Or maybe, as with Section 232, we’ll see plenty of exemptions granted to allies such as Japan. Right now, it’s hard to do more than speculate.

Trump also has talked about replacing tax revenue with money from tariffs. How will that work?

CRU’s economists don’t see that happening without some major deficits. Trump supporters might note that such projects don’t properly account for the growth that will be stimulated by lower taxes and less regulations. They might also note that big cuts to government spending aren’t being accounted for.

Once again, there might be more questions than answers until Jan. 20. But Inauguration Day is not that far away, and we’ll know the answers soon enough.
