S

teel prices finished 2019 on an upward trajectory, giving the market hope for a stronger 2020. That trend proved short-lived, however, as prices stalled in January and February, leaving both buyers and sellers wondering what to expect in the weeks and months ahead.

Steel prices rose in November and December on a series of price hikes by the mills with the benchmark price for hot-rolled hitting $610/ton by early January, up from a low of $470/ton in October. As of Feb. 11, the price had lost that momentum and backtracked by about $30/ton.

Most of the service center and manufacturing executives polled by Steel Market Update (SMU) at that time predicted prices would continue to move sideways or decline even further in the near term. By that point, SMU had shifted its Price Momentum Indicator from "higher" to "neutral" on all flat-rolled and plate products, waiting for the market to establish a clear direction.

According to SMU’s canvass of the market in the second week of February, spot orders of hot-rolled coil had an average price of $580/ton ($29/cwt) FOB mill, east of the Rockies, with lead times of three to five weeks. Cold-rolled orders were averaging $740/ton ($37/cwt) delivered with five- to eight-week lead times. Galvanized was at $770/ton ($38.50/cwt) with four- to eight-week deliveries. Galvalume was slightly higher at $790/ton ($39.50/cwt) with seven- to eightweek lead times. The average price for steel plate, with four- to five-week lead times, was $700/ton ($35/cwt).

Which direction will steel prices move from here? That’s the question on steel buyers’ minds. Is today the best time to buy, or will steel be cheaper tomorrow?

Polling the market in early February, SMU asked: Will steel prices over the next 30 days go higher, lower, or remain the same? Forty-five percent of those responding said steel prices would remain the same. Thirty-eight percent expected prices to move even lower, and just 17% higher (see

Figure 1

).

Like all products, steel prices are heavily influenced by demand. SMU asked executives: Are you seeing demand for your products improving, remaining the same, or declining? Roughly three out of four steel buyers (73%) characterized demand as flat or stable, while 8% saw demand from their customers declining. Only 19% saw demand improving (see

Figure 2

). The flattening of steel prices seemed to mirror the flattening of demand.

One big component of steel demand is how actively service centers are buying. Polling the distributors, SMU asked: Does your company need to build back inventories, or are you comfortable with the levels you are holding on your floor? By far the largest group, about 79%, said they were satisfied with their current stock levels, while 13% were looking to reduce inventories. Only 8% said they were in a buying mode (see

Figure 3

). Muted demand from the distribution channel has likely contributed to the recent weakness in steel prices.

Lead times for spot orders from the mills also were unusually flat in January. Lead times for steel delivery are an indicator of demand at the mill level. The longer the lead times are, the busier the mill is and the less likely it is to negotiate on price. Hot-rolled orders had an average lead time of 4.81 weeks, little changed from the 4.85 weeks in early January. The same was generally true for cold-rolled and coated steel products. While the numbers suggested relatively healthy order levels, their lack of movement made it difficult to gauge the market’s direction.

Other factors were contributing to the downward pressure on prices in January. Domestic steelmakers continued to crank out steel at a capacity utilization rate averaging more than 82% for the year. Are the mills now in danger of oversupplying the market? Ferrous scrap prices also declined in February, making it more difficult for the mills to justify higher finished steel prices.

Despite all the market’s uncertainty and the disturbing headlines on presidential impeachment and the deadly coronavirus in China, steel buyers remained fairly optimistic in February. SMU’s Current Sentiment Index registered +61, while the Future Sentiment Index was at +63—down slightly from January, but still very positive compared with six-year lows in the +30s in September and October 2019. Steel buyers still feel pretty good about their companies’ ability to be successful today, as well as three to six months into the future.

What Steel Buyers Are Saying

Of course, sentiment can change pretty quickly, depending on how steel prices play out in Q1 and Q2. Here are some comments from manufacturers and steel distributors about how they see steel prices going:

• "Many people in our specific industry (agriculture and roofing panels) have a good outlook for 2020 based on quotes in the hopper and contracts already signed for building projects in 2020. Hence, I believe most of us are willing to pay smaller increases as we think we are getting ahead of some potentially more aggressive increases as the seasonal demand picks up in Q2."

• "In the immediate term I expect lower prices, but with low velocity. The main question is whether or not we see that trend extend or whether we see a more pronounced drop. Mill output is still pretty robust and holding up at over 80% capacity utilization, and January imports were as strong as expected, with an influx of slabs. Demand seems weaker than we (and our customers) expected, so that is a concerning development."

• "Where prices will go depends on the particular market. Hot-rolled will go down into the $550s … and a case can be made that we are already there. Coldrolled and hot-dipped galvanized will directionally follow Houston imports. The hot-rolled/cold-rolled spread is uncomfortably high; weakness in energy markets considered, the spread will likely remain high. We need oil at or over $60/barrel for the hot-rolled/cold-rolled spread to begin compressing back to more comfortable levels."

• "It seems like lead times are about the same, but our prepainted number has risen by $1/cwt each of the past few orders. I feel like the pricing will continue to move higher, but I do not get the impression that the increases will be really aggressive."

• "Lead times for coated steel seem to be lengthening somewhat. Supply is fairly tight. Hot-roll is still bouncing around six weeks. I think prices will move sideways through February. If scrap goes up in March, we will likely see the mills try to push for another $40/ton."

• "Lead times are holding. I keep watching for them to decrease, but no visible signs yet. Mills on occasion delay reductions on their lead time sheets, which hides reduced order books. The question remains: How are the mill bookings? Prices are slightly down due to buyer resistance. Consumption is down 15 months in a row, but mills have decent tonnage due to fewer imports."

• "The mills have some holes to fill. Prices to move sideways to lower. Demand seems to be lower than the mills expected."

• "Lead times are totally location-dependent. There are some holes at certain mills. Prices will move lower from here; lack of demand."

• "Lead times vary from steel mill to steel mill. We have from mid-March to April ship dates. It appears that steel prices are starting to move down due to reduced order entry. We are hearing that the pipe and tube mills are backing off."

• "If lead times are an indicator of market strength, then the mills are poised for more increases."

• "Prices will move sideways to down. Q1 demand was pulled forward with Q4 buys. Scrap is falling. The demand environment overall is stable, but not robust."

• "Pretty fragile. Mills want about equal to resale for replacement, so if the price doesn’t move up (and I expect flat to slightly down), a current buy requires you to eat the freight. The only thing better than last year is that service center inventory values shouldn’t fall $300/ton this year."
