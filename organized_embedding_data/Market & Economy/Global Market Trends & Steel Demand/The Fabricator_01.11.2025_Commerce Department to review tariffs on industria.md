# Commerce Department to review tariffs on industrial machinery

[TARİH: 01.11.2025 The Fabricator]

Around Washington

The president will determine if tariffs need to remain in place because of national security concerns

OMAR NASHASHIBI

T

he U.S. Department of Commerce has launched two separate national security investigations under Section 232 of the Trade Expansion Act of 1962, focusing on imports of advanced manufacturing equipment and critical medical goods. Notices for both reviews were officially published in the Federal Register on Sept. 26.

The first investigation, initiated on Sept. 2, targets robotics and industrial machinery. The review encompasses a wide array of equipment, including industrial robots and milling, turning, grinding, deburring, stamping, and pressing machinery. It also covers automatic tool changers, specialized production systems such as autoclaves and industrial ovens, as well as laser and waterjet cutting machines, and metalworking and finishing systems. Injection molding machines also might fall within the scope of the review.

The Fabricators and Manufacturers Association filed formal comments with the Commerce Department raising concern over tariffs on imports of high-tech machine tools from Europe and Japan not available in the U.S. The administration of President Donald Trump sought input from the public to evaluate whether U.S. reliance on foreign sources of this equipment poses a national security risk. Stakeholders are asked to provide information on domestic production capabilities, import dependency, foreign subsidies and pricing, workforce impacts, and the potential to expand U.S. manufacturing to meet strategic needs.

At the same time, the Bureau of Industry and Security is reviewing imports of personal protective equipment, medical consumables, and medical devices.

BIS is requesting detailed feedback on U.S. dependence on foreign suppliers, potential supply chain vulnerabilities, domestic manufacturing capacity, and the impact of foreign trade practices and export restrictions. Public comments for both investigations are due by Oct. 17. Once the comment period closes, the Commerce Department will prepare reports for the president, who will decide whether these imports present a national security threat and, if necessary, determine appropriate measures such as tariffs, quotas, or other trade restrictions.

New Section 301 Vessel Fees Now in Effect

As of Oct. 14, the U.S. has officially implemented new Section 301 vessel fees on ships owned, operated, or built in China, as well as foreign-built vehicle carriers. The fees are part of a broader U.S. strategy to counter China’s dominance in global shipbuilding and maritime logistics and to strengthen domestic industrial capacity.

The Office of the U.S. Trade Representative first outlined the policy in April 2025 following a yearlong Section 301 investigation that found China’s shipbuilding practices to be "unreasonable" and harmful to U.S. commerce. Updated details were published in June 2025, and U.S. Customs and Border Protection (CBP) has now begun enforcing the new structure. Under the finalized rules:

Chinese-owned or -operated vessels (Annex I) are charged $50/ton upon arrival at a U.S. port.

Chinese-built vessels (Annex II) are subject to the higher of $18/ton or $120/container discharged.

Foreign-built vehicle carriers (Annex III), including roll-on/roll-off vessels, are charged $46/ton.

Liquefied natural gas tankers, designated as vessel type 132 under the International Classification of Ships by Type, are exempt, along with other limited categories specified in the Federal Register.

CBP has emphasized that the burden to determine and pay the applicable fee rests with the vessel operator, not CBP. Ships without proof of payment may be denied clearance or prevented from loading or unloading cargo until payment is verified.

The new fee regime underscores the administration’s broader effort to confront foreign subsidization, industrial overcapacity, and market distortion in strategic maritime sectors.

DOL Issues Opinion Letter on Overtime for Joint Employers

The U.S. Department of Labor (DOL) has released new guidance clarifying overtime calculations under the Fair Labor Standards Act for workers employed by multiple related entities. Opinion Letter FLSA-2025-05, issued Sept. 30, addresses how employers should handle overtime when two businesses are deemed joint employers.

According to the DOL, when two entities are sufficiently connected—such as through shared management, supervision, facilities, or scheduling—an employee’s hours worked at both must be combined to determine overtime eligibility. The agency reviewed a case involving an employee who worked for a hotel restaurant and a members-only club operating under different business names but within the same facility. The DOL concluded that the businesses were joint employers due to overlapping ownership and integrated operations.

The opinion reiterates that separate corporate structures do not automatically protect employers from joint liability. Instead, the DOL applies an "economic realities" test to assess whether entities share control over employees, coordinate schedules, or jointly influence pay and working conditions. When those factors are present, both employers are responsible for compliance with wage and hour laws.

The opinion letter aligns with the DOL’s broader trend of expanding its interpretation of joint employment.
