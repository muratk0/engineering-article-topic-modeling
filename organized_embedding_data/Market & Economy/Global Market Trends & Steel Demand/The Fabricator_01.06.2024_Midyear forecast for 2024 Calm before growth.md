# Midyear forecast for 2024: Calm before growth

[TARİH: 01.06.2024 The Fabricator]

Fabrinomics | Economic Insights

Dr. Chris Kuehl

Surprising weakness in the Q1 GDP report was followed by a weaker jobs report for April. Also, weaker manufacturing reports for April have added to concerns over economic slowing. Interestingly, only one in our cadre of models was showing any signs of weakness, and it came out of the primary metals sector. All others are showing stable signs of growth for the near term.

The Federal Reserve is leaving rates unchanged for the time being, but weaker economic data has helped increase the likelihood of at least one interest rate cut for 2024. If that first cut were to come by the July meeting, our prior forecast for two cuts is still likely in play, with the second one coming after the election at the December meeting. That would bring the Fed Funds Rate down from 5.25% to 4.75%.

Q1 Real GDP came in weaker than expected with growth of just 1.6% (against expectations for 2.2%). However, most estimates have removed the risk of recession from the forecast. As of early May, however, several broad economic metrics have turned weaker.

One of the key factors that drives consumer spending is inflation-adjusted disposable income. This has a direct correlation with auto sales. Disposable income grew at just 1.4% through March. And looking forward, that rate of growth will continue through most of 2024. Inflation-adjusted income has now fallen back in line with the longterm trendline dating back to the 1990s. If tax cuts expire, that will further hit broad-based disposable income in 2024 and 2025, and pull spending down with it.

Consumer spending accounts for 70% of GDP, and one of the most direct metrics showing spending expectations comes from the New York Fed. Spending expectations over the next six months were 12.2% weaker than levels from a year ago. They remain within the long-term trend for expectations, and total spending expectations are still slightly ahead of rates held just before the pandemic.

Considering all the data, you could justify both a glass-halfempty and glass-half-full outlook, and current opinions run the gamut. The expectation last year was that growth would stumble at any moment, and it never did. Now that slowdown may be upon us, but the latest data from the Atlanta Fed shows an expectation of 4.2% growth in Q2.

Global Manufacturing Decelerates

Global manufacturing did experience some deceleration in April. Only 13 countries had manufacturing sectors in contraction, but the conditions in 19 of 30 markets slowed from prior growth rates. Manufacturing typically accounts for 19% to 25% of developed nations’ GDP; the services sectors account for 60% to 70%.

Regionally, European markets showed more improvement this month. North American markets were generally decelerating, and Asia was mixed. New orders were still struggling across the entire global environment, but raw materials and input costs in general were increasing. Manufacturers reported having a difficult time passing on these higher input prices to their customers. This could trim profit margins in the short term.

The models are still showing a stable output environment throughout most of the sectors. Construction is going to continue to drive a lot of manufacturing activity, and nondurable consumer essentials will drive manufacturing as well. Defense sector spending is increasing and, as always, food and beverage manufacturing will remain stable. Other types of manufacturing may be slow to build momentum, however, and it may take more depletion of inventories before manufacturing activity takes off in response. Again, with inventories balanced, small increases in new orders will drive more raw material, energy, labor, and assembly line activity.

The March Transportation Demand Index (the latest available) inched up in the first quarter. It continued to show demand in a range commensurate with the 10-year trend prior to the pandemic. But sources continued to suggest that capacity was loose. The composite TDI index came in 3.3% higher M/M and was 10.0% higher Y/Y.

The demand index functions much like a leading economic index; it shows what the demand environment will be over the next six months. Rates are still low, and truckload carriers (especially independents) are scrambling to find loads, with many willing to take loads at highly reduced rates. The load-to-truck ratio (LTR) produced by DAT Solutions still shows a weaker LTR, but some southern regions were beginning to see tighter truck capacity starting to emerge.

Recovery in Trucking Sector

The trucking sector is showing some signs of recovery. Remember that this forecast incorporates between 18 and 22 different economic metrics trained over 23 years to produce its outlook. Based on current data, the forecast is predicting tonnage to rise in 2024 by 7.5% Y/Y. This will adjust as we update the model in the coming months, but for now the bias is in a strong upward trajectory, improving in the first half of the year then maintaining that momentum. The forecast is giving us a much stronger total volume expectation for freight.

This largely depends on recovery in the manufacturing sector. With inventories now balanced, the trucking sector will perform more in-line with traditional cycles—and will be driven by normal fundamentals. This has been "broken" for the past 18 months.

Truckload producer prices were still down in March (latest available). Truckload prices (according to the producer price index) are still 6.5% lower year-over-year despite inching up by 0.1% month-over-month. Less-than-truckload (LTL) prices were performing slightly better, up 0.5% month-over-month and 5.2% year-over-year. This is obviously slightly impacted by the Yellow closure and tightening capacity as a result.

The big input costs for manufacturing are labor, commodities, and logistics, and right now all three are sporting highs that put pressure on the manufacturer. Wage inflation is still driving at over 5.0%, and the costs of commodities have escalated. Logistics costs have been similarly escalating and show little sign of fading.

The fabricated metals sector is largely dependent on other sectors of manufacturing, and 2024 looks like it will bring growth of roughly 3.2%. The overall outlook has improved, now showing some sharp growth for the second half of 2024 and into 2025. The long-range outlook is expected to remain well above the 20-year average. Fabricated metals models have been running 93% accurate three months out and 98.3% six months out.

The fabricated metal products forecast is predicting stronger growth near the end of 2024 and into 2025.

The latest model shows a much lower recession risk. Some sectors that have been chronically overstocked with inventory are getting their inventories pulled down, which would reload the entire upstream portion of the supply chain and keep fabricated metals strong.

Primary metal industrial production is forecast to fall by 7.9% in 2024 after growing by 4.8% in 2023. This volatile sector is being driven heavily by changes in raw material demand. As a feeder industry into other segments of industrial production, it is heavily driven by activity in other sectors. Based on the current outlook, this would be the only model signaling a recession. If it is indeed just a sectoral recession, it is difficult to process why. It is coming from demand weakness based on the inputs into the models.

Both primary and fabricated metals represent input costs for various manufacturing sectors, and they have been more volatile than usual, especially in primary metals. Within the primary metals sector, producers are just not seeing clear signals as far as demand and do not want to risk over supply and the potential for big profit cuts.
