# Will the new administration bring more protections for steel?

[TARİH: 01.11.2024 The Fabricator]

Steel News

The election’s impact on business is uncertain, but trade protections are highly likely

Michael Cowden

H

eading into the election, companies didn’t quite know what to make of the presidential race, at least according to an October survey by Steel Market Update.

Survey Says

What does the election outcome mean for steel? For starters, the steel market participants we surveyed aren’t convinced it will have an appreciable impact (see

Figure 1

).

Thirty-six percent said the outcome of the election would matter to their business. Twenty percent said it would have no impact. And the most common response (44%) was that people weren’t sure whether it would or not.

Here is what some survey respondents had to say:

"Uncertainty is not a friend to steel buyers. Post-election, regardless of outcome, the buying will start to improve."

"People are waiting. Each candidate has very different plans, and each could help spur business, but in different ways."

"Our business is hooked to energy. We are either going to be ‘drill baby drill’ or green."

"It’s a ‘wait-and-see’ attitude. Businesses want Trump."

"People are still ordering and are planning for the future."

"Many businesses are likely feeling some effects of election uncertainty on their operations, strategic planning, and workplace dynamics."

"It depends who wins and their actual policies."

"Everyone is driving down their inventory and waiting; not even sure some buyers understand what they are waiting for."

"Much more affected by interest rates. Never bought into the presidential effect."

"‘Maybe’ to ‘No’ here. It won’t really impact our business, or steel pricing for that matter (short of a potential Section 232 2.0)."

The takeaway: Whether they think the election will impact them or not, a lot of businesses are in "wait-and-see" mode. That uncertainty is probably understandable. Headlines about the election have often focused on hot-button social issues like immigration and abortion. Sometimes lost in all of that are specifics on trade and industrial policy.

Section 232: A Brief History

As I’ve noted before, Republicans and Democrats might not agree on much. But they arguably agree more than on anything else (for better or worse) on steel and trade policy. So, the response that caught my attention was the one about a potential Section 232 2.0. Because you could make a case that it’s already happening.

Recall that Trump shocked markets in 2018 when he rolled out the national security tariffs not only against U.S. rivals (like China and Russia), but also against U.S. allies (including the EU, the U.K., and Japan). Biden continued Section 232 but eased the measure against allies.

What comes next? Alan Price, a veteran trade attorney with Washington, D.C.-based law firm Wiley, might have provided a hint of that in a recent column for SMU. His column focused on potential measures that could be used to reign in excess steelmaking capacity in China.

"China assured the world that it would curb capacity and production. Instead, it replaced and expanded existing capacity, even while demand fell," Price wrote.

"As a result, global excess capacity is forecast to grow to 630 million metric tons by 2026—the highest level since 2016," he added.

Section 232 was adopted in part to address a surge in finished steel products from China to the U.S., Price noted. And perhaps it was a victim of its own success in some respects.

Section 232 2.0?

China now exports very little finished steel directly to the U.S. Instead, China has supported the growth of steelmaking capacity abroad—in Southeast Asia in particular. And with its Belt and Road Initiative (BRI), Beijing has expanded those efforts to the Americas—notably the Dominican Republic in the case of aluminum extrusions, Price wrote.

Steel- and metal-intensive downstream products need protection too, he added. And by downstream, you could make the case that it goes all the way from things like aluminum extrusions to auto parts—even to electric vehicles.

If Price is right, to solve the overcapacity conundrum, the U.S. will have to target not just China but also third-party countries—places such as Thailand, Malaysia, and Vietnam that have become "export platforms" to the U.S.

So, you can also expect more multi-country trade remedies. We arguably see that in the U.S. already, with the most recent example being a sprawling trade case that targeted coated (think galvanized, Galvalume, and aluminized) flat-rolled steel imports from 10 countries that the Commerce Department initiated in late September. (Perhaps worth noting as well is that the case, whatever the final result, was filed largely against U.S. allies and trade partners.) What products, whether finished steel or downstream, might be next?

Also needed will be increased melted-and-poured regulations (or smelted-and-cast for aluminum), Price wrote. The U.S. has long had such requirements.

And they were stepped up for Canada and Mexico under the terms of the United States-Mexico-Canada Agreement (USMCA), the successor to the North American Free Trade Agreement negotiated by the Trump administration. Lifting Section 232 tariffs from Canada and Mexico in 2019 was a precursor to USMCA being enacted in 2020. The idea at the time seemed to be to create what I’ll call a "fortress North America" trade policy, with Canada, Mexico, and the U.S. playing by roughly the same rules.

What Happened to Fortress North America?

We’ve seen efforts by Canada, Mexico, and the U.S. to synchronize trade policies. Mexico was arguably among the biggest beneficiaries of policies aimed at weaning the U.S. off factories in China. The trend of nearshoring or friend-shoring has clearly benefitted Mexico. And it arguably just makes sense for Mexico to have more manufacturing capacity given that developing economies consume more steel than developed ones like the U.S. and Canada.

But it’s not so clear now whether fortress North America will remain the strategy in the years ahead. The Biden administration has reimposed Section 232 tariffs on Mexico. Cleveland-Cliffs Chairman/President/CEO Lourenco Goncalves has found an enduring soundbite with a quote about taking the ‘M’ out of USMCA. And Nucor Chairman/President/CEO Leon Topalian, who doesn’t always see eye to eye with Goncalves, has expressed concerns about unfair trade from both Mexico and Canada.

Vice President Harris has said USMCA has made it "far too easy" for companies to outsource jobs, according to Inside U.S. Trade, a publication focused on trade matters. She has said she’ll address those concerns during a 2026 USMCA review process. And Trump, who negotiated USMCA, also wants to renegotiate its terms.

In short, no matter who wins, I’d expect more trade protections for U.S. mills and manufacturers in 2025.

Neither candidate is talking about free trade or reducing tariffs. And there seems to be a consensus that the World Trade Organization is not up to the task of upholding the post-WWII trade orders.

In North America, are the established supply chains among Canada, Mexico, and the U.S. durable? Or could those be disrupted too? I wish I knew.

Come to think of it, I can see why a lot of you are in wait-and-see mode. I am too.
