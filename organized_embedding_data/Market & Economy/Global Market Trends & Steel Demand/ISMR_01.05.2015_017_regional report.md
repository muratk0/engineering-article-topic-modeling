A new generation of Asian companies has emerged to take advantage of increased spending power and opportunities in the region.

By 2030, the Asia Pacific will be home to two thirds of the world’s middle class (up from 28 per cent only a few years ago), an astonishing 3.2 billion people. Indeed, by 2030, the Asia Pacific middle class will dwarf Europe and North America combined. This projected growth correlates with an intensification of consumerist behaviour in the region, according to a report by CSIRO/Telstra. It represents a huge opportunity for manufacturers supplying the region.

“The rise of Asia over the past few decades has transformed the global economic landscape. Since the 1980s, major economies in Asia have seen impressive economic growth. This has lifted millions out of poverty and created huge new markets for domestic consumption. Although growth rates have dipped in recent months, especially as Chinese market growth has slowed, the future looks bright for Asia,” commented Ernst and Young (E&Y).

“This economic transformation has created a highly fertile environment for business growth across Asia. Over the past decade, a new generation of Asian companies has emerged to take advantage of increased spending power and opportunities in the region. These new multinationals are now fanning out across the globe, capitalising on a platform of strong domestic markets, government support and readily available capital to become major players in international business.”

## Asia market forecasts

In terms of global trade, according to Christine Lagarde, head of the International Monetary Fund (IMF), “Many emerging market economies, especially in South Asia and Latin America, can still benefit greatly from integrating into the global economy through traditional trade liberalisation. This may include unilateral efforts to open up trade and encourage foreign direct investment, especially in infrastructure. In Asia, in this decade alone, overall national infrastructure investment needs are estimated to be US$ 8 trillion...”

The IMF expects growth in Asia and the Pacific region to hold steady at 5.6 per cent in 2015, easing slightly to 5.5 per cent in 2016. Behind this stability, it sees considerable differences across the countries. For some natural resource exporters, such as Mongolia and Malaysia, it revised down its growth forecasts. A housing-led slowdown, it believes, will continue in China and New Zealand.

“By contrast, growth is expected to pick up in Japan, India and most ASEAN countries. Behind this robust growth, domestic demand still remains the main driver of growth, especially for non-advanced Asian countries. We expect the windfall boost to real income from lower oil prices and strong labour market conditions will offset the modest tightening of global financial conditions,” explained Keiko Utsunomiya of the IMF, during an April briefing on the Asia and Pacific region.

“China’s growth rate is expected to continue to edge lower to 6.8 per cent in 2015 and 6.3 per cent in 2016 as adjustment in real estate, shadow banking and local government finances continues. This slowdown is largely policy-induced and welcome as it will contain vulnerabilities and promote a more sustainable growth path. In Japan, growth is expected to pick up to about 1.0 per cent in 2015 and 1.2 per cent in 2016, which is faster than potential growth rate. Private consumption will accelerate on low oil prices and rising real wages. The wage negotiation is crucial, so having higher wages will help Japan to continue on this recovery path.”

India, said the IMF, is a new bright spot in Asia, and its growth rate is expected to strengthen to 7.5 per cent in 2015 and 2016, benefiting from recent policy reform announcements and lower oil prices. The implementation of these reforms will reinforce confidence and increase potential growth.

“ASEAN countries are expected to grow by 5.1 per cent in 2015 and 5.3 per cent in 2016 with Malaysia’s slow growth, because of the natural resource price decline, more than offset by a significant pick up elsewhere, notably in Thailand, the Philippines, Myanmar and, to a lesser extent, Indonesia. Most frontier and developing economies in Asia and Pacific are expected to grow above 6 per cent in near-time, but risks remain. Fiscal and current account imbalances are building in Laos, Myanmar and Papua New Guinea. Public debt is still high and requires attention in Vietnam and Sri Lanka. Mongolia has been hit by large terms of trade shocks and is facing twin fiscal and current account deficits – while reserves are relatively low,” continued Keiko Utsunomiya of the IMF.

## Asian sheet metal hub

Mack Brooks Exhibitions Ltd, organiser of the leading global sheet metal working event EuroBLECH, announced the rebranding of the BLECH China series of exhibitions last year. Now known as AsiaBLECH 2016, an international sheet metalworking trade show, it will take place from 11-13 May 2016 at Suzhou International Expo Center, China, on a two-year cycle. It will encompass the sheet metal working technology chain only.

“With AsiaBLECH we are drawing a clear parallel between EuroBLECH and AsiaBLECH, which we believe has the potential to follow a similar growth path in Asia,” commented Stephen Brooks, the Chairman of Mack Brooks Exhibitions.

“Suzhou is a perfect location, with all the advantages of a strong manufacturing base and proximity to the hub of Shanghai, without many of the drawbacks of being in Shanghai. In recent years, China's economy around the Yangtze has become the engine of China's flourishing economy, with Suzhou at its heart. Many of the best known suppliers of sheet metal equipment, such as TRUMPF, Bruderer and many others, have located their Chinese operations in Suzhou with good reason. The region generates 26 per cent of the country's GDP and, because it is the economic centre of China’s National Development Programme, it is an important international gateway to the Asia-Pacific region and beyond,” added Dan Wu, Exhibition Director, Mack Brooks' Shanghai operations.

## A view on China

A property downturn, factory overcapacity and high levels of local debt have slowed China's economic growth this year. This slowdown has had an impact on Asian market revenues but China remains a very significant consumer and manufacturer in the global market. Leading Chinese companies are making innovation a strategic priority in their business models to stay competitive in global markets, according to a new report conducted by the World Economic Forum in collaboration with Strategy& (a part of the PwC network).

“The Emerging Best Practices of Chinese Globalisers: Develop the Innovation Models” report was based on a series of comprehensive interviews and surveys of 120 Chinese globalising companies conducted over the past 12 months. Half of the surveyed companies are regarded as globalisation champions within China that have outpaced their peers in establishing global presence.

In analysing how innovation contributes to these companies’ globalisation efforts, the report unravelled a dual-path framework. In the first path, companies become successful innovators in the domestic market first and then leveraged their advantage on innovation on the global market. In the second path, companies successfully expanded within the global market first and then leveraged its resources to enable innovation for long-term development.

“Innovation and globalisation are intertwined, reinforcing each other as Chinese companies rise on the global stage,” said Olivier Schwab, Executive Director of the World Economic Forum’s Beijing office. “We found that high-tech companies tend to adopt the first path, while large state-owned enterprises often choose the second one at an early stage and then shift to the first as their globalisation progresses.”

A closer study of the practices of Chinese globalisation champions revealed the following four steps to keep them innovative on the global market:

■ Make innovation a strategic element of globalisation efforts.

■ Tailor innovation strategies to meet global challenges.

■ Balance polarities between overseas and domestic innovation operations.

■ Develop formal and structured innovation processes and systems.

■ Establish local innovation teams.

■ Empower and entrust overseas innovation teams.

■ Develop an innovative corporate culture.

■ Develop long-term innovation capabilities to expand globally.

“As Chinese companies are upgrading along the value chain in the new economic normal, we found that globalisation champions are taking a long-term perspective on building innovation capabilities rather than looking for short-term returns,” said Steven Veldhoen, Partner at Strategy&.

According to Strategy&, over 45 per cent of globalisation champions put innovation as their top priority in overseas strategy; more than 80 per cent focus on business model innovation to satisfy customer needs and one third of them employ 50 per cent or more local employees.

China’s outward foreign direct investment (FDI) has seen significant growth over the past two years as Chinese companies have continued to invest overseas, rising to US$ 108 billion in 2013 from US$ 88 billion in 2012. In addition, the government continues to propose new international cooperation frameworks such as the “Silk Road Economic Belt” and the “21st Century Maritime Silk Road” to facilitate economic cooperation with other regions and countries, providing favourable international circumstances for Chinese companies to go global. These, and other such macro policies and economic incentives, will continue to facilitate the globalisation process of Chinese companies.
