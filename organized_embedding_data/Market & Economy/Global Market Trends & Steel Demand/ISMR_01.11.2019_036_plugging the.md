## Engineering has a recognised skills gap, with staff shortages potentially putting future innovations at risk.

70% of international HR managers are concerned about the global skills shortage.

One recent published review suggested that a skills shortage could cost science, technology, engineering and technology (STEM) businesses as much as £1.5 billion per year in recruitment, temporary staffing, inflated salaries and additional training costs.

Finding talent with the right skills to stay on top of a changing playing field has been an uphill battle for many businesses, with a recent survey finding 70% of international HR managers are concerned about the global skills shortage.

According to a UK government study, for the engineering sector to gain enough candidates to reduce the skills shortage, it would need around 186,000 skilled recruits each year until 2024. Proactively building a workforce of highly skilled staff with access to the latest enterprise technologies will enhance the competitive edge of any organisation and is an important way to secure long-term profit margins.

## Challenges and shortfalls According to Engineering UK, key challenges include:

- Too few STEM teachers.
- Limited access to STEM careers activity.
- Too few women becoming engineers.
- Too little home-grown talent.
- Too little understanding of apprenticeships.
- Diversity is a major challenge in the industry, and needs addressing.

"Not only is the mechanical engineering sector disproportionately male and not ethnically diverse, it is also ageing, with the bulk of the baby boomer engineering population reaching retirement at the same time as an expansion in engineering and other related technologies," commented IMechE head of education and skills, Peter Finegold.

"Given that 90% of UK engineers are male, and tend to come from a relatively narrow social background, the sector must do more to challenge and change the culture, retain the relatively high number of women engineers who leave the profession in early career and encourage more people who do not fit the engineering archetype to join and then remain in the profession."

In its 2017 report, Engineering UK put the annual shortfall of graduate engineers at 20,000, which is a conservative estimate. The Institution of Engineering and Technology (IET) revealed in a report that up to 62 percent of engineering employers say that graduates cannot offer the right skills, which is a contributing factor, and also suggests that not enough is being done by schools and universities to prepare future engineers for their debut in the workforce.

Machine tool manufacturers, responding to the skills shortage and the lack of skilled workers in engineering and sheet metal workplaces, have consequently designed more 'intelligence' into their products. Machine tools have become more user-friendly and easier to operate, with better and more ergonomic Human Machine Interfaces (HMIs). Software, and the increased use of automation and robotics, is supplementing human expertise and experience.

## A focus on ‘upskilling'

To meet the challenge posed by a chronic shortage of suitable recruits coming into the UK manufacturing sector – a key factor hampering growth – bosses are increasingly 'upskilling' their existing employees. At the same time, said a new report, a rapidly increasing number of manufacturing firms are seeing turnover and profits nosedive compared to this time last year.

These are two headline findings of the latest SME Manufacturing Barometer, a quarterly survey of the sector's leading decisionmakers compiled by SWMAS (South West Manufacturing Advisory Service) and Economic Growth Solutions.
