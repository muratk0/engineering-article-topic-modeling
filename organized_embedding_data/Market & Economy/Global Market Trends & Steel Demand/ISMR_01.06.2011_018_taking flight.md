# TAKING FLIGHT

[TARİH: 01.06.2011 ISMR]

ISMR reviews growth trends in global aerospace markets for manufacturers.

The global aerospace manufacturing industry is expected to grow at a healthy rate with the majority of the manufacturing work shifting toward the Asia Pacific region, according to analyst Frost & Sullivan. Countries in this region have increased their share in the global aerospace manufacturing market and the figure is expected to reach up to half of the total revenues by 2013.

“The aerospace industry across the value chain can look forward to recovery and plan for better days ahead. Aerospace manufacturing was slightly shielded from the global meltdown, and the outlook is that it is poised for high growth trajectory soon.

“The effect of the recession and the path to recovery had differing degrees of impact and therefore calls for different approaches for the future across the aerospace manufacturing supply chain. The effect would also be different for manufacturers across segments,” said the company.

## AN EYE ON ASIA PACIFIC

The performance of the aerospace manufacturing industry in APAC (Asia Pacific region) clearly shows that the engine and the component manufacturing businesses hold the maximum share of the revenues, said Frost & Sullivan. Engine manufacturing would continue to drive growth. The demand for greener, more fuel-efficient engines due to the extreme pressure on the environmental impact posed by jet emissions would keep ‘research constantly on’ in that segment, it told ISMR.

The Asia Pacific, Latin America and Middle East regions are rapidly emerging as the new aerospace manufacturing hubs. According to Frost & Sullivan’s Asia Pacific Consultant of Aerospace & Defence Practice, Kunal Sinha, several countries in the Asia Pacific region have developed strong expertise in the engine manufacturing segment.

“Countries such as Singapore, China and Japan are already on the top rung of the OEM ladder. Hence, more focus should be laid toward this segment to enhance the competitiveness of the aerospace manufacturing industry in Asia Pacific,” he said.

Analysis from Frost & Sullivan, “The Changing Face of Asia Pacific Aerospace Manufacturing Market”, found that the market earned revenues of over US$ 45.12 billion in 2009 and estimates this to reach US$ 84.26 billion in 2016.

Escalating cost pressures have forced many western aerospace manufacturers to shift the majority of labour-intensive work to the low cost Asia Pacific countries. Offset policy, which mandates western manufacturers (from which most APAC countries buy equipment), has resulted in the set-up of shops in APAC countries, thus fashioning new manufacturing space. This has resulted in the creation of a wider range of aerospace manufacturing opportunities in the Asia Pacific region.

However, the Asia Pacific aerospace manufacturing market is facing tough competition from the Latin American region, primarily Mexico and Chile. To reduce the supply chain risks, many North American aerospace giants have established their low cost bases in Latin America.

## AEROSPACE MANUFACTURING

Manufacturing costs are constantly heading upwards and cost structures will need to be re-examined in order to deal with stronger competition, added Kunal Sinha. Competition will drive the industry to strategically re-align its business model to sustain itself in the long term, regionally and globally.

“Lean manufacturing today has already become more important for most OEMs and, as such, right sizing of their operations is imperative. A lot of OEMs have adopted Six Sigma, Kanban, Kaizen, JIT, Performance-Based Logistics and any other best practices to reduce the cost of operations and increase their efficiencies,” stated Kunal.

“Many OEM integrators, such as Airbus and Boeing, are in the process of shifting their production facilities to low labour cost countries in Asia Pacific. Attracted by low labour costs, global aerospace and defence manufacturers and contractors are shifting their manufacturing activities to China, India, Malaysia, Singapore and other Asian countries. Even after accounting for transportation and other costs associated with managing the supply chain, total savings of 20 to 30 per cent can still be achieved.”

OEMs are also actively seeking ways to reduce manufacturing costs by outsourcing more “design to build” packages rather than just “build to print” to Tier 1 OEMs. This has lead to Tier 1 OEMs becoming responsible for the maintenance programmes for assemblies and parts that they design and manufacture. Some of the other emerging areas in aerospace manufacturing are fleet recycling and conversion, green manufacturing and smart mergers and acquisition, particularly among the medium-sized companies. Frost & Sullivan has noted an increasing trend in the aerospace industry to be vertically integrated to reduce supply chain risks and improve productivity.

While other industries are cutting down manpower, the demand for manpower in aerospace manufacturing in Asia Pacific due to expansion and issues with labour shortages is rising. A shortage of skilled labour would drive cost up further.

“Investment in training is also a good opportunity in the aerospace manufacturing sector,” Kunal concludes.

## MILITARY AND CIVIL MARKETS

Manufacturing in the military aircraft segment is not affected as much as the civil aircraft segment. The long-term bet on commercial aviation means that growth in the coming years would be higher in order to catch up. Over the following ten-year time frame, Frost & Sullivan forecasts that the commercial aviation manufacturing sector would grow at a slightly higher rate of 5.5 per cent, compared to military aviation manufacturing which would grow by around 5.2 per cent. Engine manufacturing would continue to be the main thrust sector.

Aerospace manufacturing is also expected to become more high-tech in the coming years. This would require more investment in the sector. However, for the market to stay healthily profitable for potential investors, there has to be huge numbers of consolidation in the market. At present, there are large numbers of small-scale manufacturers.

“Once the market consolidates, the manufacturers would be able to reach economies of scale and scope and reduce their costs for providing high technology manufacturing solutions. It is a foregone conclusion that next generation aircraft systems will be more electrical, and employ fewer pneumatics, mechanics and hydraulic systems,” concluded Frost & Sullivan. ■
