# Tariffs and now war

Reacting to Economic Convulsions

Dr. Chris Kuehl

One of the most difficult questions for an economist is "What might the future bring?" This seems a bit odd, given that economists are supposed to be in the forecast business. The reality is that economic conditions and pressures change daily, often hourly.

FarzadFrames/Getty Images Plus

In just the past few months, the economic reality has been whipsawed by on-again and off-again tariffs, a Supreme Court ruling that triggered a massive rethink of the whole process, a regime change in Venezuela, trade disputes, and, most recently, a war with Iran. The sudden war in Iran has thrust the oil sector back to the forefront. Per-barrel prices soaring past $100 will reverberate for months and will affect growth this year and next. Iran may well be defeated militarily in short order, but its ability to unleash terror attacks through its proxies will remain, and these can be very disruptive in local areas.

What will all this mean going forward? Four economic issues, ones with the maximum impact on business in general and manufacturers specifically, help put everything in context. At the top of the list is growth, but inflation threats are a close second. The third area of maximum concern is the status of the supply chain, and the fourth involves the labor markets. These last two areas, supply chain and labor, manifest in the growth and inflation arena.

The State of Economic Growth

The U.S. was expected to see a sharp decline in growth in 2025, but that is not what transpired. The tariff shock was felt in Q1 as growth slipped to near-recession levels (0.5%). That was a significant reduction from the 2.4% pace in Q4 2024. That slide reversed in Q2 as the economy grew by 3.3%, and the pace of expansion continued into Q3 with a rate of 4.4%. The expectation for Q4 was very high as well, but that was before the government shutdown took its toll. The expected rate of 5.1% fell to 1.5%, but the current estimate is for a bounce back to 2.3% or even 3.0%.

The driver for that growth last year, consumer spending, is expected to drive growth into 2026. This comes as no shock in an economy traditionally 70% driven by consumption. There has been a shift in that spending that compels closer examination.

Nearly all spending comes from the upper one-third of income earners (those making $100,000 or more). Those making less than $50,000 are essentially out of the economy and living paycheck to paycheck. Those in the middle are spending as long as their jobs are secure. For those in the upper one-third, the threat is a correction in the markets, as much of their spending has been propelled by the gains made there. Those in the middle are watching the job market very closely.

Regarding consumption, 75% of it is now devoted to service as opposed to goods purchasing. Boomers have all the things they want and need, and now they want to pay people to do the things they really don’t want to do (lawn care, snow removal, cleaning, cooking). The key questions about growth revolve around sustainability: Will those upper-income consumers keep spending, and on what?

Checking In on Inflation

The next area of intense focus is inflation. (When isn’t it?) This is another example of an issue that was not all that urgent but has been suddenly more serious. The rate of inflation had risen a bit, from 2.6% to 2.8%. This was higher than the Fed would prefer, as its goal was 2.0%, and the rate is headed in the wrong direction.

Bear in mind that this is the personal consumption expenditures 12-month trimmed mean measure of inflation. Also know that there is a difference between core and noncore readings. Core rates do not count food and fuel, as their volatility complicates efforts to make historical comparisons. Consumers object to this measure, as they devote a lot of their income to food and fuel—and so feel that pinch. Lower fuel costs have been offsetting higher food costs this year, but the war in Iran has drastically altered that pattern. Crude oil has jumped from $60 to over $100 a barrel, and it’s still rising. Inflation is now expected to crest above 3.0% sooner rather than later.

All types of issues accompany an inflation spurt beyond the fact that everything costs more. The impact of oil-price-driven inflation is especially ubiquitous. It obviously affects the price of gas for consumers, but it also impacts all transportation and thus the overall supply chain. Airfares go up, as do freight costs. All those petroleum derivatives rise as well, everything from plastics to fertilizers. There will be a lag before these costs appear, but only a few weeks or months.

This will affect the Fed’s attitude toward rate reductions. The consensus only a few weeks ago was that the Fed was leaning toward a rate cut in a few weeks (perhaps a quarter point, though some were pushing for 50 basis points). Now that consensus view is evaporating as the inflation threat ramps up.

Growth is still a concern as the labor market fades, but the Fed has no desire to pour more fuel on the fire of inflation. Now the thinking is maybe a rate reduction in the summer if—and only if—the inflation rate stays below 3.0%. Some members of the Federal Open Market Committee would vote to ignore that inflation threat, but they are in the minority.

Supply Chains and the Tariff Tumult

The third area of concern to manufacturers, and everybody else for that matter, is the state of the supply chain. We’ve already endured the near collapse of the global supply chain during the pandemic. Nothing could leave the Chinese ports, and ocean traffic nearly came to a halt. Then came the closure of the Panama Canal due to drought, and we’ve endured the Red Sea crisis brought about by attacks from Houthi rebels.

The situation was starting to improve during the past few months—that is, before the war with Iran. Now the Red Sea is a mess again, and the Strait of Hormuz has been effectively shut down. Insurance companies will not touch any ship trying to make the journey. Much of that closure is affecting oil, but a great deal of other traffic has been affected as well. The supply chain stress indicators have been subsiding over the last year or so, but that trend is now reversing, which introduces yet another level of uncertainty.

As jumbled as the war has made the situation, the bigger influence has been the tariffs. The decision by the Supreme Court did not make tariffs in any way illegal; the court simply ruled on whether the IEEPA could be used. There are several alternatives that can be deployed, and all are now under investigation. Each has its own advantages and disadvantages.

Section 301 of the 1974 Trade Act is very sticky and would stay in place for a long time, but it requires a 12- to 18-month investigation by the United States Trade Representative. It is already in use against Chinese exports of primary metals.

Then we have Section 232 of the Trade Expansion Act of 1962. It uses national security as a justification and is sector specific. It requires an investigation by the Commerce Department that generally takes about 270 days. It is already in place for steel and aluminum.

Section 122 of the Trade Act of 1974 does not require studies, but it is limited to 150 days before Congress must weigh in—and it can only impose a 15% tariff. Trump turned to this tool immediately after the Supreme Court decision, but that means that Congress gets a say right before the midterm elections.

Then comes Section 338 of the Smoot-Hawley Tariff Act of 1930. It can be put in place by the president alone, but it is limited to 150 days. Tariffs can go up as far as 50%, but this has never been tested in the courts, and there will certainly be challenges.

If all this sounds like it should be called the "Attorney Work for Life Act," you would be right. The businesses that were forced to pay tariffs are now seeking refunds, and at last count that was well over 10,000. The overall business community is utterly frustrated by the total lack of clarity.

Labor and Immigration

The last major concern—labor—has affected nearly every sector of the business community. The workforce now can be essentially divided into two camps. In one camp, people fear their jobs being replaced by some form of new technology, everything from AI to robotics. The other camp has people with skills in high demand in the modern era. Too many are in the former category and too few are in the latter.

We face a demographic cliff ahead. By 2030, every boomer will have reached retirement age. Not that all of them will suddenly leave the workforce, as boomers are notorious for staying on the job. The point is that more than 20,000 people start drawing Social Security every day, and few have focused on where their replacements come from.

Plenty of blame gets spread around: an education system that fails to note what the business community needs, politicians who focus on everything except what business needs for growth, parents and advisers who don’t know what to guide their children into. This crisis took decades to build, and solving it will take at least as long.
