# Is King Coal Dead?

[TARİH: 01.09.2015 The Fabricator]

showdown of the decade

By Dr. Chris Kuehl, FMA Economic Analyst

This may very well be the showdown of the decade. On the one side you have a growing realization that climate change is an issue that deserves attention, and on the other side you have a whole series of industries that will be profoundly affected by any move to really alter the path of that climate change.

We know now that there are changes in the patterns of global weather attributable to the levels of greenhouse gas in the atmosphere. We are not sure what has contributed the most to this change, but it is pretty clear that pollution plays a role. The No. 1 contributor to these greenhouse gases has been the utility industry, as the majority of power plants burn coal to provide the energy businesses and people consume in the economy.

a growing realization that climate change is an issue

The rules the White House has issued will require massive changes in the industry and are designed quite deliberately to limit the further consumption of coal. This is just the first salvo, and most assume that there will be major modifications to the plan as it works its way through Congress and through the various states most affected by the changes.

Most of the discussion thus far has been focused on the impact on the utility industry itself, but many other sectors will be affected nearly as much. The first to feel the pinch will be the rail sector, as trains haul the vast majority of the coal used by the power plants. This has been the cash cow for rail for years, and investors are already starting to reject the major coal carriers as the expectation is for much reduced business.

The second major impact will be on manufacturers, as they can expect prices will rise — and sharply. The goal is to shift utilities to other fuels — everything from natural gas to wind and solar and other renewables. The problem is that alternatives are not financially viable in most parts of the country. The Germans have been aggressively trying to shift to wind, solar, and other options, but this has not been possible without massive and expensive subsidies from the government. The consumer has also been paying much higher prices for energy in Germany.

The utilities will obviously demand an opportunity to defray the costs of this shift, and most will be granted that option. Right now the price of gas is allowing many to convert effectively, but in the past these prices have been volatile and could rocket back up.
