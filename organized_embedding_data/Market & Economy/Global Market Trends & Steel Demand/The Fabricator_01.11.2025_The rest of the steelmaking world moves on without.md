# The rest of the steelmaking world moves on without the U.S.

[TARİH: 01.11.2025 The Fabricator]

Steel News

Sustainability remains a top concern for global steel community

ETHAN BERNARD

As most of the steelmaking world looks to move forward with sustainability goals set before the second President Donald Trump administration, the U.S. steelmaking community is focused on tariffs and profits.

A

recent meeting highlights the parallel realities inhabited by the U.S. and other countries on sustainability and tariffs.

We’ve all wondered what we would do if we had access to a time machine. Well, at the end of September, I had the chance to venture back, no assistance needed. It wasn’t a trip to the Middle Ages or some time right before World War II. Instead, if you can even imagine it, I journeyed to the time before "Liberation Day," before President Donald Trump’s second administration even put the tariff train in motion. At the International Iron Metallics Association’s (IIMA) biannual meeting in São Paulo, Brazil, from Sept. 29 to Oct. 1, the order of the day was decarbonization and sustainability.

Just a little over a month earlier at Steel Market Update’s Steel Summit 2025 in Atlanta, the buzzword of the conference was "uncertainty." This was linked to the ever-shifting trade landscape, driven by Trump’s tariff regime, as well as economic headwinds and geopolitical instability. With all of that, though, despite lip service being paid to decarbonization and "green steel," hard economic realities were staring conference participants in the face.

Mind you, I think U.S. steel mills have made it clear that they’re still committed to the decarbonization targets set before the second Trump administration came into office. But all along the value chain at Steel Summit, one got the sense that bottom-line concerns were taking precedence over decarbonization. At least from an SMU headline perspective, tariffs have a clear lead recently over decarbonization.

At the IIMA meeting, though, it was as if I had entered a parallel universe, with talk of net zero and definitions of green steel, of course. The word tariff was hardly mentioned at all. There was some euphemistic talk of "turbulence" and "disruption" in the U.S., but the bulk of the presentations were squarely focused on decarbonization.

Perhaps something that CRU Principal Analyst Thais Terzian said during her presentation at the meeting regarding decarbonization sheds light on the issue. She noted that policy decisions are driving decarbonization in metals, sometimes with industry input and sometimes without. In the U.S., the current presidential administration has taken its foot off the gas when it comes to sustainability. Rather, it’s even shown it’s not averse to gas itself. (Remember "Drill, baby, drill?")

Therefore, it makes sense that, especially when demand is not great and there are economic headwinds, decarbonization wouldn’t be the focus here in the U.S.

For much of the rest of the world, though, and especially Europe, that’s not the case. Governments are pushing ahead with sustainability targets and initiatives. The EU’s Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism (CBAM) officially goes into effect on Jan. 1. The U.S. has always been opposed to such an initiative. At the IIMA meeting, though, participants seemed convinced that the whole world was poised to adopt such a system.

However, CBAM, a fee tied to greenhouse gas emissions on imports, remains a major sticking point between the EU and the U.S. At some point, it would seem an international standard for handling carbon emissions needs to be agreed upon. It’s hard to see how the U.S. can remain siloed from the rest of the world on these issues. Hard decisions and bargaining need to come sooner rather than later. To think that the world’s largest economy can stay decoupled on these matters does not seem a viable long-term strategy. Some decisions will be reached. With the current administration’s track record, some fireworks also could be involved.

Although the term tariff was hardly invoked at the IIMA, I did see the outlines of its influence. An executive from Vale spoke about the international mine planning "mega hubs" in various global regions, including one on the U.S. Gulf Coast. These would make lower-emission iron ore products for use in the steel industry and would involve multiple facilities. The project was still being studied (no further info was provided as far as cost, exact location, etc.). Still, the executive said "all systems were a go" and there were "American boots on the ground" dealing with issues.

Of course, this was a subtle nod to Trump’s trade policies—an international company working within the American system for a complex project with global implications. Of course, depending on who occupies the White House in 2028, we could see the clock spin back. A new administration could perhaps align more closely with the rest of the developed world. But that’s still nearly four years away. In the meantime, the pre-Trump II track of decarbonization and sustainability is going to collide with the "Liberation Day" present here in the U.S. What will happen on impact is anybody’s guess. Let’s hope there are plans afoot to keep the turbulence to a minimum.
