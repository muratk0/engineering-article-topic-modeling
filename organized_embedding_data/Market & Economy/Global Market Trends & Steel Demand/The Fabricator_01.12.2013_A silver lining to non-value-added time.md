# A silver lining to non-value-added time

[TARİH: 01.12.2013 The Fabricator]

Biz Talk

Manufacturing needs technology, but only people can make a real difference

Tim Heston, senior editor of

The FABRICATOR

timh@thefabricator.com

Cost reduction remains king. Those moving production back stateside may feel good doing so, but the decision is financial. Labor costs in Asia are rising, and dealing with a supply chain that requires shipping container loads at a time isn’t ideal for many products. A global supply chain isn’t necessarily an agile supply chain.

So even if manufacturing is moving back stateside, that fierce focus on cost reduction hasn’t gone away. And much of that has focused on labor costs per hour:

How much

production can you get out of the workforce for the

least

amount of money? This question could apply to just about any business these days, and it may be why many of those businesses are having trouble finding good people. The most engaged and ambitious go where they see the most pay, benefits, and (perhaps most important) job stability—and they often don’t see these in manufacturing.

That’s why it was refreshing to hear one question raised at LeanFab Workshop & Tours, a conference held in Chicago in late October. Organized by the Fabricators & Manufacturers Association International® (FMA), and led by Dick Kallage of Barrington, Ill.-based KDC & Associates (and columnist for this magazine), the event is dedicated to continuous improvement in metal fabrication, and one fabricator present asked an intriguing question: Why do manufacturers use labor cost reduction as a metric for improvement? As attendees discussed, reduced labor costs are a

result

of continuous improvement, not a cause.

And besides, direct labor in metal fabrication isn’t a dominant cost. In FMA’s

2013 Financial Ratios & Operational Benchmarking Survey

, half of the respondents said direct labor costs made up less than 15 percent of sales. For many, it was less than 10 percent.

If you measure overall manufacturing time for sheet metal parts, you’ll notice that most of it is taken up with non-value-added time sitting in a rack or on the floor, waiting their turn for the laser, punch press, press brake, or welder. The actual value-added time amounts to just a few hours. The non-value-added time adds up to weeks or even months.

Boiled down, value-added time amounts to machines and people producing the right number of parts at the right time. A machine producing extra parts that aren’t needed immediately, to amortize setup time over more pieces, effectively makes that machine unavailable to produce jobs due soon.

The more those machines are available to produce needed parts, the better. The easier it is for employees to keep machines producing needed parts, the more effective an operation can be. Shortening changeover time is central to this. So is reducing the average batch size such that welders and assemblers receive kits of parts sooner. Speeding part velocity increases shop floor capacity and, in turn, reduces every job’s labor content. You’re writing the same checks for labor every two weeks (though perhaps with less overtime), but over that same time employees produce more revenue. They’re producing more not with less, but by using better tools and processes.

Easier typed than done, that’s the basic argument for lean manufacturing and other improvement methodologies. It’s not about laying off workers; it’s about making existing employees more valuable by making their jobs easier, with less time spent looking for tools, materials, and information. With this, ideally, comes more job security—not less.

That’s one argument that shows why a career in manufacturing can be so rewarding. Along with technical and mechanical skill comes a refreshing logic, one that’s often lacking in corporate America, academia, and government. Manufacturers make a product, and they don’t dwell on abstract ideas. Sure, you may see manufacturing managers take a few leadership training courses to motivate and engage employees. But the nuts and bolts of making a part still requires, well, actual nuts and bolts.

There’s another argument: The pay isn’t bad. This isn’t a get-rich-quick field. Starting pay, particularly for those with no experience, isn’t great. Some machine operators—even welders—have e-mailed me to say that they had to choose between welding and Wal-Mart, and they chose Wal-Mart because the job was easier and paid the same.

But how many retail clerks make more than $80,000 a year? That represents some of the reported salaries of code-certified welders in the

2013 Salary/Wage & Benefit Survey

, recently published by FMA. The average salary is far lower, $42,086, but the wide range between the minimum and maximum pay shows a financially fulfilling career path, something a stocking or checkout clerk probably won’t enjoy.

There’s been a sea change in manufacturing thinking that has gradually permeated this business. A really fast laser gives you plenty of capacity, which can be extremely important in the unpredictable world of high-mix, low-volume fabrication. Still, what about the speed of welding and forming? And what if there’s a shipment error, or a problem with packaging? A mistake that far downstream can be extraordinarily expensive. Overall manufacturing cycle time is what really matters.

Rajan Suri, professor emeritus at the University of Wisconsin-Madison, is also the founding director at the Center for Quick Response Manufacturing (

www.qrmcenter.org

). QRM is an improvement methodology designed for the high-product-mix manufacturer. In his book,

It’s About Time

, Suri shows a bar chart that compares the “touch time” with what he calls “white-space time.” The chart encompasses order entry, fabrication, assembly, and final packaging and shipping. The touch time is a matter of hours, while the total lead-time is measured in weeks. That’s a lot of white-space time.

If it was all about shortening touch time, it really would be all about technology. But it’s not. In a fab shop, jobs spend most of their time waiting for customer approval; waiting on material; or waiting in between operations as work-in-process. All that non-value-added time shows how much of a difference smart people can make in this business.

Modern machines have become powerful, indispensable tools. Shops often can’t compete without them. But to be really effective, those tools need good people to use them. Only good people can dream up ways to reduce all that white-space time, to make a real dent in shortening overall lead-time. For the next generation of workers, this may be manufacturing’s best selling point.
