Asia Calling Emerging markets in Asia are growing rapidly and are likely to remain engines of global growth

R apidly growing markets in Asia are likely to weather the ongoing Eurozone crisis and remain engines of global growth, although many will see expansion slow this year says Ernst & Young. Their expansion is expected to accelerate once more in 2013, helping to stimulate a wider pick-up.

“By adjusting their growth patterns towards more reliance, the major emerging Asian economies would allow greater exchange rate flexibility,” said Alexis Karklins-Marchay, Co-leader of the Ernst & Young Emerging Markets Centre, in the report. “In 2011, emerging Asia accounted for just 14% of global consumer spending in US dollars. By 2020, its share will be 25% and by 2030 that share will be 40% — a near threefold rise in just 20 years. In India, 47% of households had income exceeding US$ 5,000 in 2010; by 2020, 80% will.

“Emerging market consumers are also, on average, significantly younger than their rich-world counterparts, and are increasingly concentrated in cities, making them readily accessible. As they become richer they want new homes equipped with modern amenities and filled with consumer durables. Sales in western markets hinge upon replacement cycles and the pace of new household formation; in emerging markets, demand growth is much faster because many households are first-time buyers: that explains why refrigerator sales in China rose by 25% per year from 2005-09.”

In India, 47% of households had income exceeding US$ 5,000 in 2010; by 2020, 80% will.

The report notes that the number of emerging market households enjoying an income of over US$ 30,000 will more than double to 149m by 2020, overtaking the US (120m) and the Eurozone (116m). However, it also underlines that the major emerging Asian countries that have run surpluses in recent years must adjust their growth patterns towards more reliance on domestic demand and should allow greater exchange rate flexibility. Rebalancing Asian RGMs will not only make the world economy more stable but will also help the Asian countries themselves, making high growth rates more durable.

“By increasing private consumption, investment and infrastructure spending, and realigning price dynamics, large welfare gains can be made across Asia. The services sector will grow strongly in coming years across the RGMs (rapidly growing markets),” it explained.

China and India are predicted to drive the iron ore mining market over coming years, according to a new report by natural resources expert GBI Research. It states that future iron production growth in the Asia-Pacific region, which is the largest producer and consumer of iron ore in the world, is expected to rocket, primarily due to demand from these two emerging economies.

“Asia-Pacific’s iron ore consumption increased from 348.2 million metric tons in 2000 to an estimated 1.6 billion metric tons in 2011. During 2012-2020, iron ore consumption in Asia is expected to increase at a CAGR of 5.2% to reach 2.6 billion metric tons in 2020. This is owing to rising demand from the steel industries of China and India, which are expected to account for a combined demand of 2.3 billion metric tons of iron ore by 2020,” says the report.

“The Asia-Pacific region’s substantial reserves of iron ore represent a major strength over other iron ore mining regions across the globe. According to the United States Geological Survey (USGS), Asia had 61.3 billion metric tons or 36% of global crude iron ore reserves at the end of 2010. These reserves are mainly located in Australia, China, Kazakhstan and India. The region’s iron ore production stood at 1.8 billion metric tons in 2011, and is expected to grow at a compound annual growth rate (CAGR) of 6.1% to reach 3.1 billion metric tons in 2020, surpassing consumption by 500 million metric tons.”

## China in focus

According to Oxford Economics, China’s GDP is expected to rise by 7.9% in 2012 and expand by 8.7% in 2013.

“Over the next 10 years to 2021, GDP is predicted to grow on average by 7.8% a year. Manufacturing output growth is forecast to be higher than GDP growth over the next decade. Manufacturing output is expected to increase by 8.8% in 2012 and expand by 9.4% in 2013. Over the next 10 years to 2021, manufacturing output is expected to grow on average by 7.9% a year,” says its Chinese market forecast.

Christine Lagarde is the managing director of the International Monetary Fund (IMF)

“As a result, the share of manufacturing output in GDP is projected to rise from 34.0% in 2011 to 35.1% by 2016 and increase to 35.6% by 2021. Over the same period, the share of service sector output in GDP is expected to expand from 41.7% in 2011 to 43.8% in 2016 and rise to 45.5% in 2021. The three fastest growing sectors in Chinese manufacturing over the next decade will be electronic components & boards, paints, varnishes etc. and basic chemicals & fertilisers.”

The overall value of industrial production in 2011 was CNY 11,681.3 billion at 2005 prices. Within this, the largest sectors are iron & steel (CNY 1,087.6 billion), coal & lignite mining (CNY 952.7 billion) and utilities (CNY 921.8 billion). By 2016 industrial production will total CNY 17,653.9 billion (2005 prices), rising to CNY 25,015.7 billion by 2021. The largest sectors in 2021 will be utilities (CNY 1,998.1 billion), iron & steel (CNY1,887.7 billion) and basic chemicals & fertilisers (CNY 1,467.7 billion), the report adds.

The share of consumption goods in total manufacturing output was 26.9% in 2011: by 2021, Oxford Economics predicts this to decline to 23.9%. The share of investment goods is expected to increase to 34.8% from 31.5% over the same period, while the share of intermediate goods is forecast to fall from 41.6% to 41.4%.

Construction output is expected to increase by 7.4% in 2012 and expand by 7.3% in 2013. Up to 2021, construction output is expected to grow on average by 7.6% a year. Overall industrial production (manufacturing, extraction and utilities) is forecast to rise by 8.0% in 2012 and expand by 8.4% in 2013. This compares with an increase in world industrial production of 3.0% in 2012 and rise of 4.1% in 2013. Up to 2021, industrial production in China is expected to grow on average by 7.2% a year, compared with a growth rate of 3.8% a year for the world as a whole.

The construction equipment industry worldwide experienced a steep decline in its market size after 2008 as a result of global economic downturn. The declining trend in the global market for this equipment continued into 2009 and 2010. However, now this market has slowly started to reshape itself and the construction industry in the Asia-Pacific region is now booming.

“As a result, the share of China in world industrial production should rise from 17.5% in 2012 to 20.0% by 2016 and expand to 23.2% by 2021,” concluded Oxford Economics.

“The Chinese economy achieved a remarkable transformation over the span of three decades to become the world’s second largest economy. This involved growing by an average of 10 per cent a year, and doubling in size every seven to eight years. It also resulted in an 18-fold increase in per capita income during a single generation,” said Christine Lagarde, IMF managing director during a visit to Beijing and the China Development Forum earlier this year.

“When the crisis hit, China demonstrated policy commitment and leadership yet again, deftly combating the negative spillovers. This put China in the enviable position of being able to provide a much needed lifeline to global growth and to assume a greater role as a development partner for low-income countries. The weak global environment has intensified the focus – both inside and outside China – on the need to accelerate efforts to transform China’s economic model.”

She added that China’s external balance has come down considerably – reflecting weaker global demand, a worsening in China’s terms of trade and very strong domestic investment. Attention is now shifting towards the growing internal imbalances and, notably, the persistent and very high levels of investment. Domestic consumption, she pointed out, now needs to assume an even larger role in driving growth.

She welcomed the fact that China’s 12th Five Year Plan emphasises the objective of shifting towards consumption-led growth. Lagarde further outlined the policy priorities needed to propel it on this journey. These included boosting household incomes and promoting inclusiveness, preparing for the coming demographic challenge (e.g. an aging population) and financial reform (supporting growth). ■
