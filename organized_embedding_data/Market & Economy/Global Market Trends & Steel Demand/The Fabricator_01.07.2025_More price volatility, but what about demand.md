# More price volatility, but what about demand?

[TARİH: 01.07.2025 The Fabricator]

Steel News

Another tariff hike leads to a small increase in steel prices

MICHAEL COWDEN

I

’ve received some questions about what might happen next with President Donald Trump and tariffs. That’s like trying to predict where and when lightning will strike. All I can say is that the sky is cloudy, and I hear thunder.

We saw some impact on the price side shortly after Trump announced plans to double Section 232 tariffs to 50%, even if it was nothing dramatic.

Just think of the context of the announcement: Trump was speaking at a rally near Pittsburgh on a Friday evening. The event was billed as a celebration of Nippon Steel’s acquisition of U. S. Steel. I don’t think many people had it on their bingo card that Section 232 tariffs on imported steel (and aluminum) would go from 25% to 50%.

A Modest Impact, So Far

SMU’s hot-rolled coil (HRC) price increased to $845/ton on average in the week after the higher tariffs were announced, up $5/ton from a week earlier. A $5/ton increase is such a small gain that I ordinarily wouldn’t read much into it. But it did mark a shift from the trend of flat or falling prices that we’d seen since mid-March.

We saw something similar from Nucor, the largest domestic steelmaker. The company kept its list price for HRC unchanged at $870/ton immediately after the 50% announcement. And it raised prices only by a modest $20/ton a week later. (I say modest because in past upcycles, we’ve seen U.S. mills announce price hikes of $100/ton or more.)

Echoes of that trend unfolded in the scrap market. The June trade was initially expected to be higher, then strong sideways, and then there were fears that the market could decline maybe $10/gross ton to $20/gross ton on soft demand. The announcement of 50% Section 232 tariffs didn’t send scrap prices soaring higher. But it might have contributed to the market settling sideways instead of falling.

A New Market Floor

Sheet and plate prices going higher from the moment that Trump announced the doubling of 232 tariffs seemed almost inevitable. That’s because, on a very basic level, the floor for flat-rolled steel prices, typically provided by imports, would be significantly higher.

Let’s talk ballpark numbers. Say the lowest price for HRC delivered to the Gulf Coast was approximately $700/ton when the Section 232 was 25%. And let’s say the number increased to roughly $800/ton to $900/ton when the tariff went to 50%.

Basically, let’s assume HRC imports increased to $850/ton on average in the week or two following the doubling of Section 232 tariffs. (I realize the actual number might be higher or lower depending on whether you’re talking material from Canada, Turkey, Vietnam, or somewhere else.) That would represent the floor for the domestic market.

Sure, every now and then (like during COVID) the domestic market falls below the rest of the world. But typically, the U.S. market carries a big premium to everyone else. So, assuming nothing akin to a global pandemic has been unleashed by Trump’s trade and tariff policies, domestic prices should rise. The question is by how much?

What About Demand?

That gets us back to the thorny question of demand.

Look at the comments submitted by SMU subscribers in a survey we did the week after Trump announced Section 232 would be doubled:

"With the 50% tariff announced, it seems pricing is headed back up."

"The tariff threat put in a higher bottom."

"I thought we’d see a continued collapse. Now I’m not so sure."

"The new announcement on tariffs has the potential to shorten the downside."

"Hard to say with the tariff announcement. Mills are already seeing low demand."

Sure, people think prices will go up, but few are talking about a price spike.

When we asked people specifically about the impact of tariffs on their business, most (62% in one survey) said they’re not sure what the impact is. Some (35%) said tariffs were hurting their business. Only a small minority (3%) said tariffs were helping their business.

Here is what some of them had to say:

"We have been hopeful tariffs would help. And we still are … further out into the future. But so far, they are hurting us."

"Every time we get a Tweet, post, etc., it makes people worried and cautious. We want everyone spending money!"

"Uncertainty kills the market regardless of the tariffs, which change from week to week."

"The initial price bump was favorable, long-term projection is TBD."

"I am hearing about some reshoring, but not yet seeing it or hearing about it from my customer base."

"Everyone is looking for advice these days, including myself—and I am a consultant."

If a unifying theme exists, it’s that uncertainty around tariffs is keeping buyers on the sidelines. Meanwhile, there is little evidence of the onshoring that tariffs are supposed to encourage. (To be fair, you wouldn’t expect to see that yet.)

Also, it’s summer, when OEMs take seasonal downtime, and prices often dip as a result. So, at this point, while I expect prices to rise, I don’t anticipate seeing frenzied speculation about HRC prices exceeding $1,000/ton, which is what we saw ahead of Trump’s first overhaul of Section 232 in March.

That said, we saw prices soar over the summer of 2018 when Section 232 was unexpectedly applied to Canada and Mexico as well. Is it possible we’ll see something like that again?

I have my doubts. Mostly because of the increased complexity of the tariff regime this time compared to Trump’s first administration. We’ve got Section 232s in effect or threatened against a much broader array of products this time around.

Then there are the reciprocal tariffs—which are uncharted territory.

Maybe We Shouldn’t Be Surprised

But on second thought, maybe we shouldn’t have been so surprised about Section 232 tariffs going to 50%. I wrote in March that some countries were probably happy to see that the 25% rate was applicable to everyone and that quotas had been eliminated.

Basically, if you were a mill previously subject to 25%—maybe in Egypt, Turkey, or Vietnam—nothing had changed for you. If you were Brazil or South Korea, there was no quota to hold you back. You also faced less competition from traditional U.S. allies such as Canada, Mexico, and the E.U., for whom the 25% tariff was a big change.

Some thought ahead of Liberation Day that the Section 232 tariffs and the reciprocal tariffs would stack. Once it was clear that wasn’t the case, then a 50% Section 232 on imported steel probably became more likely.

Let’s not forget that we saw Trump on a random Friday in August 2018 increase Turkey’s Section 232 to 50% via tweet. (It was still Twitter back then.) So it’s not like a more broad-based 50% was without precedent.

The Only Certainty: Uncertainty

I was at the American Iron and Steel Institute’s (AISI) annual conference in Washington, D.C., in early June, shortly after Trump announced the 50% rate on imported steel. I thought U.S. Sen. Elissa Slotkin, D-Mich., who spoke at the event, had a good point. She said that she wasn’t anti-tariff; she just wasn’t pro-chaos. She strongly suggested that automakers in Michigan shared that point of view.

That echoes what we’re hearing from our survey respondents, who are predominantly steel consumers. What are the rules of the road? Will Trump cut a deal with another country, like he did with the U.K., which might result in a lower 232 tariff? Or might he decide that 50% isn’t enough and that Section 232 needs to go to 100%? And what happens if someone mounts a successful legal challenge to the higher tariffs?

It’s not so much that they’re against tariffs or against buying from domestic mills. It’s just that they don’t know what to expect from one week to the next.

That might be good for the news business, but it’s not so great if you’re trying to run a steady business making things out of steel.

But if there is one thing the steel market has gotten good at since 2020, it’s navigating the kind of volatility that would have been unthinkable a decade ago.
