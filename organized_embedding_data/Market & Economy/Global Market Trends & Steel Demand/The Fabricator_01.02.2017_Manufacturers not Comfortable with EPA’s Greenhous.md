Machinery, computer, and appliance manufacturers are also in the crosshairs should this October 2016 proposed rule be finalized. Of course, the Trump administration has threatened to cancel some regulations either proposed or finalized by the Obama administration, and this EPA proposed rule may fall into that category.

The tailoring rule issued in 2010 was based on two EPA clean air regulatory programs, the Prevention of Significant Deterioration (PSD) and Title V permitting programs. Those two programs require companies to control emissions of "conventional" pollutants, such as ozone, particulate matter, carbon monoxide, and nitrogen oxide.

GHGs are not subject to those permits, at least they never had been before. However, using its authority and the Obama administration’s desire to limit GHG emissions, the EPA in the tailoring rule said that new or modified manufacturing facilities that emitted above the 75,000-ton-per-year limit would have to control those emissions, even if their emissions of conventional pollutants were minimal.

That announcement led to a legal challenge and the Supreme Court’s decision in 2014 that the EPA had not technically supported the requirement to enforce the 75,000-ton level. The EPA went back to the drawing board, conducted more research, and again proposed the 75,000-ton limit in October 2016.

Groups aligned with the National Association of Manufacturers want the agency to increase that 75,000-ton level. In comments submitted to the EPA, the groups wrote: "EPA has unreasonably limited the potential range of

de minimis

thresholds by only considering values capped at 75,000 tons per year."

"Made in America" Requirement Not Watered Down

Quite a dust-up occurred in December as Congress passed a water infrastructure bill.

One of the controversial aspects of the bill as it was moving through Congress was whether to keep the Buy American provision that applies to a host of components made from steel used in projects funded under the Safe Drinking Water Act. President Trump supported keeping the provision in the Water Infrastructure Improvements for the Nation (WIIN) Act (S. 612). Congressional Republicans generally opposed it.

Trump won. The provision stayed in the bill.

The requirement applies to all iron and steel products that have to be sourced in the U.S. before they can be used in projects funded by the Drinking Water State Revolving Fund (DWSRF). That program has the federal government sending about $1 billion to the states, and the states then pass the funds on to cities and towns in the form of low-interest loans for the purpose of building and maintaining drinking water systems. The bill lists products to which the Buy American language applies: lined or unlined pipes and fittings; manhole covers and other municipal castings; hydrants, tanks, flanges, pipe clamps, and restraints; valves; structural steel; reinforced precast concrete; and construction materials.

The administrator of the EPA can waive the Buy American requirement in any of three instances:

1. When buying U.S. steel is inconsistent with the public interest.

2. When iron and steel products are not produced in the U.S. in sufficient and reasonably available quantities and of a satisfactory quality.

3. When inclusion of iron and steel products produced in the U.S. increases the cost of the overall project by more than 25 percent.
