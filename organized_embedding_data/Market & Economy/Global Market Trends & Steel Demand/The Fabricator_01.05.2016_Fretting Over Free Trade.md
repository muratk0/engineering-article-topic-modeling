# Fretting Over Free Trade

From the Editor-in-Chief

Concern about free trade deals is up, but it’s misguided

www.thefabricator.com/author/dan-davis

A

t a March 30 trade event in Chicago, I had the opportunity to learn how Italian machine tool builders are making a real impact by selling their most advanced equipment to U.S. manufacturers. Machine technology represents only a portion of the $60 billion in trade between the U.S. and Italy, but it’s a significant portion. It helps the U.S. retain its standing as the world’s manufacturing leader.

"If you can make it here, you can make it anywhere," Matteo Benedetto, vice president, marketing, Prima Industrie S.p.A., said in response to a question about why the U.S. is such an important market for exporters of technology. As an example, he pointed to the fast-paced advancements occurring in additive manufacturing, and how it’s important for his company to be a part of research programs with companies such as GE Aerospace. To be on the cutting edge of tomorrow’s manufacturing possibilities, these companies need to be in the U.S.

It’s hard to ignore what the impact of positive trade relations between two countries means when you are in a room with the likes of Sergio Marchionne, CEO, Fiat Chrysler, and Marc Allen, president, Boeing International. They represent billiondollar companies that employ thousands in the U.S. and all over the world, while at the same time produce profits for demanding shareholders. It’s a big-money relationship that benefits companies in both countries.

At least, that’s the way most economists see it. Increasingly, many U.S. voters don’t view it that way.

Free trade has been a political piñata for presidential candidates on both sides of the fence in 2016. Donald Trump arguably has been the most vocal critic, claiming that these trade deals are "killing" the U.S. Neither is Bernie Sanders shy about pointing the finger at free trade deals as being the main culprit of the "collapse of the American middle class."

According to a recent survey from the Pew Research Center, 51 percent of Americans believe that trade agreements between the U.S. and other countries have been a good thing, and 39 percent believe the opposite. (Currently the U.S. Department of Commerce reports that the U.S. has 14 such trade agreements with 20 countries.) Support for these types of free trade accords peaked at 59 percent in 2014.

Today politicians are simply looking for a bogeyman to blame sluggish economic rebound since the Great Recession. Wages are stagnant, and obviously people are feeling like they are falling behind with the rising cost of health care and higher education. It’s easy to see why people may be concerned about their financial future.

Blaming free trade agreements is wrong, however. You can’t enjoy $500 big-screen televisions and year-round fresh produce and still complain that free trade agreements have brought nothing but misery. There are as many positives associated with these deals as there are negatives.

Critics of such trade deals claim that they have destroyed U.S. manufacturing. But that’s not the whole story. It is true that the U.S. has seen about 5 million manufacturing jobs disappear since 2000. In truth, though, the dot-com bubble burst and the Great Recession hastened an already stark reality in manufacturing: It just takes fewer people to do the job that once took a lot more people.

Fabricators know this simply by looking at their own facilities. Welding robots used to exist only in automotive factories producing hundreds of vehicles per week. Now robotic welding cells can be found in high-mix, low-volume manufacturing operations because the price for this type of automation has decreased over the past several years and the software has advanced, making it easier to program jobs. Automation is now common at all levels of the supply chain.

Fabricators have shown concern about finding the next generation of manufacturing talent, but they haven’t stopped the stamping presses because of it. More often than not, they have turned to technology to keep up with demand. It’s a lot easier to buy a piece of equipment to do a job than it is to train someone to do it.

The U.S. needs cutting-edge manufacturing technology to remain a vibrant economy. Trade agreements help the country to keep up with the "velocity" of the modern economy, said Eugenio Bassan, CEO, Salvagnini America, at the Chicago event.

"The most important point for manufacturers is having the right part delivered at the right moment," Bassan told the audience. "That’s what’s needed in manufacturing today."

That means obstacles to free trade have to be minimized as much as possible.
