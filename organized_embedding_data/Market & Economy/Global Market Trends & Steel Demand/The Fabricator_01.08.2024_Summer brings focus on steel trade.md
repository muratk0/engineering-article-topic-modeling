# Summer brings focus on steel trade

[TARİH: 01.08.2024 The Fabricator]

Steel News

As Congress makes a dash to the August recess, elected officials want to show that they support the domestic steel industry

Ethan Bernard

T

he start of summer has brought a spate of trade activity in Washington. From melt-and-pour requirements for Mexico, taking further aim at China and unfair trade in general, and the status of Vietnam as a "nonmarket economy," it has been a bustling time along the Beltway.

We asked two steel trade groups if there was anything behind the recent push. Of course, in the background is a tumultuous election, made even more so with the recent attempt on former President Donald Trump’s life and President Joe Biden exiting the election.

"There are at least a few factors that led to a lot of activity in Washington," Philip K. Bell, president of the Steel Manufacturers Association (SMA), told Steel Market Update (SMU) on July 12.

He noted that the approach of the end of a congressional term always brings a flurry of activity as lawmakers try to get their bills to the floor for a vote before heading home for the August recess and to prepare for the election.

"The end of a president’s four-year term also creates some additional incentive for the executive agencies to score some wins before the election," Bell added.

Kevin Dempsey, president and CEO of the American Iron and Steel Institute (AISI), agreed.

"A busy legislative calendar is very common in July as Congress prepares to recess for the month of August," he told SMU, also on July 12. "However, it is sometimes even more so during an election year when members of Congress are looking to spend more time in their districts/states and are focused on key constituencies—like the American steel industry."

Let’s look at some of that activity from the end of June and beginning of July, and what could be coming on the horizon.

Melt and Pour

On July 10, the U.S. and Mexico governments announced measures to prevent tariff evasion and protect the North American steel and aluminum industries.

Biden decreed that steel imports from Mexico would again be subject to 25% Section 232 tariffs only if the products are melted and poured in a country other than Mexico, Canada, or the U.S.

Also, the president issued a smelt-and-cast requirement for aluminum imports from Mexico. If the primary country of smelt, secondary country of smelt, or country of most recent cast is China, Russia, Belarus, or Iran, it will be subject to 10% 232 duties upon entering the U.S.

The rule applies to imports of steel, steel derivatives, aluminum, and aluminum derivatives entering the U.S. on or after July 10, according to Biden’s presidential proclamation.

Importers will be required to provide documentation to U.S. Customs and Border Patrol (CBP) where they identify the country where the raw steel used to manufacture steel products was originally melted and poured.

Also, Mexico will now require importers to declare the country of origin for any steel coming into the country, according to a joint statement from Biden and the president of Mexico, Andres Manuel Lopez.

"The deal announced by the White House and Mexico July 10 does not address all the domestic steel industry’s concerns, such as the surge in rebar imports from Mexico," Bell said.

"And it is still not clear how the melt-and-pour requirement will apply to products coming to Mexico from Brazil," he added. However, Bell said the announcement does represent a step forward for trade relations between the U.S. and Mexico.

Leveling the Playing Field 2.0 Act

Also on July 10, three steel trade groups and the United Steelworkers (USW) union hosted an event on Capitol Hill stressing action was needed on strengthening legislation against unfair trade. AISI, SMA, the Committee on Pipe and Tube Imports, and the USW touted the benefits of the Leveling the Playing Field 2.0 Act (LTPF), which was introduced into both houses of Congress last year.

There were nearly 30 attendees at the event representing steel producers, pipe and tube manufacturers, and union workers across the U.S. Companies participating included Nucor, Cleveland-Cliffs, Steel Dynamics Inc., and SSAB Americas.

Dempsey said the LTPF "is the only piece of legislation that would address China’s expansion of its trade-distorting practices beyond its borders through its Belt and Road Initiative."

Prove It Act

On July 9, Reps. John Curtis (R-Utah) and Scott Peters (D-Calif.), introduced the "Prove It Act" into the House of Representatives. Known officially as the Providing Reliable, Objective, Verifiable Emissions Intensity and Transparency Act, the bill would require "the Secretary of Energy to conduct a study and submit a report on the greenhouse gas emissions intensity of certain products produced in the U.S. and in certain foreign countries, and for other purposes," according to the proposed legislation. Steel and steel products are included among the goods to be compared among the U.S. and other nations.

The U.S. Department of Energy, in coordination with other federal agencies, would be required to publish the study within two years. The assumption is this would provide proof of U.S. industry as cleaner in regards to carbon production.

A similar Senate version of the legislation passed the Senate Environment and Public Works Committee in January.

"We have applauded, and strongly support, this bipartisan measure to improve the accountability of the most GHG [greenhouse-gas]-intensive global producers," Dempsey said.

One thing to note is that "the legislation explicitly states that it does not provide any authority to impose new taxes or establish new mandatory reporting requirements on domestic production," the statement from Rep. Curtis’ office said.

Vietnam, Nonmarket Economy?

The deadline was July 26 for the U.S. Commerce Department’s determination if Vietnam will still be considered as a "nonmarket economy." The agency announced in October it would begin reviewing Vietnam’s status shortly after Vietnam filed an official request to be considered a "market economy." The 270 days expired in the middle of July.

Vietnam is currently listed as a nonmarket economy in U.S. antidumping proceedings. The Congressional Steel Caucus wrote a letter in late June urging that Vietnam remain in that category.

The office of Caucus Chairman Rep. Rick Crawford (R-Ark.), pointing to the letter, released a statement noting that Vietnam remains "a top-down, government- controlled economy."

Bell was on the same page, saying, "We urge them to keep Vietnam’s status just as it is—a nonmarket economy controlled by the Communist party and operating at the beck and call of China."

Outlook

With this quick snapshot, we can see that measures to safeguard domestic industry are at the forefront of concerns in Washington.

"Policymakers on both sides of the aisle recognize that a healthy domestic steel industry is essential to American economic and national security," Dempsey said.

With just a few months until the election, it is unlikely this flurry of activity will abate any time soon.
