The price of steel is high — the understatement of the year. No matter whether we are talking cold rolled, hot rolled, galvanized, etc., the prices spiked. As recently as 2015, hot rolled was a little over $500 a ton, and galvanized was around $800 a ton. These prices hit near all-time highs earlier in the year but have since calmed down a little. The declines from that peak still leave prices higher than they have been in some time and the question is when, and if, they will continue to fall.

The expectation has been that prices would return to the levels seen in the last few years, but nobody seems to have a sense of that timing. Lately, there has been a decline in the price of iron ore, but scrap is more important to the U.S. producer, and it has continued to increase. There’s an expectation that prices will fall due to more limited demand, but supply chain destruction has been upsetting more than a few predictions.

The construction sector remains the primary consumer of steel with automotive coming in a fairly close second and both have had major supply chain issues. The wait for the steel truss needed for many of the warehouse and distribution facility projects can be close to a year. There are rebar shortages and delays in almost all deliveries. The auto sector is not expected to be out of the supply chain woods until the middle of 2022. It is not just the semiconductor shortage at fault — there are shortages of steel and aluminum and plastic and textiles, and these shortages affect all the thousands of suppliers the auto assemblers are dependent on.

THERE’S AN EXPECTATION THAT PRICES WILL FALL DUE TO MORE LIMITED DEMAND, BUT SUPPLY CHAIN DESTRUCTION HAS BEEN UPSETTING MORE THAN A FEW PREDICTIONS.

A big problem over the last year has been China. They remain the world’s largest consumer of steel and the largest producer, but they have been reducing their output and for reasons that are not necessarily connected to economic issues. The EU has started a program that taxes nations that are deemed to be insufficient in terms of addressing climate change. China has been so identified and that means that everything entering Europe from China is taxed until they bring their carbon emissions down. This has prompted the closure of everything from coal mines and utilities to steel operations.

To further complicate matters, there has been an intense crackdown on the regions by Beijing. The perception is that the regional governments are getting too independent and one of the ways to clip their wings is to inhibit their economic independence and that has led to some forced closure of key industries, such as steel production. China used to assume it could simply sell any surplus steel on the global market, but they now face tariffs and restrictions from the U.S., Europe, and even the emerging market states, so that export option is not what it once was.

The bottom line is that reduced demand and overproduction will drive steel prices down at some point. The challenge is determining when this might take place and to what extent.
