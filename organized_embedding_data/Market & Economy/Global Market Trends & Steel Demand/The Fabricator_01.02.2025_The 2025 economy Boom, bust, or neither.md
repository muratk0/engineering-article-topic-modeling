# The 2025 economy: Boom, bust, or neither?

Contextualized data for changing times

E

arly into 2025, the economy looks pretty good. Maybe. Well, it depends on whom you ask, and a good person to ask is Chris Kuehl. He’s economic analyst for the Fabricators and Manufacturers Association (FMA) and managing director at Armada Corporate Intelligence out of Kansas City, Kan.

Every month, his firm, in partnership with John Nelson of Morris, Nelson & Associates, publishes The Watch, which gives a macroeconomic outlook that spans the gamut of industries metal fabricators serve.

I’m writing this two days before the presidential inauguration. Changes are coming, and some will have already arrived by the time you read this. Regardless, know that change is happening in an economy that isn’t going that badly for fabricators. Many industries fabricators serve are still in growth territory, and at present, companies are scanning the changing political landscape for opportunity.

As the latest The Watch report put it: "The transition period is upon us, and there will be uncertainty in the midst. Some of that is the uncertainty that comes with new opportunity. As executive orders are used to spur growth, firms will be trying to figure out how to ‘play’ this new environment, and new green shoots of growth will be closely watched."

Outside of a new president entering the White House, no other metric has been more closely watched than inflation. The Watch tracks the gap between the inflation rate and wage growth, which helps put real inflation in context—and helps detect that wage- and price-hike vicious cycle that business owners fear. Recently, wage growth (between 3.8% and 8%, depending on the sector), has started to outpace inflation (at 2.7%) in a dramatic way.

Still, even the wage-inflation gap needs further context. This is where one of my favorite metrics The Watch tracks comes into play: the growth in real disposable personal income, tracked by the Bureau of Economic Analysis (BEA). "We have found strong correlations between disposable income and all types of big-ticket purchases, everything from machinery and farm equipment to autos, boats, powersports equipment, and many other categories."

Peaks in disposable income growth occurred just before the pandemic, in 2018 and 2019, a time of low unemployment and low inflation. During those years, disposable income averaged 3.4% and peaked at 4.8%. Just before the 2024 election, the BEA reported disposable income at 3.1%, a number that rivaled 2019 levels. But alas, an adjustment the BEA released in January pulled the numbers back down to 2.7%.

Many fabricators find some of their greatest growth opportunities in that disposable income arena: exercise equipment, ATVs, recreational vehicles. When interest rates rise and demand for big-ticket items falls, metal fabrication takes a hit. More broadly, though, the disposable income metric paints a more complete picture. The more disposable income people have, the better they feel about making a purchase. The "sticky" disposable income numbers show that, despite rising wages, they have less money to spend on "fun stuff" than they did before the pandemic. Is that number poised to shift upward in the coming months? Some are betting it will—but, of course, a lot could happen between now and December.

Topping the uncertainty list, of course, are tariffs—or, as The Watch put it, the "T word." "The T word has become seemingly the most feared. Threats of new blanket tariffs on China and USMCA trading partners have many executives scrambling to get ahead of the situation. Many companies are expediting the ordering of products for Q1. What isn’t known is how much of this activity is helping the global manufacturing indexes, creating a ‘pulled forward’ scenario [going into 2025]. If inventory-building activity is truly being front-loaded because of the tariff risk, there will be an adjustment period sometime this year in which orders scale back. But based on the volumes currently inbound, it doesn’t look like the overall impact would be too dramatic at this stage, and that’s reinforced by early January data."

All this sets the stage for The Watch’s 2025 forecast for the various sectors fabricators serve. After disruption caused by the Boeing strike, aerospace manufacturers should see steady improvement.

The machinery sector will likely contract slightly by 1.3% by the end of the year. According to The Watch, "This category includes farm equipment, transportation equipment (including trucks and trailers), and construction machinery. Many of the subsectors that were struggling to get inventories rebuilt in the years following the pandemic have recovered, and there is some overproduction across many categories."

Moreover, farmers have had a rough go over the past few years. As The Watch put it, "In most parts of the country, [2024] was a bad year for farming. Farm incomes are down, and most farmers are getting by on their nonfarm incomes."

Electrical equipment manufacturing is still on the rise. "The back end of the curve is showing a bit of a roller-coaster ride, but 2025 is now expected to grow by 2.2% year over year."

Meanwhile, The Watch predicts the automotive sector will slip slightly, by 2.7%—not the robust growth seen in 2023 (6.6%) but slightly ahead of the 3.6% contraction the industry saw last year.

Feeding into all this is, of course, metal fabrication. "The long-range estimate continues to show a strong outlook—4.4% growth in 2025—and one that could return to pre-pandemic levels of output."

All that is good news indeed, though it still needs context. Not too long ago, I drove with a fab shop owner to another plant location, and he pointed out the window toward the parking lots of a nearby OEM customer. The fuller that parking lot is, he told me, the busier he’s likely to be. Another fab shop owner told me he always paid attention to the truck traffic on road trips.

No forecast is perfect. Economists, including Kuehl, joke about that all the time. But analyzing good data, put in the right context and combined with simple observation, gets you at least a little closer to perfection.
