Zekelman Industries offers employee bonus in wake of steel trade policy changes

Chicago-based Zekelman Industries, an independent steel pipe and tube manufacturer and provider of modular construction technology, has announced that each of its 2,300 employees will receive a $1,000 annual bonus beginning when President Trump’s planned steel trade policy changes take effect, for as long as they remain in place. The Trump administration recently announced plans to levy tariffs on steel and aluminum imports as a result of the Commerce Department’s Section 232 investigation on domestic steel.

In a letter sent to Zekelman employees, Executive Chairman/CEO Barry Zekelmansaid, "The Section 232 investigation confirmed what we have known for decades: that the domestic steel industry has been victimized by unfairly traded and dumped foreign products and the resulting damage has been both massive and a threat to impair national security."

Zekelman also noted, "The policies announced today will have a tremendous positive impact on our ability to compete and thrive.

The playing field is being leveled and we will win in a fair fight."

Zekelman Industries includes the operating divisions of Atlas Tube, Picoma, Sharon Tube, Wheatland Tube, Western Tube, and Z Modular.

OSHA will enforce beryllium standard starting in May

OSHA has announced it will start enforcement of the final rule on occupational exposure to beryllium in general, construction, and shipyard industries on May 11, 2018.

In January 2017 OSHA issued new comprehensive health standards addressing exposure to beryllium in all industries. In response to feedback from stakeholders, the agency is considering technical updates to the January 2017 general industry standard, which will clarify and simplify compliance with requirements.

OSHA also will begin enforcing on May 11, 2018, the new lower eight-hour permissible exposure limit (PEL) and short-term (15-minute) exposure limit (STEL) for construction and shipyard industries. In the interim, if an employer fails to meet the new PEL or STEL, OSHA will inform the employer of the exposure levels and offer assistance to ensure understanding and compliance.

Brian Robbins, the "Steel Man of the Year," talks about bringing sexy back to the metals industry

The Association of Steel Distributors honors the CEO of MidWest Materials Inc. for his dedication to his company and manufacturing

Not all get to walk in the footsteps of their parents, and even fewer get to follow the path of their grandparents. Brian Robbins is one of the fortunate ones.

He currently is CEO of MidWest Materials Inc., Perry, Ohio, a company co-founded by his grandfather in 1952. But Robbins’ path wasn’t always headed for the metals industry. He went to law school at New York University and was enjoying a successful career, until he got the call to join the family business back in Ohio. It was a call he couldn’t turn down.

Since joining MidWest Materials, Robbins has spent time fostering relationships with the company’s customers and vendors and focusing on improving shop operations and safety. Today the steel distributor is ranked as a top 100 steel service center and operates North America’s largest Leveltek stretch leveling system.

Robbins also has been vocal over the years about improving the image of domestic manufacturing and encouraging young people to consider careers in the metal distribution and manufacturing industries. These were important platforms during his presidency of the Association of Steel Distributors (ASD) from March 2014 to April 2016.

Because of his service to his company and the industry, ASD honored him with its 2017 Steel Man of the Year Award at a dinner that coincided with the Fabricators & Manufacturers Association’s Annual Meeting in Scottsdale, Ariz., on March 7. It also should be noted that Robbins’ grandfather, Joseph Koppleman, served as ASD’s president 50 years ago. He also won the Steel Man of the Year Award in 1962.

The FABRICATOR

®

had the chance to speak with Robbins about the award, his time in the industry, and his observations on industry trends. An edited transcript of that discussion follows.

The FABRICATOR:

What are your thoughts after having been named the Association of Steel Distributors’ Steel Man of the Year?

Brian Robbins:

I’m actually very humbled. It’s interesting because my marketing team keeps wanting to sort of promote it, but I keep saying it’s about the company, not about me. I wouldn’t be me without the support of my team members. So I get a little embarrassed by it, but yet at the same time, I’m honored because it really does recognize a bit of a career of service to the steel industry, the manufacturing community, and the greater community for me.

So I look at it as a team event here. And I could never do the things that I do without the fact that I’m so trusting of such a great team in place at MidWest Materials.

FAB:

How did you get involved in the family business?

Robbins:

I was born in Ohio, grew up in New York, went to school at Michigan, went to law school at NYU, and was a practicing attorney for several years. Over the years my family had often inquired as to my interest in this business, and my response pretty much always was, "Are you kidding me?" As an East Coast guy, I didn’t know anything about manufacturing. I grew up in the world of Wall Street lawyers, doctors, and bankers. So the thought of moving to the Midwest was sort of foreign to me, as was manufacturing.

I ultimately did come, of course. The family asked again on a day when I was contemplating my career, and obviously the family was very supportive of such a move.

At the time our operations were actually much bigger. I came into the business at the end of 1996, and the economy at that time was a little more precarious than it is now. We actually downsized quite a bit. Today we’re about half the size at about 75 people, and we run a modified, one-shift operation.

Back then we also were firing customers that weren’t necessarily being partners with us. So it was a very difficult time to get to where we are today. But once we got moving in the direction that we wanted, we were really able to focus on our core competencies and partner with great vendors and customers. That’s where it took off. So now, we’re very busy and are trying to grow.

FAB:

How have things changed over the years?

Robbins:

So I’ve been involved with the family business for almost 23 years. When I first came here, we were probably 50 percent hot-rolled steel and 50 percent coated products. Today we mainly focus on hot-rolled coil. We still process products such as galvanized steel, but our strength is specifically heavy-gauge and high-strength steel. We are practically all prime. And that really came from the high-precision manufacturing processes that have had developed over the last dozen years or so. They require a higher-quality steel.

That has led us to do quite a bit of capital expenditure related to processing steel, so we could meet the high-quality tolerances of fabricators. Specifically we invested in one of the largest stretch leveling systems in North America. It was to help all the fabricators who use lasers and other high-precision manufacturing techniques, and they need a good-quality surface and memory-free steel.

FAB:

What do you think about the trend of metal service centers taking on some fabricating activities of their own?

Robbins:

Many service centers have started doing more fabrication. We actually have not. We are a supplier to a good many of your readers, so we’ve chosen not to necessarily compete with them.

We’ve really focused our attention on providing the bestquality steel so that they can continue to fabricate and partner with our OEM customers. So it used to be, we’d sell steel to those OEM customers, and they would fabricate it. Now they’ve asked us to provide a part. So we’ve been trying to partner with as many fabricators as we can.

I thought it was more important to provide services to both the OEMs and fabricators, without stealing someone’s business. And it’s actually worked out very well for us. We’ve been able to form those partnerships and provide finished goods to the OEM who doesn’t want to produce the part anymore, or we just sell direct to the fabricators who have the OEM as a customer.

FAB:

Has that approach solidified your relationships with some of these companies?

Robbins:

We’ve actually grown our business in the fabrication world, because there were several fabricators who were looking for new steel suppliers that weren’t doing fabricating. Our position also has allowed us to strengthen our relationships with both our vendors and our customers.

FAB:

In the past, you have discussed the importance of improving the image of manufacturing and working to encourage the next generation to consider a manufacturing career. Is this still a passion for you?

Robbins:

Yes. It’s still absolutely the case. So I was president of the Association of Steel Distributors from 2014 to 2016. That was a huge focus for me then and continues today.

Just consider my own experience. I grew up in New York. Who knew what manufacturing was there? I still remember the first time I went to see a manufacturing plant in the middle of Ohio, and I was like, "Oh my God! I’ve never seen such a thing."

So over the last 20 to 30 years, we thought that everyone had to be highly educated service providers of some sort—lawyers, doctors, and bankers. It definitely was to the detriment of manufacturing, and we saw a limitation of interest in those industries.

I would say that in the last 10 years or so, it’s been a huge focus for me and my peers. We’ve been involved with steel mills to try to get people more interested within our own association. We’ve started a group focused on educating and mentoring a younger generation in manufacturing and the field of distribution.

Brian Robbins of Midwest Materials Inc. makes his debut as Steel Man of the Year at a recognition dinner at FMA’s Annual Meeting in Scottsdale, Ariz., on March 7.

There was a time when a lot of young people weren’t getting involved in manufacturing. It wasn’t sexy enough. So in my introduction speech when I became president, my catch phrase was "Let’s bring sexy back to steel." But it was a truth. We’ve gone through our doldrums, but in the last 10 years or so manufacturing has really started to catch on.

We need to get younger people involved, and we really have to work quickly to pass the tribal knowledge from those senior veterans to those young people. There are some great opportunities for different career paths in manufacturing, from working on the plant floor to getting involved with engineering. It’s important, and I think we all in the industry have an obligation to continue to spread the word to our children, our children’s friends, and the community that manufacturing is what keeps our country strong.

FAB:

What are the major challenges that confront the metal distribution sector?

Robbins:

A very specific, current challenge is related to logistics. Regulations in general are always a challenge, but there is a real shortage of truck drivers. So the cost of logistics has gone up.

A regulation went into effect at the end of last year, and drivers have restricted hours, which is for the benefit of safety. The hope is that by limiting driver fatigue, you will reduce accidents. But it’s also taking longer to deliver stuff. So it’s leading to a supply shortage of trucks and drivers. Just like there needs to be an inducement to get younger people to consider manufacturing, there needs to be some inducement to encourage people to be truck drivers.

FAB:

What’s your take on the recent announcement of steel tariffs?

Robbins:

The protective measures are certainly an important thing. We need to make sure we have a very fair and level playing field in the world market, and we need to protect against cheaters or countries that manipulate their markets by legal subsidies. But we also need to be careful not to be overly protective. We’ll see what happens.

It’s too early to comment though. If we all are able to pass along the added costs to our customers, myself to a fabricator or end-user to the ultimate consumer, it’s fine. We just have to ensure the price hikes are not too inflationary, so that consumers are priced out of the equation.

FAB:

Looking back at your career in the metals business, how would you gauge your tenure so far at MidWest Materials?

Robbins:

It’s certainly been exciting. To be involved in the production of items that are used in our everyday lives is very exciting.

It’s also been interesting because it’s been a very cyclical business. The steel business, specifically in manufacturing, has had a lot of ebbs and flows over the past 20 or so years. We’ve seen the economy have very similar cycles, and it’s been a challenge.

My career path, which started elsewhere, also was very challenging. As a company, we’re very connected to our community, and there’s a certain level of commitment that I specifically feel toward my team members. I feel that we’re still responsible for the families who work here. Some of them have been here decades, and their children have come to work for us. It’s a family business that is specific to our community in northeast Ohio, which is so tied to manufacturing. You feel committed to succeed for the benefit of our greater community.

I should also mention that one of my favorite parts of being involved in this industry is the amazing relationships I’ve been able to make throughout the 20-plus years. I’ve really managed to maintain great relationships with people at the steel mills, with people in the trade association groups I’m involved with, and with customers.

That’s probably the most exciting part of the reason for our endurance. We have customers that I met my first week on the job, and they’re still our customers. Some vendors are the same way.

I think manufacturing is unique in that way. Relationships in this industry endure more so than in other industries. Relationships are important, but they’re more fleeting in those other industries.

So that’s probably the thing I’ve enjoyed the most: building true friendships among the supply chain.

Flack Global Metals, First American Resources open coil coating, metal processing facility

Flack Global Metals, Chicago, and First American Resources, Atlanta, have announced plans to develop a new coil coating and metal processing facility in Athens, Ga. The 360,000-sq.-ft. facility will be designed for flatness/surface-critical applications in aluminum, stainless steel, and bare and prepainted carbon (cold-rolled, galvanized, galvalume, and zinc) for appliance, automotive, truck and trailer/transportation, architectural, and HVAC manufacturers in the Southeast.

The facility will offer coil coating for material up to 62 in. wide with gauge capability of 0.009 to 0.050 in. It also will include inline tension leveling, clean-room/clean-surface capability, continuous optical surface monitoring, a high-speed slitting line, embossing, cut-to-length, and inspection. Flack Global Metals designs and fulfills supply chains for OEMs using flat-rolled steel, aluminum, and stainless. First American Resources supplies decorative and functional coating systems primarily to the domestic residential building products and automotive markets.
