# Tariffs on Chinese goods lowered; auto parts tariffs adjusted

[TARİH: 01.06.2025 The Fabricator]

Around Washington

Tariffs on nearly all imports from China now range from 30% to 70%

OMAR NASHASHIBI

T

he U.S. and China have reached a deal to lower tariffs on Chinese goods for at least 90 days. In a statement released on May 12, the two countries announced an agreement to cut tariffs by 115%, leaving a base tariff rate of 30% on goods imported into the U.S. from China and a 10% rate on U.S. goods sent to China.

The rate for "reciprocal" tariffs imposed on China, which had reached a total of 125%, was lowered to the 10% baseline tariffs that the U.S. is imposing on all imports from all countries. This 10% tariff is in addition to the 20% China tariffs imposed and then increased in February and March 2025, respectively, under the International Emergency Economic Powers Act (IEEPA). The tariffs on nearly all imports from China now range from 30% to 70%, when factoring in other tariff actions that may apply, such as the Section 301 action that began in 2018 and the Section 232 national security tariffs on specific goods.

The decrease in tariffs went into effect May 14, and applies through at least Aug. 12 while further talks on economic and trade relations continue between the two countries.

Trump Issues Proclamation Adjusting Tariffs on Auto Parts

On April 29, President Donald Trump issued a presidential proclamation amending the tariffs on auto parts to alleviate the burden on U.S. automakers. The proclamation adjusts the tariffs and introduces an import adjustment offset on imported auto parts. This offset is calculated as 3.75% of the aggregate manufacturer’s suggested retail price (MSRP) value for all automobiles assembled in the U.S. From May 1, 2026, through April 30, 2027, the offset decreases to 2.5% for cars assembled in the U.S. These percentages were determined by calculating the "total duty that would be owed when a 25% duty is applied to parts accounting for 15% of an automobile’s MSRP value" for the first year and the "total duty that would be owed when a 25% duty is applied to parts accounting for 10% of an automobile’s MSRP value" for the second year.

Only automobiles that undergo final assembly in the U.S. are eligible for these offsets. Manufacturers must submit documentation to the Secretary of Commerce when applying, including information such as documentation "certifying the number of automobiles the manufacturer projects it will assemble in the United States, as well as a list of all plant locations where the projected automobiles will undergo final production"; the projected tariffs it will pay on imported parts "broken down by tariff costs the manufacturer will incur directly and tariff costs the manufacturer will incur from its suppliers"; and a roster of importers eligible to use the offsets.

CEQ Developing Draft NEPA Template for Agencies

The White House Council on Environmental Quality is currently developing a draft template to assist agencies such as the Environmental Protection Agency following the revocation of the National Environmental Policy Act (NEPA) implementing rule. On Feb. 25, CEQ issued an interim final rule that revoked CEQ’s NEPA regulations and replaced them with guidance directing agencies to rely on their individual NEPA regulations. Subsequently, on April 8, CEQ issued a memorandum instructing agencies to rescind their existing rules on implementing NEPA and develop nonbinding guidance.

The draft template being developed by CEQ aims to assist agencies in replacing their NEPA rules with guidance. As part of the template, CEQ recommends that agencies exempt certain types of projects to which NEPA applies and would require an environmental impact statement (EIS). CEQ suggests that agencies "[i]nsert a list of examples from agency experience that the agency knows will usually or always trigger an EIS." The document also advises agencies to "consider distilling from that list a presumptive (but nonbinding) monetary threshold above which an action will be deemed ‘major.’" Under NEPA, the requirement to prepare an EIS applies to any "major federal action" that could potentially have significant impacts on the environment. The draft template further removes certain public comment requirements, providing agencies with the option to preclude public comments on reviews and draft EIS documents.

Executive Order Issued to Modernize Federal Workforce Development Programs

On April 23, President Trump signed an executive order (EO) aimed at improving and modernizing the federal government’s workforce development programs. The EO directs the Secretaries of Labor, Education, and Commerce to review all federal workforce programs to modernize, integrate, and align them to address critical workforce needs in emerging industries.

The order instructs the Departments of Labor, Education, and Commerce to develop within 90 days a report, after reviewing all federal workforce development programs, outlining strategies to integrate systems and realign resources to address critical workforce needs and in-demand skills of emerging industries, invest in the upskilling of incumbent workers to meet rapidly evolving skill demands of their industries, and identify credentials and assessments as alternatives to the four-year college degree that can be mapped to the specific skill needs of prospective employers.

The EO also mandates the secretaries develop a plan within six months to reach and surpass 1 million new active apprentices by focusing on ways to expand registered apprenticeships to new industries, such as in emerging sectors.
