# Four issues to fret about: Inflation, recession, supply chain, and labor

[TARİH: 01.04.2023 The Fabricator]

Fabrinomics | Economic Insights

Dr.

Chris Kuehl

Manufacturers and economists face the same worries and concerns at the start of 2023 that they confronted at the end of 2022. What are these, you may ask? Obviously, there is a very long list of things that can keep one awake at night as every business has its unique issues, but four major issues will command attention from just about everyone.

The Big Four

Issue No. 1: Inflation.

Many of the other issues derive from this one, and it was the focus of much angst last year. The good news is that it appears there has been a peak in the expansion of global inflation. Europe just reported that inflation numbers fell below double digits for the first time in several months, but the pace has slowed somewhat. It is now at 8.5%, and the hope had been a decline under 8.0%, while the U.S. has seen the Consumer Price Index (CPI) drop to 7.1%.

The index the Federal Reserve uses for determining inflation is the Personal Consumption Expenditures (PCE) Price Index as it is more accurate than the CPI. The problem is that the PCE index tends to lag, and the most recent data is from December. The PCE dropped from 6.1 to 5.5, and the PCE index minus food and energy dropped from 5.0 to 4.6. It is clear enough that inflation has started to fade, but it is equally clear that it is still higher than preferred as the Fed would like to see PCE at no more than 2.5. What can be asserted at this stage is that we appear to have made it through the worst of it and can expect more improvement through the year.

Issue No. 2: Recession.

Inflation and recession are inextricably linked. The prime motivation for the recession threat now is the attempt on the part of central banks to thwart inflation with higher interest rates. The notion is that these banks hike rates until something breaks, and then they start to lower rates to fix what they broke. The fact that inflation has started to subside means that rate hikes may start to slow down and maybe reverse later this year. If one reads the comments by economists carefully, it is apparent they are not predicting as much as they are warning. Essentially the message is, if you keep up this financial pressure, you will see a recession. The call is for these central banks to ease off now that inflation seems to be retreating, and if they do so, the recession threat fades. The European Central Bank is slated to hike by another half point, and with the slowing pace of inflation reduction, there will likely be more.

The U.S. Federal Reserve is making the same noises. The bottom line is that they know full well that hiking rates puts pressure on the economy, but they are also mandated to deal with the ravages of runaway inflation. To stimulate, they lower the rate and to slow things down they raise it. The challenge now is determining whether they have slowed the economy enough to declare that inflation is no longer enough of a problem to warrant high rates. The messages they are getting from the economy are not clear.

Basically, there are two schools of thought competing with one another on the subject of recession. The true dismal scientists predict a recession in 2023 and a fairly deep one. They point to a variety of factors ranging from a slowdown in industrial production to slumping retail and the retreat seen in measures such as the Purchasing Managers’ Index. They mostly assert that the central banks are still committed to dealing with persistent inflation, and that will propel them toward even higher interest rates. They cite the comments by Jerome Powell as he indicated that inflation as measured by the PCE is still over twice as high as the Fed prefers. They want that rate at 2.0% or slightly below, and right now it stands at 4.6%.

The other school asserts that 2023 will see nothing more dramatic than a minor downturn that starts to evaporate by the second or third quarter. They look at the fact that inflation has started to erode (probably peaked at the end of Q4 2022).

They believe the Fed will slow down their rate hikes, and they point to the still solid job numbers. They look at the fall of commodity prices and the surge in industries such as automotive and aerospace.

Data from the Armada Strategic Intelligence System (ASIS) shows that we are somewhere in the middle. There was a substantial peak in 2022—a holdover from the rapid growth that was seen in 2021. That started to ebb last year and is expected to keep dropping for a while this year before tracking back up to what it was in the last decade. I know I have talked about the ASIS before (for more, check out

asisintelligence.com

), and we continue to be impressed by the performance—accuracy rates of close to 96% month after month. In the more detailed breakdowns, we see some significant variations: booms in automotive and aerospace, declines in machinery, less volatility in fabricated metal, and more volatility in primary metals.

Issue No. 3: Supply Chain.

This has been at the top of the list of concerns for months, but there are signs of improvement showing up. According to recent Producer Price Index numbers, there has been a reduction in factory gate prices, which reflects an improved supply chain. Companies have been able to find replacements for goods that came from China, and now there is potential improvement of that Chinese supply since the move away from the lockdowns. It is not that supply chain woes are over, as it is obvious that many sectors are still being affected. The good news is that it is less of a driver for inflation than was the case at the start of 2022. There has been much talk of a move away from globalization, but that is a little overly dramatic. A better description would be a move toward more supply chain diversity. If there is an alternative to China, manufacturers will be seeking it out.

Last year China missed its projected growth numbers. This was not unexpected given the impact of the lockdown and other factors that affected production and exports. The latest estimates for China’s GDP growth are for 5.0% this year—much more modest than had been expected as there has been an assumption that China would start to boom again once the lockdowns were lifted and the population had had an opportunity to recover from the COVID outbreak that soon followed. It appears the Xi regime would rather proudly proclaim that expectations were exceeded rather than have another year of disappointing results. It is also clear that Xi is continuing to consolidate power in the Chinese Communist Party as well as the government. His rivals have essentially been kicked out of all the powerful positions and have been replaced by loyalists who know better than to question any of the policies being put in place.

Issue No. 4: Labor.

This is certainly not a new or unique issue, but the situation has changed and presents different challenges in 2023. The chronic problem has been the worker shortage. Nothing substantial has been done to address this in years, and it is now hitting crisis level. By 2030 every single Boomer will have reached retirement age, and that is 74 million people (out of a total population of 330 million). These people will be very hard to replace, and it will be expensive. The primary way that companies have recruited new workers has been through poaching from one another. That is expensive as this requires higher pay, and it also means paying existing workers more so they are not poached. The inflation worries centered on supply chain and commodity prices last year, and in 2023 the focus will be on labor costs. The growth of "alternative" work further complicates this issue. The "gig economy" is a real thing and has drawn millions of people into a work environment that is not easy to categorize.

A Puzzling Year

There is a substantial degree of uncertainty to contend with, and that forces companies to develop a wide range of contingency plans. The outlook for the coming year depends on factors such as the Fed’s willingness to halt or even reverse rate hikes. There is a fear that political gamesmanship will lead to a default over the debt ceiling, which could shove the economy into reverse. Global activity will play a huge role as well. Does China finally resume its production activity, or has the world moved on enough to blunt that impact? Commodities have been down, but there are still major disruptive threats to oil supply as well as industrial metals. Leftist regimes in Latin America have already impacted copper, aluminum, and other metals. Europe asserts that it is no longer staring a recession in the face, at least not a severe one.

Does that mean more markets for U.S. goods or more competition from European producers? Probably both. The bottom line is that companies are facing a year of unknowns after a couple of years of predictability. We all knew 2020 and much of 2021 would be miserable, and we knew part of 2021 and 2022 would feature growth. We just don’t quite know what to do with 2023 yet.
