Dr. Chris Kuehl

A Trip Down the Black Hole of Economic Theory

I know you can barely control your excitement. Takes you back to those fun-filled hours in Econ 101, doesn’t it?

In truth, there are some key issues that are a bit easier to understand if one is aware of what economists think this all means. These include the question of which is worse, inflation or recession? Then there is the debate over what the "neutral" rate of the Fed Funds Rate is. Another important conversation is over what to make of the gloom merchants that predict the end of the world by 2030. There are lively debates over whether a low rate of joblessness is a good thing or not.

In the interests of full-disclosure, I am a devotee of Milton Friedman and the Chicago school. But I also have elements of the Austrian School of Ludwig Von Mises and Friedrich Hayek. In other words, conservative, free-market capitalist.

Take the issue of unemployment. It would seem a good thing to have that rate low, as that means that there are more consumers with money to drive an economy that relies on consumption for roughly 70% of its GDP. But low levels of unemployment have a cost as well—higher wages. The driver of inflation right now is wages as there is a worker shortage. We have seen very active unions and will continue to see this. It is hard to hire people, hard to motivate those who are hired, and these employees are more expensive and less productive than has been the case in years. How do we reverse some of these trends? A lot of layoffs make people a lot more grateful for having a job. However, too much unemployment and the consumption levels plummet. So, a balance has to be struck.

Which Is Worse, Recession or Inflation?

Most economists and central bankers will assert that it is inflation. The reality is that recession doesn’t hurt everybody and often benefits some companies and people. If your business is well run and comes into the recession with cash, there is an opportunity to buy up competitors or expand or develop opportunities upstream and downstream. A full 60% of the Fortune 100 expanded or started in a recession. Individuals with cash find lower prices everywhere.

Inflation clobbers everyone as money loses its buying power. The poor get hit the hardest, but the rich suffer as well. Everybody loses in an inflation surge, and that means that central banks will

always

pursue lower inflation with rate hikes, even if it means inviting a recession.

This is where that neutral rate discussion comes in. It has been assumed for decades that the neutral rate was 1.0%. Go over that and the economy slows; go under it and the economy speeds up. Now the thinking is that neutral may be 2.0% or even 2.5%. That changes the target for inflation. The Fed has operated on the assumption that 2.0% is the preferred rate, but if the neutral rate is 2.0%, the Fed could rework that assumption and conclude that 3.0% inflation is acceptable, and that is about where we are right now (3.9% annual inflation as measured by Personal Consumption Expenditures Price Index).

Fodder for the Doom Merchants

Finally, what should we do with the doom merchants chanting dirges for the end of the decade? Let’s be clear. Economists can’t predict next Tuesday, much less six or seven years from now. Why do it then? These are less forecasts than they are warnings. The economist is trying to get our attention. The real message is that there are major problems, and if they are not addressed, the likelihood is a major economic collapse.

We have six to seven years for the powers that be to deal with a massive government debt that could be as high as 150% of our GDP, the retirement of the entire Boomer generation (all 76 million of us), the massive shortfall in infrastructure maintenance (we get a D- from the civil engineers), the impact of climate change (regardless of who and what to blame, we know we are seeing change), chronic worker shortages, and so on. If these are not dealt with, the economy very likely hits that wall, so these strident assertions of a massive economic collapse are actually pleas to do something now to avert that fate. The cynic in me is not willing to hold my breath while hoping our leaders do much to avoid this pain.

There are some reasons for both optimism and pessimism when looking at all this. If the Fed keeps the focus on inflation, it will be helpful in terms of getting those costs under control. And if that invites a shallow recession of a quarter or two, there may be more people looking for work, fewer people demanding higher wages, and perhaps even a workforce happier to be employed. Much of the growth that unexpectedly showed up in the Q3 data from last year was due to inventory-build effects, and that means that manufacturers were starting to see the re-order cycle get back in sync.

And the winner is … Mexico

It is pretty obvious who the loser is when one looks at the issues of the supply chain crisis, investor angst, and geopolitical tension. China is losing its status as the low-cost production platform of choice. This has been reinforced by the observation that the rate of foreign direct investment has hit levels not seen in decades. It has further been noted that many other nations have stepped into that supply chain void in one way or another. India has advanced quickly and so has Vietnam, Thailand, and even Indonesia.

No nation has seen more of that shift than Mexico, however. At the very time that China has been slipping, the Mexican economy has been positioned to reap a large reward. This is not to say that Mexico’s problems have vanished overnight, and it is clear that all this expansion has meant far more to the northern sections of the country than to the central and southern regions.

Monterrey has become the epicenter of this expansion, and the data supports enthusiasm. Industrial real estate expansion has been on fire and vacancy rates are at less than 2.0%. There is a project underway to build the tallest skyscraper in the city, taller than the Empire State building. The city has attracted a great deal of attention from U.S. companies, as would be expected, but there has been a similar surge of interest from companies headquartered in South Korea, Japan, Germany, U.K., France, and even China.

The lure is obvious. Mexico is close to the U.S., has a business-friendly reputation, is surrounded by sophisticated trade schools, and has become a magnet for workers from all over Mexico. Mexico is now the No. 1 trade partner for the U.S. as it has surpassed both Canada and China.

The U.S. imports more from Mexico than from China, and the range of imports has widened far beyond the car parts and oil that once dominated trade. Mexico has always been capable of producing the consumer goods the U.S. fills Walmart shelves with, but China was cheaper. Now, that is not the case.

Mexico as a whole is growing at a faster clip than expected despite the significant inhibitions that have been thrown up by the government. AMLO (Andres Manuel Lopez Obrador) could never be described as pro-business, but the regional leaders in the north most certainly are. In January 2023, the estimate for Mexican growth was at around 1.0%. At this writing, it has been revised to 3.3%, and many assert that 3.5% is within range.

What stands in the way as far as Mexico’s progress? The growth doesn’t automatically eliminate the problems that have vexed Mexico for generations. This is not exactly the same situation as has long faced Brazil (the country of the future and always will be), but it is close. The key issues remain as they have for years—infrastructure, bureaucracy, and crime. The expansion of interest in northern Mexico has attracted workers from elsewhere in the country and from throughout Central and Latin America. This has strained the city’s resources, and it has become one of the most congested cities in the world. There is not enough housing, and there are woefully inadequate city services.

The legendary Mexican bureaucracy remains a huge problem and has been made worse under AMLO as he has been anything but supportive when the issue is business. His government is determined to be a hindrance as opposed to help. Crime remains a huge issue as the drug cartels are still strong given the proximity to the U.S. and the millions of desperate and vulnerable people that live in the region.

Expectations are high for 2024 as the elections feature two women who assert they are technocrats and committed to the development of the economy. Claudia Scheinbaum is former governor of Mexico City and a member of AMLO’s party. A scientist by training, she has a reputation for pragmatism and seems less hostile to the U.S. than AMLO. She would not only be the first woman to become president but the first Jewish leader of the country. Her opponent is very strong in the north as leader of the PAN party, but she also has support in the southern regions as her father is from the indigenous population. Xochitl Galvez is a former business consultant and also described as a technocrat. Mexico makes history this year, and the potential exists for a much closer relationship with the U.S.

It is not that U.S. manufacturers have been unaware of Mexico; there has been enormous expansion in the last few years. Manufacturing now accounts for almost 90% of Mexican exports. The potential for extremely close connections now exists as U.S. companies become engaged and aggressively partner with Mexican counterparts.
