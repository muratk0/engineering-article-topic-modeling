What? Me Worry?

What Happened to Stagflation? Production Stoppages? Food Shortages?

Dr. Chris Kuehl

Many have endured some serious disasters of late. For those in the thick of it, the economy becomes a lot less important, especially when lives are at stake. Still, during all of this, when thinking about the economy, it’s important to take the long view.

Take your mind back a couple of years to when the issue of economic collapse and sky-high inflation first started to dominate the headlines. Granted, this was the era of overwrought media and politicians, as nobody had a real sense of how to handle the pandemic and the subsequent economic crisis that accompanied an attempt to shut down the entire world.

The Habit of Catastrophizing

If you recollect the warnings, there were assertions of an imminent return to the disasters of the 1970s. There would certainly be some kind of stagflation, perhaps worse than it was before. There would be a certain global recession as all production would stop in the wake of a consumer lockdown. Oil prices would soar to around $200 a barrel due to the sanctions imposed on Russia, and there would be a global food crisis due to that same attack on Russia and Ukraine. The sense was that the world was on the edge of the next Great Recession, and it was time to build a yurt in the backyard and learn to eat insects.

Fast forward to today. It is not as if we have avoided every economic challenge, but there has been no stagflation. Inflation grew fast, to be sure, which prompted a lot of central bank reaction, but the growth has been there as well. In fact, it has been that very growth that helped fuel inflation.

GDP and Inflation

The U.S. has been averaging growth of over 2.0% a quarter for nearly two years, and Europe has managed to avoid actually slipping into a recession as measured by quarters of negative growth. Even Japan has been growing fast enough to prompt the Bank of Japan to hike its historically high rates. There have been nations facing economic slowdowns, but many have surged as well.

The central banks came under a lot of criticism for not acting fast enough to quell inflation, but in truth the surge in prices was a result of panic behavior by governments trying to react to the pandemic challenge. The banks reacted, and the threat subsided. The pace of inflation is down now, but the price hikes will linger for a long while. Are they cutting fast enough now? Only time will tell, but the impact from a rate reduction is far slower than one from a hike.

Automotive Sector Shows Some Improvement

The latest data on the automotive sector shows an improvement in production. The forecast calls for the performance to move above the 20-year trend line in the next several months. Most of the producer price categories have trended up slightly, but there are still declines in sales of new and used vehicles.

The question now is whether there will be a reaction to the lowered interest rates. The decision by the Federal Reserve to reduce rates by a half point rather than the predicted quarter point will not immediately force auto loans down, but there will be a reaction within the next few months as there will be a rush by dealers to take advantage of the lowered financing costs.

The automotive sector has long been something of an overall indicator for the economy. The purchase of a vehicle is the largest commitment the average person makes outside their home.

Traditionally, three factors motivate people to buy. The most important is job stability. People will be reluctant to take on debt if they are unsure about their jobs, and lenders will be cautious if there is no stability.

The second factor is the cost of the vehicle, and that should be a disincentive at the moment given that the average price of a vehicle is still $47,870. This is down from the level set in 2022 ($49,929) but remains much higher than it was prior to the pandemic.

The factors that drove prices higher were primarily in the supply chain, but there has been a marked improvement. The good news (to a degree) is that buyers rarely pay much attention to the total price and only concern themselves with the monthly payment. The average monthly payment for a $50,000 car is over $700.

The last factor is need. Does the current car need to be replaced? The average age of a vehicle is now close to 13 years, and many simply elect to keep repairing rather than to buy new.

The issues that had been affecting the manufacturing of vehicles had been fading as the supply chain started to correct, but now that threat has reemerged with the shortage of container ships and containers. This is due to the fact that shipping through the Red Sea has come to a halt. The insurance sector will not cover this transit as the Houthi attacks have been impossible to thwart. The inflation on container shipping has been stunning—up over 200%, even on routes that do not involve the Red Sea.

China Evolves on the World Stage

There has been a shift in attitude regarding China. The notion of China as a trade partner and a fully-fledged member of the global economy has altered substantially. It is clearly the enemy now, and nations are focused on blunting its influence in many ways. This means impacting its exports of nearly everything.

China has been flooding the world with cheap steel for years. The fact is that China has over 450 steel mills and doesn’t need the output from those mills as the property market has collapsed and the economy has slowed. In a market system, these mills would have been shut down. But the emphasis in China is preserving jobs, and these mills stay open and keep churning out steel that is subsequently dumped on the rest of the world.

There have been complaints from U.S., Japanese, Brazilian, and European steel producers, and the EU is now getting intense pressure to establish much stricter tariffs and other limitations. The threat is that Europe will lose virtually all of its domestic production capacity, and the U.S., Japan, and others are worried as well.

China is facing a nearly united front on the subject of tariffs and restrictions—and not just on steel. The political winds have shifted dramatically in the U.S., Europe, Japan, and nearly everywhere else. The old trade pattern was designed to favor the consumer, and that came at the expense of the producers in these nations. Now the desire has shifted to protect domestic industry from export-centered nations like China. These barriers will be applied to others as well—India, Vietnam, and perhaps even Mexico.

Just a few years ago, western nations were more than willing to cede the development of rare earth materials to China and a few other developing nations. Getting these materials could be an environmental disaster involving extensive strip mining and toxic processing. Better to let a nation like China deal with all that. Then came the realization that western nations had become utterly dependent on China for materials that drive high-tech.

Now, a coalition of 14 western nations has formed the Minerals Security Partnership. It is designed to focus development finance and export credit agencies on an effort to work with private industry to develop these resources in the U.S., Canada, Australia, and other nations. This effort will still have to contend with the opposition to these often-destructive extraction efforts.

Western nations are desperately vulnerable when it comes to rare earths and other critical minerals. China has already restricted the exports of materials such as germanium, gallium, and antimony. There are many more products that could face restriction, and many recognize that this must be addressed.

Don’t Overreact

Economic challenges remain, from uncertainty over the election to seemingly unceasing global conflict, including a broader war in the Middle East. The critical lesson from all this may be to temper the tendency to react hysterically to shifts. The last few years have featured one massive overreaction after another, and analysts have been falling all over themselves trying to sound dire warnings. This is not to assert that problems don’t exist. There are plenty of them. The point is that there are always steps to be taken and policies pursued that ameliorate these impacts.

If there is one lesson to learn from all this, it may be to avoid the tendency to panic and overdramatize. It is not that the economy has been without reversals, but the gloom and doom is often not justified. Economies as large as that of the U.S. can be very versatile and extraordinarily resilient.
