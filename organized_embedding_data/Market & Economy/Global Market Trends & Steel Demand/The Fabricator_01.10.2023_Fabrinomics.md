The Economist’s Favorite Codeword:

MIXED

Dr. Chris Kuehl

What does it mean when data is described as

mixed

? This has to be one of the most common of economic catch-phrases and seems to say,

We have no idea what is going on

. In some respects that is true, but the "mixed" description goes deeper than that. What we are seeing now is not uncommon with an economy the size of the U.S. Some sectors are doing quite well and others are underperforming.

The U.S. generally avoids complete recessions and instead experiences sector recessions and regional recessions. The overall consensus among economists is that the U.S. is not going to hit a true recession this year or in 2024. Growth rates have been above 2.0%, and few see them falling significantly. Given this, what is the conclusion those connected to the economy should reach?

Simply stated, being in the right place at the right time will permit some pretty healthy growth, and if one is in the wrong place at the wrong time, there is a good chance of decline. The question is, what sector is likely to be in that growth environment a quarter or two from now? What sectors will not see that growth, and what are the factors that would alter this assessment?

The Global View, and the Sinking PMI

The U.S. version of the Purchasing Manager Index (PMI) is tracking similarly to that in Europe with readings in the mid-40s. They were in the 60s just a year ago. But in Europe especially, contraction is well underway.

The Eurozone readings in late summer fell to a 33-month low of 47. The decline was triggered by an unexpected decline in the services sector combined with continued deterioration in the manufacturing sector. The fall was more precipitous than economists had expected as they thought it would slip a little to maybe 48.5. The real worry is the abrupt fall in the service sector as thus far these businesses have seemed to be resilient. The majority of the downward shift has been attributed to a return of inflation threat as energy prices went up and wage pressures increased substantially. Wage costs have driven the service sector down, and this will likely have an impact on the ECB. They are less likely to raise rates with this new data as it no longer supports their optimism regarding Eurozone growth.

Data Points to Growth—in Certain Sectors

Our models show some robust growth in U.S. manufacturing through the bulk of this year, but questions start to emerge in 2024. One of the consistent sectors for expansion has been motor vehicles. Our data has shown it above the trend line for months. It has been driven by a combination of factors — pent-up demand from 2020, the age of the U.S. fleet overall (12.5 years), and the steady employment numbers that have allowed people to borrow. The threat comes from the high price of a new car and the tightening of loan standards. The expectation is still growth into 2024.

Aerospace has been getting more robust after a dramatic drop in 2020, but it is still below the 10- and 20-year trend line. This growth will accelerate, however, as the demand factors are very strong, and the growth is likely to be above the trend line in 2024.

The machinery sector has been slumping for the last several months but has started to make a major turn toward the positive. This has been driven by the continued investment into robotics and technology. The labor shortage is not going away, and that forces even smaller manufacturers to invest in machines. The reshoring pace has accelerated as well, and that also boosts this kind of investment. Capital spending ebbed but remains historically high as adapting to new technology demands investment. The concern is that this wave of investment may have peaked, and it might be a while before further investment is needed.

Overall, the electrical equipment sector is expected to grow above the trend line but should start to slow down a bit next year. There is a significant factor that has just started to manifest and could alter the trajectory. The effort to push conversion to electric is well underway, and it is loaded with incentives for consumers and producers alike. If the consumer does indeed respond and shifts, the demand levels will accelerate. The computer and electronics sector has slowed a bit but is still in a growth environment. The rush to accommodate increased reliance on communication and tech has eased, but it is still a motivator.

What does one do with all this strategically? There are three points to take from the data thus far. First, the rapid growth in 2021 and 2022 came to an end but did not go into reverse. Second, the threat of a recession in 2023 was somewhat overstated, although slowdowns were noted in sectors like retail and residential construction.

The third point is the most important takeaway. The data shows 2024 as a growth year, although not one that will move at a blistering pace. The GDP numbers expected are very close to the 25-year average, and that means that companies can count on expanded demand and growth opportunities, but not so robust as to compensate for bad decisions. The chief focus for manufacturers will continue to be labor — shortages as well as higher wage demands. The second key concern will be the cost of commodities and other inputs as these will be affected as much by geopolitics as the economic drivers of supply and demand.

In general, the data supports a pretty positive outlook, and manufacturing has played a key role in avoiding the recession that was expected by this time. There had been projections of a significantly weaker economy by this point (negative numbers in the first and second quarter). Instead, there has been growth close to the 20-year average of 2.5%. The demand for exports has improved and consumers are still active, but there is a major issue looming: inventory.

About Inventory

When business inventories build in the U.S., it is good news for GDP. It adds to broader economic growth activity. But, in this cycle, it is bad news for areas of the supply chain that are already being impacted by bloated business inventories. In the latest data for the inventory-to-sales-ratio, the ratio was 6.9% higher year-over-year in April (latest available). When compared to historical measures, it is performing similar to what we saw in 2014 and 2015—which is bad news for the broader logistics and supply chain industry.

Today, there is too much inventory, and that has all but destroyed the usual reorder cycle. That profoundly affects the manufacturer counting on those orders to replenish stock. According to the latest inventory-to-sales ratios (latest available was June), 65.2% of the product side of the economy was overstocked relative to the decade prior to the pandemic.

The good news is that the general merchandise stores (like Walmart and Target) are balanced headed into the peak season. If they go in lighter, they could come out much leaner and be ready to restart their supply chains. When companies are overstocked (like 65% are today), they stop ordering new products (which we see in the manufacturing reports). That shuts off the upstream portion of the supply chain (procurement of raw materials, use of energy, hiring workers, transportation of inputs, etc.).

One factor that could make this even worse is that there is a big discrepancy between the Chinese Yuan and Japanese Yen and the dollar. This helped boost Japan’s GDP as exports surged over the past quarter. If companies see this as a buying opportunity (especially for those companies sitting on a bunch of cash, so they don’t have to use debt at high interest rates to purchase these products), the overstock situation could get worse.

Manufacturing depends on consistency. Everything in the process relies on some level of systematic predictability. There is an expectation that what a given manufacturer produces will be used up and that more of that output will then be required. This keeps the system moving optimally. When there is an interruption in that pattern, there will be issues. This usually comes because demand falters — the impact of a recession. This inventory-led crisis is led by too much accumulation. Companies that lost faith in the traditional supply chain started to load up on inventory to protect themselves from shortages, and now they have far too much to reasonably work through quickly. The load in inventory has stymied normal production patterns. The thinking is that these gluts will begin to erode by the end of the year or early in 2024.
