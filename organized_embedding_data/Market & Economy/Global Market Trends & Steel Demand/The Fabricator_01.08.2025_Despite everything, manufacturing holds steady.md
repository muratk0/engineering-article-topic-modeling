# Despite everything, manufacturing holds steady

[TARİH: 01.08.2025 The Fabricator]

Fabrinomics | Economic Insights

Automotive holds its own, data center demand continues

Dr. Chris Kuehl

Even with all the gyrations created by tariffs and tariff threats, data on manufacturing is showing remarkable stability. Many factors could shove the projection up or down, and tariffs are just one.

The more interesting development is a discussion of the NTB, or nontariff barrier. At this writing (in early July), 73% of the barriers to imports are rooted in NTBs. These are far more permanent than tariffs as they are based on regulatory issues or rulings on trade practices, such as dumping. We block a lot of products that have been deemed unsafe or inappropriately produced, and changing these rules can be extremely complex.

It has been surprising that manufacturing has held up as well as it has given the uncertainty surrounding trade patterns. There seem to be two primary reasons for this. The most important has been the decision to buy ahead to beat the tariffs. There was a huge influx of imports as the tariff fights started—enough to distort the Q1 GDP numbers. This insulated manufacturers to some degree, but that period may be coming to an end as those inventories are getting depleted, and companies will have to start the reorder process.

All this said, if one asks the average manufacturer whether they think the sector is doing better or worse than before, there will be no consensus whatsoever. It all depends on the sector one serves. Automotive is holding its own (more on this below) but aerospace is still in a funk. Construction (especially nonresidential) continues to drive much of manufacturing, and there has been demand from the medical sector as well. Retail and hospitality are both down. The initial impact of the tariffs has been impossible to determine as they have been too erratic. The big surge in manufacturing was driven by companies trying to build inventory as a way to dodge the immediate impact of the tariffs—but what happens when they move from that strategy?

The All-Powerful Automotive Sector

There is no sector of the U.S. economy more influential to manufacturers and fabricators than the automotive sector. It may not quite be as vital an industry as it was when the head of GM commented that "what is good for GM is good for the U.S.," but it is close. Many have asserted auto demand would soon crater, given all the consumer stress, but this has not happened as yet (and may never take place given the ongoing love affair with the car). The most recent data is still solid, though there could be some negative trends emerging later in the year depending on tariffs and inflation factors.

Automotive inventories have started to see some signs of overall recovery, but there is a stark difference according to the vehicle model. Dealers are reporting weak demand for lower-priced vehicles as these are aimed at the lower third of income earners (making $50,000 a year or less). They are barely making it from paycheck to paycheck and have no ability to buy any kind of vehicle. Trucks, SUVs and CUVs are still the dominant sellers.

The vehicle sector is truly a global business. It uses parts from all over the planet; assemblies and subassembly operations dot the planet as well. If all of the threatened tariffs are imposed, major manufacturing disruptions will ensue and inventory levels will falter. The sense is that automotive will be getting more than its share of exemptions, but nothing is secure these days. The most important concession thus far has been made toward Canada and Mexico due to the USMCA. This formal trade agreement between the three nations has more legal and functional stability.

Mexico and Canada have been at the forefront of discussion over auto manufacturing and tariffs. The two nations are intimately tied to the U.S. process, with a vehicle crossing the borders several times during its assembly. Mexico plays a significant role in U.S. auto manufacturing, though its share remains less than a quarter of the total. The country’s manufacturing capacity has grown over time, contributing to 18% of its total manufacturing output, with more than 50 automobile manufacturers operating there. Canada has fewer operations, but they are generally larger facilities. It is next to impossible to separate the assembly process, and the majority of the export and import activity is intra-company.

Sales continue to be determined primarily by income group. As I have noted before, the consumer sector has divided into three parts. The upper third (income of $100,000 and above), the middle third (between $50,000 and $100,00), and the lower third (below $50,000). The impact of inflation has been very different for all three groups. The higher rates wrecked the lower third, who are barely staying afloat—no vehicle buying in this cohort. The upper-end vehicles are selling briskly as this group has money. That leaves the middle group, and they are spending as long as they think their jobs are secure. As unemployment remains at record lows, the expectation is they will keep spending on vehicles as well as other higher-value items.

The Data Center, Energy, and Manufacturing

At first blush, it would not seem that big advances in a tech field would mean all that much to manufacturers, but the infrastructure required to support this surge is impressive. Machines are needed to build the facilities and machines to run the processes. The level of metal needed will be comparable to sectors such as automotive, aerospace, and medical, and the growth numbers are nearly exponential.

The data center is officially the "thing" in terms of construction and economic development. It is estimated that a new data center is being built every week in the U.S., and it has been suggested that an additional 44 terawatts of power will be needed to handle the data centers being constructed for AI alone. The assertion is that every business will be more reliant on technology and AI than ever, and the one limiting factor will be energy.

Colocation data-center capacity in the U.S. has already doubled since 2020, and by 2030 the country is projected to account for more than 37% of global demand, adding nearly 85 gigawatts of capacity over the next six years—up from 25 gigawatts in 2024. Construction activity in the sector is projected to hit $18.35 billion in 2025 and climb to $41.4 billion annually by 2030. These are impressive numbers, but many assert they are understated.

Multinational corporations have announced plans to invest over $120 billion in U.S.-based high-tech projects, all requiring significant construction efforts. They are projected to break ground in late 2025 or 2026. Further investment in U.S. data center infrastructure is also anticipated, with strong interest from international companies looking to establish operations domestically to sidestep potential trade and tariff disputes. A new motivation has been developing as companies try to determine the course of tariffs and other trade issues. Many operations are moving back to the U.S. to avoid the potential limitations.

The e-commerce market continues to surpass expectations, with global growth projected to average more than 10% annually through 2028. The market is currently valued at $6.09 trillion and is expected to reach over $83 trillion by 2030 (a staggering compounded annual growth rate of over 18%), with U.S. sales alone projected to hit $1.3 trillion in 2025. Increasing consumer demand for faster and continuous order processing, streamlined automation, efficient return management, and online sales support are driving a significant rise in the need for advanced data handling and processing capabilities.

The U.S. e-commerce market had an 7.8% growth rate in 2024, up from 7% in 2023, and 2025 is expected to hit 8.5%. Inflationary pressures and stable wage growth of nearly 4% annually have helped boost consumer spending. Growth in 2025 is expected to remain between 8% and 9%. Being able to process data quickly will continue to drive demand for data center capacity. The Boomer has become the dominant user since the pandemic due to ease of delivery.

This has been a classic case of good news and bad. The good news is that demand for data center support is nearly exponential. The bad news is that many of the needed resources for that expansion are in very short supply. It will take a lot of ancillary development to take full advantage of this growth. Energy demand is a major concern, with an estimated 44 terawatts of additional power needed over the next five to six years to support AI alone (not including other demands such as cooling systems). Texas alone will require at least 25 new "peaker" plants to handle peak demand, and various energy sources—including oil, gas, nuclear, solar, wind, and coal— will be explored to meet these growing needs.

Workforce shortages pose another challenge, as the construction sector is already strained, and the specialized skills required for data center development are in particularly short supply, with little focus on workforce training. Regulatory approval processes further complicate expansion, often taking months or even years, though efforts are underway to streamline these procedures. Finally, rapid technological advancements mean that today’s infrastructure may quickly become outdated, with many data centers built just a few years ago already requiring upgrades or replacements. While these challenges could slow expansion, they also create opportunities for FMA members, particularly in power development, workforce solutions, and infrastructure upgrades.
