# Companies make last-ditch effort to thwart Chinese tariffs

[TARİH: 01.08.2024 The Fabricator]

Around Washington

White House still lacks success in tackling Mexican-fabricated products made of Chinese steel slipping over the border into the U.S.

Stephen Barlas

A

s President Joe Biden and his administration close in on decisions regarding higher tariffs on Chinese metal and metalworking equipment, metal fabricators are in many cases opposing what would be tariffs rising from a current 7.5% to 25%. That would be on top of the 25% tariff on imported steel from many countries, including China, imposed by former President Donald Trump in March 2018.

In an interview, Todd Adams, the owner of Sanitube, which imports Chinese-made steel tubes for its dairy process equipment manufacturing, said his total Chinese tariff load would be around 57%. Even though his two American competitors do not import the same Chinese tubes, they would be able to raise their prices to take advantage of Sanitube’s higher costs.

"The Chinese companies we import from are small, privately held, and outside of the purview of China’s network of large, state-owned or subsidized enterprises," he said. "This tariff code does not comprise products requiring a high level of proprietary technology, intellectual property, or innovation for their production."

One might argue that dairy equipment manufacturing is a very small sector of the economy compared to health care. Soumi Saha, senior vice president, government affairs, Premier Inc., a major supplier of hospital equipment, added in her comments to the U.S. Trade Representative (USTR), "With steel serving as a primary component in medical devices, health care furniture, and surgical equipment, there is concern that tariffs may impact the health care supply chain as upwards of 53% of global steel supply is derived from China."

Companies facing higher Chinese tariffs had very little time to adjust prices and make any supply chain accommodations before Aug. 1, which was the date that the USTR planned to put the new tariffs in place.

Even companies that support the higher tariffs do so conditionally in some instances. That’s the case with Thomas & Skinner Inc., which imports flatrolled alloy silicon electrical steel from China. Ed Richardson, one of the company’s principals, told the USTR that, for the tariff to be effective, it must "also apply to Chinese electrical steel that is slit, cut, stamped, or otherwise processed in NAFTA-based operations, prior to importing into the United States." The Biden administration has been working with Mexico on the problem of Chinese steel dodging tariffs and making its way into the U.S. through Mexico in metal fabricated forms, but it has only a limited amount of success to show for the work. Electrical steel is used in electric vehicles, Department of Defense weapon systems, and the U.S. power grid.

Some manufacturing equipment used by steel fabricators could be exempt from the higher Chinese tariffs. For example, Thomas Rosensweet of Newport Metals wants an exemption for magnesium anodes used to prevent corrosion in underground gas, oil, and water pipes and propane tanks.

"To our knowledge, it is only made in China. There is no U.S. industry to protect, so the tariff amounts to a tax that is ultimately paid by the users of gas, oil, and water, virtually everyone who lives in the United States," he told the USTR.

New Overtime Rules for Salaried Workers

New overtime payment rules went into effect on July 1. The Department of Labor (DOL) raised the threshold for which salaried workers are entitled to overtime. Those thresholds are applied to two categories:

regular

and

highly

compensated workers.

Currently set at $35,568 per year for regular roles, it will rise to $43,888 per year once the rule is implemented July 1, 2025, and then increase to $58,656 on Jan. 1, 2026. Additionally, the threshold for highly compensated employees will increase from $107,432 to $132,964 and then to $151,164 at the start of next year. Moreover, the rule mandates automatic updates to these thresholds every three years, starting on July 1, 2027.

There have been at least three lawsuits filed seeking to stop the new levels from going into effect. A federal judge issued an injunction late in June stopping implementation of the new provisions for state workers in Texas. The other lawsuits have broader potential and argue primarily that the DOL did not follow the Administrative Procedure Act in the rulemaking.
