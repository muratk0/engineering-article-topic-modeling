# "Summer doldrums" could start early this year

[TARİH: 01.06.2025 The Fabricator]

Steel News

A consensus is shaping up that flat-rolled steel could be in for a slow summer

MICHAEL COWDEN

O

EMs typically take downtime in June and July. It’s one of the reasons why you rarely see prices shoot higher in the early summer. (I sometimes wonder why mills do outages in the spring when their customers do them in the summer, but I digress.)

Sure, mills might announce price hikes over the summer, but often it’s to try to slow any price declines.

In recent years, prices have regularly peaked in April, slipped in the summer, and then recovered in the fall. (Let’s forget last year, which was a little unusual. There was no recovery in the fall—just one very long bottom and no recovery until winter—but you get the picture.)

Generally speaking, we’ve seen sheet prices rise in the summer only if there has been an external shock, such as the surprise imposition of Section 232 tariffs on Canada and Mexico in 2018 or the unexpected rebound in demand throughout 2021 following a pandemic-stricken 2020. We saw prices rise through the summer in both years.

Peak Tariffs in the Rearview Mirror?

You could make a case that the big external shocks of 2025 are already past us.

The first shock came in February from the imposition of tariffs on Canada, Mexico, and China. That was under the International Emergency Economic Powers Act (IEEPA) and framed as necessary to combat trafficking of illegal drugs.

Another shock came in March from the reimposition of Section 232 tariffs of 25% on imported steel from U.S. allies who previously had been exempt from the duty.

We arguably hit peak tariff madness on April 2—aka Liberation Day–when President Donald Trump’s administration clobbered the entire world with tariffs. You could argue that’s when the tariff action hit the point where it could never be topped, leaving only room for retreat.

The panic buying we saw earlier in the year halted. Uncertainty took over, and buyers moved to the sidelines. Among the questions that gave them pause: Was removing Section 232 quotas really a good idea, and would 25% be enough to stop some of the lower-priced import options?

Tariffs Remain Unpopular Among Steel Buyers

Steel Market Update has meanwhile seen support for Trump’s tariffs plunge among steel buyers. We’ve been asking people since March whether tariffs help their business, hurt their business, or whether they’re not sure.

Steel prices, which normally fall a bit during the summer, appear to be heading downward even before the summer season officially begins. This mostly seems to be linked to a forecast downturn in demand.

claffra/iStock/Getty Images Plus

About one-third of survey respondents have always been against Trump’s tariffs, and that hasn’t changed since March. What’s changed is that the roughly one-third of people who said tariffs helped their business has dropped into the single digits.

Meanwhile, survey participants report little evidence of reshoring. Short-term pain for long-term gain is a tough sell in a democracy. Just ask former President Joe Biden how that worked out when it came to infrastructure and other initiatives.

Let’s Make a Deal

Maybe that’s why we’ve seen the U.S. and the United Kingdom take steps to reduce trade barriers. The tentative agreements could allow the U.K. to import more cars into the U.S. It also opens the possibility that Section 232 tariffs on imports of U.K. steel and aluminum could be eliminated or moderated.

The U.K. is not exactly a titan when it comes to steel. It exported approximately 265,000 short tons of steel to the U.S. last year. Canada and Mexico, in contrast, shipped more than 10 million tons to the U.S.

The concern for domestic steelmakers (and the opportunity for steel consumers) is that the U.K. deal could set a template for reducing tariffs elsewhere.

That might sound very familiar. The Biden administration on Oct. 31, 2021, reached an agreement to ease Section 232 against the European Union. (I remember because I had to drop everything and work while trying to get two kids ready for trick-ortreating.) That agreement served as a template for subsequent deals with Japan and the U.K.

What happens to U.S. steel prices if deals are announced with Canada, Mexico, South Korea, Brazil, and other countries that have been big players in the U.S. market in recent years? Sure, U.S. prices have slipped, but they’re still well above those of the rest of the world. Additionally, U.S. steel prices dwarf those in Southeast Asia—thus all the concern about hotrolled coil imports from Vietnam.

Concerns About Demand Remain

I’ll use Cleveland-Cliffs’ first quarter earnings call in May—and the idlings announced ahead of them—to illustrate that point.

One thing was particularly telling: Cliffs said on the call that Canadian flat-rolled steelmaker Stelco, which it acquired in November 2024 for $2.5 billion, was selling all its production within Canada in response to U.S. tariffs. Cliffs Chairman, President, and CEO Lourenco Goncalves made clear that he wasn’t happy about the situation. He suggested that he wouldn’t have bought Stelco if he had known that tariffs on Canada were just around the corner. Also, he stressed that the U.S. should return to treating Canada as the friend and ally it has traditionally been.

Still, I thought there might have been at least a silver lining to that cloud. Stelco had been a thorn in the side of U.S. mills, particularly integrated mills along the Great Lakes. With Stelco’s tons stuck in Canada, surely Cliffs’ U.S. mills would be running full?

And yet that doesn’t seem to be what happened. I was surprised to see Cliffs announce that it would be idling its hot-strip mill in Riverdale, Ill., just outside of Chicago. The company said it would transfer that business to its mill just across the state line in East Chicago, Ind.

The steelmaker used similar logic when it idled the hot end of its mill in Dearborn, Mich., and transferred the business to its mill in Cleveland. All told, the company has idled or planned to idle mines, flat-rolled mills, its rail mill, and a plate-finishing facility—approximately 2,000 workers facing layoffs. That’s a big turn of events for a company that came close to acquiring U.S. Steel in 2023!

All that aside, Goncalves has said that he wants to produce 16 million tons per year, or 4 million tons per quarter. If Cliffs wants to maintain that market share, or grow it, then it probably will have to reduce prices. If Cliffs does that, so will other mills.

Timing the Restock

I’m not saying this is a "death spiral," as steel industry guru Peter Marcus used to call the steep declines in prices that occur from time to time in the notoriously cyclical steel industry. It’s just hard to see, as we head into a typically slow time of year for steel, any good reasons for prices to go up.

Except for this one: Inventories aren’t low. But they aren’t high either. Let’s assume that day-to-day business continues. Maybe it’s not as good as some had hoped it would be earlier in the year, but it’s not terrible either.

At some point, steel buyers will have to step back into the market to restock. Does that happen in July, in August, or in the fall? I don’t know. That’s always hard to handicap.

What isn’t is that buyers tend to jump back into the market at the same time, so maybe we’ll see a bump in prices when that restock happens—even if it’s hard to know exactly when that will be.
