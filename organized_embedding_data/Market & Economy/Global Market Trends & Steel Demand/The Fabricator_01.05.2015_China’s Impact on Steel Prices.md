# China’s Impact on Steel Prices

[TARİH: 01.05.2015 The Fabricator]

One of the issues that matters most to the average manufacturer is the cost of the metals that will be used in production — perhaps the most obvious statement one could make at this point. For the last few months the prices of these metals have been falling, and this has analysts in something of a quandary. In 2011 iron ore hit its all-time high price of $120 a ton, and since then it has fallen 60 percent to its current price of $46.70. The fall in the price of iron ore has now captured the attention of many as there is an expectation of a further drop in the price of steel as a result.

The big three mining companies (Rio Tinto, BHP Billiton, and Vale) are still trying to adjust to a world where they do not have the ability to set and control prices as was the case for many decades. The Chinese had a lot to do with breaking that system, and for the last few years it has been the demand for steel in China that has affected the overall price for iron ore and by extension steel itself. The problem with China as the determinant of price is that the steel is extremely politicized. Steel is as much "symbol" as "business" in China — a sign of modernization and strategic security. It is a business that every region wants to have a piece of as it gives that region status and provides jobs. The result has been overproduction and the need to get rid of the accumulated output.

While China was engaged in a massive infrastructure build, the steel could be absorbed to some degree, but this pace has slowed dramatically over the last few years. Now steel is essentially being dumped on the global market at prices that may well be under what it cost to make it. This has also meant a slowing of Chinese production and therefore a collapse in the price of iron ore as China now needs far less.

Thus far this all seems to mean that prices will keep falling, but at some point the producers are forced to react to these conditions and reduce their output to match demand. This is a familiar pattern and one that eventually results in bottlenecks, shortages, and ultimately higher prices.
