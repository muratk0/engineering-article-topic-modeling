igh service center inventories, high U.S. import levels, and uncertainties accompanying low oil prices continued to drive questions about the long-term outlook for steel market finished prices—even in the face of continuing strong manufacturing trends and the improving U.S. economy.

Hot-rolled coil prices had begun to break below the $600/ton level free-on-board Midwestern steel mills, down substantially from about $655/ton in mid-October and $675/ton two months earlier. Cold-rolled coil averaged $745/ton in mid-December, down from $775/ton in October and $790/ton in August.

Purchasing executives noted that the energy sector has been an important driver of steel consumption in recent years, and that while lower energy costs are often thought of as a net positive for industrial customers, the fall in oil prices could result in "too much of a good thing."

Although some steel prices have been dropping, U.S. raw steel production also continued at a high level with both production and plant utilization higher than they had been a year earlier.

Iron ore prices were cut in half during the year and were ending 2014 at five-year lows, around $70/metric ton. China, the global leader in steel production, has reduced production and was not expected to increase output substantially through the winter, according to analysts.

Steel manufacturers noted that U.S. finished steel imports in the first 10 months of 2014 had increased 32 percent compared to the same period in 2013 and that total steel imports were up 37 percent. Finished steel imports had captured 28 percent of the market this year compared to 23 percent a year earlier, according to the American Iron and Steel Institute, which expected that trend toward historically high import market share to continue.

U.S. steel service centers had three months of steel supply on hand at the end of November and reported that inventories of 9.2 million tons were 15.2 percent higher than at the same point a year earlier. Based on service center shipment levels, year-end inventories totaling three months of supply represented the highest inventory to shipment level of the year, up from 2.4 months in October and 2.6 months a year earlier.

Ferrous scrap prices rose by as much as $10/ton in December because of tight supplies of obsolete scrap grades like heavy melt and shredded scrap. That gain has bolstered dealers’ expectations that scrap prices will be higher in January. Most are figuring on a $10/ton increase, but some believe prices will rise by $20/ton or more.

The $60/ton price cuts in October and November, along with the seasonal slowdown in scrap collections, have reduced the flows into dealers’ yards. Low year-end inventories at some mills could be another driving force. Some mills typically buy less scrap in December. Dealers were banking on mills needing to rebuild inventories in January and paying more.

Several domestic mills bought shredded from the docks and large Eastern shredders during November and December, but were still short scrap. Some were offering $20/ton more than they had paid at the start of the month for additional scrap. Dealers in several regions of the Midwest had not offered much scrap in December in part because intake was lower but also because they were expecting prices to rise in January. Scrap sold the prior month at lower prices but not shipped that month must be delivered to the mills at that price the next month.

A Look at Nonferrous Metals

As 2014 came to an end, global commodity prices for nonferrous metals had dropped significantly. Important changes in world economies have reduced overall demand (some geographical tightness still exists) and created surpluses, resulting in much lower prices and premiums. China, as the world’s largest consumer of industrial metals (40 percent), has finally slowed down. The unsustainable double-digit growth of the Chinese economy is now expected to grow at 7 percent this year. European and Japanese economies are at recessionlike levels with new monetary policies being explored. Adding further worry is the uncertainty of the Russian government with falling crude oil prices along with a tumbling ruble.

The lower activity in China during the second half of 2014 is expected to continue at least into the first quarter of 2015. Both refined and scrap copper have been especially affected by the slowdown because wire and cable products are major components in commercial buildings, homes, and the automotive sectors. Premiums are expected to continue to drop as the metal is no longer being absorbed in China, but needs to find a home in the U.S. or Europe. In the U.S., copper scrap inventory is already building as scrap dealers wait for better prices.

Aluminum is facing a similar situation with the exception of the automotive sector. Japanese and U.S. Midwest premiums may have peaked at $430/metric ton and 23.75 cents/lb., respectively. Manufacturers and traders suggest that demand may be picking up after the first quarter, with premiums staying flat or falling slightly. Scrap has become more available and allowed traders to fill their January into February orders for mills.

Both zinc and lead premiums are expected to be higher for 2015, supported by strong fundamentals. Zinc supply caused by mine shutdowns and lack of investment will be dwindling, but continued demand from the galvanizing sector is still expected. Lead appears to be headed higher for 2015, with very little scrap available for consumption in Europe and margins for consumers becoming thin. Prime lead premiums rose in December and were expected to continue into January.

Nickel had a volatile 2014 with daily price moves of over $400/metric ton. The Indonesian ore ban, a possible Philippine ore ban, warehouse inventories, Chinese nickel pig iron inventories, and Russian ruble problems—all had investors in and out of the market in 2014.

Sixty-five percent of nickel demand is used for stainless steel production, which rose sharply in 2014. Many banks and economists forecast nickel to reach $21,000/metric ton in 2015, but others are even more bullish.
