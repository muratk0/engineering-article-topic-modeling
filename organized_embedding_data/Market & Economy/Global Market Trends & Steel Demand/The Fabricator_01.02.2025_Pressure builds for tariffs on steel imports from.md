# Pressure builds for tariffs on steel imports from Mexico

[TARİH: 01.02.2025 The Fabricator]

Around Washington

Canada likely to escape potential Trump tariffs, at least for the near term

STEPHEN BARLAS

U

.S. metal fabricators face substantial supply chain pricing threats in 2025 given President Donald Trump’s promise to go hog wild with new tariffs apparently on every country in the world. Higher prices for steel and aluminum from some countries are probably on the way, but not all countries.

Take Canada, which, along with Mexico, has been a prime target for Trump’s tariff threats, not just for steel and aluminum exports, but for all products. Statistics can be a bit fungible, but our neighbor to the north has generally been the biggest steel exporter to the U.S.—at least until recently. In October 2024, the U.S. Department of Commerce said Canada was actually the No. 2 steel exporter to the U.S. at 2.8 million metric tons, based on year-to-date data. For that same period, Mexico assumed the No. 1 spot at 3.6 million metric tons. That was out of 6.9 million metric tons of steel imports to the U.S.

Trump has threatened to slap a 25% tariff on Canadian and Mexican imports. Steel imports from both countries have been exempt from the 2017 Trump Section 232 national security tariffs because of the U.S.-Mexico-Canada Agreement (USMCA), signed in 2019.

Are Canadian steel tariffs in the offing, or is Trump blustering as a negotiating tactic? Probably the latter. Here is why. First, the USMCA cannot be renegotiated until mid-2026. So new Canadian steel tariffs are not imminent. But that is not the most important reason why Canadian steel is likely to be exempt from the new president’s initial wrath.

United Steelworkers, which has been an enthusiastic backer of steel tariffs as a way to maintain domestic jobs, opposes Canadian tariffs.

"If applied, these extraordinary tariffs leveled on Canada would dramatically harm workers in both our countries," said United Steelworkers (USW) International President David McCall. "There is no question that we must address the holes in our global trading system, but Canada is not the problem."

Trump showed sympathy for the steelworkers union by agreeing to oppose Nippon Steel’s purchase of U.S. Steel. So the union’s opposition to Canadian tariffs may loom somewhat large in the president’s decision-making process.

U.S. manufacturers also oppose Canadian steel tariffs. When Jay Timmons, president/CEO of the National Association of Manufacturers, was in Canada on Nov. 21 for a big manufacturing event, he said that part of that prosperity will be continuing and strengthening the USMCA, which "has demonstrated that the regional economic activity that has been generated has been beneficial for all three countries."

Moreover, Canada has appeared to react positively to Trump’s complaints about lax immigration practices, his main reason for threatening tariffs. In mid-December, Dominic LeBlanc, Canada’s minister of finance and intergovernmental affairs, said the Canadian government would devote nearly $1 billion U.S. dollars on new border integrity plans.

Mexico is likely a different story. Dissatisfaction with its export practices, primarily but not wholly in the case of steel, is widespread. Advocacy groups in the U.S., such as the Coalition for a Prosperous America (CPA), which has a number of steel manufacturing members, has complained that Mexico has violated the USMCA, which required it to limit steel imports to the U.S. below certain historical levels.

According to the CPA, Mexican steel shipments to the U.S. have skyrocketed—reaching 472% above the agreed-upon levels set in 2019. This year, Mexican steel exports to the U.S. are on track to reach 700% above these baseline levels.

Congress also is increasing the pressure to enact tariffs on steel sourced from Mexico. Last February, leaders of the Congressional Steel Caucus, which has 100 members in the House, introduced the Stop Mexico’s Steel Surge Act. The legislation would reinstate certain tariffs on steel imports from Mexico. The bill has bipartisan support, although it did not get a vote in 2024 in the U.S. House Committee on Ways & Means.

The United Steelworkers, which opposes additional Canadian steel tariffs, supports the Surge Act.
