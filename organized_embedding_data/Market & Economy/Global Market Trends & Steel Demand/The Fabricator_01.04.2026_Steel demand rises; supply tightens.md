# Steel demand rises; supply tightens

Steel buyers accept that uncertainty is the new normal, move ahead with material orders

By

Kristen DiLandro

ariffs, trade wars, transportation, and taxes; the T words shaping the steel market show no sign of stabilizing. Unlike in 2025, when many market players stalled decisions awaiting clarity, activity in early 2026 is moving forward. Rather than pause and wait for better visibility, participants are navigating through uncharted territory.

Prices for domestic hot-rolled coil and plate have risen steadily. During the first week of January, the average HRC price was $945/short ton, according to Steel Market Update. By the week of March 10, SMU sources reported the average climbed to $1,005/st, an increase of roughly 6%. Plate averaged $1,035/st in early January and rose to about $1,145/st by the week of March 10, nearly an 11% gain.

But recent macro indicators do not point to a sudden, economy-wide surge in steel demand. Reports such as the Federal Reserve’s Beige Book, the Dodge Construction Network momentum readings, and ISM’s PMI show mixed activity rather than broad strength. Market participants say the price gains reflect a combination of concentrated project demand, constrained supply options, and precautionary buying rather than a single, nationwide boom.

A LOOK AT DEMAND

Two project types are repeatedly cited as meaningful, but concentrated, demand drivers. First, FMI Corp. and other construction trackers identify data centers as a notable bright spot amid otherwise muted commercial construction. Second, the large border security program included in President Donald Trump’s Big Beautiful Bill represents a concentrated, steel-intensive undertaking.

For example, on March 10, Reliance Steel announced a multiyear contract with the U.S. Department of Homeland Security to supply bollards, plates, and logistics support. This contract, effective from Feb. 11, 2026, to Dec. 31, 2028, has a maximum value of $2.24 billion.

Data center construction and government spending add meaningful demand for specific product lines but do not, by themselves, fully explain the broader price rise.

Steel buyers are pushing ahead with orders even in the face of rising prices and tight domestic supply. Some are turning to imports from Canada or South Korea to attain high-quality materials.

Ratchat/iStock/Getty Images Plus

Supply-side dynamics have tightened choices for buyers. Some U.S. purchasers report turning to imports from Canada and South Korea to secure high-quality material, even as tariffs and trade policy remain a backdrop. Many participants report they have moved past debating tariffs and that operational needs, delivery timing, and material quality are now driving purchase decisions.

Mill lead times have lengthened. Mills are quoting deliveries into May, and hot-rolled lead times have extended to as much as six weeks.

Buyers report reduced negotiating leverage as mills prioritize throughput that meets their operational objectives.

Geopolitical developments around transportation routes and increasing fuel costs caused by the conflict with Iran continue to add layers of uncertainty. Markets are assessing the day-to-day impacts and expect the conflict to introduce unpredictable demand and logistical effects that could ripple through supply chains.

Rather than waiting for a new map, market participants say they are adapting. Strategies now include more flexible ordering, inventory adjustments, and executing evolving plans to meet customer needs. Some orders are postponed, others accelerated. Success in the current market increasingly depends on agility and contingency planning.

In short, recent price gains reflect concentrated project demand, tighter supply options (domestic and imported), and precautionary buying amid elevated geopolitical and policy uncertainty. Participants are proceeding, and adapting, rather than pausing for a clearer picture.
