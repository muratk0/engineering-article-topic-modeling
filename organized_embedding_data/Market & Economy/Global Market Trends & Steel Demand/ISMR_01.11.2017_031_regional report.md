# REGIONAL REPORT

[TARİH: 01.11.2017 ISMR]

NORDIC WATCH

ISMR outlines economic and manufacturing forecasts for Nordic countries.

The Little Mermaid statue in Copenhagen, Denmark

The Nordic region consists of Denmark, Norway, Sweden, Finland and Iceland, as well as the Faroe Islands, Greenland and Åland. It includes some of the world's most advanced technology nations and can rightfully claim to be 'one big high-tech cluster'. Structural differences between countries (for example, among the manufacturing sectors of Denmark, Finland, Norway and Sweden) include different currencies, competitive strengths and labour environments, as well as varying degrees of dependence on exports and natural resources. As a result, each nation has its own set of challenges and policy priorities.

Nordic regions rank highly globally for internet usage and information technology penetration and have produced companies such as Skype; MySQL; ClickTech; Spotify and Just Eat. Overseas investment by organisations such as Google, Apple, Yandex and Facebook have strongly impacted the region and, in some cases, influenced the emergence of digital eco-systems. The region is also a stronghold for the oil and gas industry as well as for clean technologies such as wind, solar, wave and hydropower. It also has a strong research and development base.

"Both Denmark and Finland are beating the euro area average, with Norway catching up. Sweden is one of the few countries in the world where growth has slowed, but the slowdown is from a very high level, and growth remains strong. As small open economies, the Nordics are very dependent upon global growth," said Danske Banke in its October 2017 Nordic Outlook. "The remarkable Finnish recovery this year is also likely to lose a little momentum in 2018. But all Nordic economies are set to remain well supported by global demand, even if at a slightly lower level of growth.'

## Denmark and Finland

Key industries in Denmark include machinery and equipment, food and beverages, transportation equipment and metals

"The Danish economy is growing. We have revised up our forecast for this year and now expect GDP growth of somewhat above 2%. Companies are reporting significant problems finding qualified labour, while DA (Confederation of Danish Employers) has noted wage increases of 2.4% in the private sector – considerably above inflation and the average abroad," commented Danske Bank.

"The economy has struggled to keep up the brilliant pace seen at the outset of the year. Although growth still clocked in at a solid 0.7% rise in quarterly terms in Q2, household consumption growth was flat from the previous quarter despite a tight labour market and stronger wage compensation. The third quarter is unlikely to have yielded better results, with soft retail sales data throughout the July-to-September period and car sales still subdued due to protracted negotiations on the revision of vehicle registration fees," commented Focus Economics.

"However, economic momentum remains resilient, with ultra-loose monetary policy conditions, a buoyant housing market and healthy real wage growth all buttressing the domestic economy. In addition, firming economic performances across Denmark's main trading partners amid the upsurge in global trade is expected to prove a boon to the country’s all-important external sector in H2."

Finland’s competitive strengths include strong talent, affordable energy, access to natural resources and well-established R&D links among industry, universities and government-funded research.

"The Finnish economy steamed ahead in Q2 17. GDP rose 3.0% y/y in H1 after rising 1.9% in FY 2016. Leading indicators continue to be elevated. We expect Finnish GDP to grow 2.8% in 2017 and slow down to 1.8% in 2018, which is still above potential growth," commented Danske Bank, in its October 2017 Nordic Outlook.

"Exports of goods and services rose 7.9% in Q2 and business surveys imply growing order books. The outlook is better thanks to growth in export markets, also Russia, improving price competitiveness, several large ship orders and new production facilities in forest and automotive industries. Growing demand, higher confidence and low interest rates have boosted manufacturing investment significantly."

According to the IMF (International Monetary Fund), Finland's economy continues to recover. It expects strong growth to continue this year and the next, but warned that employment and productivity need to increase further to raise potential growth and support Finland's social model. Important structural reforms are being implemented, notably to pensions, unemployment insurance and product markets. But more flexibility in the collective bargaining process, to allow more differentiation between firms, is needed to maintain competitiveness and to ensure that workers find the most suitable jobs, it said. Health and social services reform is vital to boost public sector productivity and contain age-related pressures on public finances.

## Norway, Sweden and Iceland

Norway depends less on manufacturing for employment – the oil-and-gas industry drives the Norwegian economy. Traditional manufacturing industries, such as wood products, furniture, fabricated metals and foods, depend heavily upon access to domestic natural resources. Norway also has healthy machinery and transportation-equipment industries.

While the nation has some of the region's highest labour costs, its advantages include very well-educated talent, high productivity, latest technology and affordable energy costs. As offshore investment drops from its peak and oil prices retreat from their high in 2014, the Norwegian economy is moving through a transition away from oil dependence.

"The Norwegian economy is entering a new phase. Growth was above trend in H1 and looks set to remain there for the rest of the year. Capacity utilisation is therefore rising and gradually putting more pressure on the economy, with wages and prices starting to accelerate. This has helped eliminate the possibility of further rate cuts and a first-rate hike has moved closer. Coupled with the higher oil price, this means a stronger NOK. The housing market is the key risk factor, but low interest rates and falling unemployment are reducing the risk of a serious downturn. We expect growth to hold above trend for the next couple of years, so this picture should continue," said Danske Bank in its recent Nordic Outlook.

"Strong economic growth has held firm in Norway, boosted by higher oil prices, robust household spending and healthy external demand. The oil and gas sector contributed to the overall trade balance in September, which posted a healthy surplus. According to Statistics Norway’s most recent business survey, manufacturing output increased in the third quarter of the year, and is expected to do so again in the fourth quarter. The fiscal deficit in 2018 will be funded by withdrawals from the country's US$ 1 trillion sovereign wealth fund," said analyst, Focus Economics.

According to the SEB's 2017 Nordic Outlook, strong capital spending in Norway's mainland economy – excluding offshore oil and gas – has contributed to a turnaround, and it expects the recovery to broaden this year as private consumption and traditional goods exports rebound. Growth in mainland GDP will accelerate to 1.7 percent this year, it said, and 2.0 per cent in 2018. Inflation has fallen quickly since it peaked last year. The main driver has been lower goods inflation, as the effects of earlier currency depreciation fade.

Key Swedish industries include machinery and equipment, automobiles, basic materials and medical equipment. Sweden has traditionally based competitiveness on a comprehensively trained talent base, high levels of automation, a great deal of R&D spending and access to resources such as metals, wood and competitively priced energy. Sweden is one of the most cost-competitive Nordic countries.

"After a soft patch in late 2016, GDP growth in Sweden accelerated in the first half of 2017. The impetus came from residential construction activity, which is at its highest since the Million programme was launched in the late 1960s. In H1 17, about one third of GDP growth, or about one percentage point, was the direct result of residential construction. Rising construction activity and housing-related private consumption have been major factors bolstering economic growth over the past couple of years," commented Danske Bank in its recent Nordic Outlook.

"The economic picture continues to look fairly positive. Economic sentiment surged in September to its highest level in well over six years on the back of a more optimistic manufacturing sector, while retail sales were buoyant in July and August, probably due in part to low unemployment. However, growth in industrial production has disappointed over the summer. In late September, the government presented its 2018 budget, which takes advantage of the country's enviable fiscal position to boost spending by SEK 43.8 billion ahead of next year's election. Significant additional funding has been allocated to defence, healthcare, education and the environment, which should foster more inclusive growth. However, next year's spending plan could eat into the budget surplus and limit fiscal flexibility in subsequent years, due to spending commitments for 2019-2020 and the need to meet the government's new surplus target," said analyst, Focus Economics.

An IMF 2017 Article IV Consultation highlighted Iceland's continued real GDP growth, driven by tourism. Growth reached 7.2 percent in 2016 and is projected at almost 6 percent in 2017 before tapering to about 2.5 percent over the medium-term. Bank credit to the non-financial private sector remains muted, said the IMF, growing only 4.3 percent in 2016, but it is expected to pick up. Thus far, growth has been driven not by leverage but by exports, private consumption and investment. Iceland’s current account surplus is projected by the IMF to shrink modestly over time, with some export sectors suffering while others thrive.

"Iceland's economy has been bustling this year thanks to skyward growth in the tourism sector and a plentiful catch; the technical recession in H1 was only the result of last year's high base. Fixed investment – especially in tourism and manufacturing – has climbed this year with the recovery in FDI, and household spending has been benefiting from higher real wages supported by low unemployment and weak inflation," said analyst, Focus Economics.
