## Europe in focus

The EU Framework on state aid to shipbuilding has just been renewed for a period of two years starting from 1 January 2012. Under this Framework, the EC may authorise aid to the shipbuilding sector. Three areas for state aid are identified in the framework – regional aid, innovation aid and export credits.

In recent decades, the EU shipbuilding landscape has changed significantly. Global competition has increased, especially from Asia where low-wage countries have taken over building of more standard type ships and manufacturing of many components. According to the European Commission’s Enterprise and Industry pages, the European shipbuilding industry is still a global leader in the construction of complex vessels such as cruise ships, ferries, mega-yachts and dredgers. It also holds a strong position in the building of submarines and other naval vessels. Equally, the European marine equipment industry is a world leader for a wide range of products from propulsion systems, large diesel engines, environmental and safety systems to cargo handling and electronics.

There are around 150 large shipyards in Europe, with around 40 of them active in the global market for large sea-going commercial vessels. Around 120,000 people are directly employed by shipyards (civil and naval, new building and repair) in the European Union. Today, Europe has a lead position in high value, high complexity segments such as cruise ships, dredgers and other specialised ship types. The shipbuilding industry in Europe covers both shipyards (new-building, maintenance & repair) and the marine equipment industry (suppliers to shipyards).

Greening provides new opportunities for the European shipbuilding industry that respond to the current strengths of the sector. The main objective of a recent study was to identify market opportunities for the EU shipbuilding industry that follow from greening trends in shipping and other areas. Taken together, EU industry appears well positioned to capture a substantial part of the green growth potential. Based on its competitive position, its share in global markets is roughly being estimated at between 15 and 50%.

The EU shipbuilding industry, in response to tough competition from mainly Asian players, is now focused on the higher value, more complex ship types such as cruise ships, offshore vessels and installations and dredgers. These segments include ferries and passenger ships, non-cargo vessels and general cargo ships, which are characterised by a high degree of specialisation and complex production processes. Europe has a 77 per cent market share in building passenger vessels (including both cruise ships as well as ferries) and a 17 per cent share in the construction of non-cargo vessels.

Russia currently holds an estimated 0.4% of the world’s civil shipbuilding market. However, Russian shipbuilding offers strong opportunities for sheet metal suppliers – even during the crisis period of 2009, growth levels in civil shipbuilding in Russia were 40%. The primary goal for civil shipbuilding is the upgrade and construction of new shipyards.

Russia is expected to order 50 types of ships such as dredging ships, tugs, icebreakers, cargo ships, passenger ships, rescue ships, LNG carriers, scientific, research and exploration ships. For general sea transport operations, Russia will require, in the next 15-20 years, 446 transport cargo ships, 48 passenger ships and 348 service fleet ships. Ports development is also an essential element for the future of Russian transport and transit. The Russian Fisheries Fleet programme for 2009-2013 includes construction of 208 new fishery vessels. By 2020, Russia will require 2207 new fishery vessels of different types.

## An eye on Asia

Asian enterprises have been keen competitors in global shipbuilding markets. Japanese yards entered the global shipbuilding scene in the 1950s and 60s, followed by Korean yards from the 1970s and 80s onwards. Chinese players entered the market from the 1990s. China is expected to become the leading shipbuilding nation by 2020.

According to a June Commerzbank report on Germany’s trade with China, “China has achieved the objective behind its industrial policy of becoming the largest shipbuilder in the world much earlier than originally expected as a result of massive capacity expansion and various forms of state subsidy. Chinese production has increased by more than 50% compared to 2009 and the country’s capacity is set to grow again by a quarter by 2014.”

The Chinese shipbuilding industry has grown rapidly. A lot of money is being spent on R&D - the main focus is on improving the quality of ships, the production process and the shortening of delivery times. Besides imported foreign procedures to obtain knowledge, China has also established joint ventures with Japanese and Korean yards. The purposes of these joint ventures are the transfer of knowledge, skills and production expertise.

Chinese enterprises have also started eyeing European marine companies. Weichai Group recently acquired a 75% interest in Italian-based Ferretti Group, the world’s largest luxury yacht manufacturer. Ferruccio Rossi, the newly appointed CEO of Ferretti, said that Ferretti’s China strategy had brought new opportunities to the company. With an ever-expanding Chinese middle and upper class, there will be more consumers and higher consumption, which is certain to boost the development of yacht market in China. The CANSI estimates that by 2020, China’s yacht market will be worth RMB 50 billion. Weichai Group is a wholly owned subsidiary of SHIG, one of the most competitive equipment manufacturing groups in China.

Major Korean shipbuilding industry players have started an initiative to accelerate green shipbuilding technology development. The initiative should be the core strategy to strengthen the competitiveness of the Korean shipbuilding industry. ■
