he latest assessment from the IMF describes in detail what most have already been observing. The states and companies that are producing oil are looking at a massive windfall as a result of the skyrocketing price per barrel for oil. The Gulf states are looking at a $1.3 trillion level in just the next four years and the oil companies around the world are also enjoying the surge. To many this is a trigger for massive government intervention and the establishment of windfall taxes — calls of anger are directed at BIG OIL again. Nobody seems to remember that the per barrel price was at $11.26 in 2020. That was the year when nobody made money with oil. These wild price gyrations are market-driven and they always have been.

There are few sectors less understood than oil and energy in general. The assumption that most people make is that energy producers function similarly to every other business in that they have the ability to set their own prices. Obviously, every company faces some limitations as far as determin- ing price. They have to take their input costs into consideration as well as their collection of fixed costs, labor costs, taxes, regulatory expenses, and so on. They also have to determine what the consumer will tolerate but in general, their ability to manipulate pricing is a key part of their business strategy. The oil industry is fundamentally different in a very important sense. The price for a barrel of oil is largely set by the oil market itself.

Oil is a globally traded commodity and that means that every barrel produced is handled like an investment. The demand and supply factors govern what will be paid for that barrel and oil is described as an "inelastic good". This means that its consumption is not all that closely tied to price. People use what they have to use. There are few that will suddenly use more fuel because it is cheaper and there are few that will radically change their habits when it gets expensive — at least not right away. The markets today are extremely nervous regarding global production. They see the sanctions on Russia digging into their capacity to produce and they see countries trying to limit what is purchased from Russia and that convinces investors that supply will shrink. That translates into higher prices. At the same time, there is evidence of demand destruction as recession concerns build and that translates into lower prices. The mood of the investor determines the price of a barrel of oil.

The oil companies have control over perhaps 30% of the price of a gallon of fuel — the costs involved with refining, distribution, and marketing. They can do little to influence the costs involved with the crude itself or taxes.

What does this mean for manufacturing? The price of energy is obviously a huge factor as far as the manufacturing sector is concerned. It goes beyond the energy consumed by operations; it also affects the costs of logistics. The recent surge in pricing at the per barrel level has been the primary driver for inflation over the last couple of quarters and has only recently started to fade. The question is how long this will last. Demand has been declining a little — mostly due to the end of the summer driving season but it is anticipated that demand will increase again by the first of the year. Does this allow per barrel prices to surge back towards the $100 levels?
