# MATERIAL MOVES

[TARİH: 01.06.2014 ISMR]

Colette Swires outlines material usage trends for automotive applications and the move to lightweight, stronger materials in the industry

ISMR SAYS:

“Innovative lightweightmaterial technologies help automotive OEMs to reduce fuel consumption, increase material strength andrespectemission targets”

The latest EU regulations on CO emissions for vehicles are driving the automotive manufacturing industry towards new and innovative solutions to meet these targets. The UK has already met the European 2015 target of 130g/km, while the 2020 target of 95g/km is yet to be achieved (which represents quite a significant hurdle). Automotive giants in China and the US are facing a similar challenge.

The use of innovative lightweight materials and associated new manufacturing technologies, once the preserve of luxury sports’ cars, has become the focus of automotive manufacturers as a way of reducing fuel consumption to meet the latest emission targets.

There is a fine balance between creating a lightweight vehicle, which is also inherently strong, whilst fulfilling current Euro NCAP crash test safety regulations. Consumers increasingly expect the latest safety equipment and gadgetry to be included in each subsequent model, all of which adds to the weight of the vehicle. Cost is a significant factor as lightweight materials, traditionally employed in the manufacture of luxury cars, come at a high price, presenting a further challenge for the automotive mass market.

## An eye on aluminium

Aluminium is currently the industry’s lightweight metal of choice, being less costly than some other alternatives such as titanium. Audi has been one of the trailblazers in its use of aluminium. In 1997, it introduced the Audi Space Frame developed with Alcoa for the Audi A8 which heralded the first mass-market car with a lightweight aluminium chassis. However, the structural body-in-white frame, where strength is paramount, continued to be manufactured in steel.

Thanks to the latest innovations in the fabrication of aluminium, this is now changing. The 2014 Audi A8 3.0 TFSI, a sedan weighing in at 1,830kg, has a lightweight body which, apart from the B-pillars between the front and rear door, is made entirely of aluminium. The latest generation of the Audi Space Frame weighs just 231kg, around 40 per cent less than the equivalent steel construction.

Audi is not alone. The Jaguar XKF and Land Rover’s Range Rover already use aluminium for complete bodies, including doors. Many car manufacturers are finding new ways to employ aluminium, a metal not regarded as being inherently as strong as other metals such as mild steel, for use in the load-bearing parts of vehicle construction where strength is paramount.

One of the keys to harnessing the strength of aluminium lies in a new aluminium bonding process developed by Alcoa and now available under licence to suppliers of aluminium to the automotive industry. Steel car bodies are manufactured using spot and seam welding techniques; however, these methods of electric resistance welding are not able to be used for aluminium since oxides on the surface of the metal compromise a strong bond. Alcoa’s product, Alcoa 951, coats the aluminium which is either sprayed or the metal is dipped – the result inhibits corrosion and prepares the surface for the use of adhesives. Enabling strong bonding with aluminium parts allows aluminium to be used not only for body panels but also for the load-bearing, structural parts of the vehicle.

Later this year, Jaguar is launching the Jaguar XE, the first monocoque chassis or integral body frame in aluminium. In perhaps an even bolder move, Ford Motors is swapping steel for aluminium in its best-selling F-150 pickup truck. The Ford F-150 cab and rear cargo sections, traditionally made of welded steel, are being constructed (for the most part) from lightweight aluminium. Various components in the engine and chassis will also be made from aluminium.

This step change will reduce the weight of the new truck by 15 per cent compared to the 2014 steel model. Ford expects the new 2015 F-150 to achieve close to 30mpg, meeting the US Government’s projected fleet mileage for a pickup truck of 30.2mpg by 2025.

## Stiffness, strength and weight

Altering the perception of aluminium as a flimsy material will be one of the challenges in marketing the Ford F-150. However, in reality, it is the form and process involved in the manufacture of aluminium which dictates its stiffness and strength. In terms of crash protection and inherent strength, aluminium which is produced in thicker sheets and stamped can, in some cases, surpass steel.

The key lies in the industrial processes for aluminium during manufacture. For the Ford F-150, 6000-series heat-treated aluminium sheets will be used, heated to 750 degrees Fahrenheit for two hours followed by quenching in water. Rivets and industrial adhesive, rather than the spot welding process used in steel, also adds to the strength of the overall structure, while the number of rivets required will be almost half.

However, rivets still add to the cost and cannot be used to join steel with aluminium. Engineers at Honda Motor Company have therefore come up with a solution, a method of joining aluminium and steel using the technique of friction stir welding. A stable metallic bond is produced between aluminium and steel sheets by a rotating tool on top of the aluminium which is lapped over the steel at high pressure, producing a welding strength greater that traditional metal inert gas welding. Honda has managed to adapt this method to mass-produced vehicles, such as the Honda Accord, using a six-axis robot, whilst an infrared camera and laser beam inspects each unit for faults. The electricity saved in this new welding process is reportedly close to 50 per cent.

Steel manufacturers are equally conscious of the need to adapt to shed weight on their own materials and participate in the automotive industry race to reduce weight and increase fuel efficiency. The latest generation of steel for the automotive industry, the Ultra Advanced High Strength Steels (U-AHSS), allows for the reduction of weight whilst delivering an increase in strength. Micro-alloyed steel withstands greater temperatures and fatigue.

ArcelorMittal has launched a new advanced high strength steel (AHSS) for use in the automotive industry. The HF1050 has increased steel strength for cold stamping. The company maintains that it also offers a 10 per cent weight reduction in the structural components of the vehicle.

## Magnesium in focus

One of the most innovative solutions appears to lie not only in new methods of fabrication and manufacture but in the application of metal alloys. Magnesium is emerging as a frontrunner in the lightweight metal alloys’ market. The composition of magnesium alloys commonly includes aluminium, zinc, manganese, silicon, copper, rare earths and zirconium.

Magnesium is around one third lighter than aluminium and can be readily cast into mechanical parts which are in fact lighter and stronger than their aluminium counterpart. The 2015 Corvette Z06 Convertible employs magnesium in the frame structures for its seats which it believes provides greater rigidity and strength than steel frames. However, it is the application of different combinations of lightweight metals in one vehicle that really provides the edge. Combining both aluminium and magnesium in the manufacture of the gearbox makes it 4kg lighter than previous models. The addition of magnesium to aluminium increases heat treatability and strength.

China has the world’s greatest magnesium reserves. Last year, it produced 770,000 tons of primary magnesium, representing 89 per cent of the world’s output, which it imports to Canada, Japan, India and Europe. The consumption of magnesium appears to be increasing, with exports of unwrought magnesium from China last year up by just over 20 per cent.

Magnesium has been used in car manufacture since the 1920s where its light weight and strength were used in the production of racing cars. It is a metal which is easy to work with, both in machining and in die casting, although it does not adapt well to roll forming. Magnesium was adopted in the powertrain of the VW beetle in 1971, after which its use in the automotive industry began to decline as it became evident that magnesium has some significant disadvantages such as high temperature creep, flammability and corrosion.

However, advances in the technology of alloys and the need to revisit lightweight metal resources have meant that the use of magnesium in the automotive industry will soon be on the increase. The introduction of scandium and gadolinium addresses the issue of temperature creep, whilst calcium reduces flammability. However, until recently, corrosion has remained a significant obstacle, resulting in the use of magnesium alloys stagnating at less than one million tons per year, compared with the almost 50 million tons of aluminium alloys being used annually.

Researchers at Monash University in Australia may have turned the fortunes of magnesium around by developing an innovative method of producing stainless magnesium. Professor Nick Birbilis and his team at Monash University discovered that, by introducing the additive cathodic poisoning, the formation of free hydrogen gas which promotes corrosion is inhibited. The primary active ingredient is arsenic, and by adding one third of a per cent of arsenic, the corrosion rate of the magnesium alloy is reduced by a factor of almost ten.

At the same time, advances in the methods for machining and forming magnesium are being fostered. In Japan, researchers have discovered a new roomtemperature rolling process which, compared with the hot rolling process, will increase the efficiency of magnesium sheet production. In Shanghai, the GM China Advanced Technical Centre revealed its latest innovation, in partnership with the research team in Detroit – the Vertical Squeeze Casting machine for producing superior magnesium castings. The process maintains the molten metal surface keeping oxygen at bay which would otherwise produce oxide inclusions leading to corrosion. The squeeze method of casting means that it can replace certain forged components, resulting in reduced cost.

## Titanium spreads its wings

Titanium, 40 per cent lighter than steel, is another important lightweight metal. Although magnesium is actually 50 per cent lighter than titanium, titanium has many inherent advantages over magnesium. It is more easily obtainable for the rest of the world, unlike magnesium for which the lion’s share of the resource resides with China. Also, in contrast to magnesium, titanium is highly corrosion- and temperature-resistant.

However, titanium does not perform as well in manufacturing processes. The forming techniques for titanium, hydroforming and deep drawing are difficult and welding also presents a challenge since, at high temperatures, titanium tends to react with oxygen and nitrogen. Argon shielding gates have been introduced to prevent oxidisation, resulting in extremely high production costs.

Due to its high processing costs, the use of titanium in the automotive sector has been largely reserved for high-end sports cars. Titanium requires a very high forming temperature, over 800 degrees centigrade, but has the disadvantage of possessing a tendency to stick to forming tools. However, the Fraunhofer Institute in Germany is finding new ways to reduce production problems and associated costs. It has developed a method of hydroforming, replacing the complex multi-stage forming process, using a specially coated tool which prevents the titanium from adhering to the tool.

In the UK, Metalysis, a specialist metals manufacturer, mindful that much of the inherent cost of producing titanium lies in expensive, energy-hungry smelting processes, has discovered a method of using electricity to separate the metal from the metal oxides. This is a highly significant advance in technology representing a potential cost saving of 75 per cent on the titanium powder production process. This advance would potentially place titanium back in the league of U-AHSS steel and aluminium in terms of cost.

Whilst the content of steel, even new U-AHSS, is gradually being reduced in favour of lighter weight metals, the A and B pillars of the frames and side-impact door frames will continue to be produced in steel for the foreseeable future. As a result, innovative lightweight metal alloys such as magnesium, titanium and aluminium are being employed alongside materials traditionally used in the automotive industry, such as steel. Engineers at Brigham Young University have developed a friction bit joining process using a small consumable bit to create a solid state joint, which allows the bonding of aluminium to cast iron by inserting a thin layer of steel, facilitating the combination of steels and light metals in one vehicle frame.

The current trend in the automotive industry involves the utilisation of a variety of different metals in the construction of a single vehicle to exploit the advantages of each of the materials. Ongoing research into metal alloys, in tandem with new technology and innovative manufacturing processes, is continuing to support this trend every step of the way. ■
