# U.S. manufacturers win with One Big Beautiful Bill Act

[TARİH: 01.08.2025 The Fabricator]

Around Washington

H.R. 1 paves the way for strategic investments

OMAR NASHASHIBI

I

n a sweeping legislative victory for U.S. manufacturers, on July 4, President Donald Trump signed into law H.R. 1—the "One Big Beautiful Bill Act"—marking the most significant tax overhaul for manufacturers in years.

At the heart of the legislation is the restoration of immediate R&D expensing, retroactive to Jan. 1, 2025. Small businesses with gross receipts under $31 million gain even more: the ability to apply the change retroactively to Jan. 1, 2022. Until passage of this law, manufacturers were forced to amortize their R&D activities over five years. In a significant shift from recent years, companies of all sizes may now elect to accelerate domestic R&D deductions that had previously been amortized, providing a powerful boost to innovation. Foreign R&D remains subject to 15-year amortization.

The bill also permanently extends 100% bonus depreciation for qualified property such as machinery and equipment, and doubles Section 179 small business expensing to $2.5 million (with a $4 million phaseout). It introduces a new special 100% first-year depreciation allowance for qualified production property, including manufacturing-related factories and plants, if construction begins between Jan. 19, 2025, and Jan. 1, 2029.

Other pro-manufacturing highlights include the reinstatement of the EBITDA standard for interest deductibility under Section 163(j), providing relief to capital-intensive firms, and the permanent extension of the 20% passthrough deduction (Section 199A) for qualified business income. New enhancements to the pass-through rules expand eligibility and introduce a minimum deduction indexed for inflation.

The bill solidifies key provisions from the 2017 Tax Cuts and Jobs Act, including the 21% corporate tax rate and the current individual rate brackets. Additional tax relief measures include a permanent expansion of the estate tax exemption to $15 million per individual, a broader paid family leave credit, and enhanced flexibility in 529 plans for workforce training.

Notably, H.R. 1 also phases out or terminates several green energy tax credits enacted under the Inflation Reduction Act. Clean vehicle credits, hydrogen production incentives, and renewable energy credits are all slated to end by 2028, signaling a shift in policy priorities toward traditional industrial and economic growth.

For manufacturers, H.R. 1 provides long-term certainty and a comprehensive package of incentives to invest in equipment, research, workforce, and facilities. The manufacturing community’s sustained advocacy has paid off with a bill that reflects their priorities and strengthens their role in the U.S. economy.

White House Postpones Rollout of New Reciprocal Tariffs to August

Trump has officially extended the suspension of his administration’s "reciprocal tariffs" initiative, delaying implementation from July 9 to Aug. 1, at 12:01 a.m. EDT. The extension was formalized in Executive Order 14316, signed on July 7, which continues the 90-day pause originally set by Executive Order 14266 in April. This additional time is intended to give U.S. trade officials more room to finalize bilateral agreements with targeted trading partners. The tariffs in question—authorized under the International Emergency Economic Powers Act (IEEPA) —are aimed at countries with what the administration considers unbalanced trade practices and retaliation against U.S. goods.

Under the reciprocal tariff framework, the administration had proposed significant ad valorem duties on goods from several nations. While these rates were temporarily reduced to 10% for most countries under EO 14266, the July order continues that suspension until August. Notably, the pause does not apply to tariffs on Chinese imports, which are being addressed separately.

As part of the broader strategy, the White House has warned of tariffs of 25% on Japan and South Korea and 30% on South Africa beginning Aug. 1 unless new trade terms are reached. Mexico and Canada face possible rates as high as 30% to 35%, and Brazil could see tariffs rise to 50%. Letters detailing these potential penalties have been sent to about a dozen countries. According to Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent, these figures are intended as leverage, not fixed outcomes. "There’s still time to talk," he said, emphasizing that agreements can still be reached before the deadline.

While the U.S. has already secured new trade deals with the United Kingdom and Vietnam and has reached a preliminary accord with China, many of the country-specific tariffs remain unsettled. Importantly, the reciprocal tariffs are distinct from broader, sector-based duties already in place. The widely known Section 232 tariffs—50% on steel and aluminum and 25% on autos and auto parts—remain unaffected by this delay. The reciprocal tariffs instead target country-specific retaliation and trade imbalances and are tied to specific product classifications in the Harmonized Tariff Schedule.
