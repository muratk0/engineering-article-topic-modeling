# Section 232 steel and aluminum tariff changes announced

[TARİH: 01.07.2026 The Fabricator]

Around Washington

Proclamation targets mobile industrial equipment and adjusts tariffs for other segments

By

Omar S. Nashashibi

T

he Donald Trump administration has updated several aspects of its Section 232 tariff program covering steel, aluminum, copper, and related derivative products. The changes, announced in a June 1 proclamation, primarily affect how certain industrial equipment is treated under the tariff regime and modify eligibility requirements for reduced tariff rates tied to the use of U.S.-produced metals.

A key feature of the proclamation is the establishment of a new Annex I-C, which will apply through Dec. 31, 2027, to specified mobile industrial equipment, including products such as forklifts, bulldozers, and other heavy machinery. Imports covered by Annex I-C generally will be subject to a 25% tariff, although a 15% rate will apply to imports from countries that have qualifying trade arrangements with the U.S.

The proclamation also expands access to a lower 10% tariff rate for products that are predominantly manufactured using domestic metals. Under previous rules, products generally had to contain at least 95% U.S.-melted and -poured steel, U.S.-smelted and -cast aluminum, or U.S.-processed copper to qualify as being made "entirely" from domestic metal. The administration has reduced that threshold to 85%, making it easier for covered products to qualify for the preferential tariff treatment.

Several product-specific adjustments also were made. Steel racks and aluminum lithographic plates have been added to Annex I-B, placing them under the 25% tariff applicable to many derivative products. At the same time, certain agricultural equipment and selected residential HVAC systems and components have been transferred from Annex I-B to Annex III, lowering the applicable tariff rate from 25% to 15%.

The administration stated that the revisions are intended to encourage domestic sourcing and manufacturing investment while maintaining the broader protections established under the Section 232 steel, aluminum, and copper tariff programs.

USTR CONCLUDES FORCED LABOR INVESTIGATIONS

A series of Section 301 investigations into foreign forced labor enforcement practices has moved into the next phase, with the Office of the U.S. Trade Representative proposing new tariffs that would apply to most imports from 60 economies.

The investigations, initiated in March 2026, examined whether U.S. trading partners have established and enforced measures to prevent goods produced with forced labor from entering their markets. USTR found that each of the economies under review maintains acts, policies, or practices that burden or restrict U.S. commerce and therefore are actionable under Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974.

Rather than targeting specific sectors or products, the proposed action would impose countrywide tariffs on imports from the affected economies. USTR has proposed a 10% tariff for countries that have adopted forced labor import bans, committed to implementing such measures through Reciprocal Trade Agreements, or established partial restrictions on certain forced labor goods. This category includes Canada, the EU, Mexico, Pakistan, Argentina, Bangladesh, Cambodia, Ecuador, El Salvador, Guatemala, Indonesia, Malaysia, Taiwan, and the United Kingdom.

All other economies covered by the investigations would be subject to a proposed 12.5% tariff because USTR determined they have not adopted and effectively enforced prohibitions on imports produced with forced labor.

The proposal contains a broad set of exemptions intended to reduce impacts on critical supply chains and products already subject to other trade measures. Excluded products would include all articles and parts currently covered by Section 232 tariffs, certain raw materials that may not be available from domestic sources, products that could create economywide disruptions if additional duties were imposed, and goods that cannot be produced in sufficient quantities in the U.S. or sourced elsewhere. Informational materials, donations, accompanied baggage, and products for which additional tariffs are unlikely to meaningfully address the identified practices also would be exempt.

USTR stated that the proposed tariffs are intended to address market distortions associated with forced labor and encourage trading partners to strengthen import controls. The agency argues that goods produced with forced labor can gain an unfair cost advantage and compete directly with products manufactured under market-based labor standards.

Public comments on the proposed action were due July 6, and USTR scheduled a public hearing on July 7. The agency plans to evaluate the record before determining whether to implement, modify, or withdraw the proposed tariffs.

The forced labor cases are separate from USTR’s ongoing Section 301 investigations into structural manufacturing excess capacity and production practices. Those investigations remain pending, with reports and potential recommendations expected in the coming weeks.

U.S.-INDIA TRADE DEAL NEARS

Trade negotiations between the U.S. and India appear to be entering their final phase, with officials from both countries signaling that an interim bilateral trade agreement could be finalized by mid-July. Recent discussions in New Delhi have accelerated efforts to close remaining gaps, and a senior U.S. delegation is expected to travel to India later this month for additional high-level talks.

The discussions build on a February 2026 framework agreement that outlined potential reciprocal tariff adjustments, expanded market access commitments, and boosted efforts to address non-tariff trade barriers. Since then, negotiators have held multiple rounds of discussions covering tariffs, regulatory issues, and sector-specific market access concerns.

India Commerce and Industry Minister Piyush Goyal recently stated that negotiators are "fast moving" toward resolving the remaining issues and that the first tranche of the agreement could be executed by the middle of July. A U.S. team led by Assistant U.S. Trade Representative Brendan Lynch held several days of talks with Indian officials in early June, and another higher-level U.S. delegation is expected to continue negotiations later this month.

Several issues remain under discussion. India is seeking assurances that any tariff rates established under the agreement will remain competitive with those offered to other major U.S. trading partners and has raised concerns about potential future tariff actions. Ongoing U.S. Section 301 investigations involving forced labor and manufacturing excess capacity also could affect the final structure of the agreement.

No agreement has been finalized, and officials from both governments have indicated that discussions will continue in the coming weeks. If completed, the interim arrangement would serve as an initial step toward a broader bilateral trade agreement between the U.S. and India.

PUBLIC HEARINGS SCHEDULED FOR OSHA DEREGULATORY PROPOSALS

The Occupational Safety and Health Administration has scheduled a series of virtual public hearings beginning Aug. 19 on a broad package of proposed deregulatory actions affecting numerous workplace safety and health standard. The hearings are part of the agency’s effort to review and revise regulations that OSHA believes are outdated, duplicative, or unnecessarily burdensome.

Several of the proposed changes involve substance-specific health standards that affect facilities working with metals, coatings, solvents, and other industrial materials. OSHA has proposed removing certain ancillary requirements from standards covering substances such as lead, cadmium, methylene chloride, formaldehyde, and asbestos where similar requirements are already addressed elsewhere in OSHA regulations. The agency also is proposing revisions affecting walking and working surfaces and fixed ladder requirements that could impact plant operations and maintenance activities.

According to OSHA, the proposals are intended to reduce compliance burdens by eliminating duplicative provisions while preserving core employee protections. Industry representatives, labor organizations, and other stakeholders will have an opportunity to provide testimony and submit evidence during the hearing process before OSHA determines whether to finalize the changes.

LABOR DEPARTMENT ISSUES NEW OT RULE

The U.S. Department of Labor has issued a final rule rescinding the 2024 overtime regulation and restoring the salary thresholds established under the agency’s 2019 overtime rule.

The action follows federal court decisions that vacated the 2024 rule, which would have increased the minimum salary levels required for employees to qualify for the executive, administrative, and professional exemptions under the Fair Labor Standards Act. The 2024 rule also would have implemented automatic updates to the salary thresholds every three years.

As a result of the final rule, the salary thresholds in effect under the 2019 regulations remain in place. To qualify for the executive, administrative, and professional exemptions, employees generally must earn at least $684 per week, or $35,568 annually, and satisfy the applicable duties tests. The annual compensation threshold for the highly compensated employee exemption remains $107,432.

The rescinded 2024 rule would have increased the standard salary threshold to $844 per week beginning in July 2024 and to $1,128 per week beginning in January 2025. Those increases were blocked by federal courts before taking effect nationwide.

In conjunction with the final rule, DOL also withdrew its appeals of the court decisions that invalidated the 2024 regulation. While several reports have indicated that the agency is continuing to evaluate potential changes to overtime regulations, no new proposal has been released.
