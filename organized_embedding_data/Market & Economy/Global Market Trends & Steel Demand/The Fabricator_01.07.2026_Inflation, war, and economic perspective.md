# Inflation, war, and economic perspective

[TARİH: 01.07.2026 The Fabricator]

Fabrinomics | Economic Insights

The right metrics help filter out the noise

Dr. Chris Kuehl

As this goes to press, a peace deal with Iran had just been announced, yet the official signing ceremony had yet to take place. As the world waits, we’re seeing plenty of noise in the data. During such times, it helps to focus on metrics that filter out the noise.

For the first time in many years, there is a distinct difference between average inflation as reported by the media and what the Fed calls its favored inflation metric, the Personal Consumption Expenditures (PCE) index. The measure we watch, called the Trimmed Mean PCE (TMPCE), comes out of the Dallas Federal Reserve. The Dallas Fed takes more than 180 monthly inflation measures and strips out the extraordinary highs and lows, then averages the middle to get to average inflation, the TMPCE metric.

The Fed pays attention to the six-month and 12-month measures, and both came in this month at 2.3%. That is incredibly reasonable given all the noise in the supply chain and the pressures that we have read headlines about. Again, historically this measure has had far fewer false positives and negatives than the straight PCE or Consumer Price Index.

Given the reading in the TMPCE report alone, the conclusion on inflation is that it isn’t surging on a macro, average basis (which is what the Fed is watching). If this is the case for broader inflation, you might conclude that the Fed has some room to work with here, and a rate cut this year is still on the table.

What is ironic is that if you go with core PCE (see chart above), you might draw some of the same conclusions. It didn’t change much between March and April. But at 3.8% in the six-month reading (stripping out food and energy) and 3.3% on an annual basis, it is still too far above the Fed’s 2% target rate to allow a rate cut without a collapse in GDP or labor markets. This is going to force the Fed to continue to pause.

It is not time to ignore TMPCE. It has worked well in the past, and the data is still stable, especially in a situation like this where we are trying to decipher between a shock to the system and perhaps some underlying pressures coming from elsewhere. We continue to make it the go-to measure for inflation while keeping a close eye on all the other measures.

It is abundantly clear that fuel prices have been soaring and so have the price of beef, rents in many cities, the average automobile, and many other products and services. At the same time, there has been a decline in the overall price of technology, clothing, appliances, and furniture. If you are paying higher freight costs due to diesel prices, it doesn’t much matter that you can get a cheaper loveseat or a bargain on some new slacks. Inflation numbers are very broad measures and rarely reflect the reality of pricing for a given industry or even an individual.

Energy Prices and Jet Fuel

Of course, anyone who has visited the gas station lately knows how high energy prices are right now. Still, prices remain volatile—and this includes jet fuel. The price for a metric ton of jet fuel is well off of its peak highs hit in the early days of the conflict in March, but they remain nearly 85% higher than they were at the beginning of the year. Many analysts believe that the real dropoff in jet fuel (especially in Europe) is not because of finding secret supplies. Many airlines were performing off of hedged fuel purchases, and the impact is not yet being felt.

Before news of an Iran deal emerged, analysts thought a jet fuel shortage might be coming, especially in Europe, but not right away. On May 29, Bloomberg opinion columnist Javier Blas posted the following on X: "On April 16, the [International Energy Agency] made a headline-grabbing warning: Europe had ‘maybe six weeks or so of jet fuel left.’ It’s week seven; the planes are still flying. Since those headlines, European wholesale jet fuel prices have fallen ∼30%, to a ∼3-month low."

One reason the U.S. might not feel urgency surrounding Iran negotiations is that Europe is not in a jet fuel crisis—yet. Of course, this depends how you define a crisis: Is the crisis a real shortage or an increased price per metric ton? Wall Street is more concerned about profitability, and higher input costs are always a factor. But some analysts are trying to figure out where the sky-is-falling narrative was wrong.

Farmers Hit Hard

fotokostic/iStock/Getty Images Plus

A metric called total factor productivity (TFP) shows how efficiently farmers convert inputs into final outputs. Basically, this is the equivalent of GDP data. There has been a 1.49% increase in TFP, and most of that growth has come from more intense use of inputs such as capital, labor, and (especially now) technology. This means the agricultural sector is more sensitive than ever to shifts in technology, labor availability, and the overall supply chain.

The harvest predictions are not all that promising. The year started with some decent news as there was a break in some of the drought areas, but other factors have not been so benign. The tariffs interfered with many key export markets, and the oil crisis drove prices for diesel and many other petrochemical-based products to record levels.

While all the attention has been focused on the impact of the Iran war on transportation, there has been an even more profound impact on the farm sector, given the role that petrochemicals play in agriculture. Fully one-third of the world’s urea fertilizer comes from Saudi Arabia, Qatar, the UAE, and Iran. One-quarter of the world’s ammonia comes from Qatar and Saudi Arabia. More than 20% of all petrochemicals come from Saudi Arabia, Iran, and the UAE. Diesel prices are flirting with all-time highs and will likely stay that high through harvest time.

All this is important because the agricultural sector matters more to manufacturers than many outside the industry realize. The farm sector is extremely dependent on machinery at every level of the process. The agriculture sector accounts for close to 25% of total manufacturing in the U.S. and more than 20% of all manufacturing jobs. That is twice the number of jobs accounted for by the automotive sector. A good crop year means demand for manufactured output. Remember that the farmer essentially spends money 364 days a year and makes money on one.

Economic Perspective

My father was an engineer who looked at the glass conundrum differently than most. He didn’t think the glass was half full or half empty; he simply concluded that the vessel for the liquids was wrong.

The economist has another interpretation: What if the glass is both half empty and half full? It would depend on one’s perspective. The economy now has many contradictory trends. Millions of people are worried about the security of their jobs; meanwhile, a chronic labor shortage in manufacturing, construction, and health care has meant job security for those with the needed skills.

Consumer sentiment is hitting very low levels, enough to trigger warnings regarding a recession, but consumer spending continues to drive retail numbers to record highs. Several sectors are seeing severe declines (automotive, for example); others are booming (nonresidential construction, machinery, and even aerospace). What gives?

Obviously, unique factors affect the growth of a sector or specific business. This is a massive economy. At any given moment, some regions might be growing while others seem to take the brunt of the economic collapse. Regardless, two overall drivers manage to supersede these specific and regional variations.

The first is the K-shaped recovery. The upper third of income earners, those making more than $100,000 a year, are essentially supporting the economy. Much of their income comes from the markets, and they have been watching new records set almost every week. When markets correct, these high-earning consumers will start to slow down.

The bad news (glass half empty) is that the bottom third of consumers (those making less than $50,000) have been virtually shut out of the economy, with 95% of them living paycheck to paycheck. They’re getting eaten alive by inflation. About half of the middle third—those with job security—have been contributing to economic growth. All this basically means that half the consumers are sufficient to drive the economy forward.

The second, less obvious driver is that foreign demand for U.S. products has been on the increase despite all the trade wars and tariff fights. The U.S. remains a high-value manufacturing nation. These products are in demand in the developed and developing markets. China remains a global manufacturing powerhouse, but most of China’s exports are consumer goods and lower-value manufacturing.

The U.S. is a huge nation with a GDP larger than the combined GDPs of China, Germany, France, and the U.K. At any one time, part of the U.S. flirts with recession while another part experiences a surge. I speak to associations where members are close to panic and associations where everybody reports a record year. Diversity dominates.
