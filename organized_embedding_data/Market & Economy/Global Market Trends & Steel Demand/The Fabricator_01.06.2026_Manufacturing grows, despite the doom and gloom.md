# Manufacturing grows, despite the doom and gloom

[TARİH: 01.06.2026 The Fabricator]

Fabrinomics | Economic Insights

Dr. Chris Kuehl

You know the adage: If it bleeds, it leads. Media is all about drama and the never-ending quest for eyeballs. Nothing says drama like doom and gloom.

Hypersensitivity of markets and speculators seems to increase with every bit of news. Business leaders need to be able to make measured decisions, and the fog of war makes that difficult.

I am not downplaying the impact of the oil crisis. There will indeed be a crisis point showing up soon, one that tips the scales in the oil world and delivers $200/barrel oil. It is obvious that the supply chain has been hammered by all this, but what else is taking place in the world while all the attention has been focused on Iran and oil and the Strait of Hormuz?

In late April, I was on the road talking to a variety of groups and businesses in the manufacturing space. There has been something of a theme. They are having a generally good year, in some cases a record year. The banks reported a record level of loan demand for construction projects as well as expansion of manufacturing capacity. Not that there are no concerns; they all reported that input costs are rising very quickly and putting pressure on profits.

Observing this is one thing; explaining it is another. Three factors are at work. One has been having an impact for years now, the second is connected to the oil crisis, and the third is an attempt by many companies to change their business strategies to handle the emerging future.

The first factor, reshoring, has been going on for several years now— almost $1 trillion of reshoring every year since 2010. This was taking place even before the tariff pressure, as production overseas had been getting complex and expensive. Wages have been rising in China, India, and everywhere else. They are still lower than in the U.S., but not as low as they have been. Transportation costs have increased, and customers have been getting ever more demanding. The bottom line is that business has found it profitable to shift back to the U.S.

The second factor is that companies are trying to get ahead of anticipated price hikes. If fuel costs really do hit record levels, it will challenge any sort of profitability. Companies are rushing to get ahead of these increases in the way they were trying to get ahead of the tariffs last year. This strategy will work in the short run, but they need a resolution to the supply chain crisis sooner than later.

Finally, the world is adjusting to AI, robots, cobots, and advanced technology of all kinds. The modern company must now figure out how to work with reduced staff, much narrower margins, faster rates of industry change, and more demanding consumers. Those who master this shift will prosper and thrive.

We hear plenty of dire warnings, and some are indeed justified. But one must look at the experience of one’s own company. Many are doing pretty well these days. That is no reason to ignore the threats, but it also helps to keep a level head.

Manufacturing Expansion Expected to Continue

According to the most recent report on manufacturing from Armada Corporate Intelligence, the overall news is still solid, with the recent performance well above the 20-year trend line. There are, however, some bumps starting to manifest. There appears to be a slowdown showing up in the middle of 2027 after a solid period of expansion, but then the pace picks back up again toward the end of 2027. Even with that slump, the data is getting back to levels seen before the pandemic recession. (See chart at left.)

What’s causing some of this up and down? Some predictable behaviors emerge with unexpected crises, such as massive oilprice spikes. Manufacturers work to get ahead of price hikes as they are hit from all sides with fuel costs. It affects the energy they use to operate, and it affects logistics costs profoundly, inbound as well as outbound. There is evidence of manufacturers producing more than usual to get ahead of some of this price jump, and that drives the output numbers for a while. That is generally followed by a slowdown as inventories build beyond what is actually needed.

A few factors will continue driving manufacturing growth for the next year or so, though some may soon be coming to an end—namely, orders getting ahead of price jumps.

Fabricated Metal Demand Remains Strong

The demand for fabricated metal products keeps climbing, and that has managed to reverse a long slide in demand. Numbers have not returned to levels seen before the pandemic recession, but they are as high as they have been since that downturn. This demand has been driven by a variety of factors, as fabricated metal gets used in a wide range of sectors. (See chart at right.)

Those sectors are among the strongest of current economic drivers. They’re expected to stay in that position for the next year or longer. In fact, you’ll find very few parts of the economy that do not involve fabricated metal, but some stand out—and currently the hottest markets revolve around the rush to build data centers and server farms. These machines are encased in fabricated metal and acres of racks made of fabricated metal. Add in demand for medical devices, appliances, and the building trades, and you get the picture. We expect continued high demand though this year and into 2027 before a slight slowdown.

The Lingering Crises: Oil, Interest Rates, Inflation

The current economy has plenty of encouraging news and a good deal of justified optimism. But hanging over all of this is the oil crisis, akin to the sword of Damocles, the Greek anecdote signaling imminent peril. The crisis inches closer every day that the Iran war drags on.

Oil companies see a transportation challenge, not a shortage problem. Just a few months ago, analysts warned of an oil glut, with per-barrel prices falling as low as $45. That would be disastrous for U.S. oil producers, as they can’t make money at anything less than $60/barrel (and would prefer closer to $70). Radical production now would be a disaster once the Strait of Hormuz reopens.

Furthermore, the factor that has driven oil prices to well over $100/barrel is closure of the route that almost a third of the world’s oil uses. Production is not the problem. The oil producers feel that this issue was created by politics and it will have to be solved by politics. At some point, either the U.S. or Iran will be forced to capitulate, and in the meantime oil prices will soar.

The European Central Bank and the Bank of England have both followed the lead of the Fed as they elected to keep interest rates right where they are. It is not that there is no rationale for lowering rates; the British and European economies clearly need a shot in the arm as growth has been downright anemic thus far this year. The problem is that the oil crisis has introduced a very sharp inflation surge that shows little sign of retreating.

The surge has gone on so long that it’s had a profound impact on the supply chain. Inflation will easily extend for months before receding. Some sectors will have to confront much higher inflation for the majority of the year. The banks have not only elected to stand pat for now, but they have even hinted at raising rates if the inflation numbers keep climbing. This is not the news that anybody wanted to hear.

Even so, as of this early May writing, Iran is growing weaker. It has now become a race to determine whether the U.S. or Iran is the most vulnerable, and China is at the heart of this discussion. Iran has survived the sanctions and attacks because it could ship to China, but the blockade has cut that off, and Iran is very rapidly losing its ability to make any money at all from its oil.

The nation is weeks away from being utterly broke. The regime can no longer pay its military or secure new weapons. This is when a dictatorial regime is at its most vulnerable. Will this mean that the government of Masoud Pezeshkian can finally emerge and cut a deal without the fanatics in the Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps undercutting it?

For the U.S., the tipping point might have already passed by the time you read this. If the oil crisis continues to linger, gas prices could be as high as $8.00 a gallon. For an average fill-up of 12 gallons, a consumer will be forking out nearly $100. This crushes the economy very quickly. Freight costs soar, trucking companies declare bankruptcy, and drivers simply can’t drive. To assert that this would be catastrophic for the economy would not be an understatement. This obviously imposes immense pressure on the U.S. to find a way out of the crisis.

We live in a tale of two economic stories. One tells of dire warnings if geopolitical events spin out of control. The other story isn’t so dire. In fact, the current state of the economy continues to show resilience across manufacturing, especially in metal fabrication. Data centers and the infrastructure supporting them need metal products, and fabricators are happy to provide them.
