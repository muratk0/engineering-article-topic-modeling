# Pivot, adapt, grow

Strength Through Diversification

By

Tim Heston

No. 1: Mayville Engineering Co. (MEC)

"We’ve laid out a goal to double our company size, to reach $1 billion in revenue. We still think we’ll get there. The current environment doesn’t help organic growth, but we won’t be stuck in this trough forever. In the coming years, we still expect to double in size, both organically and by using our balance sheet to acquire companies."

That was Jag Reddy, president and CEO of MEC. The Milwaukee-based contract metal fabricator has topped the FAB 40 for more than a decade. Just 10 years ago, it reported revenue of $334 million. Its 2024 revenue came in at $581.6 million. Like other major FAB 40 players, it’s also expecting softer performance in 2025.

"The direct impact [of tariffs] to MEC is extremely small," Reddy said, adding that MEC’s inputs are largely domestic, as are the products it sells. "Even so, we are concerned about the uncertainty that exists in the market."

Despite current uncertainty, MEC’s long-term growth plans haven’t changed. The company’s brand of continuous improvement—MBX, or MEC Business Excellence—continues to drive the operation forward.

Most recently, MEC has focused on greater cohesion within links earlier in the value chain—specifically, from quoting and order processing to its release to the shop floor. After so many acquisitions over the years, MEC leaders knew the company could benefit from standardized front-end processes.

Look at the history of the FAB 40, and you’ll see some of MEC’s most dramatic revenue jumps after major acquisitions, including Center Manufacturing in Michigan and Defiance Metal Products in Ohio. Smart acquisitions help grow the revenue pie and greatly diversify revenue streams at the same time. They don’t come without their challenges, though.

"Historically, portions of our organization were siloed," Reddy said, "based on previous acquisitions and previous ERP systems. We’ve now integrated all of that together, so that all of our application engineering, quoting, and revenue operations are grouped under one umbrella."

Having siloes isn’t always negative, especially after a major acquisition. The last thing an acquiring company wants to do is change everything immediately, especially if the structures in place worked well in the past. Immediate changes can destroy relationships and rock what was previously a strong, steady boat—something that made the organization worth buying in the first place. Over time, though, a lack of integration can be like an anchor thrown overboard, slowing everything down and making steering and navigation difficult.

Last year, MEC made sure that anchor never dropped. It reorganized its front-office operations.

"Before 2024, we had sales, estimating, and engineering all in one group, and we sometimes had salespeople supervising estimators and engineers," said Ryan Raber, executive vice president of sales and marketing. "We now have two distinct groups. We have an engineering team, reporting to me, that performs the application engineering, estimating, and order processing. These functional areas focus on operational excellence, and their efforts support everyone across the business. They’re end-market agnostic."

The sales teams are still separated by end markets, but they also work together to ensure revenue operations act as a cohesive unit. "They’re managing business processes," Raber said, "including anything related to compliance and pricing."

The reorganization reveals how beneficial scaling up can be; but it also reveals the challenges, which may be why relatively few in contract metal fabrication have scaled up the way MEC has. At smaller operations, a customer mix really defines how work flows through the enterprise. That’s why two fabricators might have identical or similar machines, yet still operate extremely differently. Management, sales teams, estimating, order processing, plant layout—they all vary depending on volumes, demand trends, and the nature of jobs being fabricated. They also vary with the goals (and sometimes the personality) of the owners.

At some point on the growth curve, though, a fabricator’s operational platform provides the foundation for growth. At MEC, that platform incorporates a combination of continuous improvement through MBX as well as process diversification, asking "What else do customers need?" This led to MEC’s 2023 acquisition of Mid-States Aluminum Corp., which produces custom aluminum fabrications and extrusions. MEC customers are lightweighting their designs, and MSA has the expertise and capability to serve that need.

MEC won nearly $100 million in new work last year, some of it from new program activity in commercial trucking, which is preparing to meet new EPA regulations to be implemented for the 2027 model year. "We had wins in every industry," Raber said. "We’ve been running hot in power sports, and our performance there is different than the overall market. That and commercial trucking has the heaviest weighting in our new business. Plus, like many others, we’re seeing pockets of engagement in the critical power infrastructure and data center sectors. Meanwhile, agricultural equipment is seeing the most severe pressure, but it’s only about 8% of our business. It’s one reason we focus so heavily on diversification."

Driving that quest for diversification are those front-end processes, including quoting, sales, engineering, nesting, and machine programming. It is within these areas, Reddy said, where some of the most transformative technologies, including AI and other advanced software, might be deployed in the coming years—not just at MEC but throughout the industry.

"Imagine, when you win business, having everything you need to know about how to manufacture the product," Reddy said, adding that this could include AI tools to generate G codes, laser and brake programming, welding robot programs, and more. "Technology is evolving so quickly, and it’s exciting to think about what the end state will be."

No. 3: BTD

Jared Lotzer can’t remember a time when BTD received so many quote inquiries. "With all those inquiries, the challenge will be what’s going to come back and stay?"

The vice president of business development and commercial operations at the Detroit Lakes, Minn., fabricator has been working with his team to determine what’s a good fit, what’s a real opportunity, and who might be just kicking the tires. "We’re being very cautious. Are prospective customers using domestic suppliers as part of a risk mitigation strategy, or is reshoring part of a long-term move?"

The moves might be long term if the products’ supply chain isn’t embedded heavily in Asia. Labor costs also play a role. If prospects seeking stateside suppliers have a history of chasing low labor costs, they might not be a good fit. Toolmaking requirements is another consideration. Beyond press brake forming, BTD employs a host of stamping technologies for higher-volume work, and its in-house toolmaking expertise is one of its major selling points.

The metal fabrication giant, with its five locations covering the Midwest and Southeast, has a longterm strategy that many in the FAB 40 share: revenue diversification and the ability to pivot with shifts in market demand. Bright spots over the past year have included products for the construction sector.

"And, of course, we’re seeing the strongest success in data centers," Lotzer said. "We’ve pivoted, and we’re now playing in that space aggressively."

Like many fabricators, BTD is experiencing a moderate lull in demand, and it’s focusing on cost control—though not through downsizing. "We’ve leaned down our inventory and emphasized cost controls throughout our operations, while also holding on to labor," Lotzer said. "We need to make sure we have the labor we need to pivot and react to demand."

Part of the new opportunity comes from having a clear understanding of truly available capacity. During times of transition, the worst thing any business can do is overpromise and underdeliver.

"That’s a key differentiator for us," Lotzer said. "We put a lot of energy into developing our robust SIOP [sales, inventory, and operations planning] system. We generate a lot of BI [business intelligence] reports. A few mouse clicks give us a lot of visibility."

The visibility goes down to the work center level. Sales and engineering can, at any given moment, show customers exactly how much available capacity BTD has in cutting, forming, even at a particular stamping press. Incorporating a buffer to account for real-world variability, the BI software reveals "how many hours we have available, the manpower capacity, and equipment capacity. We can really dial it all in."

Such intelligence has allowed BTD to carefully manage its finished goods inventory. The fabricator is known for offering its customers products on a replenishment basis. BTD manufactures to stock, customers draw down the inventory as they need it, and BTD replenishes as needed. Today, the inventory is leaner. Managers know they have capacity to ramp up quickly to meet demand. At some point, when shop capacity utilization grows, the company will choose to rebuild that finished goods inventory to previous levels.

In the coming months, BTD will be taking their planning a step further with DDMRP, or data-driven materials resource planning. "This tool incorporates AI to enable us to learn and understand our customers’ requirements and ordering habits, which should help us shape our production plans," Lotzer said.

When it comes to customer demand patterns, fabricators have a lot to digest, and software could certainly streamline the process. For instance, Lotzer described how the lawn and garden market underwent major, rapid swings before, during, and after the pandemic. The world shut down. People had time to mow the lawn, demand went through the roof, and the supply chain reacted.

"The supply chain [in lawn and garden] is simpler than it is for other markets," Lotzer said, "so customers could ramp up extremely quickly. We had components for lawn and garden products that were selling at three to four times their historic volumes. They then made a big correction. Now they’re back to volume that’s consistent prior to COVID."

In late April, BTD had a ribbon-cutting event in Dawsonville, Ga., opening another facility adjacent to an existing plant on the same campus. The company owns 33 acres on the same site, giving BTD room to expand even more.

Lotzer conceded that the uncertainty around tariffs has made forecasting extremely difficult. And like several other major players on the FAB 40, the company is expecting revenue to fall moderately this year. Even so, BTD isn’t standing still.

No. 7: Robinson Inc.

When it comes to scaling up, simplicity is sometimes best. About six years ago, the org chart at Robinson Inc. wasn’t so simple. The fabricator was split into six divisions, each focused on a market niche, from pipes and vessels to custom enclosures to HVAC components.

"Within most of those divisions, we’d bring in raw material and turn it into something. We began to realize that all those divisions served as a distraction as we listened to what customers were asking for. So, we shed all that and put it under one umbrella. We’re still flexible, and we still have directors that lead different segments of the business, but we do it all with one goal. We’re all pulling on the rope the same way."

That was Sam Thomas, vice president and COO of the De Pere, Wis.-based fabricator.

"We used to look at ourselves as one of the largest custom fabrication job shops in the country," he said. "That’s how we were structured. We still have that background of custom fabrication, but we now position ourselves to be more of a production-minded contract manufacturer. That has allowed us to home in on industries that really have a need for that."

This mindset has driven Robinson to revamp plant layouts. "From a growth standpoint, our facilities weren’t as flexible as they could have been," Thomas said, "especially for our power generation work. The size of those tanks, skids, and enclosures continue to get bigger. So, we started sizing all of our paint booths, lasers, forming capacity, and the plant layouts themselves to handle the largest products that come in the door. That dictated the size of our bays in assembly and fabrication, the door sizes on the end of the facilities, even the lifting capabilities for our cranes."

Within the next six months, Robinson plans to install systems that will automate most aspects of its cutting, forming, and welding. "We have seen this kind of automation before, but until recently it just wasn’t flexible enough for us," Thomas said. "That’s changing. Now, with the volume of backlog with certain products, we feel comfortable moving forward."

At this writing, the systems are far from being finalized, but the goal is to have products cut, bent, then carried directly to welding. The first time someone touches the product will be at the powder coating line.

Projects at Robinson can be extraordinarily various in nearly every respect: workpiece sizes, project deadlines and timelines, supply chain complexity, assembly. But they all shared the common thread of metal fabrication.

The space projects consume varies, from small workpieces to giant assemblies. Plants need space to transport and store them all. Easy transportation improves flow through the value stream. Meanwhile, the need for inventory space has to do with the nature of Robinson’s project-oriented business. The fabricator needs space to store even its largest projects, should the unexpected happen.

"We’re essentially trying to help our customers by being the center hub of their supply chains," Thomas said. "We might work with a customer to fabricate a large generator and have it ready to go, knowing full well that the site won’t be ready for it for another few weeks. But our customers know that the generator is ready to go. They know, should the project schedule change, that they won’t have to rush or hold up a project. For our customers, there’s incredible value there."

Robinson’s fiscal year ends June 30, at which point leaders expect annual revenue to reach about $275 million. With a third facility in De Pere—one with some serious acreage for product storage (again, to absorb that supply chain uncertainty)—the fabricator is expecting revenue next year to exceed $350 million.

That’s good news for employees, all of whom now have a stake in the business. In 2023 the company became 100% employee owned after introducing an employee stock ownership plan (ESOP). "As we look out over the next few years," Thomas said, "with potential expansion opportunities and strategic partnerships with our customers, it’s possible that by 2027, we become a half-billion-dollar company."

Thomas conceded that the tariffs have added a level of uncertainty. At this writing, projects are on schedule, so the fabricator hasn’t experienced any immediate impacts. But if global supply chains become strained—especially in the battery technology space—the business landscape could shift.

That said, Robinson is building itself to react and pivot. Its largest projects involve immense logistical costs. The fabricator bills itself as the final stopping point before large fabrications reach their destination. "We know we can help our customers reduce their freight costs if we’re strategically placed throughout the country," Thomas said. "We’re looking at an out-ofstate facility right now, in fact."

Finally, the company has launched a dedicated education facility, called the Robinson Training Academy, training people on all production positions. "We want to train ‘the Robinson Way,’ so to speak," Thomas said, "and really create career paths for people. We want to show people they’re not just coming into a job. They’re starting a career."

No. 8: IMS Companies

Steve Tokarz, president of Des Plaines, Ill.-based IMS Companies, pointed to a two-year-old automated line comprising a material handling tower, a punch, and a panel bender. Flowing through those machines are components for data center racks. The workflow in kit fashion, one different part after another. Once a kit is complete, it can move directly to assembly. Close by, a stack of parts sits near an adjacent work center. They’ll soon become power whips connecting power distribution units within server racks. At IMS, business is booming.

"When you ask about business now, it all depends on what markets and customers you serve," Tokarz said. "We’ve got electrical infrastructure, the data center market, the semiconductor business. And we’re seeing a lot in the transportation industry. At the moment, we’re bullish. … Now, there’s a huge unknown about how tariffs will ultimately affect us, but I’m still cautiously optimistic."

Tokarz has reason to be, considering the conversations he’s had with potential customers over the past few months. In most cases, the interest focuses on geography. If companies abroad are selling products in the U.S., they’re looking to make those products here, especially if it fits well within other pieces of their supply chains.

As Tokarz described, IMS has a robust process for ramping up new work. "We developed a product lifecycle process a long time ago," he said. "The process takes our part programs from cradle to grave. And we have project managers that facilitate the entire product launch. We have robust documents and procedures we follow to make sure we don’t miss a beat when we’re launching new programs."

Years ago, at a previous employer, Tokarz had to deal with the opposite of what he’s managing today. "We had to move a lot of business to China and Mexico. It was terrible. We had to lay off everyone."

Today, he’s happily doing the opposite: hiring workers and planning for double-digit growth. At present, Tokarz anticipates IMS will grow by 12% to 13% annually over the next three to five years.

IMS’s mix of work consists of gauge material that, when it comes to welding, requires a special touch. Welders carefully wield a GTAW torch around challenging joint geometries, controlling heat input to minimize or eliminate postweld processing. The skill can’t be learned overnight. "That’s my biggest concern," Tokarz said, not just for IMS but for metal fabrication and manufacturing in general. "If a significant amount of work comes back [from overseas], we simply don’t have the skill sets we need."

IMS’s operation exemplifies how fabricators are managing. The punching and panel bending cell increases throughput with zero changeover times, greatly improving throughput and worker ergonomics. Company engineers work with customers to design their parts for manufacturability so that, for instance, robots can feed parts and panel benders can form them.

They automate welding where it makes sense but also make every effort to maximize the skilled labor they do find. Tight-tolerance welding remains one of IMS’s key selling points, and skilled welders make that happen.

IMS uses real-time capacity planning tools to track the order book and backlog. As Tokarz explained, "From our ERP, we disseminate that information to the shop floor on a continual bases, so we always know what we should be working on and when, how far we’re along in a project, and if we need to make adjustments."

The company also is perfecting its quoting and new-customer onboarding, looking for ways to streamline the process—especially for work involving complex assemblies. "The key is about moving quickly," Tokarz said.

"That will allow us, as well as any other manufacturer for that matter, to really capitalize on the business that could be moving back to the U.S."
