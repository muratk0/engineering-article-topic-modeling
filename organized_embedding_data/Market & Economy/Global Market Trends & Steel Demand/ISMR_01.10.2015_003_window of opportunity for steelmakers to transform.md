# Window of opportunity for steelmakers to transform

[TARİH: 01.10.2015 ISMR]

STEEL COMPANIES around the world must find the right balance between globalization and customisation if they are going to survive and prosper, according to EY’s latest annual global steel report, ‘Globalize or Customize: Finding the Right Balance, Global Steel 2015-2016’. It is EY’s fifth annual analysis and assessment of the risks and opportunities facing companies in the steel sector globally.

Excess capacity continues to be the biggest challenge in the global steel sector, amidst declining growth in global demand driven in large part by China. Much of the surplus capacity is attributable to Chinese steel producers, although they are often displacing higher cost, less efficient production elsewhere in the world. The Chinese Government’s planned rationalisation of its steel sector over the next 10 years – to deal with its excess capacity, pollution, low market concentration and lack of profitability – is expected to create a smaller number of large and efficient Chinese steel companies.

“Non-Chinese steelmakers have a small window of opportunity to build competitive advantage before supersized, efficient Chinese steelmakers emerge more strongly in the global market. To make the most of this opportunity, the sector will need to undergo significant change and transform itself into a far more efficient and competitive sector able to create and retain value and turn profitable,”

commented Anjani Agrawal, EY’s global steel leader.

The report notes four key areas of transformation for steel companies around the world – rationalise excess capacity, increase market and product concentration, boost market competitiveness and embrace digital.

Despite consolidation from 1995 to 2005, the global steel industry is still relatively fragmented with market share of the top 10 steelmakers at just 28% – low in comparison to the automotive and seaborne iron ore markets. Steelmakers, says the report, need to focus on developing premium, value-added, niche downstream products for new sectors or specialty applications.

Blechexpo and Schweisstec will showcase a full spectrum of practical, stand-alone and complete hardware and software solutions ranging from components and modules up to subsystems and tooling.

"A large number of well known manufacturers and distributors from fields such as measurement technology, image processing and vision systems have also registered to participate at Blechexpo and Schweisstec. The Schweisstec international specialist trade fair for joining technology is a strong complement, showcasing the relevant mechanical and thermal joining and connecting procedures as well as thermal welding processes. The show remit has also been expanded to include relevant areas for processing directly from the strip coil or the wire coil for electrical and electromechanical products.

2015 MARKS THE 80TH ANNIVERSARY of the beverage can. When Krueger Brewing Company sold its first beer in a can in Richmond, Virginia in the US in 1935, the new packaging concept was met with immediate success. Compared to glass, cans were lightweight, cheap, and easy to stack and ship for the distributor. For the customer, they did not require the deposit typically paid for glass bottles, as they were discarded after use. Shortly after the introduction of canned beer, soft drinks became available in cans as well.

"The first cans were flat-topped and made of steel. Cans had to be opened using a can-piercer, a device resembling a bottle opener with a sharp point and by punching two triangular holes in the lid – a large one for drinking and a second one to admit air. Some breweries tried out cans with conical rather than flat tops, but these didn’t stack and ship as easily and were quickly abandoned," commented worldsteel.

"In 1962, the American Ermal Fraze developed a can-opening method that would come to dominate the canned beverage market. His invention was the "pull-tab". This eliminated the need for a separate opener tool. Into the 1970s, the pull-tab was widely popular but its popularity came with a significant problem, as people would frequently discard the pull-tabs on the ground as litter or drop them into the can and risk choking on them. These problems were both eventually solved later in the 1970s when the same Fraze patented the first ‘push-in and foldback’ tab. This tab remained attached to the can, and it is this design that is still used on canned beverages today.

"Since the 1980s, cans have taken on new sizes and shapes, have seen the introduction of new printing methods and embossing techniques, and have been adapted to include the wide-mouth opening. Cans have also become much lighter over time – a standard 33cl steel beverage can weighed 100g in 1935 compared to around 20g today, demonstrating an 80 per cent improvement in material efficiency without any functionality loss from the can.

“Cans have the advantage of being 100 per cent recyclable and they can be recycled infinitely. Recycling rates reached 72 per cent in the US and 74 per cent in Europe for 2012."

Beverage cans maintain a certain number of other advantages over other packaging solutions. They are easy to brand, easy to fill at high speeds, extremely rigid and tough, both on filling lines and at the point of sale. Contents are protected from air and light, which can change a product’s taste and smell, so cans are ideal for carbonated beverages, as well as being the fastest packaging format to chill. Cans are compact and stackable, which means convenience throughout the supply chain.

In 2013, around 300 billion beverage cans were manufactured globally, and the market was growing at around 3 per cent per annum. Technavio's analysts forecast the global food and beverage metal can market to grow at a CAGR of 3.01 per cent and 3.22 per cent, in terms of revenue and volume, respectively, over the period 2014-2019.
