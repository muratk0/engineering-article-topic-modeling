T

he president-elect is no stranger to the U.S. manufacturing community. In fact, his support in Rust Belt states like Ohio, Pennsylvania, and Wisconsin effectively destroyed any path that Hillary Clinton had to the White House, once battleground states like Florida and North Carolina turned red on the evening of Nov. 8. Trump even spent some time at Staub Manufacturing Solutions in Dayton in late September, taking a private meeting with company management to learn about their business and concerns.

So what does this mean for manufacturing in the U.S.? Responses from readers over the past couple of months suggest that they couldn’t be happier with the result.

Here’s what we know:

Tax cuts are coming.

This was one of the few areas where the Trump campaign was sympatico with the GOP establishment in Congress. Trump said that he’d decrease corporate taxes to 15 percent and that companies organized as S Corps, which includes many metal fabricating companies, would see a top tax rate of 15 percent.

He also mentioned closing loopholes as part of his tax reform effort, but that’s easier said than done when these loopholes are bound to be supported by interests capable of huge lobbying efforts. The Republicans may control the Senate and the House of Representatives, but they also have to run for re-election at some time. Reform is going to call for old-school compromise.

The Affordable Care Act is in the crosshairs.

After several failed, really symbolic attempts to overthrow "Obamacare" over the past several years, congressional Republicans are licking their chops to dismantle the law. The question is how they will go about it.

The first thing they need to do is have a plan to replace it, and party officials claim they are working on it. Meanwhile, don’t expect a total abolishment of the legislation. Trump has mentioned that he is in favor of preserving the law that allows people with pre-existing conditions to get medical coverage at reasonable prices, and that’s not going to sit well with the insurance providers that see it as lost revenue. Again, political compromise will have to occur, but change is coming.

Big data is overrated without proper context.

Have you had a day as bad as most pollsters had on Nov. 8? A majority missed with their state and national prognostications, and they also underestimated Trump’s popularity in key demographics, such as with women voters. They had the most advanced computing power working on these models, but in the end, they are still reliant on human input, which can be unreliable, especially in an election with controversial candidates. As any metal fabricator will tell you, tracking key performance metrics is good, if they are the ones that truly link to overall profitability.

Here’s what we don’t know:

What will become of the North American Free Trade Agreement (NAFTA)?

The candidate who extolled the idea of building a giant wall along the U.S. border with Mexico isn’t too thrilled with the 22-year-old trade agreement—at least according to the sound bites from his rallies. He’s even mentioned the introduction of tariffs on Mexican-made goods entering the U.S.

Truthfully, if NAFTA were scrapped, plants that relocated from the U.S. to Mexico aren’t likely to return to the U.S. en masse. Mexico has at least nine formal free trade agreements that connect them to 45 other countries; it has become a manufacturing launch pad to other markets. Also, most of the U.S. manufacturers that have located in Mexico are there to provide just-in-time deliveries and timely support.

The Trans-Pacific Partnership, President Obama’s attempt to set up a trade network designed to minimize China’s growing influence in the region, is all but dead now. In addition, Dan DiMicco, the former Nucor CEO, current Trump economic adviser, and potential cabinet member, is an aggressive critic of current free trade agreements. The early days of Trump’s presidency should give more indication of just how the U.S. will pivot when it comes to international trade.

In the meantime, the world leaders wait to see just what happens. Nationalism and populism have been on the rise throughout Europe. Brexit happened. But world leaders weren’t ready for the man that questioned the legitimacy of NATO to actually capture the presidency.

A portion of the U.S. electorate was stunned as well, but early predictions of an economic collapse didn’t come to fruition. On Nov. 9 the Dow Jones industrial average ended up at 257 points, or 1.4 percent, after tumbling as much as 800 points in late-night futures trading.

According to the "3rd Quarter 2016 Forming & Fabricating Job Shop Consumption Report," an FMA survey of its job shop members, 47.4 percent of those surveyed said that they had positive expectations for the final quarter of 2016, while 39.3 percent expected business to be stable. Many of those business owners are likely to be even more optimistic heading into 2017 with the GOP in control of D.C.
