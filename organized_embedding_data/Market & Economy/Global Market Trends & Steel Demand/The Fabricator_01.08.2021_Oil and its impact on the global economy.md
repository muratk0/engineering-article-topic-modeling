It is a slippery subject! Sorry, I couldn’t resist the opportunity given the topic for this column is oil. There are few commodities that evoke more reaction than oil and few that have an impact on the whole of the global economy. This stems from the fact that it’s the dominant fuel for the transportation sector and its by-products are ubiquitous. It’s also due to the fact that oil is the ultimate inelastic good. In econo-speak, this means it’s a product where price doesn’t really affect consumption. If the price per gallon of gas goes up, people will complain but they don’t stop commuting or hauling freight or even curtail their errands. By the same token, when the price per gallon falls, you don’t see people driving aimlessly or taking the long route to work because of the price.

Oil prices crashed to record lows in 2020 as demand slumped. People stopped commuting, transport of materials sagged, and shipping ground to a halt. For a few weeks, the price per barrel was less than $20. They have jumped back to prices above $70 and seem to be staying in that range. As long as demand holds, these prices will not fall appreciably unless supply increases. What would convince suppliers to produce more oil if they know that would cause prices to fall?

WHAT WOULD CONVINCE SUPPLIERS TO PRODUCE MORE OIL IF THEY KNOW THAT WOULD CAUSE PRICES TO FALL?

There are three motivations. The first is to blunt competition. There are nations that can make profits at oil priced in the 60s, 50s, 40s, and even 30s. They do not want to lose market share by allowing the price per barrel to rise to the point that marginal players can get engaged. The second motivation is that some nations need the money more than others and would like consumption levels to stay high. They do not want to risk losing income because people and industries start to cut back. Oil may be inelastic but there comes a point where people can’t handle the costs. There are even political considerations. Russia wants to see higher prices as it hurts the U.S., other nations want to curry favor with the U.S. as they boost output. Then there is the U.S. itself — the world’s largest oil producer. The suppliers here have an incentive to bolster a healthy U.S. economy. The bottom line is that oil prices are likely near their peak unless there’s a sudden issue from weather, geopolitics, or some other unforeseen event.
