# Country-specific reciprocal tariffs at 10%; trade war with China escalates

[TARİH: 01.05.2025 The Fabricator]

Around Washington

Tariffs can now exceed 170% on more than 6,800 imports from China

OMAR NASHASHIBI

T

he Trump administration announced on April 9 that it would pause the country-specific "reciprocal" tariffs announced by President Donald Trump on April 2, and instead impose a 10% tariff on those countries and virtually all others. Referred to by the administration as Liberation Day, the 10% rate will apply to those roughly 80 countries for 90 days while the administration begins negotiations with America’s trading partners.

In the same action, Trump implemented a 125% tariff on Chinese imports, which, combined with previous tariffs and existing duties, results in rates of 145%, 170%, or even 245%, in the case of some COVID-related imports.

The April 2 announcement included 10% baseline tariffs on all countries except Canada and Mexico, along with higher individualized reciprocal tariffs on countries with which the United States has significant trade deficits. This action was taken under the International Emergency Economic Powers Act (IEEPA) following a national emergency declaration by the president, citing persistent annual U.S. goods deficits.

On April 9, the day the reciprocal tariffs were scheduled to go into effect, Trump signed an executive order (EO) pausing the higher reciprocal tariffs until July 9 for all countries except China and instead subject all foreign trading partners to the 10% baseline tariff.

In that same EO, Trump increased the tariff rate on China to 125%, which follows an earlier increase on April 8 to 84% from the 34% originally established in the April 2 announcement. This 125% tariff is in addition to the 20% levied under the IEEPA on March 4 and any potential duties as part of the Section 301 tariff action. Tariffs can now exceed 170% on more than 6,800 shipments from China.

Tariffs Imposed on Autos and Parts

On March 26, Trump signed a presidential proclamation that established a 25% tariff on automobiles and parts, citing a "critical threat to U.S. national security" under Section 232 of the Trade Expansion Act of 1962. The new tariff applies to all imported passenger vehicles and light trucks, as well as essential auto parts from every country involved. Specific parts affected include motor vehicle mountings and fittings, hinges, locks, and metal stampings for bumpers and related components.

For cars imported from Canada and Mexico under the United States-Mexico-Canada Agreement (USMCA), the tariff will apply exclusively to the non-U.S. content of the vehicle. Importers are required to provide documentation to the secretary of commerce detailing the U.S. content for each vehicle model that they import, enabling the tariff to target only non-U.S. components. At present, the tariff on auto parts imported under USMCA is on hold until the Commerce Department and U.S. Customs and Border Protection devise a method to assess tariffs specifically based on the value of non-U.S. content in these parts.

The tariff on automobiles took effect April 3, while the 25% tariff on auto parts will take effect no later than May 3.

Implementation of Trichloroethylene Rule Delayed

On April 2, the Environmental Protection Agency announced another delay to the effective date of the final risk management rule concerning trichloroethylene, under Section 6(a) of the Toxic Substances Control Act (TSCA). This rule, titled "Trichloroethylene (TCE); Regulation Under the Toxic Substances Control Act (TSCA)," was first announced Dec. 17, with an original effective date of Jan. 16. However, its implementation was postponed because of ongoing legal challenges.

Earlier, on Jan. 28, the EPA had already postponed the effective date of this regulation following a presidential memorandum issued Jan. 20. This memo recommended that federal agencies consider pushing back the effective dates for any rules published in the Federal Register or those that had not yet taken effect by an additional 60 days to address potential questions of fact, law, and policy that these rules might raise.

The updated TCE regulation prohibits the manufacturing, importing, processing, and distribution of TCE in all forms. The goal is to phase out most TCE applications within a year while allowing longer compliance timelines for certain critical uses, which must adhere to strict workplace safety protocols.

The Jan. 28 final rule established a new effective date for the TCE regulation as March 21. However, following the latest announcement on April 2, this enforcement date has been postponed again by 90 days, to June 20.
