A U.S.-U.K. meeting that took place in mid-September could prove a harbinger of tariff deals to come. Yes, we’ve heard that story before. Back in May, Steel Market Update trade columnist Lewis Leibowitz published an article, "The U.K. trade agreement, a template for future trade deals?" There was talk that the U.K. would receive some type of quota or deal, or that much lower rates would be negotiated. That didn’t materialize beyond the U.K. keeping its 25% tariff rate on steel and aluminum rather than see its tariffs doubled like other nations.

An article in Politico on Sept. 12 reported that steel talks have restarted between the two nations ahead of Trump’s visit to the U.K. in the ensuing days. The article said there’s a hope that Great Britain will receive some kind of quota deal, citing sources familiar with the matter. Déjà vu all over again.

Since the writing of Leibowitz’s article, the very idea of a "template" seems an anachronism. We’ve seen a hodgepodge of deals with no clear pattern. Also, of utmost global importance, the can got kicked down the road on an agreement with China, and it’s still unknown how exactly deals will work with USMCA partners Canada and Mexico.

Courting Trouble?

But it’s not only other countries that are reacting to Trump’s trade actions. Internal cracks have occurred. Specifically, on Aug. 29, the Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit struck down "reciprocal" tariffs under the International Emergency Economic Policy Act. That means the case will be heading to the Supreme Court. A decision there could curtail the president’s ability to dictate trade policy through executive decision.

Reports suggest the BRICS nations and other countries met virtually to talk about U.S. trade policy. They and others are waiting on the White House to establish a more consistent trade message, leaving behind ad-hoc tariff punishments and vague trade threats.

Another BRIC in the Wall

While the administration faces these internal challenges, another one could be brewing. When the biggest player on the block makes demands, sometimes the other kids join forces. This was the idea behind a recent BRICS virtual meeting. Would those nations band together as a counterweight to U.S. trade hegemony?

The BRICS nations now consist of Brazil, Russia, India, China, and South Africa, with recent additions including Iran, Egypt, Ethiopia, the United Arab Emirates, Saudi Arabia, and Indonesia.

Brazilian President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva held a virtual a meeting for leaders of the nations, according to a report by Bloomberg. The publication cited anonymous sources familiar with the matter. On the docket was Trump’s trade policy.

The article stated that the meeting would not just focus on Trump’s tariffs but also "multilateralism." That would seem to signify a collision course with the White House’s "America First" trade agenda.

It doesn’t seem the group made any big moves, at least according to the South China Morning Post, whose headline after the meeting sums it up: "BRICS virtual rally falters as members appear leery of exacerbating U.S. trade war."

So, the poker game goes on, and stakes continue to rise. We’re all waiting to see who takes the pot and if everyone will at least end up showing their cards.

ORAC Says

With so much confusion, maybe AI can be of some help. At SMU’s Steel Summit 2025 in Atlanta in late August, we decided to put our money where our mouth is. We loaded all the conference discussions into SMU parent company CRU’s proprietary AI, ORAC. I asked it to give a short, five-bulletpoint summary of the most important trade issues talked about by speakers. Here’s the list:

Section 232 reset: Tariffs increased to 50% and broadened to downstream derivatives

Global overcapacity: China’s exports cited as overwhelming markets

Policy uncertainty: Ad-hoc reciprocal tariffs complicate investment planning; calls to codify

Circumvention and USMCA review: Pressure increases to tighten rules of origin and stop transshipment

Tariffs vs. quotas and cost impact: Traders push quotas; panels warned of inflation and disruption

These are the kinds of issues we’ve been writing about for the last few months, and they seemed top of mind as well for discussion on the summit stage.

When I asked ORAC what issue steel market participants would want the most clarity on by Jan. 1, 2026, one issue leapt out: legal certainty on Section 232’s scope and permanence.

Of course, it’s not a surprise, but it’s good to see our concerns are borne out by the AI analysis. I think one of the most salient points at the conference was from Steel Manufacturers Association President Philip K. Bell during a panel on trade issues: "The president is using tariff policy in some very unique ways."

With so much disagreement out there on trade policy, I think that’s definitely one thing no one can dispute.
