# The tariff game and the economy

[TARİH: 01.04.2025 The Fabricator]

Fabrinomics | Economic Insights

The Game Is On. Now What?

Dr. Chris Kuehl

Now that we have been contending with the varying tariffs and tariff threats for a few months, it is probably useful to review the whole concept of a tariff as we look at how it impacts a given sector. I’m writing this in late February, so I won’t delve into what tariffs will occur when. The situation will likely change by the time you read this.

Regardless, the economy is already feeling the effects. One of the most vulnerable of these sectors in the U.S. is construction, but most of manufacturing has been affected—positively in some cases, negatively in others. These sectors have been truly global as far as their supply chains are concerned as product and raw materials originate from everywhere in the world.

The U.S. manufacturer is generally of two minds on tariffs. On the one hand, these barriers to imports will allow the U.S. producer to regain some market share and more effectively compete with nations that have much lower costs of production. On the other hand, the U.S. manufacturer relies on imports of commodities and intermediate goods that will now become more costly. It is going to be a matter of who has the leverage and whether there will be alternatives.

Protectionism or a Negotiating Tool?

Tariffs are among the oldest trade manipulators because the concept is simple. Use taxation (tariffs) to encourage a consumer to buy a domestically produced product instead of an imported version. It generally works like a charm if certain conditions are met. The most important of these is the need for the existence of a comparable domestic product. If there is no acceptable rival for the import, the consumer has no choice but to buy the import and pay the higher prices. The domestic consumer essentially pays the tariff as the exporter will simply pass that higher price on. Remarks about the tax benefits to the U.S. are misleading as the US can’t tax another nation. The extra cost of the good affected by a tariff will be paid by the U.S. consumer as the importer hikes prices to cover the tax.

This brings up the second condition: Can the consumer pay the higher prices? The likelihood is that the domestic alternative will be more expensive simply because the costs of production are generally higher in the U.S., and the tariffs will ensure that the imported version will be more costly as well. Can U.S. consumers pay more? Perhaps, but the most likely response will be a reduction in demand as a result and scaled down projects and perhaps some abandoned efforts altogether.

It is also important to understand the "real" intent of a tariff. It is fundamentally a tool used to underpin negotiations and bargaining. A nation is threatened with a tariff unless there is a concession granted. It has been abundantly obvious that this has been a tactic already deployed by the Trump White House. Mexico and Canada were threatened with immediate 25% tariffs, but avoided them for a month (so far at least at this wrirting, in late February) by agreeing to increase their commitment to border security.

Global Response

The EU is working to blunt a tariff on exported cars by reducing their 10% tariff on U.S. cars entering their market. Taiwan is hoping to avoid tariffs on the export of silicon chips by agreeing to build additional chip manufacturing capacity in the U.S. (South Korea is doing the same). It is one trade-off after another.

If one looks back at the first Trump term, there were 18,200 tariff exemptions granted as nations negotiated deals and as U.S. companies pointed out the damage these tariffs would do to them. For example, steel and aluminum tariffs on every nation soon devolved as Canada, Mexico, Brazil, Argentina, Europe, Turkey, Japan, South Korea, Taiwan, India, and Australia were exempted to one extent or another.

Net Effective Tariff Rate

Lastly, purchasing managers are learning to look at the Net Effective Tariff Rate when gauging the potential risk of a tariff on products. The Net Effective Tariff Rate occurs after currency exchange differences are factored in. Consider (oversimplified) a product worth $100 from a country with a 10% tariff imposed. The product cost is $110 under the applied tariff. But if the country getting hit with the tariff devalues its currency by 10% (as is often the case), the product would then cost $90, and after a 10% tariff is applied, it would only cost $99 (an actual discount to the pre-tariff amount).

There are many other factors that can affect the final price. Often, the tariff is set high enough that there is a net impact even after currency devaluation. In addition, the devaluation of currency carries with it other economic impacts to the country using this strategy—such as inflationary increases on inputs, higher cost of capitalization, and negatively impacts Treasury rates. But estimates suggest that the tariffs applied on Chinese products in the 2018/2019 period were offset by currency devaluation in the yuan, making the Net Effective Tariff Rate for U.S. importers ∼15% on 25% applied tariffs and ∼5% for 15% tariff categories. This is partly why inflation didn’t move from 2018 to 2019 (it increased by 0.2% overall).

Uncertainty Continues

Where does this leave the U.S. business and consumer? Unfortunately, in a state of some uncertainty. It is not clear that a given tariff will actually be imposed, nor for how long. It may be delayed or abandoned if concessions are made.

It becomes a matter of leverage—and who has it. Most nations need the U.S. market and will be willing to do what it takes to preserve that access. For decades, the U.S. approach has been to favor the needs of the consumer at the expense of the producers. We wanted the consumer to have the best product at the lowest price, no matter where the product came from. We have lost billions in productive capacity and hundreds of thousands of jobs in the process.

Now there is a push to get that manufacturing capacity back, but it will come at a cost, namely higher prices for the consumer. If we project a few years down the road, it will be a safe bet that certain industries will return to the U.S.—mostly the more sophisticated sectors that need to be close to their consumer base. It is also a safe bet that consumers will be paying higher prices across the board. Some price-increase estimates are as high as 20% in sectors currently dominated by imported goods.

U.S. Markets Fade as Others Gain

The mood in the U.S. investment community has been fading fast. Day after day there have been declines, and there is little to suggest this is simple correction behavior. The investor is worried about the impact of the tariffs on U.S. businesses, and they are even more concerned about the deteriorating consumer confidence level. This retreat would be significant enough all on its own, but at the same time there has been a surge in confidence in the European, Japanese, and even Chinese markets.

For years, the U.S. markets have grown in large part because a great deal of foreign investment money made its way to the U.S. as the economic performance in Europe and Asia sagged. Now, that cash has started to come home. This is good news for those sagging overseas markets, but not so positive for the U.S. The perception is that Trump’s tariffs will end up hurting U.S. companies far more than those in other parts of the world.

The Trump team has moved very quickly, and that has taken the rest of the world by surprise to a significant extent. The policies have been unorthodox to say the least, and it has taken the other economies a while to adjust. Now this adjustment period has begun, and there are counter moves appearing in other nations that the investment community has been paying attention to. Some of these moves will prove damaging to U.S. companies, and the investors are fleeing.
