## Regional opportunities

Industry analyst, Frost and Sullivan has pinpointed India, China and South Africa as pivotal regions of growth in the global aerospace industry.

“China has been the fastest growing economy, expanding at 10 per cent annually, driven by exports and investment. High priority is given to economic restructuring, from an export-driven to consumption-driven economy during the 12th five year plan period. The aerospace industry is a driver of economic growth, supported by government initiatives and increased investments. The recent development plans of the Government aim at transforming the aerospace industry from an OEM for parts and components to an aircraft designer and supplier. Opportunities exist in commercial aviation, technology of core components, MRO and aircraft for low-attitude areas,” it said.

South Africa is a democratic republic with a stable political system. The economic environment is forecast to remain stable, with policy focus on employment creation through growth and stability. The country has a long-established aerospace and defence industry with large international contracts driving local manufacturing. The aerospace industry has substantial deals in progress with European companies Airbus and Boeing, which provide future potential for expansion of the industry. Expansion into the African market will provide opportunities for both airline and defence industries.

## An eye on India

Growing domestic demand for civil as well as military aircraft coupled with defence offsets is fuelling the growth of the Indian aircraft manufacturing and services industry. Beating back spirited competition from Latin America, India remains an attractive low-cost manufacturing destination with a huge engineering talent pool and proven IT capabilities. However, the domestic industry has to stay on its toes to remain the manufacturing hub of choice.

Analysis from Frost & Sullivan finds that the industry earned revenues of US$ 2.09 billion in 2010 and estimates this to grow at a compound annual growth rate of more than 16 per cent to reach US$ 4.56 billion in 2015. The Indian industry's lack of MRO companies with globally recognized certifications and inadequate expertise in providing end-to-end solutions could narrow the gap between the Indian industry and some of the low labour-cost Latin American countries, primarily Mexico and Chile.

The government's inclusion of civil aerospace products in the offset eligibility list will widen the spectrum of outsourced work and extend the benefits to a larger section of domestic industry participants. Government spending on foreign procurement as a part of armed forces modernisation also creates huge offset opportunities.

"The government is expected to spend approximately US$ 54 billion on defence procurement from foreign vendors during 2011-2015," notes Frost and Sullivan research associate, Aditya Desai. "As a part of a 30 per cent offset, winning vendors are anticipated to source components and/or services worth US$ 18 billion from the Indian aircraft component manufacturing and services industry."

The offset policies could potentially generate US$ 40 billion over the next 20 years. This will open doors for Indian manufacturers that are keen to enter the high-tech arena of aerospace manufacturing and offer them returns on their investment.

## Unmanned aircraft

Unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs) or drones have certain inherent advantages over manned platforms, motivating the interest of military forces and driving the market for military drones.

New analysis from Frost & Sullivan, “Military Unmanned Aerial Systems Market Assessment”, finds that total market revenue is likely to be US$ 61.37 billion across the forecast period 2011-2020. It is estimated that the global military unmanned aerial systems (UAS) market generated US$ 4.55 billion in revenues in 2010, a figure that is set to rise to US$ 7.31 billion in 2020.

Europe is facing intense competition in the medium-altitude, long-endurance (MALE) UAV segment, as more domestic companies are collaborating to develop indigenous equipment. Existing high altitude, long-endurance (HALE) UAVs are too expensive for many nations, even while MALE equipment has limited capability. Hence, an opportunity exists for equipment with capabilities between MALE and HALE.

## SMR SAYS: We highlight the latest material innovations here….

Today’s automotive manufacturers need to keep a wary eye on reducing vehicle weight and emissions whilst simultaneously increasing vehicle strength, robustness and fuel-efficiency. Materials play a key role in this strategy and OEMs and material producers have invested significant resources to developing higher strength materials for the next generation of cars.

At the same time, options such as materials recycling are helping to keep costs down and enabling manufacturers to meet ‘green’ targets. Here, we examine some new material developments and how they will impact tomorrow’s automotive technologies.

## MATERIALS

## Flexing new muscles

Finnish materials manufacturer, Ruukki, has just launched the Ruukki Optim 550 W structural steel, which combines the properties of weather-resistant steels and high-strength structural steels. A structure made of weather-resistant steel develops a patinated surface. This means it needs no surface treatment and can withstand corrosion attack by the elements more effectively than conventional structural steels.

“In addition to its weather-resistance, the high strength of the steel can be utilised by machinery and equipment builders to lighten the tare weight of mobile machines and thus increase their payloads. This in turn reduces overall fuel consumption,” Ruukki explained.

If colour coating is required, it adheres to weather-resistant steels better than to conventional structural steels. These steels are suitable for use in various rolling stock applications, as well as in the lattice structures of power line pylons and in agricultural trailers, for example,” added Thomas Hörnfeldt, senior vice president at Ruukki.

Ruukki Optim 550 W steel grade is used, among other things, in the body structures of trams. The use of high-strength steels helps to lighten tram weight because the steel used can be thinner and still retain the properties required. A lighter tram structure means less energy is required to operate it. High-strength Ruukki Optim steels are made using a direct quenching process developed by Ruukki. Ruukki also manufactures hard Ruukki Raex wear-resistant steels.

## Our friends electric

Korean materials specialist POSCO has succeeded in developing a safer and lighter steel body for electric vehicles. The company started developing PBC-EV (POSCO Body Concept-Electric Vehicle), a steel body for electric vehicles, back in 2010 to generate profit from high value-added products and support strengthening the future competitiveness of the automobile industry.

“The PBC-EV, the result of a full two years of development by POSCO, is 25% lighter than previous vehicle bodies. Its safety has also been proven by satisfying all international collision safety regulation standards which will start to be applied in 2015,” POSCO explained.

PBC-EV is designed to use at least 40% more high quality steel than regular automobile bodies. Cutting edge technology was used such as hot press forming which increases the degree of strength and multidirectional roll forming which allows changes in the length of the sectional area.

Manufacturing methods similar to existing automobiles, despite increased use of high quality steel, have been applied which in turn require no changes in existing production lines, thus minimising increased electric car manufacturing costs. In the Life Cycle Assessment, which measures environmental friendliness from manufacturing to salvaging, the newly developed electric vehicle steel body PBC-EV reduces greenhouse gas emissions by approximately 50% compared to previous products, said POSCO.

POSCO announced electric vehicle steel body development results on 9 March at the Incheon Songdo Global R&D Centre.

“Electric vehicles have risen to become the most realistic and environmentally-friendly replacement to gasoline-fuelled vehicles, with one million vehicles sold worldwide last year.

This figure is expected to increase to 6.78 million in 2015 and 10 million in 2020, marking an average annual growth rate of more than 30%,” concluded POSCO.

## An eye on recycling

Honda Motor Co., Ltd. and the Japan Metals & Chemicals Co., Ltd. have jointly announced the establishment of ‘the world’s first process to extract and recycle rare earth metals from mass various used parts across Honda’s diverse product range’ to help further protect the environment and the world’s limited natural resources, which are becoming increasingly precious over recent years.

As part of this effort, Honda and Japan Metals & Chemicals will begin extracting rare earth metals – a set of seventeen chemical elements – from used nickel-metal hydride batteries collected from Honda hybrid vehicles at dealers from within Japan, North America and Europe. The new operation will be the first in the world to extract rare earth metals as part of a mass-production process at a recycling plant, rather than via a small-scale extraction process in controlled and experimental conditions, which Honda has already carried using approximately two thousand battery units to assess the accuracy of extraction.

Previously, Honda had been applying a heat treatment to used nickel-metal hydride batteries and recycling nickel-containing scrap, for recycling use as a raw material for stainless steel. However, the successful stabilisation of the extraction process at the plant of Japan Metals & Chemicals Co., Ltd. enables the extraction of approximately 80% of rare earth metals contained in used nickel-metal hydride batteries, with purity as high as that of newly mined and refined metals.

Honda will strive to re-use extracted rare earth metals not only for nickel-metal hydride batteries, but also for a wide range of Honda products. Honda will also look to further expand the recycling of rare earth metals in the future as the newly established process enables the extraction of rare earth metals from a variety of used parts, in addition to nickel-metal hydride batteries.

POSCO CEO, Chung Joonyang, inspects the PBC-EV developed by POSCO on 9 March at the Incheon Songdo Global R&D Centre.

“Honda has long been committed to the recycling of resources with the 3R approach (reduce, reuse, recycle), that include, but are not limited to, rare earth metals. For instance, Honda was the first Japanese automaker to begin sales of recycled parts and to collect/recycle oil filters and replaced bumpers. This project is just part of Honda’s continued commitment to reducing the impact of its products and manufacturing processes on the environment, as well as ensuring the sustainability of its vehicles,” the manufacturer explained. ■
