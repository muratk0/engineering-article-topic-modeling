# A little help from my friends

Biz Talk

FMA Annual Meeting spurs talk of teamwork

TIM HESTON

T

his year’s Fabricators and Manufacturers Association Annual Meeting, held Feb. 25-27 in Chandler, Ariz., provided plenty of insight into the fast-changing economic and political landscape. As just one example, Omar Nashashibi, founder of Inside Beltway, detailed the inner mechanisms of tariffs and trade, including the challenges—like raw material prices—and opportunities, such as those presented by reshoring trends.

"I literally updated my presentation about five minutes ago," Nashashibi said on stage during the late-February event. "Everything is changing so quickly. We’re working with a moving target."

As I write this in early March, tariffs on Mexican and Canadian goods have been delayed until early April and additional metals tariffs had gone into effect, though how permanent they are remains to be seen. Time will tell how the tariff dust settles, but fabricators are buckling up for the ride.

All this said, the FMA Annual Meeting wasn’t a doom-and-gloom affair. Quite the opposite, in fact. Most presentations centered on a timeless theme: How to make the people who work in metal fabrication the best they can be.

Keynote speaker Capt. Jack Becker, former F/A-18 pilot and CEO of Flight Level Solutions, detailed the brief-execute-debrief-perfect improvement cycle taught at the U.S. Navy’s Top Gun academy. "Every person in this room has been plagued with a problem," Becker said. "Many members of your team have been duplicating or repeating mistakes on the job, often without even knowing it."

This is where that improvement cycle comes into play, buttressed by an idea called Crew Resource Management, "which entails seven critical skills that help us make fewer mistakes on the job: communication, assertiveness, leadership, decision-making, task analysis, adaptability and flexibility, and situational awareness."

A graduate of Top Gun, Becker can honestly say that the movie "Top Gun," while fun, isn’t reality. Its underlying theme, driven by the go-italone Maverick beating the odds, goes against the very foundation of the real Top Gun’s teachings. Top Gun’s brief-execute-debrief-perfect idea resembles Deming’s plan-do-check-act (PDCA) improvement methodology, and there’s a good reason for that. The best pilots, as well as the best teams in metal fabrication, aren’t mavericks. They never push ahead alone. The team, not the individual, gets the job done.

It’s easy to push the humanity out of metal fabrication. Automation is the future, after all, which allows fewer people to produce more.

Can’t find reliable people who show up and pass a drug test? Automate

. No one can deny that some leaders in this business think like that. I can tell you, however, that they weren’t the typical attendee at the FMA Annual Meeting.

Consider the topics discussed at the meeting’s technology panel discussion, which brought together technical experts from AMADA AMERICA, BLM GROUP, Bystronic, MC Machinery Systems, and TRUMPF. They spoke about streamlining the entire value stream. No longer will people spend time sorting parts or handling awkward pieces at the brake. They’ll instead be trained to scrutinize the data and think about how to get more out of the entire value stream in a more reliable, predictable way.

"Data is the foundation. The truth is your business is only as good as your data." That was Jason Ray, CEO of software provider Paperless Parts, who presented various (anonymous) case studies showing just how inconsistent shop data is—with different names for everything from raw materials to work centers.

Data seems miles away from the "people side" of the business, but as Ray said, it’s a foundational element—a tool that helps people succeed. If you can’t measure something, you can’t perfect it.

A fabricator might work to improve its culture by implementing improvement ideas, be it PDCA from Deming or the brief-execute-debrief-perfect cycle from Top Gun. But if the entire cycle works on bad information, you have people chasing windmills in quixotic attempts to make things better. This makes things even worse. It spurs frustration, confusion, even resentment, and shop culture suffers as a result.

Data gathering and continuous improvement carry a different weight in manufacturing. In this world of remote and hybrid work, manufacturing remains one of the few B2B workplaces where people need to gather in one physical space. A continuous pursuit toward excellence creates sustainable enterprises. Relationships in fabrication—both internal within the company and external customers—usually aren’t transactional; they can last years, sometimes generations. Established fab shops support the community, and the best among them employ people who look out for each other.

Back at the FMA Annual Meeting, during a late-afternoon roundtable on safety, Sterling Jensen, president of Richards Sheet Metal Works, Ogden, Utah, told an off-the-cuff story that exemplified how people in this niche of manufacturing think.

Relationships in fabrication—both internal within the company and external customers—usually aren’t transactional; they can last years, sometimes generations. Established fab shops support the community, and the best among them employ people who look out for each other.

"One day, our ops manager came in the office and closed the door. He said, ‘I’ve got a safety concern we need to talk about.’" A long pause ensued before the manager said what was top of mind. "We’re afraid you’re going to have a heart attack. We think we need to have AEDs [automated external defibrillators] in every building."

The ops manager, who also has a background in fire and emergency response, added, "I also think we need a safety response team in each of our four buildings."

Jensen didn’t delve into the details, only saying "I’m not your model of perfect health." He did, however, describe how those safety teams in the shop today know first aid and other first-response fundamentals. The effort started from a concern for Jensen but immediately applied to everyone.

People look out for each other. The team, not the individual, gets the job done. The Beatles lyric comes to mind:

I get by with a little help from my friends

. The best fab shops I know do more than get by. They thrive.
