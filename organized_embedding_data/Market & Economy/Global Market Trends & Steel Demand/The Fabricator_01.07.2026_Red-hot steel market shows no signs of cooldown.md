# Red-hot steel market shows no signs of cooldown

[TARİH: 01.07.2026 The Fabricator]

Steel News

Steel buyers forget uncertainty, ramp up demand that mills struggle to meet

By

Kristen DiLandro

T

he steel industry’s annual summer doldrums, also known as the historically slow market cycle between Memorial Day and Labor Day, may be breaking tradition this year. Temperatures aren’t the only thing rising this summer. Fuel surcharges and regional transportation price add-ons are only two of the factors driving up steel prices.

Spot market buyers report weekly incremental spot price increases. One need only track public Consumer Spot Price announcements from mills, such as Nucor, to confirm steelmaker increases. Spot transactions remain in lock step.

During Steel Market Update’s weekly spot price assessment on June 9, average transactions for hot-rolled coils occurred within the range of $1,080/short ton to $1,150/st. Most bought and sold spot HR at $1,115/st.

Consider the same week in 2025: Average spot prices for domestically produced HR were $860/st. In the same week of 2024, the average spot price transaction for domestic HR was $710/st.

A similar pattern exists on all domestically produced steel, from galvanized products to plate products. How have HR prices climbed 33.7% year-on-year and 61.9% from 2024? What’s causing domestic mill lead times to push out by several weeks?

The current strength of the domestic steel market can be attributed to a confluence of conditions that have tightened domestic supply. In this context, supply refers to domestic mills’ ability to produce enough steel to meet customer contract volumes and additional demand from spot-market buyers.

STEEL DEMAND IMPROVES

For starters, one condition that has improved domestically is overall steel demand. Recall the words

uncertainty

and

tariff

repeated ad nauseam in 2025. Critics and supporters waxed poetic about the benefits and drawbacks of implementing tariffs on imports to the U.S. Multiple sectors watched as fear paralyzed their industries, with both buyers and sellers unsure of how to navigate conditions. For months, steel consumers waited to see how the market would play out. In effect, fewer transactions were made, and opportunistic buys became rare.

Fast-forward to 2026, and U.S. industries have mobilized, "feeling the fear and doing it anyway," as the old story goes. No longer in a state of analysis paralysis, buyers turned to domestic producers to fulfill increasing customer demand.

Steel demand from particular niche industries, such as the data center segment, continues to swell. According to data published by Construct Connect News in May 2026, data center construction starts reached $49.5 billion YTD compared to $13.6 billion during the same period a year earlier. Dodge Construction’s May index report found that construction demand from the health care, government, and religious sectors drove growth. Service center, OEM, and fabrication source sentiments have been consistent since February; they need steel because their orders remain consistent.

Combine the growing sense of overall security among steel-intensive manufacturers with the mushrooming proliferation of data centers and institutional construction and then reduce the overall steel available in the U.S. market. Buyers find themselves competing for literal steel supplies, like products, and figurative supply, like a mill’s ability to provide enough steel to meet contract requirements and spot inquiries.

TARIFFS NOT HELPING

The additional demand on domestic producers to meet the growing market demand has been one factor tightening the domestic market. Domestic mills have been inundated with inquiries that might have otherwise been routed to foreign suppliers. If demand had remained stable compared to 2024 and 2025, domestic mills might have been able to ease into becoming the dominant source of steel for the U.S. market.

Strict enforcement of the 50% Section 232 tariffs has reduced the amount of steel available to meet current U.S. market conditions. (The U.S. Section 232 tariff on steel imported from the U.K. is currently 25%.) Purchasing imported steel requires careful planning because buyers place orders months in advance. To meet the market conditions, buyers would have had to place orders in the first quarter of the year before demand reached its current level. Few buyers were ready to risk a speculative buy. Buyers who regularly import to balance inventories placed orders accordingly.

Many SMU sources say they would have placed their orders in March had they expected the market to reach its current state. Even those who consider themselves to be market veterans did not expect lead times from both domestic and foreign producers to reach near parity. Multiple sources say they’re most concerned about having product to stock, rather than the escalating prices of spot HR.

A knock-on effect of the tariffs, early on, even played a role in domestic steel production. U.S. manufacturers that once relied on imported steel slabs (to melt and pour) at their U.S.-based facilities had to reconfigure their supply chains. Domestic producers were forced to compete for domestic slabs or pay a 50% tariff on imported slabs. Conceding to import meant that domestic producers swapped production time for shipping time.

In addition to all of the above, some domestic production facilities had to take maintenance outages, further redirecting demand to other, already strained, domestic facilities.

STEEL BUYERS TURN TO IMPORTS

To navigate the current market, several buyers told SMU that they are welcoming the return of steel imports. They believe that large service center conglomerates wield exponential purchasing power. Recent service center consolidation (the Ryerson and Olympic merger and the Worthington and Kloeckner deal, for example) has become a behemoth market force for smaller operators to contend with. In a market where spot HR remains scarce, small and midsize centers and end market buyers fear their businesses won’t survive without imports.

Midwestern sources who’ve relied solely on domestic steel told SMU they’ve become import-ready and have placed orders from abroad. When their distributors promised parity on pricing, lead times, and steel quality, they jumped. Devout domestic buyers admit that while they aren’t yet ready to begin looking at the logistics of importing steel, they hope to see "imports reset the market" for midsize and small service centers.

As long as consumer demand for steel outpaces domestic mills’ ability to meet it, the market will maintain its simmer. When imports find their way to the U.S. and buyers become adequately supplied, the doldrums may arrive in time for winter. Given the current rate of demand, it seems unlikely that mills will catch up, optimize operations, and grow capacity before imports become ubiquitous in the market once again. As foreign steel fills the void where domestic products haven’t been available, could buyers lobby to have the 50% Section 232 tariffs reduced or removed? Only time will tell, but for now, expect the heat wave to roll on.
