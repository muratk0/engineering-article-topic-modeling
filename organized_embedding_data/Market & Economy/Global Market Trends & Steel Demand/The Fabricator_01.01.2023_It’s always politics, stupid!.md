# It’s always politics, stupid!

[TARİH: 01.01.2023 The Fabricator]

Fabrinomics

▸ Dr. Chris Kuehl

To paraphrase that now famous quote that adorned the campaign offices of Bill Clinton when he ran for President.

It was the assertion of James Carville that elections always came down to economic issues, but in the end the economic issues often come down to the politics of the moment as well.

By this time, we know that pundits and pollsters were wrong again and that makes me feel so much better to know that somebody else is worse at forecasting and predicting than economists. That "red wave" that was supposed to take the GOP to total control of Congress fizzled as Democrats held on to the Senate and still have an outside chance to hold the House of Representatives (although that seems unlikely). Outside the world of the political wonk, what does this mean going forward? More specifically, what can the manufacturer expect from Congress for the next couple of years?

There is likely to be reduced levels of federal spending. The last few years have seen a spending spree and on a variety of issues and programs. Some of these have been valuable to the manufacturer – especially those that were geared toward offsetting the impact of the pandemic shutdown in 2020. Other programs could best be described as pandering to some political base or another. There is opposition among Republicans and some Democrats to more spending, and there is therefore unlikely to be any move to offset impact of inflation on the population and some of the planned infrastructure activity may be curtailed. This reduction in enthusiasm for spending will have little impact on the deficit, but at least it doesn’t get significantly worse.

Neither party is prepared to do much to impact the deficit. The U.S. is now sitting on a debt to GDP ratio near 130%, and this is never supposed to exceed 60%. If it makes you feel any better, the Chinese are staring at 280%, Japan is at 260%, and the average in Europe is 180%. The debt is primarily held by the Fed and the government itself (40%). The Social Security Trust Fund holds 16% and the Federal Reserve holds 12%. Foreign investors hold about 30% (Japan is #1, China is #2 and Luxembourg is #3). U.S. investors hold the remaining 30%.

There is a greater possibility of a debt ceiling default. Many want to use the debt ceiling as a means by which to force spending cuts, but it is a blunt instrument at best and puts the value of U.S. treasuries at risk if the confrontation lasts too long. This is the only nation that uses that tool to impact the budget process. Most nations deal with the issue of how much to spend when they do the actual budget, but the U.S. goes through the budgeting and even appropriates the money before deciding what should be spent. It is not unlike going to buy a big screen TV, taking it home, and then canceling the credit card it was bought with. If we did this, we would be in jail, but the members of Congress threaten it every year and likely will in the future. The U.S. routinely budgets to spend more money than it has and has to borrow more (sell bonds) as a result.

Limited reaction to a recession should develop. There will likely be minimal reaction to the downturn. It is not entirely clear what kind of downturn or recession is looming, but it is clear that some (and perhaps many) will be affected by job layoffs and the surge in inflation. In past years, the government has sought to cushion that blow with expanded programs. There is also the desire to curtail a recession by encouraging more consumer activity. That is the tried and true recipe for ending a downturn, but it means the government spends aggressively. This reaction is less likely this time, and response may be limited to some tax breaks and possibly some extended unemployment, but not much else.

The most significant shift may come in the world of regulation. Of all the issues that manufacturers list as challenges, the actions of the regulators rank as high priority. There are literally thousands of new regulations promulgated every week, and they affect almost every aspect of manufacturing. Lately there has been emphasis on workers’ and union rights as well as many regulations pertaining to energy use and climate change. The responsibility for the regulatory environment is still in the hands of the Executive branch, but one can expect more oversight of federal regulators – especially those related to financial community, climate change, and workers. Congress can slow the process down with questions and interpretations, and it is expected the GOP members will do much to slow down the process.

In the same vein there will be more scrutiny of appointees to Federal Reserve, Treasury, and other financial institutions. These nominations are made by the Executive, but in many cases they need to be approved by Congress (mostly the Senate). These institutions are the ones that develop and enforce those regulations.

What Does This Mean for Manufacturers?

The economic impact of Congress is actually pretty limited and subtle despite all the fulmination during the campaigns.

It has been almost laughable to listen to the statements and accusations regarding inflation – as if these politicians can do anything at all about the drivers of inflation. Can they force China to stop locking down production and free the supply chain? Can they force OPEC to produce more oil? Can they force Putin out of Ukraine? Can they alleviate the worker shortage that has created such wage pressure? Granted, there are things that can be done long term to impact these issues, but decades have gone by without these efforts and actions. For the most part, the world of the manufacturer in 2023 looks pretty similar to what it has been.
