Recent trips from Asian leaders to Latin America suggest that the two continents are strengthening ties

According to Focus Economics, after showing moderate growth in the second half of 2014, Latin America’s economy was gradually entering into recession at the outset of the year.

“The region’s GDP growth rose mildly from 0.5% in Q3 2014 to 0.7% in Q4 2014 and is now estimated to have contracted 0.5% in Q1. The expected fall in the growth rate stems from an economic contraction in Argentina, a deepening of Brazil’s decline and a disastrous plunge in Venezuela,” said Focus Economics.

“Data showed that Brazil’s GDP decreased 1.6% annually in Q1. Although Venezuela has not released official data since Q3 2014, analysts estimate that the crisis-hit economy is expected to have plummeted 6.8% in Q1. Moreover, recent economic data showed that Mexico’s GDP growth fell from 2.7% in Q4 to 2.5% in Q1, which mainly reflected the impact of a sharp deceleration in the US economy.”

## Economic prospects

Recent trips from Asian leaders to Latin America suggest that the two continents are strengthening ties. During a two-week long tour in May to South America, Chinese Prime Minister Li Keqiang visited Brazil, Chile, Colombia and Peru to unveil infrastructure projects designed to improve access to the countries’ natural resources. The plans also included a railway project in Brazil and Peru that aims to connect the Pacific Ocean to the Atlantic sea. A recent trip by South Korea’s President Park Geun-hye to the same four South American countries suggests that South Korea may be interested in expanding economic and political ties with Latin America.

Lower growth forecasts for 7 of the 11 economies surveyed, including the region’s behemoths Brazil and Mexico, prompted concerns. Growth forecasts for Ecuador and Uruguay were left unchanged and Focus Economics’ panel of experts raised its forecasts for Argentina and Bolivia. Argentina is now expected to avoid recession and expand 0.3% in 2015. LatinFocus panelists expect Latin America’s economy to expand 2.1% in 2016, due to the gradual strengthening of the global economy and the expected recovery in commodities prices.

According to Scotia Bank’s Latin America Regional Report, Brazil, which accounts for 34% of the GDP in the developing Americas, will only see a return to positive and sustainable economic growth in the latter part of 2016 following the implementation of a comprehensive and well-synchronized fiscal and external adjustment process.

Mexico will be tied to US growth dynamics with the potential to outperform its NAFTA zone partners as a result of the improved competitiveness and structural reforms under implementation. China’s influence on global trade flows and commodity prices will remain a major driver of economic activity in the Southern Cone economies (Peru, Chile and Uruguay). Infrastructure investment will be a core driver of economic activity throughout the region in the years ahead.

“Mexico has consolidated itself as a systemically sound North American economy by deepening its structural roots and convergence with the United States’ business, monetary and production cycles. In stark opposition, Brazil has steadily distanced itself from the influence of US policies and dependence on US trade by embracing an aggressive China-led pro-BRIC development strategy, and thereby reinforcing the downgrade of the Mercosur (common market of the south) bloc. In alignment to this trend, most economies in the Southern Cone region will continue to intensify trade and investment links with China in the years ahead,” commented Scotia Bank’s Latin America Regional Report (ScotiaBank Economics).

“Meanwhile, the Pacific Alliance initiative (involving Mexico, Colombia, Peru and Chile) has emerged as a US-friendly attempt to create a cohesive trading bloc with enough critical mass to act as an alternative economic zone to Brazil in the Americas. Ironically, the ultimate success of such an integrationist initiative lies in the active long-term involvement of Brazil, which will likely begin a process of rapprochement with both the US and Pacific Alliance members in the months ahead.”

## Competitiveness

According to BBVA Research, after analysing the evolution of exports from the large Latin American countries over the last decade, and examining on a case-by-case basis the determinants for each country’s performance, its study concludes that competitiveness in the manufacturing sectors of most countries in the region went down from 2007 to 2012, after relatively favourable progress in the previous five-year period between 2002 and 2007.

“This recent deterioration, which has been more noticeable in countries such as Brazil and Colombia, is related to the real exchange rate appreciation, high labour costs and insufficient progress in labour productivity. The main exception to these regional trends is Mexico, where gains in the manufacturing sector’s competitiveness continued beyond 2007, partly because the exchange rate stayed relatively depreciated and labour costs, as well as work productivity, performed better than in the South American countries. However, from 2011 onwards, the reversal of these trends has been making it difficult for the Mexican manufacturing sector to gain competitiveness,” explained BBVA.

Gains in competitiveness have been greater (and losses in competitiveness smaller) in Chile and Peru, where the institutional framework has improved and logistics and energy costs reduced or kept under control. ■
