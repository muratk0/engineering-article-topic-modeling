# Zekelman, McNamara bullish on future of tube in America

[TARİH: 01.03.2026 The Fabricator]

Tube Time

Top execs at Zekelman Industries shift company’s M.O.to accommodate data center and housing demands

By

Lincoln Brunner

W

ith a major presence in several steel tubing sectors—notably hollow structural sections (HSS), steel conduit, energy tubulars, standard pipe, fence framework, and mechanical tubing—Chicago- based Zekelman Industries has built its 149-year-old business around responding where market demand calls.

But for Zekelman, North America’s largest independent producer of steel tube and pipe, two particular markets—data centers and housing—have become so important in the past few years that it’s no exaggeration to say they’ve altered how the entire company operates.

DATA CENTERS PRESENT MASSIVE OPPORTUNITY

Many estimates call for 3,000 more data centers in the U.S. by 2030, requiring an additional 100 GW of electricity. Meeting those demands has pushed the companies that own the facilities and the ones building them to think differently about the speed of construction and their methods.

"We just have massive opportunities, and we’re trying to operate at that same rate of acceleration and that same rate of change so that we can accommodate it all and stay ahead of it and be ready when they come to us," CEO Barry Zekelman said.

That means commoditization—anathema to new architecture in, say, downtown Chicago, but essential for companies needing to construct quality buildings fast.

"It’s clear this is market-moving," President Mickey McNamara said. "These guys are building in a different way than we’ve seen historically. These guys are trying a programmatic approach to these data centers and power facilities. They don’t want to build a unicorn system. They want to build the same thing over and over again. And that’s changed the way we think about how we operate and how we’re going to operate commercially."

McNamara explained that what Zekelman’s customers are requesting has influenced the company to shift its production model away from the more traditional array of common tube products to more of what he termed a "programmatic" approach aligned with customers’ needs.

"We want to sell our basket of products programmatically as opposed to staying more in the vertical distribution lines of the individual products— and we’re putting resources to that end. We think that’s going to drive a lot of our growth going forward.

"It’s different but we’re entrepreneurial by nature, and we recruit people that think the way we think—lead and not follow. We’re always trying to be on the cutting edge and in front of things, not behind, but yeah—it is a different way of doing things."

Also driving growth is a regular flow of reinvestment that has increased Zekelman’s capacity on several fronts across its product lineup:

At multiple factories, automated warehouses improve speed and safety and prevent physical damage from traditional product handling.

Wheatland Tube factories in Pennsylvania have received major investments to increase capacity and safety and ensure quality, particularly for new domestic factories and data centers.

Atlas Tube recently created the HSS Connection Hub, a free online tool to support engineers and fabricators looking to use HSS. It offers more than 140 connection calculators for U.S. and Canadian projects.

A new in-line galvanizing pipe mill in Blytheville, Ark., focuses on fence framework.

"I saw America evolving with Google and Facebook and Oracle and Microsoft and medical innovations and automotive and aerospace and all of these things, and I can’t tell that I knew that was going to translate into pipe and tube. I’m not saying that," Zekelman said. "But I knew it was going to translate into a greater, bigger economy with more GDP. And no matter what, you have to have steel. It touches everything. And so does our steel tube and pipe.

"So, I’m invested in it. We’ve made it safer. We’ve made it cleaner. We’ve made it more efficient."

TAKING ON THE HOUSING CRISIS

Investment in the new couldn’t be grabbing more attention these days.

Tech titans’ race for AI supremacy has unleashed huge demand for steel HSS, conduit, and pipe that keeps cascading down to Zekelman and any other tube producer able to supply it. However, Zekelman also has fixed its sights on a market that’s much more consequential both from a basic human-needs perspective and the company’s bottom line: affordable housing.

Demand for single-family homes and rental housing is being driven by a growing population in the U.S. and also by the huge influx of young adults leaving Mom and Dad’s house and looking for their own homes. But those housing seekers continue to face steep odds.

Last year, the Harvard University Joint Center for Housing Studies reported that while median home prices nationwide hit a record high of $412,500, renters also faced a record affordability crisis. The salient figure in that category: A record number of renter households—12.1 million— are saddled with severe cost burdens (defined as spending more than 50% of incomes on housing).

Enter Z Modular.

A modular construction business and concept, Z Modular was originally launched in 2016 as a construction option for a variety of markets, including multifamily residential, educational, commercial, and hotel. The division, with factories in Texas and Arizona, manufactures finished modular units that contain HSS frame members, electrical conduit, and pipe for mechanical applications, all made by Zekelman. Though the customizable modules generally resemble standard shipping containers, they are usually much longer (up to 72 ft.), wider, and higher. And though final touches are completed on-site, the units arrive with all the walls, glass, and trim pieces installed and ready for tenancy.

Ten years after launch, the company is focusing its production on multifamily residential developments. With features like the proprietary Z Block connectors, units can be quickly bolted together, enabling new developments to progress from groundbreaking to ribbon cutting in less than a year. The company’s latest development (in Midland, Texas) started receiving units in August 2025 and was planning to turn over keys to renters in February.

About 90% of the steel products that go into a Z Modular housing unit are manufactured by Zekelman.

"It’s been a journey," said Mc-Namara, who was Z Modular’s original president. "But I’ll tell you that today, more than ever, we believe it’s the only way that we as a country can actually meet the moment of the housing crisis."

With an estimated 4.3 to 4.6 million new apartments needed by 2035 in the U.S., according to the National Multifamily Housing Council, even a company like Zekelman with 3,000 employees and 2.4 million tons of annual production across all product lines can only hope to put a dent in the nationwide deficit of homes—even with the improvements to its production processes.

For perspective, even if Zekelman diverted every ton of its current production capacity to Z Modular HSS tubing, it could only meet a fraction of the current need by itself. It’s a fantastically tall order, given that the company serves many markets with that annual production total and that Z Modular completes about 1,000-1,500 units annually.

"Rule of thumb: You have about 12 tons of steel in the average 1,000-sq.-ft. apartment," Zekelman said. "If you build 1,000 apartments, it’s 12,000 tons. So, imagine now if we’re building 200,000 apartments. It’s 2.4 million tons. And that’s just the tube steel. Then there’s light gauge and there’s polyconduit and other piping and all the other stuff that goes into a development."

The AI-generated demand for server farms and the attendant tubular products doesn’t touch the 2.4 million tons that housing represents, Zekelman said. That’s why the company is so attuned to growing Z Modular. Having learned some valuable efficiency lessons the past 10 years, the company bosses think they’re ready to scale up by multiple degrees.

"It’s a scalable solution in a box," McNamara said. "You can put it in a new geography and kick out thousands and thousands of units in a year in that geography in a very short period of time.

"Right now, we’re predominantly in the Southwest. As we’ve built this business and learned our lessons and sharpened our knife of what the business model is, we’ve done that locally so that we could get a lot of repeat projects and very quickly learn those lessons."

In Zekelman’s mind, that repeatable process makes ramping up to that mythical 200,000 figure less of a pipe dream than some might believe.

"This could be a massive shift," Zekelman said. "Our system and the way we do things, you can take this from the 1,000 to 1,500 apartments we build in a year today to 200,000. You need the capital to do it. You need the land to do it. You need the developments to do it. But we can scale to that."

After all the earnings reports and successful launches, the company’s success has more to do with its people than any business strategy, Zekelman maintains. As an example, he highlighted a recent exchange he had with some team members in the pipe operation at Wheatland Tube that was right in line with the company’s change-with-the-times mindset.

"I spent $100 million of cap-ex at this plant. It was a transformational project," Zekelman said. "And they came up with an idea. They laid it out, and I was like, ‘This is brilliant.’ It’s a game-changer. It provides us time to do preventative maintenance on equipment. We can take those people off and cross-train them. And I was like, ‘We didn’t see it before, but we saw it today.’

"It was just a thought process on the flow and touches and speed. It’s brilliant, and I just love the fact that they came up with it. It wasn’t me. It’s not The Barry Show. It was the team of them who came up with it. And that’s the future of our company. That’s the leadership we have and the freedom we have—that freedom and ability to dream."
