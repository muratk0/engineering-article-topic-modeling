# Trump tariffs: Negotiating tactic or fair warning?

[TARİH: 01.01.2025 The Fabricator]

Steel News

Stable steel prices might be creeping upwards in early 2025

MICHAEL COWDEN

T

he end of 2024 wrapped up with crazy news cycles, each sounding more like they were ripped from the headlines of another century: wars, tariffs, and assassinations.

Despite all of that, flat-rolled steel prices were mostly ho-hum. In fact, it became almost a trend in the closing months of 2024. Earth-shaking headlines only led to incremental moves in steel prices.

Trump 2.0 and Tariffs

You could say it’s all a media (or a social media) circus, and there is, no doubt, plenty of that to go around. But President-elect Donald Trump has been nothing if not consistent about his messaging around tariffs.

I might have lost track. We’ve potentially got 10% blanket tariffs on imports from China, 25% tariffs on imports from Canada and Mexico, 100% tariffs on the BRICs, and 200% on John Deere.

Canada might be the 51st state. Mexico could be the 52nd state. But all can be resolved if you stop by Mar-a-Lago and kiss the ring?

Seriously, though, the question of whether tariffs are a negotiating tactic or a real threat is an important one, especially as we get closer to Inauguration Day on Jan. 20. But there are no easy answers, at least not yet.

Smart people can be found on both sides of that debate. For example, CRU (Steel Market Update’s parent company) made a good case in a December article that the tough talk on tariffs might be aimed at giving Trump leverage in negotiations on trade or other issues.

Meanwhile, Alan Price, an experienced and well-connected trade attorney at Washington, D.C., law firm Wiley Rein LLP, made an equally convincing case in a column for SMU that Trump’s tariffs are not mere bluster. Price said they’ll be coming, and lightning fast at that, perhaps as soon as Inauguration Day.

We’ll see who is right on or shortly after Jan. 20. It’s safe to say that, in the meantime, companies all along the steel supply chain should be making plans for either scenario.

Don’t Forget Other Trade Restrictions

And it’s not like blanket tariffs are the only trade risk with Trump 2.0. The Steel Manufacturers Association (SMA), an influential lobbying group that represents electric-arc furnace mills, wants the Trump administration to end the tariff-rate quotas (TRQs) on the European Union, the United Kingdom, and Japan. Recall the Biden administration negotiated the TRQs to take the hard edge off Section 232 on U.S. allies.

SMA also suggested that it wants to see South Korea’s Section 232 quota reduced. That matters because South Korea is one of the largest foreign steel suppliers to the U.S., behind only Canada, Mexico, and Brazil. Rumors of a reduction in South Korea’s quota had been ping-ponging around the market even before SMA officially put the matter before the Trump administration.

Also, the trade case against imports of coated flat-rolled steel from 10 countries, initiated under President Joe Biden’s administration, remains on track. There is no speculation there. That antidumping and countervailing duties case is real. And the clock is ticking down to when we’ll see preliminary duty determinations from the Commerce Department.

You can see why industry observers speculate that if U.S. mills get an influx of business because of all this protectionism, they could see their lead times stretch out, perhaps giving them leverage to ratchet up prices.

Where Does This All Lead?

The result of all of this has been mostly sideways pricing—a little up or a little down in any given week depending on the product.

For example, Cleveland-Cliffs kept its list price for hot-rolled coil (HRC) unchanged at $750/ton from mid-September to early December. During that time, SMU’s HRC price never got to $750/ton. Instead, our prices, and those of other indices, bounced within a narrow range of approximately $675/ton to $700/ton. After the volatility of the last few years, that’s some unprecedented stability.

Additionally, we haven’t seen the Trump bump we saw following the time between his election in November 2015 and his inauguration in January 2016. Maybe that shouldn’t be a surprise. Scrap prices fell again in December, bringing to a close a no good, very bad year for the raw material. Could it see an uptick in January?

Another stubborn problem is demand, which wasn’t great at the end of the year. Unfortunately, the president-elect can’t wave his magic wand on Truth Social to reduce bloated inventories or increase energy or agriculture prices. While this is happening, new U.S. sheet and plate capacity, encouraged by Section 232, continues to ramp up.

Steel Prices on the Rise?

Cliffs raised HRC spot prices to $800/ton in mid-December. Maybe the Cleveland-based steelmaker saw something?

No doubt the fear of Trump’s social feed is real.

"I can tell you that we are taking the cautious approach to buying only from domestic sources due to the possibility of new tariffs posted on X or Truth Social Media in the middle of the night," one steel buyer told me.

A mill executive said his company was seeing slightly better prices, perhaps because lead times were deeper into Q1 or perhaps because of Trump’s tariffs threats. His take: "I honestly would not be surprised if prices are a solid $100 to $150 higher come March."

A second mill source echoed that. He said contracts had mostly been rolled over from last year. But he added that buyers who had been threatening to put fewer tons under contract in 2025 ultimately backed off.

"With the threat of Trump’s tariffs on Mexico and Canada, along with the 232 review and antidumping, no one wants to be short contract tons," he said.

Here are a couple of other questions to consider:

• Could steel buyers be underestimating the upside risk that could come with tariffs and other Trump 2.0 trade restrictions?

• Could those protections (and restrictions on immigration) spur the kind of inflation and supply chain snarls that might hinder demand?
