For a whole variety of reasons, the subject of reshoring and onshoring has become a hot topic of conversation for manufacturers. For the last 30 years, the issue has been the exodus of U.S. manufacturing to other nations where labor and production costs were far less than in the U.S. (or Europe for that matter). If the operation itself did not move to China or India or somewhere, there was more sourcing from these nations. The shift in attitude has been prompted by the difficulties of an overseas supply chain, now further complicated by the pandemic and its impact on supply and transportation. The surveys indicate that fully 69% of those doing business in China are likely, very likely, or extremely likely to reshore to the U.S. or at least to North America.

This is certainly good news but there are two elements that pose challenges. The first is that moving back to the U.S. means moving to a more expensive production environment — higher wages, higher taxes, more regulation, and higher production costs in general. There are savings from having a shorter and more reliable supply chain but most everything else is more costly. This has been a major contributor to the inflation issue which started to appear in 2021.

The other issue is labor availability. There is quite obviously an acute shortage of trained and skilled people and this has been an issue for many years. As companies contemplate moving operations back to the U.S., they are going to want to hire people and will be facing the same shortages. As companies have been exploring these reshoring opportunities, they are asking some tough questions and not getting great answers. Is there a labor supply, is transportation adequate, are tax laws fair, are regulations too restrictive, and so on? There are many reasons to come back home but there are still many reasons to remain where they are.
