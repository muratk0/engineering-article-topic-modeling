# Tariffs: The good, bad, and ugly scenarios

[TARİH: 01.06.2025 The Fabricator]

Fabrinomics | Economic Insights

Dr. Chris Kuehl

J.K. Galbraith’s insight rings true, now more than ever: "There are two kinds of forecasters—those who don’t know and those who don’t know they don’t know." This has rarely been as true as it is now given the nearly constant stream of changes to tariff and trade policy.

Reading the Early Data

What do we know now? At as of this early May writing, we know a little. Some early data points are trickling in. We have been waiting anxiously for reports on the manufacturing purchasing manager indices (PMIs). Since the April 2 Day of Liberation event and the subsequent fear and angst over China tariffs, we have all been waiting for perhaps a collapse in new orders or manufacturing activity.

All that said, the latest PMI reports available really didn’t signal that. Looking at the month-over-month change in the PMIs, we can see that nearly all of the markets decelerated in April. The U.S. was flat, but most markets will show us that trade policy is having a mild impact.

The S&P Global PMI came in flat, though the report from the Institute for Supply Management (ISM) was weaker, coming in at 48.7 (down from 49.0 last month). Even so, new orders for domestic manufacturers improved slightly.

The question moving forward is this: Based on the timing of the survey (which was mid-April), could some of the major impacts from the reciprocal tariff cliff have not shown up by the time of publishing? Perhaps. Likewise, one could argue that the global economy is more resilient than expected, and businesses are finding ways of working around the tariff risk. For instance, India was showing some signs of immediate shifting of sourcing from China. New export orders from the U.S. were up, and it was clear that India was benefitting from tough tariff policy on China.

Comments in these April surveys were interesting. European countries reported that their domestic activity was still robust with stable new orders, but some export orders were already showing the strain from tariffs. Generally, however, they were more bullish than expected. Asian countries were reporting a drop in new orders, especially in China, as one might expect. Hiring across all markets was somewhat stalled, but few firms were reducing headcount as a result. That could be another sign that firms believe that they will be seeing some ramping up in the second half of the year.

At press time, the administration pivoted again, reducing tariffs on China and giving another 90 days for trade tensions to ease. What will happen after that? We don’t know, and the uncertainty remains. Despite all this, the business community and the FMA member has to plan and set strategic and tactical goals. What can we expect in the months to come? The best that can be done at this point is to look at scenarios to determine which are more or less likely. We have three to choose from: a good set, a bad set, and a really ugly set (with apologies to Clint Eastwood).

The Good

The "good set" basically assumes that Trump the dealmaker will soon emerge and do what he has done through most of his business and political career. May’s turnaround on tariffs with China support the idea that Trump the dealmaker has already emerged. And before that, there was a retreat from the strictest of the tariffs affecting the auto sector.

Ostensibly, some nations are getting very close to establishing deals. These include Japan, South Korea, Mexico, and parts of Europe. The argument against this frenzy of deals is that it takes two (or more) to engage, and many of these nations are not prepared to offer what Trump is demanding.

There is also the fact that U.S. consumer patience has worn thin, and that begins to worry politicians that rely on constituent support. Analysts are giving this set of scenarios a 60% chance. The holdup here may well be the nations that Trump has to cut deals with. They were expected to be more than eager to reach a deal and willing to make concessions, but it turns out they have more options than originally expected. China in particular can pivot in ways that many other nations can’t. There are many countries that still crave Chinese goods and really don’t care whether the U.S. approves. China has become adept at transferring production to third nations as a way to dodge the high tariffs imposed on them, and there is simply the fact that China is a command economy and can impose hardships on its population that democracies can’t. U.S. consumers are already rebelling against inflation, and it stands to get much worse. Chinese producers are not in a position to do much about the tariff and trade war.

The Bad

The "bad set" of scenarios assumes that many of these nations resist the process and refuse to give what Trump wants in the way of concessions, at least in the short term. The talk in these nations has been around finding alternative end markets. Can they sell enough to Europe, Japan, Latin America, India, and others to compensate for loss of the U.S. market?

To be honest, there are no consumers like U.S. consumers, and replacing them is impossible. Can building new market relationships help blunt the impact? To a degree the answer is yes, but only partially, and in a few months the pressure to accommodate the U.S. will mount.

This scenario holds that deals may start to appear toward the end of the third quarter, and that means several months of global pain. The U.S. alone could see 4.5% or even higher inflation, higher levels of joblessness, and further turmoil in the markets as the impact of the tariffs starts to sink in. This one gets the support of maybe 20% of analysts.

As nations explore their options, the U.S. continues to behave as if it holds all the cards, but that is not entirely true. It will take time to build new markets, just as it will take time to develop new supplier relationships. The U.S. will be hurt by the fact that it is no longer seen as a reliable trade partner, and many nations now would prefer to work with another partner rather than trust the capricious behavior of the Trump administration.

The Ugly

That leaves the "ugly" option, and this gets pretty grim. The foundation of this scenario is a real period of economic warfare between the U.S. and China. Right now, this conflict is described in economic terms. Which nation will blink first in this game of trade chicken?

There is a great deal more at stake here. China is bent on replacing the U.S. as the world’s dominant economic and political (and even military) power. This is a long-term fight for China, one they have been engaged in for decades and have never made a secret of. The U.S. is under attack from China every day—cyber warfare, economic warfare, diplomatic warfare.

The U.S. military names China as the primary threat. If China wants to engage in an all-out trade war, they have capabilities the U.S. lacks. It is a command economy and a dictatorship. They can impose restrictions on the population the U.S. can’t, and they can engage in financial manipulation the U.S. can’t. This trade conflict can very easily escalate and can turn very nasty. Consider what happens if China overtly attacks Taiwan or turns North Korea loose. This has the support of 20% of analysts.

This is basically uncharted territory when it comes to trade. The U.S. has long been a bulwark of free trade support, as this provided the U.S. consumer with high-quality products at a low price. It was accepted that U.S. production would be sacrificed to a degree. That sacrifice has been judged to have been too much, and there are now attempts to gain some of it back. But this comes at a cost as well—this time to the consumer.

What does all this mean for manufactures? It depends. Some manufacturers have been waiting for decades for the U.S. to act against unfair global competition. Low-cost imports have meant the loss of millions of jobs. On the other hand, some manufacturers rely heavily on commodities and materials from around the world and will now face much higher prices, which they will not be able to pass on to consumers. It depends on where one sits in the trade pattern.
