Where steel prices go in the short term really depends on how the mini-mills in the U.S. react to new steel capacity coming online. They could reduce prices in an effort to protect market share against the new competitors, or they could hold the line on prices and go to battle.

T

here is a new kid in town named Big River Steel (BRS). The other domestic steel mills don’t like BRS because it is taking market share and, well, everyone wants to keep all of their toys.

BRS is located in Arkansas and is very close to two Nucor facilities. The new mill is not that far away from SDI Columbus or U.S. Steel Granite City. In January, BRS produced 63,000 tons of hot-rolled steel. Usually new mills ramp up production, so the expectation is it will produce more steel in February, March, April, and ensuing months. You get the picture.

There is another sort-of-new kid in town—Acero Junction. It is beginning to produce hot-rolled steel off the old Mingo Junction mill, which is located on the border of Ohio and West Virginia. As of early February, Acero Junction is out looking to gain market share in the hot-rolled markets (probably from NLMK, Nucor, and SDI Butler).

Fear is running rampant—at least in the financial community. They believe that the sky is falling.

No, that’s not the case, but some of the nuttiness that exists in the steel industry continues.

As of Feb. 7, flat-rolled steel prices rose $160/ton since the mills first started announcing price increases in late October 2016. At that time, hotrolled coil (HRC) was averaging $470/ton. Once the increases started, prices rose and peaked in late January when HRC hit $630/ton. That’s a good run over a 13-week period.

A majority of the domestic mills collected $160/ton of the $180/ton announced, and Steel Market Update believes that some mills collected the full $180. Depending on where a customer was when the increases started flowing, some mills might have collected even more than the $180/ton that was announced.

As of the first full week of February, hot-rolled has come off its $630 high by $20/ton to $610/ton. That’s still a good number for steelmakers, especially compared to the $470 SMU was reporting in late October 2016.

Back to the New Steel Startups

The $610/ton price ($30.50/cwt) is even more amazing when you consider that a new steel mill—with a 1.6 million-ton capacity when fully operational—came to life in December and in its first full month of production it set a record, producing 63,000 tons of HRC. Within the next few months, BRS will begin running cold-rolled and galvanized as well as hot-rolled. In early February, our sources were advising the offer prices for HRC tonnage out of BRS was $600/ton and $810/ton on cold-rolled. BRS is within our SMU index HRC steel price range, which is a good sign for a new mill, and it is fighting to gain market share.

The market was aghast when reports of lower-than-expected ferrous scrap prices were being negotiated with the domestic steel mills in early February. A week later we are finding the demise of the scrap industry was a bit premature as scrap prices have rebounded off their low. How will these now less-than-expected decreases, coupled with the new expectation of a flat to higher March scrap market, affect the flat-rolled steel prices going forward?

Yes, some of the steel mills are out looking for business. They need hot-rolled tons, and in some cases, they may need some cold-rolled and coated tons. But the SMU is not yet hearing of any panic at the steel mills.

As of early February, SMU is keeping its Price Momentum Indicator at neutral. A couple of weeks ago, we were sensing prices would move lower. (The question then was how much.) Now we are sensing that the market will go through a short period where steel prices will find their balance as we wait to see if the mini-mills decide they want to push market prices lower "show up" Big River Steel and Acero Junction and prevent them from taking market share.

BRS and Acero Junction will create natural competition among the various mills. No one wants to lose market share, especially in hot-rolled, which has been a weak product for some time. However, we keep hearing from manufacturing companies and service centers that "demand is not too bad." Some worry about auto inventories, but service centers tell us that their auto customers are not yet canceling orders. Other manufacturers are worried about energy markets, which are taking some time to come back, especially given recent investments in this area.

Foreign steel imports will shrink, and we are already seeing that in hot-rolled import numbers. So much so that the new tons coming out of BRS and Acero Junction may do nothing more than make up for the limitation on foreign HRC tons.

Traders continue to tell SMU to expect flat-rolled imports to be down 25 percent this year compared to last. We will see.

A steel buyer told SMU in early February that he spoke to BRS and told them that $600/ton hot-rolled was not good enough. He said the mini-mills have gotten aggressive. We will see how BRS responds.

The steel market is always dynamic and ebbs and flows sometimes for what seems to be no real reason at all. A conversion mill spoke with SMU as this report was being prepared, and he said, "It is what it is, and well, gotta battle and outdo your competitors anyway. This is not patty-cakes!"

At the same time, the same source said that they were not reducing prices.

"Not us—yet. $41 is a pretty good customer price [base price on coated] and is OK with our current costing. Scrap may not have gone down as much as we were thinking. Things are hanging on."

We will see what happens in the weeks to come. When new mills come to life, it is always an interesting time, but SMU continues to be optimistic as do most of the flat-rolled buyers and sellers based on the SMU Steel Buyers Sentiment Index (see

Figure 2

).

The most pessimistic we have ever seen the SMU Price Momentum Indicator was -85. (-100 is the lowest number possible.) That was in March 2009, and the sky was falling.

The sky is not falling today, just some nuts rolling on the ground.
