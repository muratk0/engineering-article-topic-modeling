This old Scottish proverb should have been written by some ancient Scottish economist. The fact is that nothing that happens in the economy is universally bad or universally good. A strong dollar hurts those that are trying to export but delights those that import. Higher interest rates are good for some and problematic for others.

In the manufacturing world there is the issue of steel tariffs. Those that are competing against steel imports from China or Russia or Japan or any other competitor would like nothing more than to see high tariff barriers erected against these products. In truth, the U.S. steel-producing community is struggling and has been for years, and the nations that are selling steel into the U.S. are not competing fairly. The Chinese subsidize steel producers as these mills provide a lot of employment in the rural areas. Russian mills are equally important politically and get support from the government. These protections and subsidies are not offered to U.S. producers. The high tariff wall would make the U.S. steel producer competitive again.

The challenge is that what is good for the producer is not so good for the consumer. Those sectors that use steel are not at all happy with the notion of high tariffs and for the most obvious of reasons. These tariffs will mean high costs for them, and there is a limit as to how much of this additional expense can be passed on. The end consumer of the car is going to feel the additional costs and so will the builder that pays more for that structural steel. If the prices go too high, the temptation to use substitute goods will be compelling – aluminum in trucks rather than steel, and composite material in construction.

There is no perfect solution. The U.S. wants a domestic steel industry, and that may not be possible without help and intervention. The U.S. also wants reasonable prices for manufacturers and their consumers. That means less intervention so that world prices can dominate.

The current tariff idea is going to be expensive – will it rescue U.S. steel or will it just drive up costs? Much will depend on whether those foreign producers react with even lower prices as they try to maintain market share.
