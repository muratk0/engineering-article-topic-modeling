# Buckle up for another ride on the tariff coaster

[TARİH: 01.04.2025 The Fabricator]

Steel News

When policy changes at the speed of a social media post, uncertainty reigns

MICHAEL COWDEN

P

resident Donald Trump’s tariffs are aimed in large part at bringing manufacturing back to the U.S. In theory, it’s simple enough. Want to avoid a big tariff? Make it in the U.S.!

Tariffs worked for steel, and you could make the case that this approach has worked in the past. Section 232 tariffs of 25% on imported steel encouraged billions of dollars of investments (and millions of tons per year) in new or expanding domestic steelmaking and coating capacity.

You could even make the case that South Korean steelmaker Hyundai floating a $6 billion sheet mill near Baton Rouge, La., is a result of tariffs (and perhaps also of the hostility Nippon Steel has faced from both President Trump and former President Joe Biden in trying to acquire U.S. Steel).

At the end of the day, that’s one thing trade policies should be aimed at doing—encouraging foreign investment in the United States.

Let’s say the steel tariffs worked. It was still a matter of years between when tariffs were first imposed in 2018 and when new capacity came into the market. Will it work for everything else?

Does the same strategy work when we’re talking not just one industry but what seems, some days, like all of them? Trump has threatened everything from imported steel and aluminum to Canadian lumber to European automobiles. Let’s say the president follows through on these threats. What’s the lag time between when the tariffs go into place and when, for example, a new automobile plant is built? And will consumers be willing to put up with any inflation they might have to endure in the meantime?

Trump has been preparing people for a rough patch. "Tariffs are about making America rich again and making America great again. And it’s happening," he said in a joint address to Congress in March. "There will be a little disturbance, but we’re okay with that. It won’t be much."

He was right about the disturbance part. Whether it’s a "little" might depend on how close you are to the blast radius of the threatened tariffs.

Making News Great Again!

I never wake up in the morning and think, "Ugh. It’s such a slow news day. How is SMU going to find enough to write about to keep the website humming?"

I’ve heard tariffs compared to a carousel because there are so many of them. They seem to rotate across both Trump’s attention and the news cycle. There are the blanket tariffs of 25% that Trump has threatened against all imports from Canada and Mexico. There is the 20% already applied to China. These are in theory necessary because of a national security emergency stemming from illegal fentanyl and illegal immigration.

Then we have the Section 232 tariffs of 25%—also based on national security grounds—on all imported steel and aluminum. And the Trump administration is considering rolling out Section 232 tariffs on copper as well.

Let’s not forget the reciprocal tariffs that could go into effect as soon as April 2. We’ve even got potential Section 301 penalties that could be applied to Chinese-built ships carrying imports to U.S. ports. And then potential "sectoral" tariffs targeting specific industries could be applied to everything from microchips to pharmaceuticals.

What’s next, executive orders putting tariffs on individual companies?

Tariffs Bend the Space-Time Continuum

The matter of timelines—or lack thereof—is also a concern.

Just before Thanksgiving, Trump threatened on social media to impose tariffs on all imports from Canada and Mexico once he was inaugurated on Jan. 21. He waited until Feb. 1 to announce the tariffs. And then on Feb. 3, he delayed them to for a month.

At the end of February, it was more of the same, but Trump insisted he was serious this time—that he really would hit Canada and Mexico with tariffs. On March 4 at 12:01 a.m. EST, he imposed the 25% tariffs, and then the next day he delayed them for a month (again).

A week later, on March 11, it was another full dose of tariff craziness. In response to Trump’s tariff threats, Doug Ford, the premier of Ontario, applied a 25% surcharge on electricity the province sells to the U.S. Trump then threatened on social media to retaliate with a 25% tariff on all Canadian imports, which, when combined with Section 232, would have brought Canada’s combined tariff to 50%.

This stuff has real life consequences. Buyers in some instances scrambled to cancel orders waiting to cross the border because a 50% tariff costs a lot more than a 25% tariff. But then a few hours later, Trump backed off. And there was a scramble to uncancel those orders.

What kind of look is this for U.S. industry? Let’s say you’re an investor, and you think (rightfully) that the domestic manufacturing sector is an attractive sector for investment. Is there price volatility? Yes. Are there tools to help mitigate that risk? Also, yes. But can investors swallow this kind of policy uncertainty? How can you offset policy risk when it travels at the speed of social media in an industry that measures lead times in weeks and where material moves at the speed of trucks, trains, and barges?

Here Come the (Imported) Tons

Then there is the matter that 25% isn’t such a big deal for a lot of the world. Getting hit with Section 232 tariffs of 25% was a big change for Canada, Mexico, and the European Union, who previously had been mostly or entirely exempt from the tariff. It was also a big change for nations such as Argentina, Brazil, and South Korea, who previously been subject to a quota.

But it wasn’t a change for the rest of the world, which has long been subject to a Section 232 tariff of 25% since 2018. Getting hotrolled from Egypt? Cold-rolled from Vietnam? Galvanized from Indonesia? Or maybe rebar from Algeria? Nothing has changed for those countries. They were already subject to the 25% tariff, and still are.

Or maybe you’re getting OCTG or plate from South Korea? Or slab from Brazil? With the quota gone, there is nothing to limit volumes.

Remember the panic-buying that happened as U.S. buyers sought to get ahead of the next round of tariffs or mill price increases earlier this year? It resulted in a torrid pace of U.S. price gains. So much so that 25% probably won’t be much of a break on imports.

The result: Some of you tell me you’re getting approached with more imports than ever.

Let’s say that import material is rolled in April, shipped in May, and arrives at U.S. ports in June or July. What happens to U.S. prices when domestic lead times get closer to summer? Could we be in for a sharp correction, or could Trump increase 232 tariffs to 50%?

Does the Tariff Roller Coaster Ever Stop?

I wrote back in January that globalization hadn’t really worked as planned for places like Pittsburgh; Weirton, W.Va.; and Youngstown, Ohio. Maybe a little corrective regionalism or everyone goes their own way-ism was called for.

But I don’t know if that’s what we’re getting right now. Sure, I realized back then that a little tough medicine, as Trump alluded to in his speech to Congress, might be necessary.

This, however, feels like something else. I don’t think a carousel is the right metaphor anymore. Some days it feels more like the carousel has turned into a roller coaster—and Space Mountain at that. You can’t see where the next drop or turn is going to be.

By the way, after you strapped in and left the station, an announcement was made that the roller coaster would continue indefinitely.
