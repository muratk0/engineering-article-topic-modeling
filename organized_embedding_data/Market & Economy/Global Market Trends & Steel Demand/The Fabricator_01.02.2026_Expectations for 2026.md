# Expectations for 2026

[TARİH: 01.02.2026 The Fabricator]

Fabrinomics | Economic Insights

All eyes are on labor, inflation, trade, and nonresidential construction

Dr. Chris Kuehl

2026 is upon us, and economic expectations vary widely. It is a classic case of glass half full or half empty. The GDP performance last year far exceeded expectations, inflation numbers seemed to come down (in some areas), job creation slowed, but layoffs did not really surge. All this was enough to give people some confidence going forward.

However (and there is always a however), GDP slowed in Q4, inflation at the food and housing levels increased, consumer confidence remained low, and investors are worried about a tech bubble bursting (or at least a correction). To add to the angst, it is an election year, and that means a cascade of government reactions designed to win votes. Unfortunately, these actions often occur at the expense of the economy.

Nonresidential Construction

Consumers will keep driving economic growth (more than 55%). The other big contribution last year came from nonresidential construction (almost a third of the total). Many are debating over whether this pace can be maintained.

Many projects have been stalled by labor and materials shortages, and that takes some of the momentum out of this sector. U.S. manufacturing construction is currently running at $223 billion annually—four times pre-pandemic levels. With more than $5 trillion in new private-sector commitments (much of it front-loaded by tax policy), 2026 and 2027 could bring one of the most active construction environments on record.

Construction projects related to transportation and health care continue to expand. But the biggest driver in nonresidential construction growth is, of course, the data center boom. Few sectors have attracted as much attention. Some assert this hyper growth will continue for years; others contend a bubble is in the making (or at the very least a bust). Nobody really knows the potential for AI right now, but the interest is fueling a radical increase in capacity for data.

Construction of put-in-place data centers hit a record $14 billion in July 2025 alone, doubling the prior single-month high and pushing starts for 2025 to $26.9 billion—almost triple 2024’s pace.

Gerville/iStock/Getty Images Plus

Bank of America tallied $40 billion in annualized spend as of June 2025, up 28% year over year, driven by Amazon, Microsoft, Google, Meta, and Oracle ramping up AI capacity. The engagement of these major companies is a factor that argues against the development of a bubble. These are not cash-strapped startups but massive operations that are embracing AI to defend their respective turfs.

Rapid investment will continue. The industry value is expected to be on track for $198 billion this year, a nearly 7% annual expansion rate that will outpace most significant nonresidential construction segments.

Expectations for the next 12 months show Louisiana adding $10.2 billion in data center starts (based on reported project budgets), Texas $7.1 billion, Virginia $6.4 billion, Ohio $5.3 billion, and Georgia more than $4.9 billion. Emerging markets in Iowa, Georgia, Washington, Pennsylvania, New York, New Jersey, Nebraska, and North Dakota also will come into play.

Need for Power

Data centers are getting larger and larger, and that means an equally insatiable demand for energy to power them (as well as other resources such as water and people to populate this sector of the economy). Availability of electricity has become the primary concern for site selections for new projects.

Texas, Virginia, Louisiana, Georgia, and Ohio are expected to now house more than 60% of the under-construction capacity, driven heavily by natural gas electricity production capacity and potential nuclear energy production. With this comes the need for electrical switchgears, busways, transformers, uninterrupted power supplies, and prefabricated power rooms.

The electrical content per MW keeps climbing. AI racks will lift electrical hardware spending per MW by 15% to 20%, versus traditional "cloud halls" used for storage and cloud computing. The primary concern given all this expected growth is the capacity to meet the demand. Will there be enough infrastructure— energy production, labor availability, or even the materials needed to build the projects planned? Many analysts believe that more than $198 billion in 2026 would be possible if materials were more readily available and experienced talent also was available.

Trade and Supply Chains

Supply chain issues have calmed down quite a bit. The physical threats from groups like the Houthi rebels have diminished to the point that shipping is moving through the Suez again. The Panama Canal also is back to normal.

The remaining issue is trade and tariff policy, which continue to be a moving target. (Editor’s note: At press time, Trump announced the potential for new tariffs targeted at Europe, related to disputes over Greenland, then withdrew the threat several days later.) The situation today is radically different than it was a year ago. Mexico is expanding its exports to the U.S. dramatically as many of the barriers have been lowered. India is paying higher tariffs than China. The U.S. has been backing away from tariffs on food but still maintains high barriers to imported copper, steel, and aluminum. China has renewed its commitment to its export economy and has targeted Europe and Japan.

China just set a record for exports, despite a 20% reduction in exports to the U.S. It has found alternative markets in Europe, Asia, and Latin America. Some of these are designed to circumvent U.S. tariff rules as the Chinese produce in nations that are not impacted by the restrictions.

Trade tensions between the U.S. and China are not expected to ease in the foreseeable future. This tariff escalation has affected bilateral trade, effectively curtailing the flow of goods between the two nations.

U.S. agriculture remains stressed. It has been a combination of familiar challenges as well as new ones. The trade war with China has resulted in severely diminished market opportunities for U.S. farmers, and this has been a very productive year for crops in the U.S. as well as around the world. That surplus means sharply lower prices, and that prompted an attempt to bail out farmers with a large payout. The problem is that this support is woefully inadequate given the loss of the China market.

Meanwhile, the government is getting more pushback from senators and representatives from states sensitive to imports and exports. The farm states are worrying about where they will sell their output, and manufacturing states are worried about their supply chains.

Efforts are underway in Congress to ease the financial impact with subsidies offered to farmers as well as tax breaks for some in manufacturing. More U.S. attention has been focused on nations that are engaged in transshipping, as China has used this tactic to dodge tariffs.

Inflation and Labor

Inflation is, generally speaking, a disease of growth. It is what happens when demand exceeds supply. That creates scarcity, and the reaction by producers is to hike prices to control that demand. There also are artificial shortages created by factors such as supply chain breakdowns, tariff policies, and various interventions.

Many have suggested that tariffs would eventually add more than 1% to the inflation rate, but the full effect varies across sectors. Overall inflation numbers have actually fallen, but this was almost entirely driven by lower fuel prices. Certain product categories directly affected by tariffs experienced inflation in the range of 10% to 20%, sometimes even more. The inflation rates for food and housing also are climbing.

Chunyip Wong/iStock/Getty Images Plus

Issues persist around how inflation is measured. The core rate excludes the costs of food and fuel, and some have even tried to assert that inflation is lower if you don’t count the cost of housing. Expectations vary, but many analysts contend that a rate between 3.0% and 3.5% is likely—about a point higher than it is currently.

Isolating the costs of commodities, the inflation rate is closing in on 5%. Nobody has to point out the price of copper to the FMA community, as it has been sitting at all-time highs. Steel, aluminum, and other commodity prices also are high.

The inflation issue has started to show a shift in emphasis. The expected surge in inflation due to tariffs faded through the summer as businesses found alternatives and tariff decisions were partially reversed. The fundamental issue is whether there is a greater threat from slow growth or inflation. The hawks at the Fed will assert that inflation remains the No. 1 concern, as there are many factors pushing it higher. Commodity costs are up, as they are still impacted by the tariffs; food prices are still climbing for the same reason. Housing prices are up, and worries persist about rising wages in key sectors.

The doves at the Fed counter with assertions that slower growth is manifesting. There was truly spectacular growth last year. The estimates for 2026 are not miserable, but they are down to the 2.5% range, and doves assert they can fall even lower.

Overall, inflation concerns have shifted away from tariff impact and toward labor costs. The impending demographic collapse in 2030 is already causing spikes. By that date, every boomer will have reached retirement age, and that threatens a major labor shortage, especially for skilled workers.

The jobless numbers remain respectable—slightly higher than they were earlier at 4.6%. Though well under what is considered a normal rate (6.0%), this is the highest it has been since 2021. The real issue is that the U-6 rate has been climbing, just recently surpassing 8%. This rate picks up the discouraged worker and the involuntary part-timer. There are some 9 million unemployed in a world where labor shortage is a key concern. This means they simply lack the means to get back in the job market and risk being permanently unemployed.
