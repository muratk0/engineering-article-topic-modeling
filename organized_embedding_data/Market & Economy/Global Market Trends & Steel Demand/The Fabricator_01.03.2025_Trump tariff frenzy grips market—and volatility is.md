# Trump tariff frenzy grips market—and volatility is back

MICHAEL COWDEN

y the time you read this, it will probably be old news. I hope it will still be accurate. But I make no guarantees.

It has long been the case that U.S. policy might swing wildly every four years depending on whether a democrat or republican was in the White House.

Now, policy is shifting by the day, by the hour, and even by the minute—often on live TV or on social media. How do you make a business plan around that, especially when, at least for now, President Donald Trump’s latest utterance on cable or on Truth Social seems to drive steel prices more than economic data? More on that in a minute.

Since We Last Spoke

I mentioned the ongoing, nearly $15 billion Nippon Steel-U. S. Steel (USS) merger saga in my February column ("Trump tariffs: Negotiating tactic or fair warning?" p. 28). That one is still ongoing—or it was when I wrote this article.

The latest was that Trump wouldn’t allow USS to be acquired by Nippon outright because "the concept, psychologically, not good." Instead, Nippon would "invest heavily." Details were sketchy when I wrote this.

Does that mean a 50-50 joint venture like AM/NS Calvert? (ArcelorMittal and Nippon Steel Calvert.) Or maybe like California Steel Industries (CSI), which is a joint venture between Nucor and Japanese steelmaker JFE? So much for the prior rumor that Cliffs and Nucor would work together to buy USS. Or maybe there is another surprise plot twist in the cards. We’ll see.

Trump Trumps Super Bowl With Tariffs

I thought maybe, just maybe, Trump would enjoy the Super Bowl. And that maybe, just maybe, I wouldn’t be chasing tariff news over the weekend (again).

That’s not how it went down. Trump said on Super Bowl Sunday (while flying to the game on Air Force One) that he would announce 25% tariffs on all steel and aluminum imports the next day.

You’ll recall that Section 232 was first implemented in 2018. It featured an across-the-board tariff of 25% on all steel imported to the U.S. Let’s call it OG S232 for fun.

New Section 232 tariffs will apply not only to semifinished and finished steel but also to downstream goods such as fabricated structural steel. This has been a concern for many in the steel industry as China has incorporated its steel into fabrications that are then imported through countries like Mexico.

Getty Images

OG S232 was subsequently ratcheted back with quotas and other exemptions for the U.S.’s closest allies and trading partners. The rumor was that Trump would bring back OG S232 (no more quotas, no more exemptions even for close allies) and then some.

The official word came late on Monday, Feb. 10. And the rumor proved to be mostly accurate. Here are the highlights:

The changes to Section 232 are scheduled to go into effect at 12:01 ET on Wednesday, March 12. (Or at least that was still the plan when I wrote this.)

Canada and Mexico will not be spared.

Quotas with Argentina, Brazil, and South Korea will be kaput.

Ditto tariff rate quotas with the EU, Japan, and the UK.

No more special exemptions for Ukraine.

One big change from OG S232: New S232 tariffs will apply not only to semifinished and finished steel but also to downstream goods such as fabricated structural steel and prestressed concrete strand.

Think that’s harsh? Consider aluminum, which is seeing its Section 232 tariff go from 10% to 25%.

Are Blanket Tariffs Still in the Cards?

Before the Section 232 ruckus, we had a frenzy over potential blanket tariffs of 25% on all imports from Canada and Mexico starting on Feb. 1. Then each nation received a reprieve until March 4.

Blanket tariffs are a lot more complicated than measures targeted at steel and aluminum. That might explain why Trump didn’t immediately impose them on Canada and Mexico.

But Trump did place an additional 10% tariff on all imports from China, and Beijing retaliated. It hit U.S. coal, liquified natural gas, crude oil, farm equipment, and certain vehicles with tariffs. It also imposed export controls on certain critical minerals—tungsten among them.

Why do I bring up tungsten? Tungsten is critical to making armor-piercing bullets and artillery rounds. And no one in the world produces anywhere near as much tungsten as China.

That’s a little harsher than targeting Kentucky whiskey or Florida orange juice, as U.S. allies have done in the past.

Put IEEPA on Your Radar ASAP

At the SMU/PTB Tampa Steel Conference in February, the tariff situation was changing even as I was on stage interviewing smart folks like Wolfe Research’s Timna Tanners, Institute for Supply Management (ISM) CEO Tom Derry, and veteran Wiley trade attorney Tim Brightbill. It felt like we were doing a cable news show in front of a live audience.

With so much flux, what can I say now that will still be relevant when you read this? For one, you should add a new acronym to your vocabulary ASAP: IEEPA, which is short for the International Emergency Economic Powers Act.

IEEPA allows Trump to invoke a national security emergency; with China, Canada, and Mexico, that emergency was illegal immigration and fentanyl. That emergency then allows him to impose tariffs without going through Congress.

Whether or not you agree that immigration and fentanyl are national security emergencies, the policy pattern that’s emerging seems clear enough: use IEEPA to threaten tariffs, extract concessions, and move on to the next region the U.S. might have a beef with. And Trump seems to have made it clear in public remarks that more IEEPA tariff threats are coming.

Maybe the European Union is next. Trump might say that Europe is not spending enough on defense to support NATO, and that’s a national security threat. Perhaps he’ll single out Taiwan and say advanced semiconductors are a national security threat. The BRICS (Brazil, Russia, India, China, and South Africa) should also have their IEEPA radar active, because Trump really doesn’t like the idea of them backing a currency that might rival the greenback.

You could hire a political scientist to determine the risk profile for each nation or region from which you might want to source steel. You might also want to ask whether the U.S., with all our political upheaval, is still the best risk-adjusted place in the world in which to do business.

How Is the Consumer Holding Up?

That brings me to some of the things that ISM’s CEO Tom Derry said about supply chains and potential tariff- or immigration-related inflation.

Automotive companies, for example, already had a very tall task making long-term plans when U.S. policy might swing depending on who was in the Oval Office. Now policy can make tectonic shifts at the speed of social media.

And the biggest concern of ISM members is tariffs on imports. That’s not to say that things are grim out there. As Derry noted in his speech in Tampa, manufacturing activity has been picking up recently after tough times in 2023 and throughout most of 2024.

As ISM sees it, the U.S. was in a manufacturing recession for 25 months. That’s historic, even if it didn’t get many headlines. The Great Recession in 2008-09 led to a manufacturing recession of 18 months. (For history buffs, the recession of 1982 led to a manufacturing recession that lasted for 19 months.)

Things should get better from here. Assuming no major shocks, that is. And, boy, have we had a lot of shocks lately.

Manufacturing is a key part of our economy. But it’s a smaller part than it used to be. I hope it is a more important piece of economic activity going forward. That said, and as Derry warned, the one thing you really can’t do in a consumer-driven economy is overwhelm buyers with high prices.

There is a new, radical mood in D.C. A lot can change in a situation like that. Will the Trump administration tear down trade policy as we know it and build back something better? Or will ripping down the old order instead lead to supply chain chaos and inflation, as we saw during the pandemic?

I wish I had a crystal ball on that one. In the meantime, whatever you might think about Trump, he has given us no shortage of things to write about.
