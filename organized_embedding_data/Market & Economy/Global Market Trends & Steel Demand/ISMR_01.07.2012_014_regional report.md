A Latin American Focus

Latin America's growth has remained resilient in the face of the global financial crisis

"Latin American countries' drive to increase infrastructure investments to improve regional integration and reduce logistics costs through better transportation routes has opened up new investment opportunities in the region."

In an uncertain financial world, Latin America's growth has middle classes are expanding and poverty is falling. Governments across the region have implemented fundamental reforms to their economies to make them more open and competitive. The population of Latin America is close to 600 million people and contains the largest group of democracies outside Western Europe.

With 4-5% growth rates predicted for the next five years and a GDP greater than India and Russia's combined, Latin America provides a range of excellent opportunities for exporters. It has a larger middle class than China and India combined and can be an easier place to do business than many other emerging markets.

Latin American countries' drive to increase infrastructure investments to improve regional integration and reduce logistics costs through better transportation routes has opened up new investment opportunities in the region. The Latin American market will especially benefit from a spate of construction activities in Brazil, which has lined up a wide range of projects to improve its infrastructure in time for the World Soccer Cup in 2014 and the Olympic Games in 2016, said analyst Frost and Sullivan.

## Keen investment climate

Countries across Latin America have ambitious plans for their future. Colombia is investing US$ 54 billion in infrastructure over the next three years. Venezuela has investment plans worth more than US$ 200 billion to develop its oil and gas reserves and infrastructure by 2030. Over the next four years, Brazil has US$ 350 billion worth of investment planned.

In Brazil, renewables currently represent 45.4% of its energy matrix and 86.2% of its electricity matrix, making the country's energy sector one of the world's cleanest. Hydropower accounts for 80% of Brazil's energy consumption. Generation from alternative renewables is expected to double from 8% to 16%. With the growth in Colombia's oil and gas sector, there are plenty of opportunities for suppliers to provide high value machinery to oil and gas companies there.

New networks, such as the Community of Latin American and Caribbean States (CELAC) and regional groupings such as the Union of South American Nations (UNASUR) and Mercosur reflect a growing self-confidence in Latin America about the region's increasing importance on the world stage.

## A regional spotlight

As the largest industrial city in Latin America and the most important economic centre in the country, the metropolitan region of São Paulo in Brazil is one of the major industrial locations in the world. Over half of the gross national product of the South America is generated here. The economy of Brazil is the world's sixth largest by nominal GDP and is expected to become fifth by the end of 2012.

Brazil, one of the world's largest automobile manufacturing markets, has been witnessing healthy growth compared to the global auto industry due to strong domestic demand. The Brazilian economy is also the largest in Latin America, with oil and gas, power generation, aerospace and automotive sectors being the main revenue generators.

High investments, in particular, strengthened the gross domestic product.

Several major projects will ensure that further highlights can be expected from Brazilian economic growth. According to various studies, investments in airports, stadiums, transport as well as security and environmental technology of more than EUR 40 billion are forecast for the sporting mega-events, the World Cup in 2014 and the first Olympic Games in Brazil in 2016. However, with a still not fully developed industrial sector, Brazil is dependent on foreign companies to implement these ambitious projects of the future – a great opportunity for all companies which present themselves in Brazil.

The Asia Pacific aerospace manufacturing market is facing tough competition from the Latin American region, primarily Mexico and Chile. To reduce supply chain risks, many North American aerospace giants have established their low-cost bases in Latin America.

"North American aerospace companies prefer to set up their low-cost base in South America due to the lower wages, initiatives by a proactive government, bilateral aviation safety agreements with the United States, increasing talent pool and geographical vicinity," said Frost & Sullivan research associate, Aditya Desai.

"Mexico is a large and growing market. With a population of over 118 million, (74% urban; 44% under 20 years of age; 23% wealthy/upper middle class and 37% middle class), it offers a large market with almost a US$1 trillion GDP," said the US Precision Metalforming Association.

Mexico's strongest markets include agribusiness, auto parts & services, energy, environmental, housing & construction, packaging equipment, telecommunications equipment and transportation infrastructure equipment.

"Public limited companies (Plcs) can have outdated perceptions of Latin America and be unaware of the opportunities and the relative ease of doing business in the region. Latin America can provide important commercial opportunities for SMEs and an easier way into emerging markets than either China or India. Latin American governments consistently tell us that they want to increase SME links with the UK," the UK Trade and Investment (UKTI), which last year helped over 1200 UK firms on their Latin America strategy, told ISMR.

## The challenges ahead

However, the OECD has warned that the region should keep a wary eye on the global economy, particularly in the Eurozone.

"The region should strengthen its macroeconomic policy space to guard against uncertainties in the global economic outlook and volatility in international capital markets. The global economy continues to be the main source of uncertainty for Latin America and the Caribbean. Large capital inflows, due to significant interest rate differentials between the region and the developed economies, and the subsequent exchange rate and inflationary pressures were defining features of the first semester of 2011 and still deserve special attention," it said.

"Fiscal problems in the euro zone could have serious repercussions in international financial markets, including emerging markets, where capital flow reversals could trigger large swings in the real exchange rate with disruptive effects on economic activity. Trade with China – which more than tripled during the 2000s - was one of the factors that facilitated the region's quick recovery. Nevertheless, today this makes the region more exposed to a potential growth slowdown in China, which would affect it through lower export demand and commodity prices. Both would have a strong impact on fiscal accounts in many countries in the region."

Infrastructure issues and the productivity gap are also challenges for Latin America and the Caribbean, it says. This reflects the limited diversification of the region's economies, their specialisation in non-technology-intensive sectors and scant investment in research and development (R&D) and innovation. ■
