# Thoughts on recession and inflation: A changing landscape

[TARİH: 01.09.2023 The Fabricator]

Fabrinomics | Economic Insights

Dr. Chris Kuehl

It is early days yet, but there has been a dramatic shift in attitude among many economists. Not that there is true consensus at this point, but in a poll commissioned by the World Economic Forum, 45% asserted the U.S. would not be in recession this year, and that opinion is spreading.

The data over the past year shows a series of significant misses. Q3 of 2022 was slated to come in at 0.04%; instead, the economy grew by 2.6%. The estimate for 4Q was 0.05%; the actual was 2.9%. Many expected Q1 of 2023 would finally bring a recession. Nope. The economy saw a gain of 2.1%, and now the estimate for second quarter is close to 2.0% again. What recession? The view now is that a soft landing is possible and perhaps even likely. There is more and more support for the position that 2023 will be a year of slight growth.

Sectoral Variation

The reality is that the U.S economy rarely experiences a total recession (or a total boom for that matter). Some sectors slide into recession while others boom, and there is a lot of variety between regions. The sectors that have been growing aggressively have been automotive and aerospace, while machinery has been down from the pace set last year. Retail is still strong but predominantly in the upper end, while the sectors aimed at the lower income earners are slowing.

The manufacturing economy reflects this. Some sectors have been growing – durable goods orders are up in sectors such as electrical equipment and appliances as well as automotive and the often-overlooked defense sector. On the other hand, there has been reduced growth in the metals market, both primary and fabricated.

Different trends affect the manufacturing and fabrication sectors. In general, the more production taking place in the U.S., the better. The export sector has been growing, but as reshoring becomes more common, the demand has shifted to U.S. facilities. There is also more interest in domestic production on the part of the consumer (although price still dominates most purchase decisions). There was an estimated $125 billion spent on this in 2022, and there will be as much as $6 trillion committed over the next seven years.

There continues to be an emphasis on automotive, aerospace, and food manufacturing, and agriculture in general has been identified as a high priority. The U.S. has been embracing industrial policy for the first time in decades. This is common in other nations, but the U.S. has long been the chief advocate for free trade and global competition. Now there is an emphasis on protecting domestic production and reducing dependence on imports.

Food Crisis Averted

The expected global food crisis has not developed as expected. Russia and Ukraine (combined) accounted for 25% of the world’s wheat and 20% of the world’s corn exports. They also produce as much as 30% of the fertilizer used in Europe. It was anticipated that this production would be cut off, but much of Ukraine’s output has found a market in Europe. The EU altered tariffs and other barriers to allow Ukrainian wheat and other grains to flow, and that drove prices so low that for the first time in over five decades the milling industry in Kansas is buying Polish wheat because it is cheaper than wheat grown in their own state.

The nations that bought food from Russia and Ukraine also found other sources as many nations had good harvests this year. The crisis never manifested as feared. The issue is as it has been in past years – transportation and conflict in the recipient nations. This still makes food transportation, processing, and storage issues of prime concern.

The Vexingly Positive Labor Market

The behavior of the labor market remains vexing and at the same time encouraging. The vexing part is that there is a chronic labor shortage that shows no signs of going way. By the end of this decade the entire boomer generation will have reached retirement age, and that is some 76 million people. There is no ready replacement for this cohort.

Then there is the emergence of the gig economy. These jobs have siphoned off millions of people who would otherwise be employed in the semi-skilled job markets. The unemployment rate has remained very low, and that has affected the Fed’s decision-making. As long as these numbers are low, the Fed feels it has room to hike rates. The prime driver for inflation now is wages, although they have started to come down to the same levels as overall inflation lately – still far too high. Wages spark the wage/price spiral, and once this starts it is very hard to interrupt.

The Whole Inflation-Fighting Toolbox

The assumption is that central banks only have one real tool when it comes to fighting inflation. The standard approach is to lift interest rates until the economy teeters on the edge of recession. It is about as subtle as a baseball bat. Remember back in the days of Paul Volcker when he explained his radical policy of record-high interest rates? Record-high rates would mean business failures and withdrawals. That would translate into mass layoffs, and that would mean people would have no money. If the consumer has no money, producers couldn’t hike their prices and,

voila

, there is a halt to inflation. Of course, this also brings recession, and this is precisely what the U.S. experienced in the aftermath of the Volcker regime.

While manipulating interest rates will always be the most effective short-term tactic, there are other tools. One of these involves the bond markets. Overall, this is referred to as

quantitative tightening

. It means having the central banks selling off a large part of the bond portfolio accumulated when the goal was stimulating growth. The central banks buy government-issued bonds as a means by which to boost growth. Select banks handle these transactions, and that pumps cash from these banks into the system. In the last couple of decades, the central banks have been very active – the Federal Reserve had a balance sheet of just $800 billion at the start of the 2008 debacle, and soon ran that up to over $6 trillion as it bought treasuries aggressively during quantitative easing. Today that balance sheet shows $8.5 trillion.

The European Central Bank (ECB) has a very similar situation, and so do most of the other central banks. It is what was required to get out of the 2008 recession. If one remembers, the U.S. went through three rounds of quantitative easing (although the second had relatively little impact).

Now, the banks arguably have too much money tied up in these bonds, and the suggestion is that they start selling off these assets much more rapidly to control inflation. The ECB and the Bank of England (BoE) each hold more than a quarter of their nation’s (or grouping’s) debt, and the Fed holds more than a fifth of the total U.S. debt.

With all the angst over the size of the U.S. debt (130% of GDP on average), it is important to note who holds that debt.

Around 30% is held by U.S. investors and roughly 30% is held by foreign investors. The rest is in the hands of the Fed and the U.S. Treasury itself. For the last year, there has been an attempt to unwind that debt level and reduce the balance sheet, and thus far it has not had the negative impact that had been anticipated. It was assumed that sales of this debt would dry up the market for other bonds—corporate, municipal, and others. That has not been a major issue, and that convinces many that an accelerated selling pace could be sustained.

There is a connection between rates and bond sales. Many assume that reducing the balance sheet would allow the more hawkish members of the central banks to relax a bit and allow rates to stay somewhat lower. These bond sales serve to slow down the growth of the economy. Rather than buying newly issued debt, the investor would be buying bonds that have already been factored into the overall economy.

When the U.S. Congress authorized the raising of the debt ceiling, it meant the government would soon be issuing another $1 trillion in bonds as this was what Congress had allocated in its last budget. Any way you look at this, it is stimulative. It is not clear whether the Fed, ECB, BoE, or any other major central bank is prepared to accelerate their bond sales at this stage, but the justification for doing so is there.

If the banks decide to try controlling inflation with manipulation of the bond market, it will mean they will rely less on hiking interest rates and there could be a significant slowdown in the pace, leaving rates at around 5.0% rather than the projected 6.0%.

Manufacturing Construction Soars

The latest producer price index for broad construction materials shows it to be about 4.4% lower than it was a year ago, but it is still nearly 50% higher than the long-term growth rate. Some construction firms are still reporting that suppliers are facing backlogs of certain materials, especially electrical panels and selected electrical components.

All that said, the fastest growing segment of construction has been manufacturing. It is up by over 73% from last year. Normal spend has been around $60 billion, and the current estimated spend is over $190 billion. The factors that have been driving this emphasis on manufacturing include the move toward reshoring as well as the expansion of robotics and automation as these developments demand new or expanded facilities. For manufacturing in general, this is very, very good news.
