W

hat do the Trump administration tariffs on steel imports mean for some fabricators in the U.S.? It looks like higher steel prices and possibly more competition.

Since Jan. 1, benchmark hot-rolled coil (HRC) prices have increased by $240/ton in the U.S. At the beginning of the year, HRC prices averaged $640/ton (see

Figure 1

). Even though the Section 232 tariffs were not announced until early March and put into place on March 23, the domestic steel mills knew they were coming and pushed prices up. As of mid-April, HRC prices appeared to be peaking around $880/ton. Steel Market Update uses the term "peaking" with some trepidation, as we cannot assume a normal price cycle because of the government’s market interference.

"Our concern as fabricators is that by putting a tariff only on mill steel, you leave the domestic fabrication market vulnerable to increased foreign competition," said David Zalesne, chairman of the American Institute of Steel Construction (AISC). Zalesne also is president of Owen Steel Co., Columbia, S.C., one of the largest structural steel fabricators and contractors in the eastern U.S.

AISC appreciates the Trump administration tackling these important trade issues, Zalesne said, but would like to see fabricated steel products protected along with mill steel. Fabricated structural steel is a critical component of the infrastructure needed to safeguard the nation’s security and thus fits well under the Section 232 guidelines.

"The problem is, if you don’t have a tariff on fabricated steel or a ‘buy American’ plan, pretty much any fabricated product can enter the U.S. with no impact from the tariff," he said.

Testifying before Congress on behalf of the AISC in October, Zalesne noted that imports of fabricated steel products have spiked in the past three years despite historically high antidumping and countervailing duty orders on mill steel imports. Foreign steel companies have simply moved resources downstream into fabrication, bypassing U.S. duties and shipping foreign-fabricated steel directly to U.S. power plants, bridges, and high-rises.

"Imposing tariffs or quotas on mill steel might reduce imports of mill steel, but they do nothing to diminish imports of fabricated steel. To the contrary, imports of fabricated steel are rising, suggesting that the tariffs on mill steel are not only being circumvented, but that we are losing even more steel industry jobs as more value-added fabrication is done offshore," he testified.

Meanwhile, the threat of tariffs has driven steel prices sharply higher, which can affect fabricators in three ways, Zalesne said. For those with fixed price contracts who were counting on buying steel from offshore, the impact can be devastating. "There have been instances in the past where fabricators have gone out of business because of fixed price contracts and material escalation," he said.

In other scenarios, where projects are in the bidding stage, fabricators are scrambling to lock in material prices. That’s difficult, as prices can change significantly during a bidding process that can take months.

Finally, rising steel prices can affect future work as developers reconsider projects because of the added cost. "Right now, the market is good, and people are more likely to push ahead rather than put work on hold," he said, but construction demand can be fragile.

Zalesne said he remains hopeful the administration will eventually take steps to protect domestic fabricators.

"I think they understand and sympathize with our argument. I’d hate to see the market undermined by a well-intentioned policy. I just hope that whatever happens, they are paying attention, so they can take corrective action if necessary before it does too much harm."

Section 232 Update

Much of the impact from the Section 232 tariffs on steel and aluminum was blunted—at least temporarily—by the Trump administration’s decision to grant exemptions to many U.S. trading partners. Those exemptions were due to expire May 1. What happens after that remains unclear.

As it stands now, the 25 percent tariff on steel imports does not apply to Canada, Mexico, Australia, South Korea, Argentina, Brazil, and European Union countries. It does apply to imports from China, Russia, Japan, and Turkey. China has appealed the U.S. Section 232 action to the World Trade Organization and has issued $3 billion of retaliatory tariffs on goods from the U.S., which could be the initial salvo of a coming trade war.

The administration may reinstate the tariffs on some or all the countries next month and instead offer exemptions on a product-by-product basis. Since the exemptions were announced March 22, the U.S. Department of Commerce reportedly has received some 1,200 requests for product exclusions. Processing all those requests will take some time. Once a company’s request for an exemption is posted on the government website, that company will be eligible for a refund of any tariffs if the request is ultimately approved. If a shipment arrives before the exclusion request is posted, however, the duties will not be refunded.

The Trump administration is using the Section 232 tariffs as leverage in trade negotiations, notably with its NAFTA partners Canada and Mexico. Press reports indicate a deal may be struck within the next few weeks or at least ahead of the Mexico elections this summer.
