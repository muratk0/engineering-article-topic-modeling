# The big economic drivers

[TARİH: 01.10.2025 The Fabricator]

Fabrinomics | Economic Insights

Supply chain, workforce, money

Dr. Chris Kuehl

I have done my share of complaining about the way the general media covers economic issues. To be fair, the media has a challenge. Economics is a complex subject requiring detailed discussions about drivers and outcomes, but the average media consumer doesn’t have the patience for this. The result is a series of sound bites and a focus on whatever seems most current.

Zoom out, and you’ll soon realize that sound bites shout out attention-grabbing content devoid of context. To remedy this, here’s some context. The economy, especially now, has three major drivers, and getting a handle on current events means understanding all three.

First is the supply chain.

Everything revolves around getting things from place to place when needed and at a cost that is not prohibitive. The supply chain has been in chaos for several years, and there seems to be no end in sight. The progress toward a more responsive and predictable supply chain was interrupted by the pandemic as suddenly just-in-time became "eventually, maybe." This forced many companies back into extensive inventory management, and that is both expensive and highly volatile as there is either too little or too much inventory. Just about the time that companies dug out of the pandemic mess, we get the uncertainty that surrounds the onagain, off-again tariffs.

There is virtually no secure knowledge regarding the supply chain. Prices are volatile, and that has thrown the shipping companies into chaos. The price volatility has been unprecedented. That was made abundantly clear in the latest edition of the Forming and Fabricating Job Shop Consumption Report from the Fabricators and Manufacturers Association. The percentage of respondents reporting higher and unpredictable logistics costs was close to 100%.

The second major driver is the workforce.

The crisis has been inevitable and has been warned of for over a decade. Those economists who have asserted that 2030 will see the greatest recession since the 1930s are not forecasting as much as they are issuing a warning. By 2030 the entire Boomer generation—some 73 million people—will have reached retirement age; 21.7 million currently in the workforce are due to leave. There are no replacements.

The shortage is acute when it comes to the skilled workforce as training has been ignored for years. A shortage of even unskilled labor persists in sectors such as construction, manufacturing, and agriculture. Companies have been turning to technology (such as AI) as well as robotics, but these options are expensive.

Workforce participation levels are down to 62.3%—less than prior to the pandemic and close to all-time lows. Most of this decline is attributed to people electing to retire. To make matters even more complex, Gen-X workers are actually retiring at a faster pace than Boomers. The Boomer is reluctant to retire, either because they have to work or they just fear retirement. Gen Xers will benefit of the "great wealth transfer," as Boomers currently control 60% of the nation’s wealth and are seeking ways to disperse it before they lose it all to inheritance taxes.

The third driver is money.

Growth and expansion require investment, either from markets or lenders. Money for expansion is harder to get than ever as the lenders have become more cautious. The markets are certainly lively, but they have been dominated by the tech stocks as many of the others struggle. The huge and growing deficit has affected the bond market and has chased many corporate bonds out (as well as municipal bonds). Banks are tightening their standards as they do not have a good sense of what to expect in the coming quarters.

There has never been a point where there is total business stability. A study prepared for EY pointed out that over the last decade there has been a collective loss of some $360 billion due to uncertainty and disruption from factors such as the pandemic, wars, weather events, and now the tariff and trade wars. It has become very hard to plan or even to develop adequate contingency strategies.

Tariffs: Winners and Losers

For decades, the U.S. manufacturing community has struggled to be competitive against nations with much lower labor and production costs. For instance, China is a command economy rather than a market economy, and that gives it an advantage.

Tariffs and nontariff barriers are used to help U.S. companies compete. Therefore, the tariff "winners" are those that get some protection from competitors around the world. The challenge is that these protections are not enough to restore competitiveness. U.S. manufacturers have been demanding more labor training options, more investment in infrastructure, more research and development focus, and better tax and regulatory environments. These are much harder to develop than a simple adjustment to trade rules.

Then there are the tariff "losers," who are predominantly consumers. Very low tariffs on other nations were deliberately put in place decades ago to make it easier for U.S. consumers to get high-quality goods at a very low price. Today, the burden of the higher tariffs falls on consumers and any businesses that have been buying goods and services from those global producers. Inflation has been creeping up and is expected to accelerate through the year. Ideally, U.S.-based options will emerge, but that process takes time, and some products will never be appropriate for U.S. production because consumers will balk at paying the higher prices.

The idea behind the very low barriers to entry into the U.S. economy was to benefit American consumers. They would have access to the best variety of global products at very low prices. The world was crushed after World War II, and the U.S. did not fear that competition. Times changed, and that focus affected the ability of U.S. manufacturers to compete against rising economies such as China. Now the U.S. needs to bolster its productive capacity, and tariffs are tools to do that, but they are far from the most effective.

Construction: Manufacturing, Data Centers, Power

The latest data on construction spending (through July) showed some continued deceleration from month to month, and overall construction spending was down a modest 0.1%. Again, there are two different ways to look at the trends in the construction sector. First, there is some obvious deceleration almost across the board (most sectors are either slowing or flat). But second, almost every sector is still seeing two to four times more spending (even after inflation adjustments) versus periods before the pandemic.

According to one CFO of a major construction firm, current projects are fairly full; they are running about as fast as they can run, and much of it is coming in the manufacturing, data center, and energy (power) sectors. But he’s concerned about the pipeline—"a lot of lookers, but fewer buyers."

And if estimates are correct, at some point over the next two to three years, the $5 trillion in committed projects will drop on the U.S. That will usher in a host of challenges in finding enough labor and materials, but we don’t see it yet. Should construction firms take on smaller projects to fill the pipeline in 2026 (knowing that they’ll run their crew’s capacity tight and not be able to take on new projects) or to wait for some of these more lucrative projects to manifest and then compete for those? That’s a difficult dilemma.

The star on the nonresidential side continues to be manufacturing and industrial construction. It is still showing more than $223 billion in annual spending (versus $60 billion on average in the decade before the pandemic).

Power generation construction has also shot up. We all know the headline data that we must double U.S. electricity output by the early 2030s to keep pace with demand. Some civil projects and spending are still strong, but that could be tied to the infrastructure bill and some strong state-level spending that could ease in the coming years. Budget allocations across many states will get tighter, and without a civil spending stimulus of some type, it may be limited.

Spending in commercial and office spaces is still sluggish, and health care is limping along. Most of these sectors are still waiting for interest rates to come down and some of the geographic expansion trends (like migration) to accelerate. Our forecast shows the sluggish, flat situation that we are currently working through.

When we play out scenarios against this set of estimates, there really is no way to model what $5 trillion jammed into 2026 and 2027 would do to the sector. We just don’t have any prior experience to equate to that prospect. We would have to go back to World War II, proportionately, to find a comparable period. But in non-wartime, it’s unprecedented.

Manufacturers Power Ahead

The long-term trend for manufacturing is very solid. If certain variables improve—including promises of reshoring, energy development and further evolution of factory automation—we could see spectacular growth. Of course, manufacturers still need skilled workers to make such spectacular growth a reality.

Throughout the tariff battle the manufacturing sector has been able to show growth, and this should not come as a great shock. After all, the whole idea of the tariff regime was to promote and protect the U.S. industrial sector. This emphasis has come at a cost to consumers as prices rise, but the export sector has reacted to more demand for U.S. output while competition from other nations has dimmed.
