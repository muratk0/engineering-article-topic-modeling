# Do we need a dose of economic reality?

[TARİH: 01.07.2024 The Fabricator]

Fabrinomics | Economic Insights

Some insights from the ‘dismal scientist’

Dr. Chris Kuehl

Throughout 2023, most expected an imminent recession. The consensus view among economists was that growth would fall into negative territory—but it never did.

At the start of this year, the assumption was that this party was going to continue, and then Q1 growth disappointed and came in at 1.6%. Is there reason to think that recession expected last year might now manifest? Not to be a true "dismal scientist," but there are indeed some concerns.

Politics, Interest Rates, and People

Let’s start with the election, as controversial a topic as this can be. If there is a consensus view among voters and the business community in general, it is that these are both relatively weak candidates. There are concerns regarding the proposed Democratic agenda and concerns that Republicans will focus on social issues and personal conflicts. There does not seem to be any sort of strategic plan to deal with key business issues such as the worker shortage, infrastructure deterioration, regulation, taxation, or inflation reduction.

The second major concern has been vexing for the last year or more: What will happen with interest rates? They will come down eventually, but when and by how much? Some assert this happens by Q3 or Q4 of this year; others think Q1 of 2025 is more likely. Some think there will be three quarter-point cuts, and others believe there will be just one. Many projects, expansions, and acquisitions are more or less on hold as companies wait to determine the costs of financing. If the rates do come down, the costs will be reduced, so it makes sense to wait.

The third area of concern is also a familiar one, but nothing substantial has been done to address these issues, and they are all reaching a critical stage. For a decade there have been complaints about the worker shortage, and politicians have done nothing more than wring their hands. Boomers are retiring at a rate of 10,000 a day. With nobody to replace them, productivity is slumping.

The U.S. still leads the world in terms of GDP per hour worked, but this has been due to the adoption of technology and robotics. That lead has started to erode as key people leave. The national infrastructure is ranked no higher than C- by the American Society of Civil Engineers. The problems are most acute when it comes to energy production, water management, and transportation. The poor infrastructure costs the economy billions every month. Major changes in spending priorities must take place at the federal and state level, but they are not even on the agenda.

These are solvable problems at this stage, but solving them is going to require coordination and effort. In the current political environment, that cooperation is not evident.

PMI and Industrial Capacity

The level of industrial capacity has been very steady, and that has been the good news. The bad news is that it has been steady at a lower level than would be considered ideal. The system still has some slack. This means machines and technology are not being used to their preferred level, and the workforce is not as productive as it could be. Ideal industrial capacity readings are between 80% and 85%. The EU is having the same issue, with a reading of 79.10. They were above 80 for most of 2023 but have been sliding since.

Meanwhile, the U.S. had managed to claw its way back to expansion territory with the purchasing managers index (PMI), but by April and May the data had retreated to contraction with readings of 49.2 and 48.7, respectively. To be honest, the changes are not all that significant given the narrow margins between low 50s and high 40s, but there is a psychological element to consider. When the data slips below the 50-line, the sense is that there has been some kind of slowdown in the overall industrial sector.

Other data points show that the reduction in activity is not connected to some of the more critical areas, such as automotive, machinery, or any of the high-tech areas. The sector that has shown the most distinct reduction has been aerospace, and there has been some reduction in the contribution from the defense sector.

Fragility of the Energy Market

Then we have the never-ending drama of the energy market. When Russia invaded Ukraine, Europe reacted by cutting off Russia’s access to the European market. Russia now sells more oil and natural gas than it did before the Ukrainian war—but now it’s going to China, India, and other nations that did not subscribe to the sanctions.

Meanwhile, the U.S. became highly dependent on other sources of that gas and oil. The spike in prices at the time of the sanctions threatened Europe with a brutal recession as it was expected to make energy too expensive. The U.S. and other suppliers beefed up their activity, and the crisis was averted.

Overall, energy prices globally have been stable. The per-barrel price for oil has remained in the 80s for the most part, but there have been many episodes when that price jumped into the 90s, and analysts are still insisting that prices will reach back above $100 a barrel. Demand from the U.S. and Europe has been increasing back to pre-pandemic levels, and China is starting to show renewed economic growth. That alone will put pressure on oil prices.

Add in all the moves designed to reduce the consumption of oil, and the price pressure could reappear quickly. Many governments are now adding to that pressure as they want to push electric vehicles and alternative power supplies. Driving up the price of fossil fuel is a way to push that harder.

Some Economic Indicators Weakening

The Conference Board’s Leading Economic Index is supposed to give us a reading on the economy over the next six months. It had dipped to levels similar to those seen during the 2001 recession, though it has since improved a bit. The reason why is a little interesting and scary at the same time. First, the LEI has been predictive of economic cycles throughout history, until now. For whatever reason, it isn’t as predictive as it has been in the past 40 years. The data from the Conference Board shows the current LEI level, and in previous cycles, the economy would have been in recession. The fact that we have dipped below that level and are now recovering is interesting—and perplexing. The bottom line is that the LEI is still showing the economy "underweight."

The second data set shows something even more interesting, and it involves the components of the LEI and the weight they are contributing to the current LEI level. The interesting (and scary) thing about the current reading is that it is being driven by the S&P 500. If we have any weakness in the stock market, the LEI will plummet. Now, the LEI’s reading that strength in the S&P 500 likely means that those companies are doing well, consumers are spending, and companies are investing. But in this cycle, it could be different. A few stocks are driving the S&P 500 right now, and we see some general stability across the broader set of stocks.

Good news comes in the Leading Credit Index, showing that credit availability is improving. We saw that generally in the Fed’s data on the percentage of banks tightening credit standards, and they are improving. Many banks set aside funds on concerns that mortgage and home equity loan defaults might accelerate. And despite rising auto and credit card defaults, the overall risk to the banking sector is still low. That’s why many are easing their lending standards and are looking for opportunities to lend at these higher rates.

The Conference Board mentioned some deterioration in consumer sentiment, new manufacturing orders, lower building permits, and some deterioration in the interest rate spread (10-year Treasury bonds less the Fed Funds Rate). This still does not mean that the economy is headed into recession, or that it is in one now. But it does mean that there are headwinds to overcome, and right now it is hanging on the labor and stock markets.

It would be very helpful if all the data lined up and told a consistent story, but that is never the case. There is enough to gladden the hearts of the "glass half full" crowd as well as depress the "glass half empty" contingent. Enough has changed in the last few years that the usual indicators are not telling the whole story.
