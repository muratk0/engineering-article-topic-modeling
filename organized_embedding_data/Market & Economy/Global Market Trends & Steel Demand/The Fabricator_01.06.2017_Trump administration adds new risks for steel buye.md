# Trump administration adds new risks for steel buyers

A Section 232 investigation and complaints issued against China and Vietnam cause uncertainty for steel prices in the near term

W

e are now into mid-May 2017, and Steel Market Update (SMU) has been hearing the word

risk

bantered about by many within the steel industry. How one perceives the risks associated with buying and selling decisions is one of the factors determining the direction of flat-rolled steel prices which, based on our analysis of the market, are moving lower.

The concern is how much longer flat-rolled steel prices will slip and what issues exist that are out of the control of steel buyers. The latter helps to create doubts about a stable market ahead.

A good example is the Section 232 investigation called for by the Trump administration. (Under Section 232 of the Trade Expansion Act of 1962, the secretary of commerce can be authorized to conduct investigations into imports of any kind that may have an effect on national security.) Once the Department of Commerce finishes the investigation, sometime between now and the maximum allowable time, which is 270 days, it will make recommendations to the president as to what should be done next. Comments have been attributed to Secretary Wilbur Ross that the investigation will be finished sooner rather than later because of the deep cache of antidumping and countervailing duty trade cases filed by the steel industry over the past few years.

Some people are concerned about the Section 232 investigation.

"There is a dark cloud hanging over this market," one service center buyer said recently, "and it will continue until we hear about these trade cases."

The issue the quoted service center has is no one knows how this review will play out and what would happen to foreign steel should the government decide that

something

must be done to protect the domestic steel industry. What would that something be? How soon would it take effect? Who will be impacted?

"All the mills want to talk about now is Section 232, and it’s starting to become annoying," another large national service center said. "They are pounding the drums and speaking as if the outcome in their favor is a foregone conclusion, and that buyers will be surprised when they can’t get all of the steel they need. There’s very little sensitivity or understanding being demonstrated by mill personnel (in public or in private) of the very real potential for negative impacts and fallout on users of metal in the U.S., depending on the outcomes of the cases. They seem quite myopic in their views, which is unfortunate, as most in the collective industry understand and appreciate what true foreign dumping is, versus a conjured-up need driven from an archaic law, to suit other purposes."

Then we add into the mix the Circumvention Complaint against Vietnam and China on cold-rolled and coated steels where, once again, there is no sure answer as to what conclusion the U.S. government will come to. One of the key components to the circumvention case is the long-standing international understanding known as

substantial transformation

. Substantial transformation occurs when substrate from one country is converted through the rolling process to another product. As a result, the product reference code (Harmonized Tarriff Schedule [HTS] code) changes and, simultaneously, the country of origin also changes.

So, if Chinese hot-rolled is converted to cold-rolled or cold-rolled becomes a coated product in Vietnam, the HTS changes to the new product, which then determines the country of origin. This works for any product from any country, and it has been accepted by the international community for decades.

Should the U.S. government rule that the cost and investment to produce the base substrate are significantly higher than any further material transformation and that the true country of origin would be the site of the original material manufacturer, this would turn the international trade community on its head. It means those end users who are purchasing foreign steel either for competitive reasons or cost reasons could be in for a huge surprise as well!

We don’t know if that is going to happen. The issue being discussed here is the risk that must be weighed by anyone doing business with any country that converts substrate from other countries with antidumping and countervailing duties (AD/CVD rulings) against them.

We already have AD/CVD rulings on hot-rolled, cold-rolled, and coated products (such as galvanized and Galvalume). Those rulings had an impact on imports of foreign hot-rolled, which have dropped dramatically over the past year. They also have helped to boost the price of domestic coldrolled and coated steels, which are now selling at a steep premium when compared to base hot-rolled ($200 per ton). The normal spread between coldrolled/coated and hot-rolled base pricing has historically been $100-$120 per ton.

Then there is the question that Steel Market Update raised last month ("Trump administration targets manufacturing and steel trade,"

The FABRICATOR

, p. 32): How much inventory of carbon flat-rolled is really being held by the U.S. steel service centers?

As I was preparing this article, I spoke to many service center steel buyers. A service center in the Southwest told me, "I’m struggling to read the market. I heard lead times are out more than what I am seeing, and I am not seeing the offers I have seen in the past. With that said, how is the market justifying the decrease in base for June from the mills? Not that I’m complaining, just struggling to understand the dynamics."

A large service center in the Southeast said, "Lead times are pretty stable, but the level of mills willing to participate is really mixed. Some of the mills are full and very bullish, largely due to their downstream operations. That has left some gaps at other mills that we must wait and see if energy fills. Unfortunately, with automotive starting to slow down and energy is waking up slowly, some mills are starting to get more aggressive. However, aggressive pricing is not generating any business that we can tell—certainly not here. He’re in a wait-and-see mode."

He added, "There are too many mixed messages in the market to get a strong idea about where things are headed. Based on that, we are sitting on the sidelines. We’d rather miss a little downside than underestimate how far or the timing of when pricing will fall. The market is going to soften, but we are still optimistic that the market won’t overreact. Overall fundamentals are still relatively strong, and there are some good ‘events’ that could happen."

Ultimately, everything revolves around price. Will prices drop below today’s levels, and for how long will the slide last? Or will prices reverse course and move up? There are more things that buyers need to consider:

Lead times

Competitive pressures

Inventory levels

How their customers are reacting to market events

How much steel is available

What "real" offers for pricing are out there

Each risk must be analyzed and understood, and buyers are having a tough time with risks like the Section 232 that can’t be quantified easily.
