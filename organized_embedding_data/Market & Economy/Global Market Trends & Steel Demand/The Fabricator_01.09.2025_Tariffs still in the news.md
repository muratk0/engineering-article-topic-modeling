# Tariffs still in the news

[TARİH: 01.09.2025 The Fabricator]

Around Washington

Reciprocal tariff rate adjustments made; tariffs slapped on copper products

OMAR NASHASHIBI

O

n Aug. 7, the U.S. executed its updated reciprocal tariff schedule, concluding months of negotiations and policy recalibration. The revised measures, now in force, impose duties between 10% and 41% on a country-specific basis. Among the notable entries are 15% for the E.U., U.K., Japan, and South Korea; 20% for Taiwan; and 39% for Switzerland. India currently faces a 25% reciprocal tariff rate and a possible additional 25% for purchasing Russian oil.

These discussions allowed certain trading partners to secure lower tariff rates than initially proposed, in exchange for commitments on market access, regulatory alignment, and strategic cooperation.

Administration officials characterized the final rates as "balanced enforcement," designed to preserve U.S. leverage while mitigating disruption for industries reliant on foreign inputs. Customs and Border Protection began applying the new tariffs at 12:01 a.m. EDT on Aug. 7, with transit exemptions in place for qualifying shipments.

As of that time, the U.S. did not announce relief for any of the countries from the 50% tariff on imported steel, aluminum, and copper derivatives. Sources in Washington indicate that the Trump administration is considering a tariff rate quota for the metals from some countries, allowing a set level of imports at a lower rate before increasing to the current 50% rate.

U.S. Imposes 50% Tariffs on Copper Products

President Donald Trump has signed a proclamation under Section 232 imposing a 50% tariff on the copper content of semifinished products such as pipes, wires, rods, sheets, and tubes, along with derivative goods like fittings, cables, connectors, and electrical components. The duties took effect Aug. 1. Non-copper materials in these products will be assessed under other applicable tariffs, including reciprocal rates, and automotive Section 232 products will continue under the auto tariff instead. This measure reflects findings from a Section 232 investigation affirming copper dependency as a national security risk.

The tariff rollout comes with a long list of exemptions. Unprocessed copper inputs—ores, concentrates, mattes, cathodes, and anodes—are outside the scope, as is scrap copper. But the Department of Commerce will reassess cathode and anode imports by mid-2026, and if supply chain vulnerabilities persist, refined copper could face phased tariffs of 15% in 2027 and 30% in 2028. Scrap may be tariff-free, but exporters will need to meet a new rule requiring that 25% of high-quality scrap be sold domestically, with an export licensing system under consideration.

The action also includes a domestic sales mandate for U.S.-produced copper. Starting in 2027, a quarter of all ores, concentrates, cathodes, and anodes made in the U.S. must be sold there, rising to 30% in 2028 and 40% in 2029. Tariffs paid under the proclamation are not eligible for duty drawback, and the non-copper component of any product remains under its original tariff regime.

In the coming 90 days, the Commerce Department will decide if other copper derivatives should be added to the list while CBP prepares new classification and compliance rules under the Harmonized Tariff Schedule. The Commerce Department also will begin accepting submissions to potentially expand tariff coverage by late October.

EPA Proposes Big Changes

The Environmental Protection Agency is moving to dismantle the 2009 Endangerment Finding—the legal cornerstone that has underpinned federal climate regulations for over a decade. Announced by Administrator Lee Zeldin on July 30 and formally published in the Federal Register on Aug. 1, the proposed rule also would repeal greenhouse gas emissions standards for light-, medium-, and heavy-duty vehicles. The EPA frames the plan as a "historic deregulation effort," projecting billions in savings for consumers and small businesses while delivering significant regulatory relief.

Central to the proposal is the agency’s reinterpretation of the Clean Air Act’s Section 202(a). The EPA contends the statute does not authorize regulations targeting pollutants solely for their role in global climate change and argues that the scientific models used in the original 2009 finding no longer justify its conclusions. The agency also points to recent court rulings, including the expansion of the "major questions" doctrine, as grounds for reexamining its authority. The public has until Sept. 15 to submit comments, following the August virtual hearings.

Reactions have broken sharply along political and regional lines. Environmental advocates, climate scientists, and Democratic-led states—with California vowing to maintain its own emissions rules—have condemned the move as a dangerous retreat from urgent climate action. By contrast, some business organizations, industry groups, and Republican lawmakers have celebrated the proposal as a long-overdue check on regulatory overreach, insisting the Clean Air Act was never intended to serve as a vehicle for global climate policy.

With court challenges all but certain, the measure’s fate may hinge on how judges weigh evolving scientific evidence against statutory interpretation. Any litigation could take years to resolve, injecting uncertainty into auto industry planning, state climate programs, and the broader trajectory of U.S. emissions policy. If upheld, the rescission would mark one of the most consequential reversals of federal climate authority since the Massachusetts v. EPA decision opened the door to such regulations in 2007.
