# Is recession imminent?

[TARİH: 01.08.2022 The Fabricator]

Economic Insights

FABRINOMICS

A

re we on the edge of recession? This has become the paramount economic question of the day and it would be splendid indeed if there was a simple answer to this simple question. There isn’t. The latest survey of some 50 economists by the Financial Times and the Booth School at the University of Chicago indicates that the majority of them are of the opinion that a recession will hit by the second quarter (or maybe third) of 2023. Are they correct? One thing to recognize about economists and their predictions is that we have successfully forecast 13 of the last 3 recessions. The reality is that we have a hard time predicting next week, much less a year from now. What looks like a forecast is more of a warning. The economist is asserting that if certain changes are not made the economy will tip into some kind of crisis — a recession or an inflation surge or that miserable combination of the two which is referred to as stagflation. The assertion that recession is imminent is best taken as that kind of warning because we are clearly not in one right now.

The logic behind predicting a 2023 recession is clear enough. A very high rate of inflation triggers the central banks (including the Federal Reserve) to react with higher interest rates. The intent is to cool off the economy by restricting the money supply. Banks will presumably reduce lending and that affects business expansion. It is a very indirect process and it takes time. If there is a great deal of extra cash floating around, businesses will keep expanding, consumers will keep spending, and inflation will continue to build. That would encourage the central banks to hike rates even higher and faster. The analysts are looking at the inflation numbers and concluding that interest rates will have to hit high levels if they are to have an impact on the economy.

The Fed (and the other central banks) have been making a point of describing where the inflation pressure is coming from and that could make a significant difference as far as their tactics are concerned. This is not a money supply issue, or at least that is not the primary driver. In 2021 it could be asserted there was way too much cash in circulation as the U.S. economy was growing at nearly 6.0% and that is easily twice as fast as normal. At the start of the year, there was almost $5 trillion in extra savings for the consumer to work with. The stimulus in 2020 backfired in many ways. The consumer received a surge of money to offset the ravages of the shutdown but that very set of closures kept the consumer from spending and savings rates skyrocketed. We normally save at around a 7.0% rate but in the 2nd quarter of 2020, we hit 38.0%. That cash started to hit in 2021 and set up issues like the supply chain breakdown, labor shortage, and overall inflation. The good news was that U.S. consumers are really good at consumption and we blew through the majority of that overhang by the end of the year. So, what is now driving this 40-year high when it comes to inflation?

To the surprise of few, the culprits are Russia and China. The Ukraine invasion was shocking and an unmitigated disaster for the Russians (as well as the Ukrainian people). The western reaction was to launch a financial attack on Russia with sanctions. These have essentially excluded the Russians from the energy and food markets and the world is now facing extremely high prices for oil, natural gas, wheat, corn, and other commodities. By some accounts, the high price of fuel has accounted for 70% of the inflation the world now contends with. The Chinese have insisted on dealing with the pandemic through a policy of zero-tolerance despite the abundant evidence that this strategy doesn’t work and it destroys the economy of the country in the process. Billions of dollars of production have been lost or delayed and that supply chain disaster courses through the entire global economy. There are severe shortages where there shouldn’t be, and shortages create the ideal conditions for inflation. There are only two responses to shortage — either a given product is simply not available at all or the price of that scarce commodity rises. A fundamental principle of economics is that scarcity results in high prices — whether that scarcity is natural (rarity of something like gold) or artificial (supply chain breakdown). The upshot is that central banks do not have the tools to deal with this kind of inflation and that means they are reluctant to push rates too hard and too fast. If they remain cautious the chances of an interest rate-triggered recession diminish. On the other hand, the inflation impact continues as there is no solution other than ending the Ukraine war, lowering sanctions on Russian energy, and convincing the Chinese to reopen its ports.
