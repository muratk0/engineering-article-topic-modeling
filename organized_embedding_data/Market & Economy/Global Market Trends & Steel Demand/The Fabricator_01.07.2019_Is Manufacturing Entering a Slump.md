In true economic style, the answer to this question is maybe. Certainly some indicators have been causing concern — most notably the latest Purchasing Managers’ Index (PMI

®

) from the Institute for Supply Management. In June it tracked at 52.1, and that is the lowest reading seen since 2016. This is the lowest level since President Trump took office, and there has been an attempt to link this steady decline to the policies that have emerged under this administration. In truth, there are likely several reasons for the reduction in growth, the White House policies on trade and tariffs being just one of them.

The consensus view among most economic analysts is that manufacturing has slowed in the U.S. because of problems in other parts of the world. In the past few years, the U.S. has regained a lot of its manufacturing reputation, and this sector has gained in prominence as far as the national GDP is concerned. Manufacturing is worth over $2.7 trillion in GDP, making manufacturing GDP larger than the total GDP of Brazil or Canada. This has been accomplished in large part through expanded exports. It has been estimated that manufactured product exports account for roughly 6 percent of the U.S. economy. That doesn’t sound like much, but it amounts to $1.2 trillion.

The threat to manufacturing now is that markets overseas have started to fade. Europe is facing anemic growth levels of less than 1.0 percent, and Japan is still in economic doldrums. Plus, many nations that traditionally buy from the U.S. sell to China, and as the trade wars and tariffs affect Chinese growth, they also affect the growth of those that depend on that Chinese demand.

To make matters worse, the U.S. dollar continues to gain strength as the Federal Reserve is about the only central bank to have hiked interest rates. The tariff wars have had a direct impact, as for every tariff imposed by the U.S. a countertariff has been imposed, which means less buying from the U.S. Most of the countertariffs coming from China have affected U.S. manufactured goods. The bottom line for U.S. manufacturing is that its recovery continues to depend on what happens around the globe.
