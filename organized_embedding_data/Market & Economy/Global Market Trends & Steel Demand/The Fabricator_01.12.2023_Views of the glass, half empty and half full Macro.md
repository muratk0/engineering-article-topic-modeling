# Views of the glass, half empty and half full: Macroeconomic insights for 2024

[TARİH: 01.12.2023 The Fabricator]

Fabrinomics | Economic Insights

Dr. Chris Kuehl

It would be tempting to say that one’s outlook depends on whether one is a "glass half full" or "glass half empty" kind of person as there is evidence for both a gloomy outcome as well as continued recession escape.

There is certainly a significant level of good (and unexpected) news given the sharp hike in the Q3 GDP numbers, but there are issues looming on the not-distant horizon, and there are many sectors deciding to start retreating in anticipation of a slower second half in 2024. That said, the data still shows that industrial production has been staying well above the 20-year trend line. The concern is that the dip starts in early 2024 and takes the numbers close to that trend line. If the inventory-to-sales ratio improves, the reorder cycle will resume normal levels by the second quarter, but that depends on consumers gobbling up that inventory.

Optimists see opportunity and pessimists see storm clouds. As the following points show, there’s good reason to listen to them both.

Inflation and Energy Prices

. Some volatile factors seem to be controlling the pace of economic expansion, most notably oil and gas. The primary reason that inflation has been falling is a drop in energy costs — especially as compared to what was expected just a few months ago. At the time of this writing, the per-barrel price of West Texas Intermediate was in the low 80s, and even Brent crude was only in the mid-80s. Should these prices spike, as many assumed they would when the Hamas war started, the pressure on consumers will be significant. For now, consumption has been propelling growth in the U.S.

Effects of Friend-Shoring

. This refers to the desire of companies to avoid geopolitical inhibitions to trade. Most of the attention has been focused on China, but the concept can easily be extended to any relationship between nations that can impact trade.

Companies doing business in China have to navigate an immensely complex series of restrictions on what can and can’t be sold and what can be purchased. Tariffs and regulatory demands add costs to every transaction. These political considerations also involve dealing with Russia, Iran, Cuba, North Korea, Nicaragua, Venezuela, and many others. Sanctions and trade restrictions are in lieu of open warfare or other hostile gestures, but they have their costs.

For the most part, these costs are heaped on companies and, ultimately, consumers, who pay more for the products and services affected.

The European Central Bank polled 65 companies and discovered that 45 of them have deliberately shifted business to nations considered "friendly" to Europe as a whole, and specifically to their own nation. More important, 60% report that efforts to avoid potentially hostile trading partners had driven costs up significantly—often by more than 10%. The majority now attribute this pattern to the rise in inflation, which is more influential than other supply chain issues. It’s about restricted competition. Those companies in "friendly" nations know they have an advantage over companies in "unfriendly" locations, and they exploit that advantage.

Wage Growth

. The Fed is watching the relationship between inflation and wage growth closely. Lending Club surveys show that 60% of U.S. households are living paycheck to paycheck, with more than 20% of those households struggling to pay essential bills. Until Q3 of this year, inflation had been outpacing wage growth for nearly nine months; that has finally inverted.

Wage growth averaged 2.5% in the decade prior to the pandemic, and the Fed knows that it will eventually slow to that 2.5% pace. It must get inflation to its 2% target rate to ensure that households can build discretionary income. Wages now outpace inflation, but both are still too high. This will keep the Fed "higher for longer."

Interest Rate Hikes Peak?

The Fed may be nearing its peak hike cycle period; that is, we might be nearing the end of rate hikes. Rate reductions are still not in the cards until at least the end of Q2 in 2024, if not extending to the end of 2024. Much of that is scenario-specific. Consumer health is also a concern. Current consumer spending is strong, even when adjusted for inflation. But it is being focused largely on essential items; discretionary spending is weaker based on data through September.

Many factors will shape a decision to stop future hikes, and with so much global uncertainty, it is difficult to know if the Fed is truly finished hiking. The concern looking forward is that the full impact of this entire hike cycle has not been fully realized. Much of the pressure in the financial markets, bank credit tightening, and interest rate impacts on consumer spending have yet to be fully felt.

U.S. Retail Outlook Strong

. Consumer spending accounts for 70% of GDP and forms the core of U.S. economic activity. Retail has been strong—much stronger than was anticipated and that is both a blessing and curse. It has been referred to as the "Wil-E-Coyote" effect. Despite outside pressures, consumers are still spending, and their actions defy predictions. If they realize they have run off the cliff (Wil-ECoyote style), there could be an abrupt drop.

Despite all this, the retail sales outlook models show that retail spending is forecasted to remain strong through the end of 2023 and into 2024. According to our current outlook, retail spending will grow by 2.5% in Q3 and 2% in Q4. For the full year 2023, that would put growth at roughly 6.7%. For 2024, the retail sales forecast remains firm, but growth slows to 3.59%. The long-term curve shows retail sales setting new highs in 2024.

Current models are seeing no consumer recession. For now, the forecast shows no soft periods on the horizon. Job openings remain high, unemployment is low, wage growth is stable at 4.1% (if not too hot), and inflation is cooling. All of those factors would feed consumer income and fuel spending. Offsetting that is of course higher interest rates for interest-bearing purchases, higher debt loads for middle- and lower-income households, and weaker consumer sentiment.

An Evolving Debt Landscape

. There was a lot of fear about the restarting of student loan payments in October. Surveys suggested that between 40% and 50% of borrowers were saying they may default on those repayments rather than selling vehicles or products holding other forms of debt that they took on over the past two years. Although bank earnings reports are slow in coming, it looks like defaults are rising in the last quarter, but not beyond bank expectations.

Several major banking groups reported that data showed some early repayments starting in July, and estimates suggested that as much as 50% of those impacted by student loan repayments may have already taken it upon themselves to restart. Those restarting loan payments may not have a big impact on consumer spending after all. That is also why Q4 spending estimates are coming in more stable than expected.

Inventory Reset After Q4?

The manufacturing sector is critically reliant upon getting inventory overstocks sold through. If consumers continue to spend and shift more spending away from essentials and into discretionary items, they might help reset the entire global supply chain for the first time since Q2 of 2022. Again, sell-through during the peak retail season is critical in getting new orders restarted, the rebuilding of raw materials, rehiring in the manufacturing and wholesale trade sector, use of energy, and the restarting transportation and logistics services.

About 64% of the manufacturing, moving, and selling market is overstocked relative to where they were prior to the pandemic. We believe that those inventory "resets" will take place after Q4. The global supply chain will see some general improvement across the board in the first half of 2024, and companies will move back into a continuous replenishment strategy of inventory management.

Industrial Production Outlook Softens

. The outlook for overall U.S. industrial production in manufacturing includes both durable and nondurable goods. The latest model showed some slight softening in the tail-end of the curve, with the second half of 2024 coming in weaker under a new set of scenarios. The model is now showing full-year growth for 2023 at 3.97%. The 2024 outlook improved slightly but remains weak with the full year contracting at an estimated 2.18% Y/Y rate. This view strips out the utilities and mining portion of industrial production and focuses solely on manufacturing activity.

The longer-term view is still showing a more moderate dip on a historical basis (an even shallower recession), but overall production appears to stay just below the long-term trend line. These models are updated monthly and there appears to be a downside bias in the data for the second half of 2024. Some durable sectors are seeing strong new order demand, while some nonessential nondurable and durable categories are weaker.

Defense Spending’s Positive Impact

. It’s not an all-handson-deck, Rosie-the-Riveter moment from World War II, but we also don’t know that it won’t be. NATO and U.S. officials have asked the defense sectors to ramp up production of all munitions and other defense supplies. Countries on both sides of these conflicts are ramping up production of defense goods, and the competition for raw materials that go into those products is also ramping up. This spreads to aid materials and humanitarian supplies to support several million people.

With two major ongoing conflicts in two theaters and some challenges surfacing in the Baltics, Armenia and Azerbaijan, the Pacific Rim, and other regions, demand for warfighting materials is growing quickly. The U.S. and every country impacted by the various conflicts around the world are ramping up production of key military support products. This spans across weaponry to also include supply chain and troop support products (food, clothing, gear, transportation). Defense aircraft spending is up 13% Y/Y, and other defense new orders are up 15.6%. This is based on procurements made thus far and would not include additional appropriations that are currently in front of Congress. We will continue to watch this sector as well as other forms of government spending covered under the CHIPs Act, Inflation Reduction Act, and infrastructure, all of which will see spending increases in 2024.
