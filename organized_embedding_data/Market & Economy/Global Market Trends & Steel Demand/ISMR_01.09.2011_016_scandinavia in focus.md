# SCANDINAVIA IN FOCUS

[TARİH: 01.09.2011 ISMR]

ISMR analyses economic indicators in the Scandinavian market.

Thanks to their shared traditions and history, Scandinavians have a similar way of life. With the exception of Norway, all the countries making up Scandinavia are members of the EU.

After recovering from the economic crisis at the start of the 1990s, the Scandinavian economy enjoyed an economic boom, driven principally by the IT sector, although natural resources such as oil, fish and timber have also contributed to the success of these Northern European countries. A marketing strategy tailored to Scandinavian culture and tastes brought economic success to Northern Europe across a wide range of sectors including food, clothing, textiles, domestic appliances, motor vehicles, pharmaceutical and technical products.

The global economic downturn of 2008/9 took its toll on the Scandinavian economy but Sweden, in particular, appears to have largely recovered from its ravages, showing impressive improvement in 2010, falls in unemployment and GDP growth of 5.5 per cent. European stainless steel order intake increased significantly during Q1 2011 in most of the Scandinavian countries.

## ECONOMIC INDICATORS Norway

Norway is richly endowed with natural resources – petroleum, hydropower, fish, forests and minerals – and is highly dependent on its oil production and international oil prices, with oil and gas accounting for a large proportion of exports.

In March 2009, the Government presented a new national transport plan (NTP) which sets out strategies for Norway’s transport policy for the next ten years. The new transport plan has an overall framework of NOK 321.9 billion for the whole period. That is 45 per cent higher than NTP 2006 – 2015, and entails an annual increase in funding for road transport of 39 per cent, 58 per cent for rail transport and 77 per cent for sea transport.

Major investments are planned in transport corridors that connect Norway directly with international markets. NOK 24.3 billion will be allocated for the development of roads and railways to link Oslo with the other Nordic capitals.

“The economic situation in Norway today is significantly brighter than one year ago. Active use of public funding to stabilise the economy helped stop the Norwegian economy from falling into a deep recession in 2009. The decline in business investments on mainland Norway came to a halt, and showed an increase in 2010. Household real income is increasing and makes an important contribution to further growth in private consumption,” the Veidekke group told ISMR.

“GDP for mainland Norway rose by 2.2 per cent from 2009 to 2010. The level of activity was significantly higher in the second half of 2010 than the first half, which gives a good impetus into 2011. On the whole, the prospects are for growth in the Norwegian economy for 2011 and 2012.

“The oil investment survey shows that oil investments fell more than expected in 2010, and are down to a low level. This growth will probably recover strongly. Together with other investment plans, increased building and construction activities and low interest rates, the total investments in the mainland economy will increase by up to 5 per cent this year and next year. The GDP growth for mainland Norway is expected to rise by about 3 per cent in both 2011 and 2012.”

According to Darmstahl Stainless Steel Solutions, the Norwegian stainless steel market is still missing strong demand stimulus from consumers – however, it added that increasing oil prices, new projects in the oil and gas business as well as economic growth will lead to a substantial improvement in the next few months. According to Veidekke, the key points for growth in 2011 are:

■ 3 per cent GDP growth in 2011.

■ Weak decline in unemployment.

■ Market interest rates of about 4 per cent at the end of the year.

■ An increase of up to 5 per cent in construction investments in 2011.

## ECONOMIC INDICATORS Sweden

Aided by peace and neutrality for the whole of the 20th century, Sweden has achieved an enviable standard of living under a mixed system of high-tech capitalism, a modern distribution system and a skilled labour force. Timber, hydropower and iron ore constitute the resource base of an economy heavily oriented towards foreign trade.

“Recently-published statistics show that the Swedish economy is continuing to expand rapidly. In Q4 2010, the GDP grew by 1.2 per cent, compared with Q3. The annual growth is up to 7.3 per cent. Domestic demand has now taken over as a growth engine in the Swedish economy. Private consumption was 0.5 percentage points and investments were 0.3 percentage points of the growth in the last quarter, while net exports showed a decline of 0.1 percentage points,” Veidekke told ISMR.

“The average growth in GDP in Sweden was 5.5 per cent. For the year as a whole, industrial production and exports contributed to the growth, while domestic demand has now taken over as the most important growth factor. There are still signs that point in the direction of slowing the growth. We now see a weaker development in retail trade and intermediate goods. Households’ trust in Sweden’s economy is still strong, but increasing interest rates, rising prices and increasing housing costs may limit the growth in disposable income.

“Throughout 2010, the confidence indicators for the Swedish economy pointed upwards. Export is also continuing to grow, at the same time that there are signs that the restoration phase after the recession is nearing an end. There is therefore reason to assume that the strong growth we have seen during the past year will decline somewhat both this year and next year. The GDP growth for 2011 is assumed to be about 4.5 per cent, dropping to 4 per cent in 2012. Financial activity will still remain at a high level. Public finances are still developing very positively as a result of the fast restoration of the economy and earlier reforms.”

According to Darmstahl Stainless Steel Solutions, Sweden’s stainless steel market will most likely grow stronger in 2011 than in 2010. The automotive sector is back on track (passenger cars / trucks). Subcontractors in the automotive sector are currently increasing staff again after heavy layoffs in 2009. According to Veidekke, the key points for growth in 2011 are:

■ GDP growth of 4.5 per cent in 2011 and 4 per cent in 2012.

■ Unemployment down, but still high – 6.5 per cent.

■ The interest rate level will normalise, market interest rates up to 3 per cent by the end of the year.

■ A 6 per cent increase in construction investments in both 2011 and 2012.

■ Private non-residential buildings up 9 per cent in 2011.

■ House prices in Greater Stockholm up 6 per cent this year.

■ 27,000 housing starts in 2011, 30,000 in 2012.

## ECONOMIC INDICATORS Denmark

“The Danish economy showed positive growth during the first three quarters of 2010. Four subsequent quarters of positive economic growth were replaced in Q4 2010 by a 0.4 per cent decline. The fall was due to weaker export figures. However, this growth for the year of some 1.7 per cent did not have a positive effect on the labour market. Wages increased more than in competing countries and, together with a weak productivity trend, contributed to weakened competitiveness. Domestic demand showed a positive trend in 2010 and contributed positively to the growth in the economy.

“There are prospects for cautious growth during the next two years of 1.5 – 2 per cent annually, driven by growth in investments and exports. This growth will not contribute to fully restoring the level of activity. At the end of 2012, the GDP will be about 1 per cent below the level before the crisis.

“GDP growth of 1.5 per cent in 2011 will not generate many new jobs. Productivity in the Danish economy fell throughout the crisis. Improving the productivity trend has taken time. A weak decline in unemployment in 2011 can therefore be expected,” Veidekke told ISMR.

According to Darmstahl Stainless Steel Solutions, Denmark’s stainless steel market shows substantial signs of improvement in Q1 – order intake also took a positive direction. Key points for growth in 2011, says Veidekke, are:

■ GDP growth of 1.5 per cent in 2011.

■ Increasing inflation.

■ Construction investments rise for the first time since 2006 – 3 per cent.

■ Construction investments rise by 5 per cent.

■ Still weak housing market – prices up 2 per cent.

■ About 12,000 housing starts.

## FINLAND IN FOCUS

Finland has a highly industrialised, largely free-market economy with per capita output roughly that of the UK, France, Germany and Italy. Its key economic sector is manufacturing - principally the wood, metals, engineering, telecommunications and electronics industries. Finland excels in high-tech exports, e.g. mobile phones. Except for timber and several minerals, Finland depends on imports of raw materials, energy and some components for manufactured goods.

Because of the climate, agricultural development is limited to maintaining self-sufficiency in basic products. Forestry, an important export earner, provides a secondary occupation for the rural population.

## AN EYE ON ICELAND

According to Oxford Economics, the GDP fall slowed in 2010 but was still 3.5 per cent as a return to growth in Q3 gave way to renewed decline in Q4. This fall mainly reflected a slackening of export growth, whose revival was expected to return the economy to growth in Q1 this year. But fiscal tightening will hold down the pace of recovery in 2011 and it could stall if recent investment strength is not maintained. ■
