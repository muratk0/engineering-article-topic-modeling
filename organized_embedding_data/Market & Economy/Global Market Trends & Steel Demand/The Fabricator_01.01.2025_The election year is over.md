# The election year is over

[TARİH: 01.01.2025 The Fabricator]

Now Comes the Hard Par

Dr. Chris Kuehl

First, the good news: It is now safe to watch TV again (except for all the ads from lawyers). The campaigns are over, and our choices have been made. Some are happy and some are not. What happens next is transitioning from promises and electioneering to policy decisions and actual governing. What can we expect, and what should these political leaders be focusing on?

Two things stand out as far as projected policy. Trump and the GOP are committed to what has been described as nationalist populism. This approach means the U.S. will become more isolationist when it comes to world affairs and more protectionist when it comes to the economy. This is the intent, in any case, but making this a reality will be challenging.

The first major shift will involve trade. Trump asserted that he would impose a 20 percent tariff on anything that was exported into the U.S. and a 60 percent tariff on goods from China. That is easier said than done. In his first term, he tried to impose significant tariffs on imported steel and aluminum, but by the time the plan was implemented there were exemptions for Canada, Mexico, Europe, South Korea, Australia, Brazil, Argentina, and even Japan. China dodged the tariffs by trans-shipping. Countries hit by tariffs retaliate with tariffs and restrictions of their own. Remember when Japan struck back with a major tariff on imported bourbon to get the attention of Mitch McConnell? Many assert that Trump plans to use the threat of tariffs as a negotiating tool. If a nation gives the U.S. something, they will be exempt. There could also be reciprocating trade deals made.

The second issue is immigration. There is universal agreement that something has to be done to control illegal immigration. This is an issue in Europe and Asia as well. The challenge is that the patterns have changed. The migrants today are better classified as refugees as they are fleeing tyrannical regimes, drug wars, and the like. They are not interested in assimilation or even working in the U.S. Ultimately, they want to go home. This creates a major drain on U.S. resources.

The border will be tightened, and this will be expensive. At the same time, the U.S. faces an acute labor shortage and needs migrants with the right skills and willingness to adapt to living in the U.S. The burden will fall on business to prove that employees are legal, and this can be a very expensive proposition. Many smaller businesses will choose to avoid anyone who appears to be from another nation.

Three Demands for the Newly Elected

What should we be demanding from those we just elected? From the business perspective, there are three issues that emerge at the top of nearly every list. It starts with labor. Economists and analysts have been sounding the demographic alarm for years. By 2030 the entire Boomer generation (76 million) will have reached retirement age. There has to be a focused effort to train and educate people for the jobs of today.

The second major issue is regulation. Thousands are promulgated every year, and they have become a major burden. Many have nothing to do with the core business and are directed at social issues. Many are contradictory and expensive overall. The average cost of regulatory compliance for a company is $277,000 annually (19 percent of payroll costs). A small company with 20 employees is paying $1 million in compliance costs per year. Manufacturers pay $350 million in compliance costs annually. That is 12 percent of the sector’s value to GDP.

The third issue is infrastructure support. The domestic transportation system has been graded D- by civil engineering associations. The power grid is woefully inadequate. The data center expansion alone will require 45 terawatts of energy. This means radical expansion of power plants fueled by everything available (oil, gas, coal, nuclear, wind, solar).

The ports are inefficient as unions block the use of technology, and water availability has become a major issue as well. The U.S. is welcoming reshoring, but companies can’t move to the U.S. if there are no workers, no power, no water, bad roads, and regulations that cost them millions. And then there is the issue of taxes. They are very often distorting and too high for many businesses to handle.

The K-Shaped Recovery Continues

As of early December, the latest estimate of Q4 GDP growth was at 2.5 percent— but the K-shaped recovery is still a factor. The upper third of income earners are still being driven by a robust market, and the middle third has been reassured by the better-than-expected job numbers. As we slide into the fourth quarter, the National Retail Federation (NRF) is still expecting growth of 2.5 percent to 3.5 percent.

The good news is that easing inflation quickly activates the bottom third of income earners, which account for 78 million households. This sector has not been able to purchase a lot of discretionary items over the past three years, and activating this bottom third will significantly change overall demand. It will help boost many sectors, assuming that saving that bottom third takes place. Pulling energy prices down, housing, and others (by increasing supply) will help. The expectation is that Q4 economic growth will continue to be good—with growth of around 2.5 percent, which would be consistent with prior years.

As published in The Watch, the Armada models (covering more than 85 percent of GDP) still show a low recession risk. The statements from the Fed suggest that they still expect a soft landing, and this is coming from hawks as well as doves. Corporate spending continues to be stable, and that helps push overall demand much higher.

The economic future appears to be on solid ground, though there is still too much dependence on that upper third of income earners, and they stay engaged as long as the markets are strong. The reduction in the rate of inflation will not alter the situation for the bottom third quickly, but it may provide an opportunity to start catching up.

Trends in Overall Industrial Production

Overall U.S. industrial production, both for durable and nondurable goods, shows some rapid growth in 2025. Note that our model strips out the utilities and mining portion of industrial production and focuses solely on manufacturing activity. Regardless, the model is still showing full-year 2024 expanding at just 0.5 percent, and are now showing some strong improvement in the second half of next year. The major factors shifting the model are lowering of interest rates and the impact that has on the dollar and exports.

Manufacturers’ new orders have been holding up well, but they remain sluggish according to various purchasing manager survey results. This discrepancy could be a function of some large events such as the Boeing strike and the impact from the hurricanes. On a global basis, this remains a challenge. The sluggishness in new orders really is a global phenomenon.

Regardless, most manufacturers around the world are optimistic about 2025, and our models are corroborating this. Supporting this outlook are several key factors, including part availability in the supply chain, technology and labor, and positive purchasing manager surveys from around the globe.

The Drive Toward Automation

Skilled labor remains in short supply (still showing 7.4 million jobs open against a historical norm of 6 million). The shortage will get much worse as more of the Boomer generation exits the workforce. The workforce participation rate, at 62.9 percent, is still near low historical levels. That is below the long-term average, but it has flattened. The solution is technology, and that has been driving a great deal of capital spending. Even smaller job shops have been forced to turn toward automation. This is a good strategy as long as there is demand. It gets tricky when demand fades. Once, the manufacturer could adjust to the shifts by changing the number of employees. But with expensive machinery, the cost of capital and maintenance could overwhelm some firms. Still, it is likely a worthy trade-off for most.

Industrial Numbers Are Holding Up

Industrial numbers have been holding up better than expected, but there is concern about continued momentum. Most supply chain issues have receded, but now there are concerns regarding the impact of tariffs. At this point, there are more questions than answers, and priorities have not yet been set by Congress or the White House. The moves toward protecting U.S. manufacturing and encouraging the reshoring effort are welcome, but details will matter. Much will depend on how other nations react to the moves the U.S. chooses to make.
