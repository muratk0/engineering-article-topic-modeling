ne would have thought that given the discord between Republicans and Democrats in this Congress that a bill that passed fairly easily and is somewhat consequential would have attracted more ballyhoo than it did. That is doubly true for a bill that takes a swing at better enforcement of foreign goods arriving at U.S. borders. Donald Trump certainly has elevated the issue of U.S. trade imbalances in the 2016 presidential race.

Coincidentally, the Trade Facilitation and Trade Enforcement Act of 2015 contains provisions that aim to give U.S. Customs officials additional tools to ensure that imports are labeled accurately as to their country of origin and that import duties are assessed accurately and collected. That will be done via educational seminars for U.S. Customs and Border Protection and U.S. Immigration and Customs Enforcement personnel.

The yet-to-be-established seminars would be based in part on input from private industry. Specifically, the idea is to ensure that countervailing duties are collected properly.

To stimulate small-business exports, the State Trade and Export Promotion grant program also has been reauthorized and improved.

Just before the Senate passed the bill in mid-February, National Association of Manufacturers (NAM) President and CEO Jay Timmons said, "Our nation’s current customs and border policies are costing manufacturers billions of dollars each year and putting us at a competitive disadvantage. By streamlining procedures that will cut red tape and facilitate legitimate trade, this bill would provide one of the most concrete improvements to our customs and border policies in over a decade."

EPA Wants to Upgrade Risk Management Plans

The Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) is proposing to upgrade its risk management program, and fabricated metal product manufacturers are among the approximately 12,500 facilities that would be affected.

The risk management program requires companies that use certain chemicals to assess what would happen to workers and the community if an accidental release were to occur, undertake steps to prevent releases, plan for emergency response to releases, and summarize this information in a risk management plan (RMP) submitted to EPA.

The release prevention steps vary depending on the type of process, but progressively gain specificity and rigor over three program levels (i.e., Program 1, Program 2, and Program 3). Those program levels are linked to the potential risk of the processes employed at a facility, with Program 1 companies presenting the lowest risk and Program 3 the highest.

To stimulate small-business exports, the State Trade and Export Promotion grant program also has been reauthorized and improved.

The EPA’s proposed additions would require all facilities with Program 2 or 3 processes to conduct a root cause analysis as part of an incident investigation of a catastrophic release or an incident that could have reasonably resulted in a catastrophic release, such as a near-miss. Program 2 or 3 participants would have to contract with an independent third party to perform a compliance audit after the facility has a reportable release.

Compliance audits are required under the existing rule, but are allowed to be self-audits. Some manufacturers would have to add an element to their process hazard analysis (PHA), which is updated every five years. Specifically, owners or operators of facilities with Program 3 regulated processes in industries such as paper manufacturing, petroleum and coal products manufacturing, and chemical processing would be required to conduct a safer technology and alternatives analysis as part of their PHA.

The proposal also looks to enhance the rule’s emergency response requirements. Owners or operators of all facilities with Program 2 or 3 processes would be required to coordinate with the local emergency response agencies at least once a year to ensure that resources and capabilities are in place to respond to an accidental release of a regulated substance.
