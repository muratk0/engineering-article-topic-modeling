I

n early April several press reports regarding a rumor that the Trump administration is going to call for an "investigation" into steel and aluminum first began to appear. What metal fabricators may find interesting are hints that the order may include manufactured products.

We have seen reports from the press and attorneys for steel service centers, and we have spoken to Washington, D.C., trade attorney Lewis Leibowitz, who is experienced with trade law, especially as it relates to national security and antidumping and countervailing duty rulings.

Steel Market Update (SMU) does not have any direct knowledge of exactly what would be included in any trade investigation, and no official word has emerged out of Washington as to exactly what is going to happen. Nonetheless, the rumors are in the market, and we feel that we need to make our readers aware of what we have heard and where things may go from here.

Those involved in steel production, distribution, and consumption have their opinions about what might happen, but Leibowitz reminded SMU what "investigation" means to attorneys.

"You are right to put ‘investigation’ in quotes. When we lawyers think about investigations, we think of established investigations with evidence-gathering, hearings, briefing, etc. We don’t know if that is what the president and his staff have in mind," Leibowitz said.

"An executive order would likely look similar to the executive orders made on March 31. A study would be ordered to determine the extent to which foreign unfair trade practices are aggravating the trade deficit," he continued. "This would not be an official ‘investigation’ with a set of decisions, but a study that would lead to a discussion of options. Since Jan. 20, that is generally what the president has done on trade."

Those presidential actions on trade include things such as Buy American provisions for pipelines, collection of penalties related to trade violations, and a closer examination of causes of the trade deficit.

Leibowitz told SMU about an option open to the government through the Department of Commerce or Congress.

"One possibility is a request for a study by the International Trade Commission under Section 332 of the Tariff Act of 1930. The U.S. trade representative and the trade-related committees of the Congress have the authority to request studies of issues such as this," he said. "They would take several months, and after the commission issues its report, Congress and the administration would have some evidence to go on concerning what approaches might help achieve their stated objectives."

SMU has heard through various press reports that the president may use a Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974. This is a broad statute that authorizes the president to take all appropriate action, including retaliation, to get the removal of any act, policy, or practice of a foreign government that violates an international trade agreement. A Section 301 permits the president to "impose duties or other imports restrictions" or to suspend the benefits of trade agreement concessions.

"Section 301 would be focused on either violation of trade agreements or on ‘unreasonable’ restrictions on U.S. trading opportunities," Leibowitz said. "The latter ground has not really been used since 1995 and would be difficult, but it could happen. The former ground should be preceded by a finding—usually by the World Trade Organization—that an agreement has been violated.

"So an investigation under Section 301 should be started with a complaint in the WTO," he added. "Starting a 301 without the prerequisite could start a trade conflict, and possibly would not be the first move."

Press reports are saying another possibility is for the president to use Section 201 of the Trade Act of 1974. This is also known as a "safeguard" action. Section 201 permits the president to grant temporary relief against imports of particular types of goods with respect to imports from all countries.

What is interesting is the Section 201 does not require the showing of an unfair trade act. It requires the U.S. International Trade Commission (ITC) to determine if specific imported goods are increasing at a noticeable rate as to suggest there will be serious injury or a threat of serious injury to the U.S. industry. In a Section 201 case, remedies include temporary increased tariffs or quotas.

President George W. Bush used the Section 201 mechanism against imported steel in 2001. In that particular case, the affected manufacturing companies fought against the Section 201 decision, and ultimately the restrictions against foreign steel imports were removed.

"A Section 201 case requires an ‘import surge.’ I don’t think that is going to work," Leibowitz said. "As I’ve said before, even if there were a surge in imports of certain products, the U.S. statute is extremely vulnerable to attack in the WTO. Retaliation against U.S. exports could be swift and certain."

What will be interesting is where the study originates and does it begin as a study of the causes of a trade deficit, what part does dumping and subsidies play in the trade deficit, and what remedies are available to correct the conclusions of the report.
