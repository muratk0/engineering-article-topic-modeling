The U.S. International Trade Commission (ITC) validated claims of foreign steel dumping in the U.S. and sent two separate cases to the U.S. Commerce Department to set final tariff and countervailing duty levels. Preliminary tariff and duty assessments from the Commerce Department before the ITC decisions were in some cases as high as 200 percent. The ITC made the key threshold finding that U.S. steel manufacturers had been "materially injured" by imports either sold below cost of manufacture (worthy of tariffs) or subsidized by the foreign government (countervailing duties applied).

There are two separate cases. One involves imports of corrosion-resistant steel products from China, India, Italy, Korea, and Taiwan. The tariffs and duties will apply to products entering the U.S. after a certain date, likely sometime in 2015. Imports from the countries at issue reached $2.1 billion in 2015—more than the total from all other countries, which was $1.5 billion. The products at issue are certain flat-rolled steel products, either clad, plated, or coated with corrosion-resistant metals such as zinc, aluminum-, or zinc-, aluminum-, nickel-, or iron-based alloys.

The ITC’s second finding involves imports of Japanese and Chinese cold-rolled steel flat products. This second category is a bit smaller in terms of volume of imports, with China and Japan sending $432 million to the U.S. in 2015, slightly less than the total from Brazil, India, Korea, Russia, and the U.K., whose imports totaled $468 million. Other countries chipped in $712 million.

Made in U.S.A. Labeling Bill Moves Forward

The House is finally getting to a "Made in America" bill that would give the Federal Trade Commission (FTC) the ability to pre-empt state laws on labeling products with domestic content. An identical bill passed the Senate Commerce Committee in March, and the action then turned to the House, where its manufacturing subcommittee cleared its version of the bill (H.R. 5092, Reinforcing American-Made Products Act of 2016) by a voice vote in June. The full House is expected to pass the bill before summer is out. That would likely mean the Senate would bring its bill to the floor soon after, if not before.

The problem here, for the most part, is one state: California. In 2011 the California Supreme Court allowed a lawsuit to proceed where plaintiffs challenged a company’s "Made in the U.S.A." claim under the state’s unfair competition law and false advertising law, effectively establishing a state standard that diverged from the FTC standard. The FTC allows a "Made in the U.S.A." claim if "all or virtually all" of the parts of a product are produced in the U.S.

After the court decision, California codified its own "Made in the U.S.A." standard under which a product can bear the "Made in the U.S.A." label in California if 95 percent of its contents are domestically sourced. If a company can certify that some components are unavailable in the U.S., then a 90 percent threshold applies. That is a much tighter standard than the FTC standard.
