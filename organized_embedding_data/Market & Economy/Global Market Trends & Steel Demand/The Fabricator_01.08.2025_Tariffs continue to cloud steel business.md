# Tariffs continue to cloud steel business

[TARİH: 01.08.2025 The Fabricator]

Steel News

Even with recent tariffs, the floor for steel prices hasn’t seen much movement upwards

MICHAEL COWDEN

I

t’s hard to write about U.S. trade policy these days because whatever I’ve written will probably be out of date by the time you read this sentence.

The problem is a lot trickier for anyone moving steel (or any physical good, for that matter). Trade policy moves at the speed of social media. Steel moves at the speed of trucks, trains, and ships.

But when you read this, this will still be true: President Donald Trump will continue to alter trade flows with the stroke of a Sharpie or a post on Truth Social. The roots of this go back to Trump’s first term in office. It’s no different when it comes to using U.S. trade policy to settle political scores.

Recent tariff activity might cause U.S. steelmakers with electric arc furnaces to rethink where to procure some of the raw material that they need to dilute impurities in their melts.

Turkey’s Precedent

Let’s rewind to Trump 1.0. On a seemingly random Friday morning in August 2018, Trump doubled Turkey’s Section 232 tariff on steel to 50%. He cited a slide in the Turkish lira and relations with Turkey that were "not good."

Speculation at the time suggested that the beef was over more than exchange rates: namely, over Fethullah Gülen, a self-exiled cleric living in Pennsylvania. Turkish President Recep Tayyip Erdoğan wanted to bring him back to Turkey to face trial for alleged connections to a coup attempt.

Brazil’s 50 Percent

Let’s consider the case of Brazil more recently. Trump’s aggressive increase of Brazil’s reciprocal tariff rate from 10% to 50% was overtly over a political beef with Brazil’s current president, Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva. It centered on Brazil’s former president, Jair Bolsonaro, who faces trial for an attempted coup. (Trump thinks it’s a "witch hunt," according to a letter posted on Truth Social.)

The difference: The spat with Turkey was a big deal for steel. This time, the 50% reciprocal tariff for Brazil hits everything from coffee and beef to pig iron. It seems almost custom-built to inflict as much pain as possible on Brazil’s economy.

Brazil was already the biggest supplier of semifinished steel slab to the U.S. Steel products like slab were already subject to a Section 232 tariff of 50%.

The South American nation is also the biggest supplier of pig iron to the U.S. And while Section 232 tariffs don’t apply to metallics, the reciprocal tariffs do.

In other words, the primary raw materials for slab converters and electric arc furnace (EAF) mills have gotten more expensive.

Brazil’s Changed Fortunes

You might remember the shock that followed Trump’s imposing 25% Section 232 tariffs on slabs in 2018. That was big news because semi-finished goods had previously been exempt from duties and tariffs, just like most raw materials were (until this year).

In 2018, U.S. mills, including slab converters owned by Russian steelmakers, had to pivot away from procuring slabs from their own parent companies. What did they turn to? Brazilian slabs, which were cheaper because they weren’t subject to the tariff but instead to a hard Section 232 quota. That quota, with its quarterly volume limits, changed the cadence of work for everyone from rerollers to dock workers.

We’re now in for another period of churn. Brazilian slab prices have largely decoupled from U.S. hot-rolled coil (HRC) prices since the imposition of across-the-board 25% Section 232 tariffs in March. Trump probably severed whatever links remained when he increased Section 232 tariffs on steel to 50% in June.

Slab Shakeup

The higher tariffs on semis make ArcelorMittal look sharp for its decision to invest in a new EAF at its mill in Calvert, Ala.

For Calvert, it also marks a complete reversal from the business plan embraced by German steelmaker ThyssenKrupp, which originally built the mill. That model relied on melting slabs in Brazil and rolling them in the U.S.

ThyssenKrupp has long since exited the Americas, at least from a production standpoint. And I wonder if that kind of business model is one that a less-globalized world can support.

I hear from industry contacts that mills on the West Coast, for example, are now sourcing slabs from Japan, Australia, and Southeast Asia—wherever they can get a competitive price on slabs and on freight. Why? Because Brazilian slab no longer has the cost advantage now that it’s not exempt from the Section 232 tariff.

What Does It Mean for EAF Steelmakers?

It will be interesting to see how this plays out for U.S. mills, especially EAF steelmakers who rely on imported pig iron. Virgin metallics like pig iron are necessary to dilute impurities in their melt and perhaps to keep a lid on prime scrap prices as well.

Nucor has its bases covered by direct reduced iron (DRI) plants in New Orleans as well as in Trinidad and Tobago. This assumes, of course, that the reciprocal tariff on material from Trinidad and Tobago doesn’t go through the roof.

Big River can source pig iron from U. S. Steel, which installed a pig iron caster at its Gary Works in northwest Indiana.

Stelco, now part of Cliffs, has sold merchant pig iron before. Will it do so under Cliffs? I guess that depends on whether it can make more money on HRC and whether it wants to supply competitors who might be in a pinch.

Cliffs also has a DRI and hot briquetted iron (HBI) plant in Toledo, Ohio, that it uses mostly to supply its own operations. But it had initially hoped to sell that material to EAF mills.

There was once talk about DRI/HBI units being built all over North America. Some of that hinged on hydrogen hubs, a Biden-era initiative that has been abandoned by Trump 2.0. A lot of the investments that were talked about didn’t pan out.

That said, U. S. Steel has plans to build a DRI unit at Big River Steel. If reciprocal tariffs on raw material suppliers like Brazil remain in place, could we see others do the same?

What About Steel Prices?

The more immediate concern is what all of this means for steel prices. The floor for domestic prices rose with the imposition of 50% Section 232 tariffs in June. You’d think tighter raw material supplies might lift that floor even higher, yet we didn’t see mills immediately increasing prices as happened ahead of higher Section 232 tariffs in March and again in June.

A lot depends on inventories. We know from our surveys and speaking to some of you that steel buyers have been mostly maintaining or reducing inventories.

Caution is the word of the day with so much uncertainty around tariffs and demand. Service centers are buying from each other if they have holes or maybe buying secondary or excess prime.

My question is this: At what point does that kind of buying start to decrease inventories across the supply chain? There is the assumption that Trump always chickens out (a.k.a. TACO). Does that still hold if Trump now recognizes that people don’t believe his trade threats will come to fruition?

Maybe we will see everyone jump back in at once to restock at some point over the next month or two. A lot comes back to demand. Higher tariffs have driven up U.S. steel prices. The impact of tariffs on demand, however, is less clear.
