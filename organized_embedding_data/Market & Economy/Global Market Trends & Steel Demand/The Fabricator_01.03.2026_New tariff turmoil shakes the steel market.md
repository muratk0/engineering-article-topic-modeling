# New tariff turmoil shakes the steel market

[TARİH: 01.03.2026 The Fabricator]

Steel News

Businesses seek certainty, leading to more pressure on the White House to remove some tariffs

By

Kristen DiLandro

I

n 2025, U.S. tariffs reached levels the country hadn’t experienced since World War II. The tariffs weren’t the highest in U.S. history, but the levels are unprecedented for many businesses and consumers. The surge in fees has given rise to new anxieties for multiple market participants. The steel market has not been spared.

President Donald Trump has been outspoken about his belief that tariffs are a powerful bargaining chip in global trade negotiations. Since implementing tariffs, the president has carved out a lower rate in negotiations with the U.K., for example. But the administration underestimated pushback from multiple U.S. industries trying to conduct business amid the instability caused by uncertainty about whether tariffs would remain in place.

Affordability is now a growing concern for most voters, so the Oval Office is taking notice. On Feb. 13, the president said he plans to scale back the implementation of Section 232 tariffs on derivative items. Unlike past administrations, which allowed manufacturers to seek tariff exemptions, the Trump administration abolished the formal exemption process. That process allowed original equipment manufacturers, automakers, and other producers reliant on steel or aluminum items (deemed too unprofitable to make domestically) to avoid paying the full tariff on those items.

At the same time it abandoned the exemption process, the administration introduced an inclusion process, which allows producers and sellers to add derivative items containing steel and aluminum to the list of products subject to Section 232 tariffs. In part, the rationale for an inclusion process was to prevent nations from circumventing the Section 232 tariff. It’s not uncommon for a nation to send finished products rather than raw materials to avoid paying higher tariffs. With that said, the extensive list of derivative items subject to added costs and the lack of an exemption process have hindered many U.S. businesses.

Steel buyers are wary of big purchases of steel for fear that the price might drop. Many hoping are hoping that the removal of certain tariffs might jumpstart steel-buying activity.

While the news of tariff relief assuages many fears in the steel consumer market, the changes have stalled steel buyers again. Buyers are fearful of overpaying, causing the market to stall until there is more clarity, sources told Steel Market Update.

On Feb. 11, the House of Representatives took its own action, passing a resolution that disapproves Trump’s national emergency declaration that led to the imposition of tariffs on Canada. Consumers and businesses turned up the heat on their representatives, prompting six Republican members of the House of Representatives to join Democrats in a 219-211 vote to end tariffs on Canadian goods. They sent the bill to the Senate for a vote.

Republican lawmakers in the Senate have been cautious about putting the measure to a vote. If the Senate votes to pass the measure, it will go to the president, who can then veto it. Some analysts suggest it’s unlikely that Congress could drum up the two-thirds majority votes needed to overturn a presidential veto.

The U.S. Supreme Court, meanwhile, is also weighing the president’s actions under the International Emergency Economic Powers Act. Back in February 2025, Trump issued three executive orders invoking his authority to declare states of emergency with respect to imports from Canada, Mexico, and China. The president said the country’s North American neighbors and allies were not sufficiently monitoring and thwarting drugs and people from entering the U.S. across their borders. Trump made the same accusation against China. The president imposed tariffs as part of his IEEPA action.

Later, the president expanded his IEEPA tariffs when he rolled out reciprocal tariffs on more than 100 countries. Because the U.S. Constitution technically gives Congress the power to regulate foreign commerce and impose import tariffs, the Supreme Court is determining whether the president violated the separation of powers and overreached in using national security as his reasoning for implementing the IEEPA tariffs.

It should be noted that the IEEPA tariffs did not target steel and aluminum products. But the tariffs have contributed to higher costs of doing business for consumers and businesses, including those in the steel industry.

A Midwest-based steel plate distributor told SMU, "Business conditions were improving but are plateauing. It’s the uncertainty with prices and tariffs again."

Crucially, participants in the steel market are looking to see whether easing the Section 232 tariffs on derivatives and the IEEPA tariffs for products from Canada, Mexico, and China will kick-start a stronger market. Demand for steel has been subdued, but prices have remained strong because of producer discipline. What multiple steel market sources want to see now is whether buyers will take a chance, spurring broader economic growth that could give way to lower inflation and potentially an interest rate cut.

Patience may be a virtue, but it is not a sufficient long-term business strategy. The steel market is ready for some semblance of certainty. It’s looking to lawmakers to make timely decisions about tariffs and help accelerate it beyond the starts and stalls that capped its potential in 2025.
