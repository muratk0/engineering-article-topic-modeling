The primary driver for the burst of very high inflation has been oil — and that is very often the case. Oil is an "inelastic good" and that simply means that consumption is not affected all that much by price. Not that people don’t notice a higher price at the pump and it is not that people do not complain bitterly when they see that price. The point is that there is very little they can do about it. It is not as if they can suddenly stop driving to work or swiftly move to reduce the length of that commute. The consumer will consume what they always have. By the same token, a drop in price doesn’t mean that people will start driving aimlessly for hours because gas is cheap.

The price of fuel is a dance between the consumer and the producer as it is expensive to store fuel (as well as somewhat risky). The current surge in price is due to a surge in demand but one that strikes the producers as temporary. The origin of the current problem is primarily geopolitical. Russia is putting pressure on Europe over authorizing the Nord Stream 2 gas pipeline and the fate of Ukraine. This pressure includes restricting gas supplies to Europe and that has caused the price of natural gas to soar by over 600%.

THE ORIGIN OF THE CURRENT PROBLEM IS PRIMARILY GEOPOLITICAL.

This has triggered U.S. utilities to start burning more oil as it was cheaper than gas. Hence the rising price of oil. The producers could step up their output, but they do not think this situation will last.

They expect either Russia or Europe to give in and this would mean a resumption of gas shipments. The oil producers are watching the daily U.S. commute as that is how Americans consume oil. As long as 60% of us work from home and don’t take to the highway, there is reduced demand for fuel and the producers have little incentive to boost output.
