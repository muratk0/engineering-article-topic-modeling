# Issues that miss the headlines

[TARİH: 01.03.2026 The Fabricator]

Fabrinomics | Economic Insights

Yet might be more significant

Dr. Chris Kuehl

We are bombarded with assessments of inflation, employment, interest rates, tariffs, and trade. The conversation regarding AI is constant and intense, and it’s OK if you continue to be confused by what it can and can’t do. Amid the bombardment, though, some issues get lost in the shuffle. These less headline-worthy subjects may prove more significant than those that are dominating the news cycle.

Lost Skills

VM/iStock/Getty Images Plus

We are all familiar with the workforce shortage that has been predicted for more than a decade. We know that by 2030, we face a demographic meltdown as every single Boomer will be eligible for retirement. We know that we lack trained people to replace them, but there is another element that doesn’t receive enough attention.

Those coming out of the trade schools and other institutions may be well versed in modern tech, but they don’t have experience with the old tech that still dominates most businesses. That training must come from the people who have been dealing with these machines for years, and too often they retire before passing that knowledge on.

Most manufacturers have been accumulating machinery for decades, and most of that equipment is still in daily use. The experienced operators know their quirks by now and know what kind of adjustments have to be made. In the not-so-distant past, the "old heads" would pass that knowledge on to the new workers by example. Companies stopped hiring people to wait in the wings and gather knowledge. Now, experienced workers retire and new workers are hired to replace them. There’s no opportunity to pass on that knowledge, and this results in months of expensive trial and error.

Shrinkflation: Size, Quality, and Service

We are all familiar with the higher prices that have come along with inflation of around 2.7%, officially. (Inflation should be somewhat higher by now given the challenges of tariffs, trade turmoil, and higher wages.) Producers, however, can deal with inflation in other ways. Sure, they could hike prices to reflect the higher costs of inputs, but they then risk losing consumers who no longer can afford the product or service.

The most common alternative strategy is shrinkflation. The price may stay the same, but the product is smaller—often significantly so. Or the service is reduced so that there is a longer wait. Notice how slow "fast food" has become? A request for a service call now takes far longer as staffs have been reduced and hiring costs have risen.

People notice the reduced product sizes, but there is little they can do about it. "Shrinkflated" services are harder to detect. Regardless, the pattern affects consumers deeply. They are routinely getting less for their money, and that erodes the relationship between the producer and the consumer. If producers don’t meet customer expectations, customers become strongly motivated to shift to a different supplier. This is even more pronounced when service is cut or reduced. In many sales situations, the selling point has been "service after sale." Everybody knows machines break down. A significantly slow service response spurs frustration, as long delays cost money.

Businesses need to rely on suppliers to do their jobs so that they can meet the demands of their own customer base. A failure at one end of the supply chain will swiftly cascade through the entire process. If a business decides not to reduce quality or service efficiency but must still cope with inflation, there will be no alternative to hiking prices and risking the erosion of market share.

Regulation

Milton Friedman once asserted that policies should be judged by their outcomes rather than their intent. Many good ideas turn out to be problematic when they encounter the real world. They contradict one another as they are promulgated by different agencies with differing mandates.

One problem occupies regulators’ attention, and they pay little attention to what happens as a result of that change. It is estimated that there are more than 1 million regulations at the federal level alone, and another 4,500 are issued every year. Keeping up with them easily becomes a full-time job.

And now we have the additional stress of tariffs. There have been over 500 executive orders issued since the start of 2025. This is more than in any year in the last three decades. The orders themselves have changed quickly and often, and that makes it even harder to keep track of what has been affected and what hasn’t. Customs officials have admitted publicly that they no longer know what a company’s financial obligations are.

Nobody wants to live in a world without regulation. There are unscrupulous people and ample reasons to seek protection for consumers, workers, and the environment. That said, there is a solid argument to be made against overregulation. In most cases, the original rule or regulation made sense in the context of the original problem. It becomes a problem when that rule or regulation comes in conflict with other goals and aims.

Lately there have been calls to cap the interest rates set on credit cards to 10%. Given that everybody has long complained about how high these rates have been, it seems a very good idea. What could go wrong? The reality is that credit card issuers are well aware of the millions of defaults every year. The rate of serious delinquencies (at least 90 days) is close to 8.0%. There was nearly $60 billion in overdue credit card debt in 2024 alone.

Credit card issuers absorb that loss by having higher rates that allow them to cover the delinquencies. If the rate is capped, they will respond with a much more cautious approach to issuing credit cards. Merchants have demanded that cards be widely available, but if rates are capped, those with weaker credit will be denied access. These people will resort to less savory options such as payday loans and outright loan sharks. The bottom line is that capping rates is a good idea in some respects, but it creates new problems—the infamous law of unintended consequences.

Freight Recovery

violetastoimenova/iStock/Getty Images Plus

Many have been calling for a freight recovery since last fall, mainly because of the rebalancing of global inventories. Most inventory data lags, and the latest inventory-to-sales-ratio data reinforces the view that the global supply chain is now back in cycle. Most of the activity now will be driven by demand and not a function of supply-side over-stocks.

Only 19% of the U.S. market was overstocked through November. Remember that this would have been a peak inventory period for many retailers and anyone that gets most of their sales from the holiday season. For comparison, the percentage of those overstocked was 59% in March 2025. That shows just how much of the market has now adjusted. The data shows that the freight sector is finally coming out of a three-year secular slowdown.

For the last few years, the data collected by FMA’s Forming and Fabricating Job Shop Consumption Report has indicated that logistics costs have done nothing but rise, and often at an alarming pace. This increased demand in transportation could break either way. Some will have an opportunity to hike prices on expectation of demand, but there also is a good chance for capacity expansion as demand heats up. It can be easy to add capacity in transportation, especially in trucking. It just involves bringing trucks out of mothballs.

Shifts in the Credit Markets

Some concerning data emerged from the New York Fed’s Consumer Household Credit report for Q4. Delinquencies across critical categories surged, but is that historically important? Let’s get into the data. For context, it typically takes a collapse in the credit market before we begin to see real recession fears surface. Recent labor data could begin to affect bank sentiment, but for now, credit markets remain generally liquid.

Broader consumer stress is visible. Student loan delinquencies surged to 10.2% in Q2 2025 from 0.5% in Q4 2024, and FICO scores continued dropping year-overyear, particularly for Gen Z. Meanwhile, consumer credit data shows spending has stagnated among the bottom 80% of households when adjusted for inflation. The top 20% now account for nearly two-thirds of consumption.

Still, all this really isn’t front page news because banks don’t seem to be overly tightening lending standards, though smaller firms are facing tightening conditions. Recent data shows about 95% of banks are tightening lending standards for business with less than $50 million in annual revenue, compared to only 5% for larger firms. However, banks simultaneously lowered spreads and costs of credit lines for large and middle-market firms, indicating improved pricing for established borrowers.

The credit world can be very murky at times, and the data often lags. In the runup to the 2008-2009 recession, manufacturers were very optimistic as they had a robust book of business. Most saw clear sailing ahead. Warnings from credit managers were largely ignored. Suddenly, the credit markets imploded, and a substantial slump overtook the economy—one that few really saw coming. We don’t know if history will repeat itself (or at least rhyme), but one thing’s for sure: The world of credit is worth watching.
