# Intelligent preparation makes the most of AI

[TARİH: 01.09.2025 The Fabricator]

Editor’s Corner

The promise of what AI can do is immense, DAN DAVIS but benefits are not automatic

DAN DAVIS

I

’ve been wanting to write about the growth of artificial intelligence for a while because of its sheer economic impact. In a report on Aug. 8 that looked at AI investment and its relationship to overall GDP, financial and investment news source Barron’s suggested that Amazon, Google, Meta, and Microsoft planned to spend $340 billion on data centers and related infrastructure, as well as products associated with the AI push.

What’s of interest to me is just how long the tentacles are from such an investment, a large portion of which is flowing into the metal fabricating sector. You’ve got the actual structural steel needed for the buildings, the racks for the servers, the power infrastructure needed to power these data farms, the generators needed to power these data centers should the grid fail, and the water systems required to cool all of these systems. That’s just what comes to mind.

Metal fabricators are building the future, but what is that going to look like? Right now, most experts agree that we are in the infancy of what AI can do, and yet you are already starting to see early examples of just how disruptive this technology can be. In early August, outplacement firm Challenger, Gray & Christmas reported that just through July, generative AI adoption was linked to the elimination of more than 10,000 jobs. That might be the tip of the iceberg.

But in a way, this trend might be beneficial for metal fabricating companies as well. Workers being forced out of entry-level service sector jobs arguably are good fits for modern manufacturing roles.

"It will become increasingly important to attract employees with strong data and computational skills," writes Bill Frahm in his August blog entry "Smart manufacturing requires smart workers" for

thefabricator.com

. (For more insightful observations on the metal fabricating industry, I highly recommend bookmarking

www.thefabricator.com/blog

.)

He specifically pointed out how he saw a future where highly trained quality analysts, operational auditors, and computing/computational analysts could work side by side with engineers and fabricators to ensure that processes are streamlined and waste is minimized. Even today, small shops are taking steps—dedicating an individual or even a team of people—to investigate new technologies and software.

That’s why it’s important to have a plan before rushing off to embrace AI tools. What’s the goal? How will it be used? What sort of feedback or metrics determine impact?

Metal fabricators are familiar with the tale. Shop invests in new equipment. Shop is not prepared for new equipment, and results do not meet expectations. New equipment remains underutilized. Return on investment is not fully recognized in the expected time frame.

The IT industry actually has a name for this: Solow’s paradox, also known as the productivity paradox. Economist Robert Solow observed that even with large investments made in IT and software by organizations in the 1970s and 1980s, productivity gains didn’t really jump as a result.

"You can see the computer age everywhere but in the productivity statistics," he said in 1987.

"It will become increasingly important to attract employees with strong data and computational skills."

— Bill Frahm, 4M Partners, in his blog entry "Smart manufacturing requires smart workers,"

thefabricator.com

Obviously, this observation might be tainted by factors like how productivity is measured in the U.S., but there also is some validity. Owning the latest and greatest machine tool does not automatically make a shop a modern manufacturing marvel.

Now is a great time to experiment with AI because everyone is at the beginning of the race. In her Executive Perspectives column in the August 2025 issue of The Tube and Pipe Journal (p. 6), Lisa Wertzbaugher described how she is now using AI to gain an extra eight to 10 hours of productivity per week in her role as director of sales for a technology services provider in the Midwest. She’s using Microsoft Co-Pilot to summarize emails and create documents for specific tasks, such as a guide for transferring accounts between sales representatives.

Some of these tasks don’t require too much critical review, but others do, like an AI-generated competitive analysis of a market. This is why it’s important to have a knowledgeable individual vetting AI-enhanced documents and reports.

AI-empowered tools are heading to the shop floor as well. Also in August, The Fabricator spoke with Xavier Jauregui, chief operating officer, Xiris Automation, about how weld monitoring cameras paired with AI are going to be able to catch welding defects as they happen ("Affirming weld quality before the inspection," p. 124). It’s not here yet, but as AI reviews more welds and learns to differentiate between what should be happening during a certain welding process and what shouldn’t, it’s going to be a powerful tool. But it’s only going to be effective if an experienced welding professional can understand why the defects are occurring and work to improve the process so that the defects don’t occur again.

AI holds the potential to supercharge manufacturing efficiency, but for that to happen, companies need to have the right people in place to maximize the use of those advanced tools. The manufacturing world likely laughed at the term knowledge worker when it was repeatedly used by pundits and industry observers to describe the modern service-based economy. Lo and behold, that’s exactly who’s needed if manufacturing companies want to excel in the 21st century.
