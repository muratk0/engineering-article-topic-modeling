# Supreme Court hears IEEPA tariff case

[TARİH: 01.12.2025 The Fabricator]

Around Washington

Early questioning raises doubt that president can unilaterally enact tariffs

OMAR NASHASHIBI

T

he U.S. Supreme Court heard oral arguments on Nov. 5 in the landmark challenge to President Donald Trump’s use of the International Emergency Economic Powers Act (IEEPA) to impose broad import tariffs earlier this year. The consolidated cases,

Learning Resources Inc. v. Trump and Trump v. V.O.S. Selections Inc.

, were argued over more than two and a half hours, following the court’s Oct. 23 order expanding the session to allow 40 minutes for the solicitor general and 20 minutes each for private business and state challengers. The unusually long schedule reflected the justices’ recognition of the case’s constitutional and economic importance, as the decision could redefine the limits of presidential emergency powers in trade policy.

The dispute centers on whether IEEPA, a 1977 law designed primarily for targeted sanctions and asset freezes, can be used to impose tariffs, a power historically reserved for Congress. In August, the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit ruled 7–4 that Trump’s use of IEEPA to levy duties of 10% to 41% on imports from multiple countries exceeded executive authority, holding that the statute "does not extend to the imposition of duties." That ruling was stayed pending Supreme Court review, allowing the tariffs to remain in effect during the appeal.

According to U.S. Department of the Treasury data cited in the litigation, the federal government had collected roughly $72 billion under the IEEPA tariffs by mid-August 2025. If the lower court’s decision is upheld, importers could be entitled to large-scale refunds, and future administrations would need to rely on other authorities such as Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974 or Section 232 of the Trade Expansion Act of 1962 for tariff actions.

During arguments on Nov. 5, several justices appeared skeptical of the administration’s expansive reading of IEEPA. Justices Elena Kagan, Ketanji Brown Jackson, and Neil Gorsuch all questioned whether Congress intended to delegate such sweeping trade authority to the executive branch. Chief Justice John Roberts noted that Congress typically uses the terms "tariff" and "duty" explicitly in trade legislation but did not do so in IEEPA, suggesting the law was not meant to authorize revenue-raising import duties. Jackson emphasized that IEEPA was enacted to limit, not expand, presidential emergency powers following the overreach of the Trading with the Enemy Act. Gorsuch warned that the administration’s interpretation risked allowing Congress to abdicate its core constitutional responsibility to regulate foreign commerce.

A few justices, including Justice Samuel Alito, raised hypothetical scenarios in which IEEPA might justify broad action during wartime or other emergencies. One such scenario discussed included whether IEEPA would permit a future president to use the law to impose tariffs on imported automobiles because of climate change being declared a national security issue, to which the U.S. government’s attorney responded "yes." The overall tone of the session suggested significant doubt that the statute authorizes the type of across-the-board tariffs at issue.

The outcome will not affect existing Section 232 tariffs on steel, aluminum, copper, and vehicles, which are based on separate national security findings previously upheld by the court. A ruling is expected before the Supreme Court’s term concludes in June 2026.

Trump Administration Expands Vehicle Tariffs

President Trump issued a proclamation on Oct. 17 imposing new Section 232 tariffs on imported medium- and heavy-duty trucks, critical truck parts, and buses while extending the existing auto tariff offset program through 2030. The action follows a U.S. Department of Commerce investigation that determined rising import dependence in these sectors threatens national security by eroding U.S. manufacturing capacity.

Effective Nov. 1, the proclamation establishes a 25% tariff on imported trucks in Classes 3 through 8 and on key components such as engines, transmissions, tires, and chassis. Imported buses, including school, transit, and motor coaches, will face a 10% duty.

For products covered by the U.S.-Mexico-Canada Agreement, tariffs will apply only to non-U.S. content once the Commerce Department finalizes a verification process. The proclamation also clarifies that these products will not face overlapping Section 232 duties on steel, aluminum, copper, or lumber inputs.

To balance tariff costs and encourage domestic production, the administration extended and harmonized the auto import adjustment offset program originally established in April 2025. That program allows manufacturers assembling vehicles in the U.S. to reduce tariff liabilities by an amount equal to 3.75% of the manufacturer’s suggested retail price of each U.S.-assembled vehicle, with a temporary reduction to 2.5% during 2026 and 2027. To qualify, automakers must certify U.S. assembly operations, volumes, and facilities with the Department of Commerce.

Under the new proclamation, this offset will now continue through 2030 and apply to producers of medium- and heavy-duty trucks and engines, as well as manufacturers of automobiles covered under the original program. Administration officials said aligning the tariff and offset frameworks for both the automotive and trucking sectors is part of a broader national strategy to strengthen industrial supply chains, promote investment in U.S. production, and safeguard industries critical to economic and national security.

U.S. and China Reach One-Year Trade Truce

A new trade accord between Washington and Beijing took effect on Nov. 10, marking a temporary easing of economic tensions that have strained relations for much of the past decade. Announced by the Trump administration on Nov. 1, the pact introduces a one-year framework of reciprocal tariff reductions, suspended export restrictions, and renewed commitments on supply chain and security-related issues.

The White House characterized the deal as a pragmatic step toward stabilizing bilateral trade while advancing U.S. national security priorities. China has agreed to suspend recently proposed export controls on rare earths and other critical minerals; to resume exports of key inputs such as gallium, germanium, and graphite under broad licenses; and to withdraw retaliatory tariffs introduced earlier in 2025.

The Chinese government also will end investigations and sanctions targeting U.S. semiconductor, shipping, and logistics companies and will intensify cooperation to block fentanyl-related precursor chemicals from entering the U.S. For its part, the U.S. will trim certain tariffs imposed under fentanyl-related trade actions by 10 percentage points starting Nov. 10, while maintaining but freezing additional duties at 10% for at least the next year. Washington also will prolong Section 301 tariff exclusions that were set to expire later in November 2025 and pause implementation of the Commerce Department’s Affiliates Rule, which would have expanded export control coverage to subsidiaries of Chinese entities.

The accord will remain in force until Nov. 10 unless both sides agree to renew it. Officials described the arrangement as a "stabilization period," providing time to pursue longer-term negotiations on technology, minerals, and enforcement frameworks.
