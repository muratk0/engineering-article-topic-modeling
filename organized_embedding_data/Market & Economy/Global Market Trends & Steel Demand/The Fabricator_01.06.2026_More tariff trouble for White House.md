U.S. Court of International Trade rules Section 122 tariffs unlawful

By

Omar S. Nashashibi

T

he U.S. Court of International Trade on May 7 ruled that the Trump administration’s 10% tariffs imposed under Section 122 of the Trade Act of 1974 were unlawful, creating another legal challenge for the administration’s trade policy.

The ruling only applies to the plaintiffs in the case, two businesses and the state of Washington itself, but does not immediately block the tariffs nationwide. The decision follows the U.S. Supreme Court’s February 2026 ruling that the International Emergency Economic Powers Act does not authorize the president to impose broad import tariffs.

The Section 122 tariffs were announced shortly after the U.S. Supreme Court invalidated the administration’s IEEPA tariffs. Section 122 permits the president to impose temporary import surcharges of up to 15% for 150 days to address "large and serious" U.S. balance-of-payments deficits. The administration implemented a 10% across-the-board tariff in February 2026, arguing the measure was necessary to address trade imbalances and protect domestic industries.

In a 2–1 decision, the CIT found that the administration exceeded its authority under Section 122. The court said the government did not demonstrate the type of balance-of-payments conditions required under the statute and rejected the administration’s interpretation of its tariff authority.

The administration quickly appealed the decision to the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit, arguing that the tariffs were a lawful temporary response following the Supreme Court’s limitation on the use of IEEPA authorities.

While the Section 122 tariffs were temporary and scheduled to expire in July absent congressional action, the administration has continued pursuing alternative tariff authorities, including Section 232 national security investigations and Section 301 unfair trade practice investigations targeting China and other trading partners.

USTR EXTENDS REVIEW OF CHINA SECTION 301 TARIFFS

The Office of the U.S. Trade Representative has formally initiated the second statutory four-year review of the Section 301 tariffs on imports from China, beginning a process that will determine whether key tariff actions first imposed in 2018 remain in effect. The review covers the July 6, 2018, and August 23, 2018, tariff actions (known as List 1 and List 2, respectively) stemming from the Section 301 investigation into China’s acts, policies, and practices related to technology transfer, intellectual property, and innovation.

Under Section 307(c) of the Trade Act of 1974, Section 301 actions automatically expire after four years unless domestic industries benefiting from the tariffs request their continuation. USTR established two separate 60-day windows for continuation requests: May 7 through July 5 for List 1, and June 24 through August 22 for List 2. As Lists 3 and 4a are considered modifications of the July and August 2018 Section 301 actions, requests can be submitted by July 5 or August 22. If continuation requests are submitted, USTR will proceed with a broader review process that includes public comments on the effectiveness of the tariffs, their economic impact, and whether modifications are warranted.

The current review follows the first statutory four-year review launched in 2022, which resulted in USTR maintaining the tariffs and later expanding them on a range of strategic sectors. In 2024, USTR finalized increased tariffs on products including electric vehicles, semiconductors, batteries, solar cells, ship-to-shore cranes, critical minerals, steel, aluminum, and certain medical products. Several of those tariff increases were phased in through 2025 and 2026.

The review coincides with Section 301’s growing use as a trade enforcement mechanism, as USTR has initiated additional investigations involving manufacturing overcapacity, forced labor concerns, and China’s implementation of the Phase One trade agreement.

USTR indicated that additional notices outlining the next steps in the review process will be issued if qualifying continuation requests are received.

OSHA UPDATES NATIONAL EMPHASIS PROGRAM ON HEAT HAZARDS

OSHA has revised and reissued its National Emphasis Program on indoor and outdoor heat-related hazards, continuing the agency’s focus on heat illness enforcement while broader federal heat-rulemaking efforts remain unresolved. The updated directive, effective April 10, replaces the prior NEP first launched in 2022 and will remain in place for five years.

The revised program updates OSHA’s targeting methodology using Bureau of Labor statistics and agency enforcement data from 2022 through 2025, narrowing the list of covered high-risk industries to 55 sectors. OSHA removed dozens of industries from the prior version while adding others, including certain plastics, electrical equipment, warehousing, retail, and manufacturing operations. Construction, agriculture, transportation, foundries, landscaping, and many manufacturing facilities remain key enforcement targets.

Under the directive, OSHA compliance officers may conduct programmed inspections on "heat priority days," generally when the heat index reaches 80 degrees F or when the National Weather Service issues a heat advisory or warning. Inspectors also are instructed to expand the scope of other workplace inspections if they observe potential heat hazards. The agency continues to emphasize both outdoor and indoor heat exposure, particularly in facilities with furnaces, hot processes, or limited ventilation.

Although the NEP is not a formal workplace standard, it provides detailed guidance for how OSHA evaluates heat-related hazards during inspections. Compliance officers are directed to review factors such as access to water and cooling areas, rest breaks, acclimatization practices for new and returning workers, employee training, emergency response procedures, and employer heat illness prevention plans. OSHA will continue relying primarily on the Occupational Safety and Health Act’s General Duty Clause when issuing citations related to heat exposure.

The updated enforcement initiative comes as OSHA’s separate proposed federal heat standard remains stalled. The proposed rule, first issued during the previous administration, would establish specific employer requirements tied to heat thresholds, including written prevention plans, heat monitoring, acclimatization procedures, and additional protections at higher temperatures.

Congressional Republicans have recently intensified opposition, introducing legislation that would bar OSHA from finalizing the rule. They argue the agency should prioritize guidance and practical workplace measures rather than adopting a nationwide heat regulation.

LABOR DEPARTMENT PROPOSES NEW JOINT EMPLOYER STANDARD

The U.S. Department of Labor has issued a proposed rule to clarify how "joint employer" status is determined under the Fair Labor Standards Act, Family and Medical Leave Act, and Migrant and Seasonal Agricultural Worker Protection Act. Published in the Federal Register on April 23 by the department’s Wage and Hour Division, the proposal is now open for public comment. According to the DOL, the rule would examine the "totality of the circumstances" in assessing joint employer relationships. This includes four factors related to control over workers—such as hiring/firing, pay rate, scheduling, and record retention—as well as broader indicators of economic dependence.

The approach moves away from narrower interpretations that focused primarily on direct and immediate control, instead allowing consideration of additional factors that may indicate a shared employment relationship. DOL states that the goal is to provide clearer guidance to employers while ensuring workers receive protections guaranteed under federal law.

The proposal comes as the National Labor Relations Board is currently applying its 2020 joint employer standard under the National Labor Relations Act, which focuses more narrowly on direct and immediate control, following a federal court decision vacating the board’s 2023 rule. While the DOL’s rule applies under different statutes, the approaches reflect differing frameworks across agencies.

If finalized, the rule would establish updated guidance for how DOL enforces joint employer provisions across multiple labor statutes, with implications for a wide range of business relationships and workforce arrangements.

EPA EXTENDS POSTPONEMENT OF CERTAIN TCE RULE PROVISIONS

The EPA has extended the postponement of certain requirements in its final rule regulating trichloroethylene under the Toxic Substances Control Act, delaying implementation of specific workplace compliance obligations until ongoing litigation is resolved. (TCE is primarily used in manufacturing as an industrial solvent for degreasing metal parts.) The notice, published in the Federal Register on May 5, applies to provisions tied to TSCA Section 6(g) exemptions that permit limited temporary uses of TCE.

The TCE rule was finalized in December 2024 and originally scheduled to take effect in early 2025. The EPA has issued several delays since then, including a February 2026 extension that moved the effective date for the Section 6(g) exemption provisions to May 18. The latest action further postpones those requirements and links the delay to the completion of judicial review.

The original rule broadly prohibited the manufacture, processing, and commercial use of TCE while allowing a limited number of critical applications to continue temporarily under TSCA Section 6(g) exemptions. The current delay applies specifically to the "Workplace Chemical Protection Program" requirements and to compliance obligations associated with those exempted uses.

These provisions include exposure monitoring, respiratory protection, worker training, and other workplace controls for facilities continuing limited TCE operations under EPA-approved exemptions.

EPA emphasized that the broader restrictions on most commercial and consumer uses of TCE remain in place and continue to be subject to ongoing legal review. The latest action does not withdraw the underlying rule itself but instead pauses immediate compliance obligations for the specific exempted uses covered under Section 6(g).

The EPA cited ongoing litigation before the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Third Circuit as the reason for the delay. Petitioners have argued that portions of the rule impose significant compliance burdens and raise legal questions regarding the EPA’s workplace chemical protection requirements.

Under the latest notice, the EPA stated it intends to align the compliance timeline with the court’s schedule while also reconsidering aspects of the regulation through future rulemaking.
