The Real Story on the Current Economy: Who Do You Believe?

Dr. Chris Kuehl

Of course, the answer is to believe the intrepid FMA economist. On the other hand, we need to remember the quote from John Kenneth Galbraith:

"The sole function of economic forecasting is to make astrology look respectable."

The bottom line is that there is never a point when the economic data is stable as we are dealing with human behavior—and people are anything but stable.

The U.S. is a country so large and diverse that it can easily host both recession and growth; it all depends on what industry one is in and even what part of the country. Then there is the issue of Canada and Mexico, two nations that are deeply connected to the U.S. and are forced to react to what happens south and north of the border.

Now that all those disclaimers are out of the way, what is the prognosis? Are we headed for recession? Are we already in one? How deep and how long?

Let’s get the depressing stuff out of the way first, then we can finish with the optimistic assessments. Several sets of indicators have been routinely referenced as recessionary. The latest set of leading economic indicators produced by the Conference Board are sitting at the lowest level in 23 years. If one looks back over the past couple of decades, there have been periods when those indicators have been in similar positions: 2000-2001, 2007-2009, and 2020. The data thus far is not showing a decline as deep as in those years, but the slump is significant.

Given the importance of consumers in the U.S. economy, their performance is critical. At the moment, fully 65% of consumers are living paycheck to paycheck due to the surge of inflation over the last couple of years. For those making under $50,000, fully 75% are spending everything they make and have been sliding into debt.

There has been a significant level of caution and angst showing up in the banking sector as credit standards keep tightening. There has been a reduction of over $100 billion in lending activity, accelerated by the banking crisis in March. Lately, banks have been trying to sell off their commercial property loans (even at a loss) as they anticipate the market dropping further. They want to sell while there is still some demand for these investments.

The U.S. did manage to dodge the self-inflicted wound of a default by approving the rise in the debt ceiling, but now Treasury will be issuing and selling over $1 trillion in bonds, and that will have an impact on bond yields as well as other parts of the bond market. All this drama in the bond market has created another of those recessionary signs—an inverted bond market. This is when the relationship between the twoyear bond and the 10-year bond flip. The short-term bonds react to interest rate changes and the long-term bonds react more to prospects for economic growth. The investors think that inflation threats will keep the Fed pushing rates higher, and that slows the economy. The yields have been inverted for months, and an inversion often signals a recession or some kind of downturn.

Other Currents Tell a Di erent Story

By now you have all elected to take to your bed with a strong drink in hand. Is it as bad as all this would suggest? Maybe not. There are other currents that seem to be telling a different story, or at least a more complex one. We can start with the behavior of the labor market. In any other recessionary period over the last several decades, there have been big layoffs and a sharp spike in the rate of unemployment. Every month there have been grim forecasts asserting that the jobless rate is due to spike and that hiring would fall off. The economy added another 339,000 jobs in June. The unemployment rate moved up slightly, but all of that gain was from people in the U-6 category shifting to U-3. This means those discouraged workers counted in U-6 started to reenter the formal search for work and got counted in U-3. Businesses are still reluctant to lay people off as they have had such a difficult time finding these employees in the first place.

Another contradictory piece of data comes from corporate investment. Spending on capex is still setting records as companies need to expand to accommodate some of the big changes in the economy. Two major drivers for this investment have been expansion of robotics and technology as well as the surge in reshoring as companies have lost faith in the supply chain and the whole concept of just-in-time. If one can’t find the needed workers, there are few other options other than investing in robotics. The breakdown of the supply chain and suspicion regarding the future of China trade has convinced many to return production to the U.S. ($1 trillion worth last year alone).

Some sectors have not been performing very well, of course, but others are doing very well. Overall, the data on durable goods and factory orders has remained robust. In fact, much of the manufacturing data we look at shows solid performance, and even the declines are relatively minor.

Automotive Drives On

The performance of the automotive sector in particular continues to defy expectations. Every month there are assessments suggesting that growth will start to ebb, and for all the traditional reasons. The average price of a new car recently fell slightly (by 1.1%) but is still over $48,000. These high prices have created some unique challenges for the industry. The average length of a car loan is now 72 months, and that has meant more sensitivity to the banking sector. These longer loans mean more exposure for the lenders, and this affects their willingness to lend.

Oil Market in Its Usual State of Flux

As is usually the case, the oil and gas sector has a great deal of volatility. The radical ups and downs noted in demand has had a profound impact. Profits plummeted in 2020, but in the last year they have reached record levels as the oil market has reacted to production decisions and improved consumption. The U.S. is on target to produce between 14 million and 16 million barrels a day this year and into next. The U.S. is now the world’s largest oil producer at between 11 million and 12.5 million bpd, while Saudi Arabia is between 9 million and 10.5 million bpd.

The OPEC announcement regarding reduced production had very little impact on per-barrel prices, which have remained in the 70s (and often the low 70s). The Energy Information Administration (EIA) estimates this level will remain through this year and into next. Natural gas has reacted similarly. The price has hovered around $2.60 MM/BTU for most of the year. Demand is rising as temperatures rise, but production has been somewhat curtailed. There was record production of 102.5 billion cubic feet per day (bcf/d) in May, but the levels have fallen a bit since then.

There has been a significant shift in attitude affecting the development of both gas and oil. Investors sensed that demand for fossil fuels would plummet in the next couple of years due to increased interest in alternative fuels and the impact of the pandemic. These assumptions are starting to alter. The expected decline in demand did not occur at the level expected as the U.S. still consumes over 20 million bpd (down from 24 million bpd prior to the pandemic).

Natural gas consumption has also increased to pre-pandemic levels. This has stimulated investment in fossil fuel alongside the interest in alternatives. There is still a great deal more incentive money directed at alternatives, and there is opposition to more fossil fuel, but the industry as a whole is reacting to consumer demand. After a year of record profits, there is considerably more investment from the energy companies in the status quo from an energy standpoint.

What’s a Manufacturer To Do?

It is the same challenge that always seems to exist for manufacturers and fabricators. What industry do you engage with? Those that are aimed at automotive have been seeing fairly solid demand, and the same can be said for those oriented toward the energy sector or aerospace. The retail sector has been struggling, and so are many parts of the construction environment. Machinery investment has been constrained to some extent by concern over inflation and potential recession this year, but that has been countered by the need to introduce more robots and the expansion of reshoring.
