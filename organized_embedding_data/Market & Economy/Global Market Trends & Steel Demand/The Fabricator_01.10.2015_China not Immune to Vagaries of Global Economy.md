# China not Immune to Vagaries of Global Economy

[TARİH: 01.10.2015 The Fabricator]

By Dr. Chris Kuehl, FMA Economic Analyst

lack of Chinese demand

Sometimes it seems that nobody is really paying attention. A few weeks ago global markets flew into an orgy of chaos on the "news" that there was slower growth in China. Asian markets were unnerved, and soon this spread to Europe and the U.S. Those who have dealt with China for a while have been describing this slowdown for years and were shocked that anybody would be surprised at it. Growth rates in the double digits have not been seen since the recession, and the measures of the manufacturing sector (such as the various PMI surveys) have been showing weakness for most of the last six months.

What does it mean to the U.S. manufacturer now that the dust has settled and investors have started to regain their composure? Two major shifts will matter in the months ahead. First, China is once again trying to promote its export sector. For the last few years the goal has been to develop a stronger domestic economy and to that end the Chinese government has overseen an increase in wages for much of the working population. This was necessary if there was to be an opportunity to develop a consumer class, but it made China less competitive compared to other low-cost producers in Asia and elsewhere. It is now apparent China will not allow exports to slip much further, which means more exports directed at the U.S. market. It also means less importing from the U.S., although China has never been a huge market for most U.S. companies.

What does it mean to the U.S. manufacturer?

The second major shift resulting from the China crisis is that market chaos changed a great many assumptions as far as the U.S. and Europe are concerned. The expectation was that interest rates would be rising soon — perhaps steadily from this point. At the time of this writing, the Fed had not yet made its decision on rates. Before the China meltdown, analysts were nearly 100 percent certain that September would be the month for a quarter-point rise, but because of the global crisis, that assumption changed, with many predicting that rates would start to rise in December.

If rates did indeed change in September, there are still expectations of far slower interest rate shifts than would have been the case prior to the meltdown. Either way, the rise of the dollar has been slowed as no signal is apparent of the onset of an aggressive rate policy. U.S. manufacturers have been concerned about the value of the dollar throughout 2015, and it is slated to be a major issue in 2016 as well. The global market collapse may have been temporary, but it has fundamentally changed the opinion of analysts regarding the rise of the greenback—it is now assumed that an attempt will be made to keep the value low.

U.S. manufacturing is holding its own, but there are threats coming from outside U.S. control. Chinese goods are going to be cheaper, which will boost their exports to the U.S. Chinese demand for raw materials will diminish as the economy continues to adjust, which means the countries that have been dependent on those sales are struggling. These are the countries the U.S. sells to, and their struggles may soon become a problem for the U.S. Australia is already facing recession and so is Brazil, mostly due to the lack of Chinese demand.

The markets really should have been aware of all this, and perhaps some investors were. The issue seems to have been unfounded confidence in the ability of China’s leaders to maneuver their way out of anything that would affect them. The real lasting lesson from this crisis is that China is no more immune to the vagaries of the global economy than any other nation.
