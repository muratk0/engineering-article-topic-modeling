his year’s FAB 40 shows a dynamic industry at the heart of the U.S. economy’s hottest growth sectors, including the infrastructure surrounding AI. It’s also an industry managing inventory corrections and soft and sluggish sectors hurt by global uncertainty. Products that hinge on consumer confidence and interest rates also took a hit in 2025. Judging by FAB 40 survey responses, many softer markets in 2025 have turned much stronger in 2026. Meanwhile, demand in hot markets—warehousing automation, power generation, and everything AI-related—continues rising unabated.

It can be tempting to look at the FAB 40 just to see how the biggest are getting bigger. And yes, this year’s top players remain as big as ever. But some dramatic revenue growth farther down the list reveals just how robust the contract metal fabrication business has become.

The Shape of the FAB 40 Shifts

This year, the top two FAB 40 companies alone—MEC and Cadrex Manufacturing Solutions—account for more than $1 billion in annual sales. It’s no surprise that both are entrenched in infrastructure for AI, data centers, and power generation. After all, a big strategy behind Cadrex’s formation in 2021 (through numerous acquisitions) was to serve the growing need for IT and communications infrastructure. And last year, MEC’s acquisition of Accu-Fab increased the contract fabricator’s presence in the key data center market.

Even so, revenue changes between 2024 and 2025 among the largest companies weren’t dramatic. Some saw gains; others experienced minor declines. That reflects broader industry trends as contract metal fabricators navigate a mix of soft and high-growth sectors.

Look farther down the list, though, and you’ll see significant changes. This year the list ended at a company with annual sales of nearly $50 million— the highest revenue yet for the No. 40 position. The list does have a few new participants this year, which shifted the rankings a bit. But this alone doesn’t account for the significant upward revenue shift, especially among those ranked between No. 30 and No. 40. The No. 30 fabricator last year reported revenue of $57.2; this year, the No. 30 company reported $77 million.

The "shape" of the FAB 40 is changing. Years ago, the largest companies accounted for the vast majority of revenue. If contract metal fabrication followed consolidation trends of other industries, those top players would continue to grow, both organically and by buying up other players, leveraging the power of scale. This has happened to some degree—recent acquisitions are proof of that. But as the FAB 40 list shows, organic growth and consolidation aren’t concentrated solely among a few top players. There’s some concentration, of course, but not to the point where just a few companies dominate the entire sector.

A decade ago, the top few in the ranking showed very high revenue. Then, reported revenue got smaller very quickly as you went farther down the rankings. For instance, in 2016, No. 10 reported just $58 million, beginning the "long, thin tail" of smaller fabricators down the list. This year, No. 10 reports revenue of $219 million, and the revenue numbers don’t decline rapidly down the list. The FAB 40’s "tail" is getting fatter.

Defining the FAB 40

Although participation is voluntary, the FAB 40 still provides an insightful "slice" into an industry dominated by private companies. The list covers contract fabricators that make most of their revenue from sheet metal, plate, and tube fabrication processes: laser cutting, plasma cutting, punching, welding. They form metal with press brakes and plate rolls. They cut tube with saws, plasmas, and lasers, and they form them on rollers and rotary draw bending machines.

Many offer some structural beam fabrication services (beam cutting, drilling, bending, and welding), but it’s not a dominant revenue driver. It’s why you won’t see some of the country’s largest structural beam fabricators on the list.

Although many FAB 40 companies do offer metal stamping—a higher-volume and specialty complement to "soft-" and "flexible-tooling" processes— a diversified mix of metal fabrication work remains core to their business. The same can be said for other supporting processes like machining, blasting, powder coating, heat treatment, extruding, and numerous others. A unique technology mix helps establish a fabricator’s unique competitive position.

Of course, FAB 40 companies employ all sorts of automation, and the more flexible that automation is, the better. They automate around customer products, but they also automate around part families and a range of part geometries connected to multiple customers. They might develop or purchase plenty of special tools for press brakes, punch presses, and stamping presses, but product-specific tooling isn’t central to their business model.

All this might be obvious to veterans of the metal fabrication business, especially those who’ve been active for years in the Fabricators and Manufacturers Association and attend the FABTECH show every fall. Regardless, describing the FAB 40 reveals just how diverse this business is. Many on the FAB 40 compete with each other, of course, but many also play in very different sandboxes. A changing customer mix doesn’t just change a contract fabricator’s competitive position. It can affect how the entire business operates.

Ingredients for Growth

The trick to growth, be it organic or through acquisition, is to shape a customer mix so that it best suits an organization’s goals, considering the fabricator’s geographic reach, plant space, manufacturing expertise, information management, supply chain integration, and culture. All these elements can change, independently or in some combination. It’s why metal fabrication remains so dynamic.

Information management has become especially important. Better data management can introduce even more benefits to scaling up. Work shifts to where the capacity resides. Material yield skyrockets, as does on-time delivery and, ultimately, a company’s reputation for being a world-class manufacturer.

With all this comes varying customer experiences. Some involve serious design-for-manufacturability efforts, project management, and long-term arrangements; others involve quick quoting and quick-response prototypes. And there’s a full spectrum of experiences in between, depending on what customers require and the type of work being quoted.

All these elements work together to service a specific range of customers, which in turn help shape a contract metal fabricator’s culture. Again, a fabricator that specializes in large industrial projects is completely different from a piece-part fabricator with laser cutting, bending, and robotic welding. The lawn and garden sector has demands that differ from aerospace or medical device manufacturing. Mix two or more of these industries on one production floor, and you create a unique manufacturing environment.

Because the customer mix defines the contract fabricator, and potential customers span the economy— from the automotive aftermarket to massive pressure vessels, from tiny brackets to huge plates for ships and submarines—organic growth and industry consolidation occur in different ways, depending on the business entities involved. One fabricator simply can’t be everything to everyone. That—combined with unprecedented opportunities in AI, data centers, and power generation—explains the "fat tail" in this year’s FAB 40. Considering the industry’s deep connection with key growth markets in the U.S. economy, the FAB 40’s tail will likely grow fatter in the years to come.
