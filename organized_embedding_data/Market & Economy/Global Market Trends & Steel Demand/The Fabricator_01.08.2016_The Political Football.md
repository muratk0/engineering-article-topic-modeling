A

t the time of this writing, it is mid-July and the Republican Convention opened today with the Democratic Convention to follow. The political season is in full swing, and extra attention is being paid to the plight of the steel industry as the candidates jockey for position in Ohio, Michigan, Indiana, and Illinois, all key steelmaking states.

I anticipate much will be said and reported over the coming months regarding free trade, the loss of jobs here in the U.S., and the impact "dumped" steel—imported steel that is sold for less than what it costs to make—is having on the steel industry. You can expect to hear harsh words about China and its steel industry in the weeks and months to come.

However, there are always two sides to a story, and many times only one side is reported as the media reacts to comments from the presidential candidates. It is hard to tell the full story in a 30- or 60-second segment during a news program. The result is the country receives and perceives the news to be accurate when in fact it could be full of half-truths and outright misrepresentations of the truth.

I was traveling through Chicago’s Midway Airport recently and, as I approached the TSA officer checking credentials, he noticed that I had an emblem on my clothing with the word "steel" in it. He proceeded to tell me about how the Chinese are dumping steel into the U.S., ruining the domestic steel industry and costing Americans thousands of jobs.

At that point in time the Chinese had already been effectively blocked from shipping hot-rolled, galvanized, and cold-rolled product, as well as OCTG and many other steel products, into the country because of U.S. Department of Commerce and the International Trade Commission decisions. The domestic steel industry had filed and won a number of anti-dumping and countervailing duty suits against China and other foreign countries that had been exporting their steel to the U.S. at prices deemed to be "unfair" or "subsidized" by their governments.

For flat-rolled, a couple of trade suits (cold-rolled and hot-rolled) are still outstanding, but the Chinese have effectively seen their ability to export steel to the U.S. closed through the use of the trade laws resulting in prohibitive duties.

The steel industry loves to bash the Chinese steel industry and, in some cases, with justification. China produces 50 percent of the total steel made in the world. Industry sources have reported that the Chinese steel industry has as much as 400 million metric tons (440 million net tons) of excess capacity. Nonetheless, the Chinese steel mills continue to pump out product whether or not there is a home market for it.

The central Chinese government has promised to reduce production by 150 million metric tons over the next two years. Since that announcement was made, the Chinese steel industry has been setting production records, and exports are on pace to reach 115 million to 120 million metric tons in 2016. To put that into perspective, the 115 million metric tons (126 million net tons) is as much steel as the entire U.S. steel industry can produce in a year’s time. Keep in mind that the U.S. is the third-largest steel producer in the world (see

Since most of the steel industry in China is owned by the regional governments, the need to produce profits is not as important as the need to keep people working and happy. The country is essentially exporting its unemployment to the rest of the world.

As I said at the beginning of this article, there are always two sides to a story. The other side of the coin is foreign steel. In particular, when the Chinese were shipping steel to the U.S., they were selling it to U.S. consumers at prices significantly below the U.S. steel mills’ pricing. The benefactors of these low price levels were the manufacturing companies and fabricators as well as consumers in the U.S. and around the world.

Earlier this year "Steel Market Update" (SMU) published an article in which we quoted Daniel Pearson, the former chairman of the ITC and current senior fellow at the Cato Institute’s Herbert A. Stiefel Center for Trade Policy Studies. He told SMU, "Antidumping and countervailing duty (AD/CVD) measures are unable to fix the low-price problem afflicting U.S. steel producers because they amount to no more than a Band-Aid that can’t heal the wound."

In an article in the Cato Institute’s "Free Trade Bulletin," Pearson wrote that trade remedies make U.S.-produced steel cost more than the rest of the world, which hurts downstream manufacturers who rely on steel for their products.

Instead of focusing on the steel industry, Pearson suggested taking advantage of the more economically significant steel-consuming sector. Low-priced steel imports benefit U.S. manufacturers, and China should be encouraged to transfer its wealth to the U.S. through the export of steel at artificially low prices.

"The United States should change the dynamic of the debate by encouraging China to continue transferring wealth by selling all the low-priced steel it possibly can in this country," Pearson wrote. "That approach is likely to get the attention of Chinese policymakers and hasten the downsizing and restructuring that is so badly needed in that country’s steel sector."

Trade duties should be used only when economic analysis indicates they will benefit the economic welfare of the U.S., Pearson said. Low-priced steel imports may seem unfair to steel producers, but trade duties are even more unfair because of the cost to the steel-consuming sector.

Pearson noted that much of China’s steel overcapacity is due to government policies. The U.S. should find ways to take advantage of China’s propensity to operate mills at a loss instead of shutting them down.

"China’s decision to run its steel mills at negative rates of return means, in essence, that China is helping to increase the competitiveness of U.S. manufacturers that use steel as an input. In terms of the underlying economics, China takes the losses and the United States reaps the gains. What’s not to like about those circumstances?"

For now the U.S. government has taken the road most traveled, using the AD and CVD trade laws to build a wall around the U.S. to prevent cheaper foreign steel from coming into this country. The results of these suits are truly staggering as U.S. steel prices are $200-plus/ton higher than the rest of the world for hot-rolled steel. The pricing spread between domestically sourced cold-rolled and coated steels and hot-rolled base pricing, which normally is $100 to $120/ton, is now exceeding $200/ton. This puts a lot of pressure on domestic manufacturers, especially those that are competing in the international markets.

Over the coming months the steel industry will face challenges, such as new sources of foreign steel finding their way to the U.S. market. Simultaneously, manufacturing companies will struggle to justify making products in the U.S. as opposed to moving them to Mexico, Asia, or elsewhere. This leaves the steel mills trying to balance much-needed profits and efforts to keep U.S. manufacturers competitive in a world market.
