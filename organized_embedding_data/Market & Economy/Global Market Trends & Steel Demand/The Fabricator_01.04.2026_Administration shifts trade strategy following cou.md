# Administration shifts trade strategy following court decision

Around Washington

Tariffs continue under alternative statutory authorities

By

Omar S. Nashashibi

I

n a 6-3 decision issued Feb. 20, the U.S. Supreme Court ruled that the International Emergency Economic Powers Act (IEEPA) does not authorize the president to impose tariffs, invalidating a central legal basis for several recent U.S. trade actions.

Writing for the majority, Chief Justice John Roberts emphasized that tariff and revenue authority historically rests with Congress and must be clearly delegated by statute. The court determined that IEEPA’s language allowing the president to "regulate" imports does not extend to imposing tariffs—particularly sweeping tariffs of unlimited scope, rate, and duration.

Following the decision, the administration has begun shifting toward alternative statutory authorities to maintain its tariff program. On the same day as the Supreme Court decision, the White House announced a temporary 10% ad valorem import duty under Section 122, which took effect Feb. 24 and can remain in place for up to 150 days. The administration said the measure is intended to address "fundamental international payment problems" associated with persistent U.S. trade imbalances. The duty has several exclusions, including certain pharmaceuticals, aerospace products, critical minerals, many vehicles, goods already covered by Section 232 tariffs, and trade with Canada and Mexico in compliance with the U.S.-Mexico-Canada Agreement (USMCA).

The court’s ruling does not affect tariffs imposed under other statutory authorities. Duties remain in place under Section 301 tariffs and under Section 232 derivative measures. As a result, many imported industrial inputs continue to face elevated duties.

The Section 122 tariffs expire at 12:01 a.m. ET on July 24, at which time the administration is expected to pivot to imposing tariffs on countries using Section 301 and on specific products under the Section 232 national security law.

SUPREME COURT SEEKS UPDATES ON PLAN TO REFUND TARIFFS

U.S. Customs and Border Protection officials told the U.S. Court of International Trade that the agency will need roughly 45 days to establish a workable refund process to return duties imposed under the IEEPA. CBP is updating its Automated Commercial Environment system so refunds can be issued on an importer-wide basis rather than through tens of millions of entry-specific payments. The agency said the new functionality is intended to streamline payments and could allow refunds to begin once the system changes are completed.

Initially, the judge assigned to all IEEPA cases at the CIT ordered the administration to begin processing refunds. In recent proceedings, the CIT signaled that refunds do not need to begin immediately, granting CBP additional time after the agency said it could not quickly process the large volume of repayments. CBP told the court that it faces an unprecedented task involving more than 330,000 importers and over 53 million entries, representing roughly $166 billion in duties collected under the now-invalid tariffs.

The Supreme Court’s February ruling held that IEEPA does not authorize tariffs, requiring the government to refund collected duties with interest. Interest on those refunds is currently accruing at roughly $650 million per month and could reach around $10 billion if the refunds are not completed by the end of the year.

USITC LOOKS AT ECONOMIC IMPACT OF ENDING CHINA PNTR

The U.S. International Trade Commission has launched a new investigation to assess the economic implications of revoking China’s Permanent Normal Trade Relations (PNTR) status. The study, required by Congress in the FY 2026 appropriations law, will evaluate how removing PNTR treatment for Chinese imports could affect U.S. industries, employment, supply chains, and consumer prices. The commission is expected to deliver its report to lawmakers by Aug. 21.

China received PNTR status in 2000, ensuring that its exports to the U.S. are subject to the same lower most-favored-nation tariff rates applied to other trading partners under World Trade Organization rules. If PNTR were withdrawn, Chinese imports would instead face the significantly higher Column 2 tariff rates in the U.S. tariff schedule, which could substantially raise duties on many products. The only other countries subject to the Column 2 rates are Russia, Belarus, Cuba, and North Korea.

According to the investigation notice published in the Federal Register, the commission will examine several policy scenarios, including the full removal of PNTR treatment for all Chinese goods, as well as phased tariff increases on selected products over multiple years. The analysis also will consider potential shifts in sourcing, trade flows, and domestic manufacturing activity resulting from higher tariffs.

The USITC is accepting written comments through April 13, though it will not hold a public hearing because of the statutory timeline for completing the report. The study is expected to inform ongoing congressional debate over the future of U.S.-China trade relations and potential changes to China’s trade status.

USITC BEGINS NEW REVIEW OF USMCA AUTOMOTIVE ORIGIN RULES

The USITC has initiated a new fact-finding investigation to assess the economic and supply-chain impacts of the automotive rules of origin contained in the USMCA. The inquiry will contribute to the commission’s third report to Congress evaluating how the agreement’s auto provisions are functioning in practice. That report, due by July 1, 2027, is part of a broader series of five mandated assessments scheduled to run through 2031.

The investigation will analyze how USMCA’s origin requirements are influencing production patterns, sourcing strategies, employment, and investment across the North American automotive sector. The commission also will evaluate how the rules are affecting regional competitiveness and whether the existing framework continues to reflect changes in vehicle design and manufacturing, including the industry’s transition toward electrifc vehicles and more advanced production technologies.

As part of the information-gathering process, the commission will hold a public hearing on Oct. 14. Parties wishing to testify must submit requests to appear by Sept. 29. The USITC also is inviting written comments and statements from stakeholders, with Nov. 2 set as the deadline for written submissions.

EPA WITHDRAWS GREENHOUSE GAS ENDANGERMENT FINDING

In a final rule published in the Federal Register on Feb. 18, the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) formally rescinded its 2009 determination that greenhouse-gas (GHG) emissions endanger public health and welfare. The action also removes federal GHG emission standards that applied to light-, medium-, and heavy-duty vehicles and engines beginning with model year 2012.

The 2009 endangerment finding concluded that six GHGs—including carbon dioxide, methane, nitrous oxide, and several fluorinated gases— endanger public health and welfare and that emissions from new motor vehicles contribute to that pollution. Although the finding was issued under Clean Air Act Section 202(a) to support vehicle regulation, it became the legal foundation for a wide range of federal climate rules beyond transportation. The EPA subsequently relied on the determination to justify GHG standards for power plants, industrial sources, and other sectors under multiple Clean Air Act programs.

In the final rule, the EPA states that Section 202(a)(1) does not authorize regulation of GHGs in response to global climate change concerns. The agency cited statutory interpretation and recent judicial precedent, including the "major questions" doctrine, in concluding that the Clean Air Act does not clearly delegate such authority for economy-wide climate regulation through vehicle standards.

The EPA emphasized that the action does not involve issuing a new scientific determination about climate change. Instead, the agency characterized the rule as a legal reinterpretation of the Clean Air Act’s scope. As a result, the rule withdraws the earlier conclusion that GHG emissions from new motor vehicles contribute to air pollution that endangers public health and welfare.

With the rescission, the EPA repeals regulatory provisions governing GHG standards for vehicles beginning with model year 2012, along with associated requirements related to certification, compliance, and emissions performance. The agency stated that broader climate policy decisions should be addressed by Congress rather than through administrative rulemaking under the Clean Air Act.

The rule is scheduled to take effect April 20.

EPA FINALIZES ROLLBACK OF BIDEN-ERA MERCURY POWER PLANT RULE

The EPA has finalized a rule concluding that the strengthened Mercury and Air Toxics Standards (MATS) issued during the President Joe Biden’s administration are not warranted under the Clean Air Act’s "necessary and appropriate" test. The decision reverses portions of the 2024 rule that tightened emission limits for coal- and oil-fired power plants.

The EPA said the original MATS adopted in 2012 have already produced substantial reductions in mercury and other hazardous air pollutants from the power sector. After reviewing the updated requirements, the agency determined that the additional compliance obligations imposed by the 2024 revisions would generate relatively limited environmental gains while imposing significant costs on power producers.

While the agency acknowledged analyses suggesting that emissions could rise without the stricter limits, it concluded that the marginal environmental benefits associated with the revised standards were uncertain and did not outweigh potential economic and grid-reliability impacts. The determination reflects the administration’s broader reassessment of power-sector regulations and a renewed focus on cost considerations when evaluating Clean Air Act requirements.

Environmental organizations and several states are expected to challenge the rule in court, setting up another legal battle over how EPA should weigh costs, technology assessments, and public health considerations when determining whether hazardous-air-pollution standards are "necessary and appropriate" under Clean Air Act Section 112.

The final rule repealing the MATS was published in the Federal Register on Feb. 24 and is effective on April 27.
