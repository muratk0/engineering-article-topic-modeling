T

he existence of a U.S. government that helps manufacturers export goods overseas is in jeopardy. The determination by some Republicans in the House to hamstring the U.S. Export-Import Bank (also referred to as the Ex-Im Bank) has been fueled by a desire to end what critics of the bank call “corporate welfare.” An example of this that is frequently cited is the support provided to large companies such as Boeing.

But the bank extends loans and other financing to many different-sized companies, all of which win big contracts overseas, which often translates into big business for metalworking companies. That was part of the point Steven Wilburn made to the House Financial Services Committee at a recent hearing. Wilburn is the CEO of a company called FirmGreen that commercializes new energy technologies.

In 2010 Gás Verde SA of Brazil invited FirmGreen to compete for a bid to upgrade a system for tapping landfill gas. The French firm Air Liquide and the German firm Linde—both multibillion-dollar international conglomerates—also were invited to submit proposals. At the time FirmGreen’s relationship with its major investment bank was tenuous because of the damage the bank experienced as a result of the 2008 recession. So Wilburn approached the Ex-Im Bank, which extended the company a loan and ultimately enabled FirmGreen to win the $32 million biogas project.

FirmGreen wasn’t the only U.S. company to garner business because of the winning bid. Other companies now involved with the Brazilian project are Pipe Valves Inc., Columbus, Ohio; DeFabCo., Columbus; Columbus Fasteners Corp., Columbus; Advance Industrial Manufacturing, Grove City, Ohio; Scioto Valve & Fitting Co., Westerville, Ohio; and Buckeye Fabricating Co., Springboro, Ohio.

The Ex-Im Bank receives no federal funding, making GOP objections a bit hard to fathom, especially because other countries have strong export credit authorities that back their own domestic companies. The Ex-Im Bank provides direct loans, loan guarantees, working capital guarantees, and export credit insurance.

In 2012 Congress reauthorized the Ex-Im Bank and that authorization expires on Sept. 30.

EPA Set to Revisit Ozone Standard

A federal court decision has forced the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) to consider tightening its ozone standard with a proposed rule now scheduled for December.

Ozone is often referred to as smog. It is created when emissions of nitrogen oxide and volatile organic compounds (VOCs) combine in the presence of sunlight. Ozone creates a variety of health problems. The current ozone standard of 0.075 parts per million was set during the Bush administration in 2008.

Like all EPA air emission standards, the ozone standard is supposed to be reviewed for possible updating every five years. That has not happened. Environmentalists filed a lawsuit in 2013 attempting to force the agency’s hand, and a federal judge in San Francisco did just that, mandating the EPA issue a proposed rule in December.

Currently, 46 cities, counties, or some other jurisdictional entities are out of compliance with the 0.075 PPM standard, which means their state must come up with policies to reduce nitrogen oxide and VOC emissions. Those restrictions often extend to new permitting requirements for industrial facilities.

Early in President Obama’s first term, then EPA Administrator Lisa Jackson publicly acknowledged that the 2008 ozone standards were scientifically and legally “indefensible.” As a result, EPA proposed to strengthen the standards in January 2010. After various delays, EPA was poised to finalize new standards in September 2011, but Obama directed EPA to delay the standards. Instead, the president said EPA would review and revise the standards by the Clean Air Act’s five-year mandatory review deadline of March 2013.

Not only did that not happen, but the EPA also has failed to finalize the state implementation plan (SIP) guidelines for the 2008 standard. Those have not yet been published, causing confusion and anger among the states that must submit SIP plans—based on the 2008 standard—by March 2015.
