# Steel buyers frustrated with market uncertainty created by tariff rollout

[TARİH: 01.05.2025 The Fabricator]

Steel News

Strong support for tariffs could erode if chaos continues

MICHAEL COWDEN

T

he tariff roller coaster continues. Where it stops, I don’t pretend to know. Does the steel market like the ride? That depends on who you ask and when. As best as I can tell, a fair amount of support for Section 232 tariffs extended further downstream. Those tariffs benefited U.S. mills in a significant way in 2018. And to companies further downstream, it seemed only fair that they received some of the benefits their mill suppliers had seen.

But approval for tariffs seems to have fallen as they have become shorthand, not for Section 232, but for Liberation Day and its reciprocal tariffs. We’ve even got some numbers to support that.

Not Anti-Trump

Before I dig into those figures, I want to be clear that Steel Market Update (SMU) is not exactly a hotbed of anti-Donald Trump activists.

At Steel Summit in August, 63% of those who participated in a snap poll said Trump would win the White House. That squared with the 58% of survey respondents who predicted a Trump victory just before the election.

We saw a similar result in August 2020 during a (virtual) Steel Summit. Fifty-three percent said they supported Trump, 41% Biden, and 6% third parties. You can even go back to 2016, when it was 48% Trump, 22% Clinton, and the rest third parties.

If Trump and tariffs are now almost synonymous, you would think there should be about 55% to 65% support for them, right?

Maybe Anti-Tariff

That’s not what we’re seeing.

Steel buyers aren’t so much anti-tariffs, but they are beginning to exhibit frustration with the haphazard way the White House is executing these trade decisions.

Wassam Siddique/iStock/Getty Images Plus

We started asking a simple question in our weekly surveys in mid-March, the week after renewed Section 232 tariffs went into place and two to three weeks ahead of Liberation Day on April 2. How are the tariffs affecting your business?

Approximately 25% said tariffs were helping their business.

40% say they were hurting business.

35% weren’t sure.

The number of people who said tariffs were hurting business has been relatively consistent. But we’ve seen the number of people saying tariffs were helping business drop from roughly 30% to about 20%. We’ve also seen the number of people who were unsure of their impact increase from about 30% to closer to 40%.

In other words, sentiment about tariffs has turned increasingly negative among a readership that mostly supported Trump. It’s not like Trump made any secret that he was going to enact tariffs. So it might not be about the tariffs themselves. I think it’s more the scope of them, the chaotic rollout, and the uncertainty that they’ve injected into the market.

Survey Says Uncertainty Sucks

Scroll through the comments people submit to us in our surveys, and you’ll find the word "uncertainty" mentioned a lot:

"At first definitely helping, but not so sure now."

"Current uncertainty is hurting our business, as nobody ever likes uncertainty."

"The market understands how to operate in a steel tariff environment but not in this new uncertain climate of tariffs on everything. The steel marketplace is paralyzed."

Flip-Flops Do Too

Stocks have staged relief rallies when the Trump administration has indicated it might soften the blow of tariffs on U.S. consumers. On April 9, for example, when Trump announced a 90-day pause on the highest reciprocal tariffs and reduced the levy to 10% for most countries.

But markets also can melt down again when the tariffs—and trade tensions—are ratcheted back up again. That’s what happened on April 10, when the president increased tariffs on Chinese goods to 145%. China then said on April 11 that it would tariff U.S. goods at 125%.

The wild swings around Liberation Day tariffs—and U.S. tariff policy in general—get the most attention. But maybe we shouldn’t have been surprised. If there is one constant in Trump 2.0, it’s the flip-flopping. Case in point: We saw blanket tariffs on Canada go from nothing to 25%, to 50%, and back to nothing in the span of about a day in early March.

It’s not only tariffs. We experienced something similar in April with Nippon Steel’s proposed acquisition of U. S. Steel. Trump ordered a new national security review of the deal. Investment firm Ancora dropped its bid to replace U. S. Steel CEO David Burritt with former Stelco CEO Alan Kestenbaum, citing the increased likelihood of Nippon Steel closing the deal. And then Trump turned around and said he just didn’t like the idea of U. S. Steel being owned by a Japanese company.

The flip-flop on U. S. Steel seemed to be based more on nostalgia than any current market dynamic. And sometimes I wonder how much of that nostalgia is informing some of Trump’s tariff policies.

Too Much Nostalgia?

Some of Trump’s tariffs are designed in theory to support steel. But is it to support the steel industry of today or the steel industry of the 1980s? As SMU has reported, the reciprocal tariffs were applied to metallics, including pig iron from Brazil and direct reduced iron (ore that has had oxygen removed from it) from Trinidad and Tobago, key feedstocks for certain domestic electric arc furnace mills.

My understanding is that the tariffs also apply to feedstock for direct reduced iron (DRI) and hot briquetted iron (HBI) plants—pellets from Sweden and Brazil, for example. That means companies making DRI and HBI in the U.S. (something you’d think we want to encourage) will have to pay more for their feedstock.

In addition, I’ve heard that certain mills are leery of continuing to source iron ore from Canada, which has ample reserves, because of concerns that the tariffs on our northern neighbor could change on a dime.

Ships and trains move slower than social media, Trump’s preferred means of changing trade policy. That’s a recipe for supply chain problems.
