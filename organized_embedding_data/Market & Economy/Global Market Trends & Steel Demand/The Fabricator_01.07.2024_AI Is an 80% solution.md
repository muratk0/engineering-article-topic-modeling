# AI Is an 80% solution

[TARİH: 01.07.2024 The Fabricator]

Biz Talk

It can get you 80% there—and that’s incredible

Tim Heston

I

recently picked up a Father’s Day card that made me cackle.

Happy Day of Fathers to you. Family is the true present. May you grill! Lawn mower.

On the inside, it read,

I had AI write you a Father’s Day card. Pretty cool huh?

That sums up society’s curious view of artificial intelligence, a technology in its infancy that tries hard but sometimes just gets it wrong. AI can seem amusing but harmless. It can also seem like it’s about to get us all, obliterate any semblance of privacy, and make all jobs obsolete.

That said, AI isn’t going anywhere. Just look at NVIDIA and the performance of other AI stocks. Sure, we could be in an AI bubble, though probably not one as large as the dot-com bubble of the 1990s. Even so, the internet changed life as we knew it, for good and ill. The same could be said of AI.

When Gene Marks, TV commentator and columnist for Forbes, spoke in March at this year’s Fabricators and Manufacturers Association (FMA) Annual Meeting, he described various AI use cases—and how AI can come up with bizarre solutions and get it all wrong. AI then starts, to use industry jargon,

hallucinating

. That leads to odd, and sometimes funny, solutions.

Hearing about how computers now can hallucinate, apparently, the metal fabricators in the audience laughed and shook their heads. Still, after his funny remarks, Marks implored them to take AI seriously. Despite its flaws, it has some real potential, and fabricators are just starting to tap into it.

"We’re seeing the best use cases for AI coming out of the front end. We had a lot of interest, and we made the choice to cultivate that interest. People are meeting, and they’re now starting to use tools inside and outside of work. In fact, one way to really learn about these tools is by using them outside of your day-to-day work."

That was Mike McManus, director of IT at Kapco Metal Stamping, a Grafton, Wis.-based metal manufacturer with a long history of metal fabrication complementing its stamping operations. In a story we’ll tell in full next month, cross-functional teams are just now starting to see what tools like the enterprise edition of ChatGPT and Microsoft Copilot can do for them.

Most important, they’re considering the time

AI tools actually save

. We might think about, say, AI writing a PowerPoint or a business proposal—then pick up a card at the Hallmark store.

Happy Day of Fathers to you

. How ridiculous, we think.

Back to the FMA Annual Meeting, though, Marks made a good point: Yes, the technology can’t do everything, but it can take you 80% of the way there. A PowerPoint might take, say, two hours to create. AI can do 80% of it almost immediately, and it takes less than 30 minutes to bring the presentation to the finish line. That shaved 90 minutes off a presentation-building "cycle time."

Such productivity improvements on the floor would be looked at as astounding. An automatically generated PowerPoint, not so much. After all, fabricators aren’t paid for making sales or business strategy presentations.

Or are they? Presentations aren’t all meaningless. They can help land new business or steer a fab shop toward overlooked opportunities. Machine productivity metrics don’t count for much if those machines have nothing to produce.

When people think of AI on the shop floor, they usually think about making manufacturing technology smarter and adaptable. But if you look at, say, a Gantt chart detailing the manufacturing timeline, you see that jobs spend most of their time as work-in-process (WIP), waiting for the next manufacturing step.

"We’re seeing the best use cases for AI coming out of the front end. We had a lot of interest, and we made the choice to cultivate that interest."

—Mike McManus, Kapco Metal Stamping

WIP absorbs inherent variability. Schedulers don’t know exactly how long something will take, so they build in those WIP buffers. Some of this has to do with variability in setup, which is where streamlined changeovers play a big role through a combination of automation (automatic tool change, nozzle change, color change in powder coating, etc.) and some basic lean manufacturing practices.

But some of it comes through variability inherent in manufacturing. People aren’t machines, and they don’t produce products to exact cycle times. Combine this with other less predictable factors, like delivery from outside suppliers and service providers, and you get a lot of variation. To absorb it all, you need that WIP sitting between work centers.

Now imagine a shop in which everything flows from one workstation to the next, with minimal wait time between value-adding operations. That could happen with AI. One day, AI could help shops develop the "perfect" schedule, where everything moves in a grand ballet of efficiency, from the initial quote to the final shipment. Work always stays on the move.

That ideal might not be achievable, but it’s worth striving for, and AI could help get us there. Today, developers already use AI to streamline their code-writing. Imagine a world where operators give feedback to shop management software, which in turn

perfects itself

over time. This could create a platform that stitches shop operations together to perfect the flow of value. Lead times shrink from four weeks to four days to maybe even four hours.

To be fair, a lot of these improvements don’t need fancy algorithms, just some good observation, standard work instructions, standardized data collection, good worker training, and solid communication between managers and operators on different shifts. (Simple … right?) If something isn’t measured, it can’t be improved upon.

Regardless, AI and related tech could take such improvements to the next level and unleash efficiency people didn’t know they had. It’s a little like generative AI automating those front-office tasks. Like WIP sitting idle on the floor, writing presentations takes time. Using AI to eliminate 80% of that time could unleash some serious potential.
