# Now on deck, the 2026 economy

[TARİH: 01.11.2025 The Fabricator]

Fabrinomics | Economic Insights

A forecast that covers all bases

Dr. Chris Kuehl

This is the time of year that economists try to cover all the bases (not to mention their own rear ends). There has been a lot going on this year, and the media has been chockful of doom and gloom, as this kind of reporting sells.

The data is not painting a picture of disaster, though weakness is clearly evident in certain sectors. The sense now is that business will have to be nimble and careful, but there remains a possibility for steady growth.

That said, volatility remains the dominant theme. A report commissioned for EY asserted that the last 10 years have seen an almost continuous series of significant economic disruptions that have cost the Fortune 100 close to $400 billion in profits. There have been pandemics, a collapse of the London gold market, the Ukraine war, tariff and trade wars, and natural disasters that have been coming faster than ever and with more impact.

Growth Amid Policy-driven

Disruption. Many of these economic reactions have been manmade, and that makes the situation even more frustrating. The volatility stems largely from policy shifts. Tariffs are being deployed as negotiating tools rather than as some sort of new trade policy, and that means they shift according to the negotiations. The negotiations are often unrelated to the industry affected by the tariffs, and that makes strategic planning awkward.

Despite all this confusion and trepidation, the economy has been growing at an impressive pace. The miserable numbers in Q1 were triggered by an excess of imports, but Q2 bounced back, and Q3 is currently sporting a growth rate of 3.9%.

Fed Talk

. The inflation rate has remained higher than preferred, and we’re seeing emerging signs of a slowdown. The latest Fed move was to lower rates by a quarter point, and there were hints of another rate cut by December. The problem is that an inflation rate above 3.0% usually convinces the Fed to hike rates rather than lower them. The job market motivated the Fed to lower rates. This has been tricky to navigate: The data right now is not all that bad, but certain trends are creating confusion.

The yield on the U.S. 10-Year Treasury—a key benchmark for long-term borrowing and mortgage rates—has also been unusually volatile, reflecting broader market uncertainty. The efforts to address the yield concern has been hampered by the mounting deficit. The recent tax and spending bill added about $4.1 trillion to the debt, which prompted rating agencies to downgrade the 10-year bond. The Federal Reserve closely monitors inflation using the Personal Consumption Expenditures (PCE) index, which tracks actual consumer spending and costs. As of now, overall inflation stands at 2.9%, above the Fed’s stated target of 2.0%.

Some at the Fed have talked of stagflation, although that remains a minority opinion. This is a very rare situation where the economy suffers from both inflation and recession at the same time. There is no real evidence that this has developed, but the potential is there.

At their last meeting, the Fed decided to cut rates by a quarter point, and the consensus opinion is that there will be another quarter-point reduction by the end of the year. The critical factor in that decision was Chris Waller, a Fed governor who shifted his stance and favored that reduction. He had been more of a hawk in prior meetings.

Trade and the Supply Chain

. Supply chain issues seem to change every few months. Last year it was some combination of physical issues, such as drought, that limited the use of the Panama Canal, or the attacks by the Houthi that essentially shut down the Red Sea and the Suez Canal. Weather played a role as well. Then came the politics, with the impact of the trade war and tariffs affecting the pace of imports and exports. The use of the Red Sea is still limited, but most shippers have adjusted to new routes (albeit more expensive ones).

The global economic slowdown, which has manifested in the last year, has depressed prices a bit, but some of this decline has been offset by the reactions to tariffs. Earlier in the year, the rush to beat the tariffs created a lot of congestion on both outbound and inbound ports, but that eased considerably over the summer.

The 90-day moratorium on several tariffs expired in July, triggering another surge in imports. Air cargo rates spiked sharply, with many major retailers chartering flights to expedite shipments—largely avoiding maritime routes due to concerns that ocean freight would not arrive in time to avoid the next wave of tariffs.

Trade tensions between the U.S. and China are not expected to ease anytime soon. The tariff escalation has affected bilateral trade, curtailing the flow of goods between the two nations and triggering widespread supply shortages. The U.S. typically imports more than $450 billion in goods from China annually.

Pushback is coming from lawmakers in states sensitive to imports and exports. The farm states are worrying about where they will sell their output; the manufacturing states are worried about their supply chains. Congress is trying to ease the financial impact, with subsidies offered to farmers as well as tax breaks for some manufacturers. More U.S. attention has been focused on nations that are engaged in transshipping, as this has been a tactic China has used to dodge these tariffs.

At the first of the year, many expected much higher inflation due to the tariffs and other trade issues, but this did not manifest as expected. Some companies simply ate the majority of the extra cost in an attempt to protect market share. Others found alternative sources, which worked until the U.S. started to impose restrictions on those sources as well. Many decided to reduce margins to keep prices stable. All of these tactics started to fade during the summer months, and now the inflation hit has started to manifest.

Many suggest that tariffs will eventually add more than 1% to the inflation rate, but the full effect varies across sectors. Product categories directly affected by tariffs will experience inflation between 10% and 20%, perhaps more. However, broader inflationary pressures have been partially offset by falling energy prices and easing wage growth.

U.S. agriculture remains a sector at risk as exports to China face ongoing barriers. Breakthroughs in trade talks could lead to a rapid improvement in international economic activity—provided there is alignment among negotiating parties. Reshoring initiatives in response to trade pressure could yield long-term economic benefits, but those gains are more likely to show up in 2026 or 2027, depending on how quickly supply chains adapt.

Commodity prices always seem to remain unpredictable and flexible as they respond to pressures from both demand and supply. The trade wars have impacted the supply chain as many nations have been using their commodity position as leverage.

China has been restricting access to rare earth materials, such as germanium, to force the U.S. to relax some of the tariffs, and that strategy has been working very well. China now has lower tariffs than India and the EU. China continues to push to keep factories running at full capacity—not to meet global demand, but to sustain employment. This overproduction floods markets. The U.S. dollar is not as weak as it has been, but it is still lower than in past years. That puts additional upward pressure on commodity prices.

The GDP into 2026

. Attention now focuses on 2026, and the opinions are mixed. It all depends on two factors: the rate of inflation and the rate of unemployment. As of now, the job numbers remain solid. The overall rate has gone up slightly, from 4.1% to 4.3%, but that’s still well below average. The trade war will have an impact, as there will be an impact on inflation, but there will also likely be an economic slowdown.

The GDP numbers have been all over the place this year, whipsawed by all the tariff and trade controversy. The latest GDPNow data shows Q3 GDP at 3.9%. Chances are this number will decline by the time actual data is released, but the growth is real enough. Fully 54% of that growth has been attributed to consumer activity, and the majority of that consumer push has come from those in the upper third of income earners.

Looking ahead to 2026, real GDP growth projections vary between 1.0% and as high as 3.5%. That uncertainty stems from ongoing trade tensions as nobody seems to know what the prices for raw materials and commodities will be. That has stalled a lot of construction projects, but it also inhibits manufacturers.

There was a slight uptick in the recent industrial production numbers in the latest reading—a gain of 0.1% as compared to a decline of 0.4% in the prior month. Manufacturing output rose by 0.2% after a 0.1% decline in the previous month. Much of this gain stems from improved automotive production activity—a 2.6% gain.

Many have expected a slowdown in the automotive sector for months, but the demand has not collapsed, and the production rate is back to 16 million annually. The mining data moved up by 0.9% and utilities fell by 0.2%. Overall Industrial production was at 103.9% of its 2017 average.

Even capacity utilization numbers held steady at 77.4, about 2.2 points below its long-term average. By and large, the industrial and manufacturing sectors have been holding steady despite the tariff chaos—and maybe because of it. Many early orders in various sectors have been placed as a strategy to avoid the tariff hit.
