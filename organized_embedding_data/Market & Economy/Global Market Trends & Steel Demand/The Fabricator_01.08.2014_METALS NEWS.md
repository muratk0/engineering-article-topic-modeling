[TARİH: 01.08.2014 The Fabricator]

Summer shutdowns affect supply, demand

By

MetalPrices.com

staff

Strong domestic demand for flat-rolled steel helped to ease the downward pressure on shredded scrap prices in June and could keep prices for both shredded and busheling at current levels in the third quarter.

Domestic steel demand is strong, even though there is some weakness in hot-rolled coil prices. Raw steel production at the integrated mills remains low because of several blast furnace outages. These will keep the big mills’ output low into August, allowing the flat-rolled mini-mills to be busy throughout the summer.

Competition could increase when the integrated mills come back at full strength and begin to recover market share they lost to flat-rolled mini-mills. Add to that industry concerns about the rising volume of imported steel. Steel imports totaled more than 4 million tons in May, and some believe they could continue at that pace if U.S. steel prices remain at current levels.

But steel imports this year also included much-needed transfusions of slabs from Canadian mills to their corporate cousins in the Midwest when they were suffering iron ore anemia in the spring or had operational problems. Some see domestic steelmakers’ complaints about unfair trade as disingenuous, since those April imports included more semi-finished slabs and billets for the steel mills.

Still, most expect strong demand for steel throughout the second half, so much so that some mini-mills pushed forward their scheduled maintenance shutdowns to participate in that expected steel boom.

Ferrous Scrap

Shredded steel was in oversupply as the summer began. Dealers cut their buying prices for light iron and old cars, but the flow did not taper off. Steelmakers reduced their buying prices for the fragmented scrap by $20 per gross ton at the start of the June buying cycle, while their offers for other obsolete grades like No. 1 heavy melt were off by only $5 to $10 per ton. Dealers began to resist those steeper price cuts for their shredded steel and forced some mills to modify their reductions.

Busheling prices were unchanged in June but were expected to inch upward in July. Flat-rolled steel demand and prices are still strong. Also, July plant shutdowns for vacations and retooling reduced the supply of industrial steel scrap.

Currently the auto industry selectively shuts down a few facilities for a week or two. These rolling shutdowns don’t reduce industrial scrap supplies as dramatically as they did in the past, but they lower the overall volume. Higher prices don’t draw out more industrial scrap like they do for obsolete scrap. Instead, mills must outbid each other by offering dealers more money.

Obsolete scrap flows have been strong since winter ended, but dealers could see a drop in supply as temperatures rise in the summer. Peddlers will park their pickup trucks and shopping carts when the thermostats reach 90 degrees F and higher. Three years ago, a 10-day heat wave that raised temperatures to a toasty 100 degrees for several days in a row created a ferrous scrap famine in midsummer. Likewise, East Coast exporters are not selling as much scrap into the domestic market as they did earlier this year. Exporters are seeing a modest rebound in the containerized scrap export market, but bulk cargo sales are weak.

The decline in shredded prices, however modest, has increased busheling’s price premium over shredded. That could drive steelmakers’ scrap optimizer buying programs to recommend using more shredded. Ironically, the shredded price slide in June boosted the value of lower-quality heavy melt to the same level as shredded in some regions. This rare pricing anomaly may have spurred dealers to reject the lower offers for shredded.

Forecasting base metal prices for third-quarter 2014 will be challenging on many fronts. For one, summer months traditionally mean factories slow down for vacations, particularly in Europe, which lowers demand. Second, markets are being buffeted by outside factors affecting supply and demand.

Aluminum

Like a perfect storm, the turmoil underpinning the aluminum market will have an inadvertent effect on prices by year-end. The market did not respond to the London Metal Exchange’s (LME’s) recent appeal to a court decision favoring Russian aluminum producer Rusal. The court’s decision to curb the LME’s plan to reduce waiting times at registered warehouses is nonetheless supporting and providing a backdrop for further increases to the P1020 premium.

Prices additionally are supported by Indonesia’s restrictions on bauxite exports; Indonesia supplies 10 percent of bauxite globally. This policy is adding support for further price increases going into the third quarter.

Conversely, prices for finished products may be tempered because much of the aluminum demand has been met for the balance of the year. The auto and beverage industries have their positions covered. Although there are reports of record deficits, aluminum stocks are at all-time highs, which might temper price increases on the LME. Regardless, P1020 premiums are showing strength and supporting scrap prices.

Another challenge for the physical side of the business is the sharp price move on the LME North American Secondary Aluminum Alloy Contract. The steep price increase has been an incentive for traders and dealers to sell A380.1 into LME warehouses, causing ingot prices to rise more than 3 percent. Secondary alloys closely related to A380.1 posted gains, but they were modest in comparison.

Copper

Forecasting exchange prices for high-grade copper and copper scrap is just as problematic. Although the market has been trading within a range, there have been marked price fluctuations. Contributing to the price moves was the announced first-quarter weak U.S. growth data, causing a weak dollar and inflating the value of copper. In addition, warehouse stocks are continuing to go down, and the International Copper Study Group reported a first-quarter copper deficit of 205,000 metric tons. Also, summer is a slow consuming season for China, and recent concerns of a Chinese trading company using copper as collateral, along with an expected slowdown in its GDP, are adding confusion to the market.

By contrast, copper scrap is still tight in both Europe and the U.S., with spreads holding firm. The clutter adds up to a speculators’ market. Look for rises in metal prices as money flows in, then profit-taking, staying in a moderate range. The U.S. is the world’s second-largest copper consumer, while China remains the first.

Lead

Lead prices were relatively flat through the second quarter, with the material expected to trade sideways throughout the summer. Demand usually drops in the warmer weather; however, with European car production in good shape, demand for batteries will continue.

Nickel

Nickel has been one of the strongest base metal performers on the LME this year. Prices had slipped to a four-year low by the end of December 2013. This year, however, the trend has reversed, and nickel has risen nearly 30 percent since January.

Contributing to the price increase are Indonesia’s supply issues. As the world’s second-largest producer and exporter of nickel ore, its export ban at the start of the year resulted in the loss of more than 20 percent of the global nickel supply, in the form of laterite ore. Another catalyst is the ongoing tension between Ukraine and Russia, which is home to the world’s largest nickel company, MMC Norilsk Nickel. Prices have had additional support as sanctions introduced by the U.S. and the EU created uncertainty in the physical market.

Earlier this year a short supply of nickel units for stainless steel production could not fulfill strong demand for the material. The largest increase in demand came from the U.S., with imported European scrap satisfying demand from Outokumpu’s new stainless steel mill in Alabama. Type 304 scrap prices have risen nearly 25 percent since the beginning of the year, but have remained relatively flat since mid-May.

Many are expecting stainless steel growth for the remainder of this year and into 2015. According to the International Stainless Steel Forum (ISSF), global stainless melt shop production for 2013 was 38.1 million metric tons, and it is forecasting 41 million metric tons in 2014—an 8.1 percent increase.

As North American and European stainless mills prepare for the traditional summer season maintenance shutdowns, prices are projected to be flat or slightly softer.
