Metal Fabrication and Construction: Housing and Office Space

Dr. Chris Kuehl

The housing sector has long been a major driver for the overall economy, and for all the most apparent of reasons. This is the single biggest expense for the consumer—whether it is a mortgage payment or rent. The health of the economy as a whole is signaled by the level of demand for housing.

And, of course, a great deal of the manufacturing and metal fabrication community remains oriented to housing. There are the goods that go into the construction itself as well as all the material that eventually goes into that house. The development of housing triggers all manner of commercial activity as well as public-sector spending on roads, fire stations, schools, and so on. Beyond the home itself, there are all the things that go into the home—everything from appliances to textiles and furniture. The bottom line is that expansion in the housing market drives the overall expansion of the economy.

Right now, two diverging elements are at work in housing. Single-family housing is down by over 20%, while multifamily housing is up over 25%. There is still thought to be a shortage of 3.2 million homes (2.5% of existing inventory). Most of this is in the lower-priced category.

The average home price has come down from the high of $479,000, but is still at $419,000. These are national numbers, and the differences in various regions can be significant. In recent weeks there has been a slight dip in the average mortgage rate, but as of early February, it was still close to 8.0% (double what it was just a year or so ago).

Four Factors that Drive Builders

The sector as a whole is driven by four factors, and these apply to single family as well as multifamily. All are rooted in consumer behavior to a degree. The first is job stability. The prospective buyer has to have confidence in their ability to make payments. Even if they are willing to take a risk, the lender and the landlord will not.

The second factor is location preference. People will choose a home according to where they work, the commute they will tolerate, the school district, weather, and so on. Each person will have differing priorities, but some of the variation is predictable. Families want schools, the older populations seek sunny climates. The growth of remote work has made location easier as people do not have to live close to the job location in many cases.

A third motivator is, obviously, the mortgage rates and the cost of the home. Both of these went up over the last couple of years, but lately they seem to have stabilized a bit and have even fallen. That process will likely accelerate into 2024 as demand weakens and the possibility of lower interest rates develops.

The last factor is related to rent. If renting becomes as much of a financial factor as a mortgage, there is even more incentive to buy. Rents have been escalating as the cost of buying has been reduced somewhat.

It is important to remember the obvious with all these motivators, and that is the regional differences. The very high prices in highly popular locations such as Austin, Nashville, Phoenix, and elsewhere will contrast dramatically with Fargo, Cleveland, and Trenton.

Less Speculative Construction

The completion data has improved somewhat, but the changes have not been dramatic. Builders are doing far less in the way of speculative construction; the norm now is building only when a contract has been signed. There has been a great deal of regional variation, and that has been following the overall population shifts.

The fastest-growing markets have been in Texas and Arizona, but there have been other communities that have enjoyed surges (Oklahoma, South Dakota, North Carolina, and Tennessee). These expansions have been driven by factors such as reshoring of manufacturing, opening up of new transportation corridors, and the exodus from urban areas that have boosted growth in the nearby suburbs and exurbs. Florida continues to see spectacular growth of high-end homes. Meanwhile, the state struggles with developing lowcost alternatives.

The housing sector has been pivoting for a few years now, and that process will accelerate into the coming year. The trend toward multifamily housing will continue as many have been locked out of the single-family market by higher costs and higher mortgages.

Both prices and mortgages have fallen a bit of late, but are still very high. Rents are also going up. The bargains are going to be found in the existing home market as more Boomers exit their single-family homes in favor of some kind of senior-living opportunity.

The fastest-growing sector of multifamily housing is senior-focused—everything from assisted care to senior-planned communities. The millennials are seeking deals in the inner-ring suburbs that Boomers are leaving, while the Gen-Z population has shown little interest in single-family homes. A recent poll showed that over 30% of Gen-Z males have no intention to move from their parent’s home until they are in their 30s. Interestingly enough only 10% of Gen-Z females feel the same way.

The number of speculative permits has declined considerably, and that is a trend that will accelerate as banks are no longer interested in speculative construction loans. The financial situation has become very tight, and that is not likely to change unless and until the Fed starts to reduce rates. The betting at this point is that rates will start to ease slowly by the middle of next year, but only a quarter point at a time.

Migration patterns have been playing a major role in the housing sector. What is triggering this migration, and where are people moving to and from? The most important factor has been the cost of living. Those states and cities that have high taxes and high cost of living are losing population rapidly. Most of the West Coast has seen drastic reductions in population, as have states in the Northeast. Older populations can’t afford to retire in these states and have left for low-tax alternatives. Younger populations have the option of working remotely and choosing cheaper locations.

Transportation has also been playing a major role for the Midwest. As supply chain adjustments are made, there has been growth in trade between the U.S. and Mexico at the expense of China. That emphasizes the corridors in the middle of the country. That has stimulated a lot of logistics development, which in turn stimulates job creation and migration.

The movement out of urban areas started in earnest with the pandemic, but even as these motivators have faded, there have been other reasons to shift away from the urban core: specifically, congestion and the long commutes; crime and overall security; and deteriorating infrastructure and lack of amenities. This last issue is especially connected to the school system. The urban school districts have been judged inferior, and parents want their kids educated elsewhere.

What About Office Buildings?

There is one looming problem that will significantly alter the construction and real estate landscape. It started even before the pandemic impact, but the COVID crisis certainly escalated the challenge. The issue is commercial building—what to do with office buildings no longer in use. The surge in remote work has left millions of offices redundant. There are very few being built at this stage, and those that exist stand empty. The problem worsened as many of these developments were financed during the low-interest-rate years, and now they will have to cope with higher rates as they undergo refinancing. These projects will not be viable, and bankruptcies will accelerate.

The U.S. is already dealing with a surge in "zombie companies" that had been kept alive with pandemic money and low interest rates. That support and those low rates are things of the past. Bankruptcy rates in the U.S. are already up by 30% in 2023, and that pace is expected to quicken in 2024.

The question is what to do with these buildings. There are basically three options, and one of these would be positive for manufacturers. The first is simply to tear them down, and that will be the fate of older structures or those in places where the land can be used in some other way.

The second option involves converting office buildings into housing or entertainment or something more exotic. Some projects have developed farms in former high-rise buildings, but these are rare. Most of the conversions are related to housing. This is the opportunity for the manufacturer as these will have to be revised to accommodate the new uses.

The third option is just to wait and see if there is a recovery in demand. Some communities might see resumed growth or the arrival of a new set of tenants. Many companies want to move people back to the office as there has been a notable lack of productivity since many people elected to work from home. Many workers have also chafed at the isolation that comes with working remotely.

Will that be enough to resurrect demand for office space? It will not be sufficient to spur a total recovery, but it could provide new life for some projects, especially in the high-growth regions. No matter how one looks at the situation, it is clear the office-space glut will be hard to address quickly.
