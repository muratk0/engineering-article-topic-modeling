# Riding the roller coaster

[TARİH: 01.05.2025 The Fabricator]

Fabrinomics | Economic Insights

Tariffs, Consumer Sentiment, and the Economy of 2025

So much can change in a matter of months. Tariff news headlines are never ceasing. Consumer and business outlooks continue to shift. Along for the roller coaster ride, everyone is looking at the track ahead, the hills and hairpin turns. No one knows what the future truly will be, but we’re all holding on for the ride.

Several key points might help us all make sense of the ride: consumer sentiment, the value of the dollar (which plays a role in trade), and what tariffs ultimately will be with us for good. Also, remember that forecasts are always uncertain—now more so than ever.

On Again, Off Again

In early April the world had a wake-up call, with Trump’s announcement of "retaliatory" tariffs affecting the breadth of U.S. trading partners. Some tariffs on China went forward, many others were delayed. The automotive industry got a temporary reprieve. So did certain categories of consumer electronics.

By the time you read this, of course, the state of world trade might be entirely different. Most of the tariff discussion today is pushing executives to merely watch and observe, to see if they ultimately will be implemented or reversed. Many are taking a simple approach: They’ll wait until the final cost increases and tariff impacts are understood and will simply pass on those increases to customers. As such, many aren’t doing much right now, waiting for the smoke to clear and calmer heads to prevail. If they get hit, they get hit. They will deal with the implications at that time.

Steel and Aluminum Tariffs Could Stick Around

One set of tariffs are forcing many publicly traded firms to change their forecast outlooks—and that pertains to steel and aluminum tariffs. At this writing, it sounds like those could be the tariffs that "stick" and hold. Mexico and Canada could get some relief under the USMCA agreements, but companies like Puma, Porsche, and others are already using that as rational for lowering sales or income expectations.

As of this writing, the 25% tariffs are hitting hundreds of downstream products, and countermeasures by the EU have hit U.S. goods ranging from boats to motorcycles and other items.

Companies are already expediting Q2 inventory builds and diversifying their supplier bases outside of USMCA-EU corridors. Some are renegotiating force-majeure clauses with suppliers, and some are proactively updating their pricing terms to reflect tariff passthrough risks. The biggest challenge is currently mapping enough of their supply chain to understand where the tariff risk is coming from.

An adage from India seems most appropriate at this point: "When elephants fight, it is the grass that suffers." The implication is that the real damage is felt by the "little guys." Not that automakers and construction firms are little guys, but the fact is that tens of thousands of parts are made of steel and aluminum, and they are sourced from all over the world. In some cases, the proposed tariffs will have only a minor impact, but other industries will be facing massive price hikes that will be beyond what can be passed on to a consumer.

Consumer Sentiment Fades

The preliminary University of Michigan Consumer Sentiment survey shows that it fell further, from 64.7 to 57.9, which was 10.5% lower than last month and the lowest since July of 2022 (the start of the Ukraine War). Inflation expectations have jumped to the highest level since November 2022, and the five-year outlook for inflation just hit its fastest month-over-month increase since 1993. Tariff concerns and the potential for a sharp increase in inflation have been at the top of the list of worries.

Consumer expectations for durable goods purchases fell 19% on worries over continually changing tariff news. And many are using the stock market as an economic barometer (rightfully or wrongly), and that is weighing on sentiment. The rapid whiplash from tariff and policy statements is too much for most people to consume and process. The impact could come on the consumer spending front, and Joanne Hsu from the University of Michigan probably summed it up best: "Consumers are bracing for a perfect storm of tariffs, inflation, and policy whiplash. This isn’t just sentiment—it’s a demand freeze warning." And since much of this is based on news headlines and administration statements on policy, it can be quickly reversed.

At the end of the day, the manufacturers depend on demand from the ultimate consumer, and any kind of turn toward caution is a concern. We have talked at length about the emergence of the "K" recovery; consumers who earn more than $100,000 annually are now driving the economy. As long as they are active, the growth will remain solid. The fear is that most of their money comes from the markets, and lately there has been a move toward a correction. The bottom third of earners (under $50,000) are basically checked out already, and the middle third remains active as long as they have job security.

A Strong or Weak Dollar—Which Is Better?

The answers depend on who is asking the question. If you are an importer, that strong dollar is great as it means you get more bang for the buck. Everything imported is carrying a discount. The exporter is not enthusiastic as the product or service one is selling is more expensive. This is the reason that countries have taken steps to devalue their currency as they seek to boost their exports. Of course, that has also meant a burden on their consumers, so most try to achieve some kind of balance.

It has long been asserted that the U.S. dollar has been too strong and has interfered with exports—and that is correct. The U.S. has a problem other nations do not share. The dollar is the world currency and has been for decades. That means other nations routinely peg their currency to the greenback. Attempts to devalue the dollar fail as other nations simply match our move.

Will the U.S. dollar be replaced by some other currency, like the euro or the yuan? There are nations that accept each other’s currency at times, but the reality is that nobody really wants to be that global currency—certainly not export-centric Europe or China. The U.S. is stuck with this. Options to weaken the dollar are not generally popular with investors and businesspeople. The plan harkens back to the Plaza Accord in 1985 and is now referred to as the Mar-a-Lago Accord. Basically, it is a plan that engages other nations in an effort to bolster their own currencies by extending the duration of their treasury reserves. The implied threat is that nations that failed to take that action would face punitive tariffs and would no longer be protected by the U.S. defense shield. These "punishments" have already been imposed, and there has been no decision regarding these treasury reserves.

The U.S. seems to have lost its leverage on this issue. There is no consensus on what the dollar position should be. If the tariff tactics do not boost exports and reduce imports as planned, will the U.S. turn to other tactics? This is what has investors and businesspeople concerned.

Traditionally, the U.S. has had little success in manipulating its currency. A weak dollar combined with high tariff costs would really slam the consumer in the wallet.

Economic News from Data, Not Drama

Tariff and trade policies have caused some dramatic swings on Wall Street, but how’s the real economy doing? Beyond the drama in Washington, there are lots of moving parts that will shape the economic environment in the first half of the year. As the first quarter ended, growth overall was still good, job growth was stable, job openings slipped below 8 million, unemployment was low, and wage growth was stable. These were the good-news elements, but that has not stopped the comments about a recession and worries of inflation.

Amid all the noise, it helps to take a step back, breathe deeply, and look in the rear-view mirror. In February, the outlook for overall U.S. industrial production, including both durable and nondurable goods, continued to show some optimistic outlooks. Our models were showing full-year 2025 growth at nearly 2%.

In fact, the data on manufactuing had been remarkably stable, and a lot of this is attributable to the improved inventory to sales ratios. The period of excessive inventory had come to an end. In the first quarter, many hoped that new orders would translate into increased production.

There’s been plenty of media buzz around recession, or at least a potential slowdown, and the buzz certainly isn’t without cause. Here again, it helps to look at the historical data. As of February, there was very little data to suggest a recession was imminent.

This, obviously, has changed. The uncertainty that has been triggered by the on-again, off-again tariffs has many companies adopting a wait-and-see attitude. Projects are stalled at this point but not canceled as people wait for the dust to settle.
