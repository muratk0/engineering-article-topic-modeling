# Manufacturing is a safe haven

[TARİH: 01.05.2025 The Fabricator]

Tech Talk

The world is poised for upheaval; manufacturing is a great hedge

CALEB CHAMBERLAIN

T

he future is unpredictable and chaotic, now more than ever. Political tension is at an alltime high. Government spending is out of control. AI technology promises to change labor markets, starting with white-collar knowledge-worker jobs. And international trade is being upended, even between traditional allies.

As I write, U.S. markets are plummeting in response to Trump’s "Liberation Day" tariff announcement. The Dow Jones Industrial Average, S&P 500, and Nasdaq are all down more than 10%, so far. It’s unclear how far they’ll fall.

Tariffs are complex and controversial. I’d rather not get into the weeds on why they might or might not make sense, or whether they are legal, or wise. I’m no expert, and smart people on both sides of the aisle seem to disagree. But whatever the long-term outcome, it’s going to be a bumpy ride. We can at least agree on that.

This isn’t the first disruptive event in recent memory. We all experienced the nightmare—and political tension—created by the COVID-19 pandemic. Global shutdowns combined with trillions of dollars of stimulus by the U.S. government created huge consumer demand for manufactured goods. Global supply chains simultaneously shut down, making it impossible to meet demand. Prices skyrocketed: At their worst, my company’s raw metal costs exceeded two and a half times their historical norm. Some companies tooled up for mass production only to see front-loaded demand dry up in the years to follow.

Leading up to Trump’s second presidency, things had in many ways settled down, at least as it relates to supply chains and prices. But one thing hadn’t changed—the U.S. government’s appetite for spending. The numbers are absurd. Deficit spending has been consistently increasing year over year since 2015. There was a brief interruption during COVID in 2019 and 2020, when the U.S. spent a cool $6 trillion more than it brought in. Afterward, we got right back on the escalating ramp we had been on before. The deficit is expected to grow to nearly $2 trillion this year.

To say that this kind of undisciplined, escalating spending is alarming is an understatement. Nobody thinks this is a good idea, yet it’s politically challenging to make cuts of any kind. The political incentives are all wrong. We can’t cut absurd defense spending or eliminate wasteful cost-plus contracts because it hurts jobs and rankles defense hawks. We can’t cut entitlements because it hurts individuals and families. We can’t raise taxes because it hurts families and businesses. We can’t reduce regulatory overhead because it’ll hurt the environment, workers, or minorities.

Traitov/iStock /Getty Images Plus

All told, when I think about the state of the world, I can’t think of a place I’d rather be than in manufacturing. I can’t think of a better place to invest cash than in value-producing equipment and infrastructure. I can’t think of a better hedge against political uncertainty than competing in a politically neutral, deeply loved market.

Politicians are incentivized to keep their constituents happy (and, of course, keep donor money flowing), but every responsible action required to balance a budget is anathema to a political campaign. So here we are. Perhaps something more dramatic is in order: Trump’s DOGE feels like a Hail-Mary effort to avoid fiscal disaster. Right or wrong, legal or illegal, I suspect that in the end it won’t be enough. We are spending ourselves into oblivion. Our children will pay the price.

Manufacturing Is a Refuge

Amid all this insanity, I’m actually optimistic about one thing: the value of U.S. manufacturing. If there was ever a great time to be a manufacturer in the United States, now is that time. Ignoring deficit spending and tariffs, manufacturing is still poised for dramatic macro change over the next decade. It’s no secret that the manufacturing workforce is aging. In 2020, nearly 25% of all manufacturing workers were 55 years old or older. Meanwhile, some estimates suggest that the baby boomer generation owns nearly 40% of all small businesses, and that less than half of those have a clear succession plan. Many businesses will close their doors inside of the next decade as owners retire.

This ongoing loss of knowledge and capacity isn’t great for U.S. manufacturing as a whole. But it does expose an incredible opportunity for those who stay in the game: net capacity is dropping, but demand is growing. All manufacturers, new and old, will benefit from this tailwind. It’s a great time to own or work for a manufacturing company.

It’s not just healthy demand that makes manufacturing great in 2025. It also works out to be a great place to invest in the face of inflationary federal spending. When the dollars in your bank account lose value courtesy of the U.S. government, capital equipment will tend to appreciate or hold value. Manufacturing is all about taking physical goods like metal, performing value-added work, and selling for a profit. When you can’t count on paper currency, it’s nice to have value-producing assets doing real things to real metal.

Manufacturing also will remain relatively insulated from AI-driven disruption for the foreseeable future. It’s true that there are a handful of companies pushing for humanoid robots to take the place of people on factory floors. So far, it’s pure hype. Demo videos show off these machines dancing or moving parts around with all the dexterity and grace of a 90-year-old wearing oven mitts. It’s not revolutionary, it’s embarrassing. Maybe they’ll get better, and maybe even soon, but here’s the thing: In my factory, keeping our machines up and running is a big job. Down equipment is extremely disruptive. The last thing I want on my floor is a robot with 50-plus degrees of freedom wandering around, breaking down, and stopping production.

That said, I’ve written recently about how AI is going to change software development. Ironically, AI will displace software engineers, accountants, lawyers, and other knowledge workers far sooner than machines are ready to displace people on the factory floor. Any job that requires complex physical work, like most manufacturing, will tend to be insulated from the disruptive effects of AI. The scary reality is that a lot of people might be looking hard for jobs. Manufacturing might be among the few industries that protect gainful employment.

Manufacturing is politically favorable as well. It’s hard to find an industry that is so well loved by politicians on both sides of the aisle. There’s something about saying "American jobs" that resonates on the campaign trail. There’s an honesty to doing real work on a factory floor. It’s true that U.S. industrial policy (or lack thereof) has gutted much of our industrial base here. Politicians’ lip service might reasonably deserve skepticism. But perhaps things are going to change.

Ostensibly, tariffs are designed to incentivize domestic manufacturing. In the short term, they disrupt supply chains, create geopolitical tension, and (currently, apparently) flex and stretch executive power. Some businesses will benefit. Others will suffer, especially those without pricing power. Long term, if tariffs disincentivize imports, it’ll create domestic demand that savvy entrepreneurs can fulfill.

U.S. markets are best in the world at finding and fixing inefficiency and pain. I’m personally convinced that tariffs could work favorably long term, provided that they are consistent, sane, legal, and that they outlast U.S. presidential cycles. I guess I’m hopeful, but not optimistic.

Digitally Enabled Manufacturing

Finally, U.S. manufacturing is due for a technological facelift. Manufacturing here has lagged the digital revolution, sometimes by decades.

But this is just starting to change. Manufacturers that understand software deeply can do pretty incredible things to improve efficiency, quality, and customer experience.

As traditional shops close, change hands, or evolve and start adopting software innovation, there will be a shift toward digital manufacturing. This "new" business model will be characterized by ultrarapid quoting and quick turnaround, customization, software automation, and online-first customer interaction.

Not all industries will be equally impacted. But part of the internet’s magic is that it breaks down barriers. Paired with automated quoting and design-for-manufacturability functionality, it allows customer acquisition to be scaled. When traditional shops close their doors, digital manufacturers with a nationwide presence seem best positioned to fill the void.

All told, when I think about the state of the world, I can’t think of a place I’d rather be than in manufacturing. I can’t think of a better place to invest cash than in value-producing equipment and infrastructure.

I can’t think of a better hedge against political uncertainty than competing in a politically neutral, deeply loved market. And I can think of few things better for the U.S. economy than contributing to our much needed industrial base. In our uncertain world, manufacturing is truly a great place to be.
