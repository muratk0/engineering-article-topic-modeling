# The immigration conundrum

[TARİH: 01.07.2023 The Fabricator]

Fabrinomics | Economic Insights

Dr.

Chris Kuehl

Immigration is the very definition of a conundrum. It’s so confusing because, in truth, few can agree what the problem is in the first place. If there were a universal agreement that no immigrants of any kind were to be allowed, there might be a strategy developed based on strict and punitive border protection. But, of course, there is no such agreement.

Tens of thousands of businesses desperately need immigrant workers—everything from high-tech specialists and medical professionals to the legions of migratory workers harvesting fruit and vegetables all over the U.S. In past years, there has been deep opposition to migrants from Ireland, Italy, Poland, and elsewhere in Europe as they sought entry to the U.S., but that faded fast as these workers assimilated and integrated. Germans resented the Turks and those from Yugoslavia, and there has been resistance to those coming from former colonies, but there has been acceptance as well—these workers helped deal with the chronic shortage of employees.

A Nation of Immigrants

The reality is that every nation needs immigrants, but not just any immigrant. They want the ones who assimilate rapidly and start contributing to the economic growth of their new home right away. Immigrants with few skills and a language barrier are not favored. Neither are those who would be better classified as refugees than migrants. The ideal new arrival is the person with needed skills who has chosen to make a new life in a new nation—willing to adapt to the culture, learn the language, and become a new citizen. This is the person who built the economic power of the U.S. as the U.S. was always a nation of immigrants.

The most challenging migrant is the person who has been chased out of their home nation by war or violence. They do not want to leave, and they do not want to leave their culture behind; they have no choice and are always seeking an opportunity to return. They do not want to assimilate and frequently reject the demands to abandon their language and patterns. The U.K. is now dealing with half a million Ukrainians who have been driven from their home, and for most of them there is no longer a home to return to. What are they expected to do now? The U.S. is being inundated by those from central America and Latin America who are fleeing drug violence or oppression.

What then is the policy? Letting in all who want to enter is not economically or fiscally possible. There is not enough work for them or enough public money to support them. They will swiftly become a burden, and there will be problems of crime as people become desperate. Blocking all access will be next to impossible, and there is a legitimate need for many of those seeking to enter.

An entire system has to be created with a focus on building future capacity. The aim should be to ease assimilation as quickly as possible–language training, job creation, cultural education. All of this will be expensive and controversial and will not even be welcomed by a significant percentage of that incoming population.

The Global Perspective

The current global geopolitical situation is about to hand the governments of the U.S. and Europe with a massive test, and by all accounts it will be one that everybody will fail. The U.S. is about to be overwhelmed by as many as 13,000 migrants coming from Mexico, Central America, and Latin America every day. Pandemic-era legislation (Title 42) allowed for the rapid expulsion of migrants on public health grounds, and it is now expiring.

There will be similar issues in Europe, and the migratory patterns will continue to pit southern nations like Italy, Spain, and Greece against the northern nations. This is a conflict that has been taking center stage for years, and has only become more complicated by the Ukraine war as tens of thousands of refugees have sought shelter in Europe–often in the central European nations that have been deeply opposed to migration for years. The Sudan conflict has already sent tens of thousands of Sudanese fleeing from the war, and many are making their way to Europe.

The bottom line is as simple as it gets: The populations in lessdeveloped or war-torn nations have no viable future in their own nation and are desperately seeking at least temporary relief. Some only seek a life outside their homeland until conditions improve, but others are determined to leave their home nations to start a new life. The problem is obvious. They are not equipped to start that new life, and the places they want to move to are not prepared to accept the migratory influx.

The nations where people seek to relocate have only two extremely difficult options. They can accept the millions who want to come and face the immense financial and cultural consequences, or they will have to place extreme barriers in place, which essentially means a militarization of the borders.

About Labor

The latest data shows that immigrants now make up 18.1% of the U.S. workforce, and by the end of the decade that percentage will be close to 20%. These migrants come from all over the world and with a wide range of skills. The people manufacturers want are in demand everywhere, and that means recruiting and retaining is harder than ever.

The manufacturing community in the U.S. and Europe have very complex responses to this challenge. On the one hand, there is appreciation that massive waves of illegal migration will be economically devastating to an economy that is not in great shape to begin with. The vast majority of those seeking to cross the border lack the skills and education needed by the manufacturer (or any other business). In past years, the bulk of the migration would have been young men seeking work of some kind, but today these migrations involve entire families, and there are very few opportunities for work of any kind.

While illegal migration is opposed, there is the very real need to attract as many skilled and educated people as possible. The U.S., Europe, Japan, and other developed nations are aging fast. By the end of this decade, the entire Boomer generation in the U.S. will have reached retirement age—all 76 million. With a U.S. population of 330 million, this is a significant segment. The U.S. needs replacement workers desperately. The challenge is that these workers will not come from the ranks of the illegal migrants, so the U.S. (and Europe, Japan, etc.) have to find a way to blunt the flow of people it doesn’t want to accommodate while welcoming the people it needs.
