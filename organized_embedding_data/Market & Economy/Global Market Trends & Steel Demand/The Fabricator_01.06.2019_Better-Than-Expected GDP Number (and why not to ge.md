I know that this column will remind people just why we economist types are oft referred to as the dismal scientists. We have the earned reputation of being the kings of buzz kill as we can always find the dark cloud no matter how bright the silver lining.

The Q1 growth number was 3.2 percent and far higher than anybody in the economic mainstream had expected. Most had thought that growth of around 2.4-2.5 percent would be the best that might be anticipated. Does this mean that all the economic analysts were wrong? Not really. At least three factors contributed to the unexpected growth, but the bad news is that none of the three are expected to be a big factor going forward.

The growth was spurred by three developments that many had noted but didn’t think would be this profound. The first was that the U.S. did a great deal more exporting than is normal and far more than had been expected. Much of that was prompted by companies working hard to get export sales in place before the expected tariff barriers would be inhibiting that activity, and buyers around the world were equally interested in getting this done.

THE LOOMING ISSUE NOW IS EXCESS INVENTORY NOW THAT BUSINESS AND CONSUMERS HAVE SPENT HEAVILY ON CONTINGENCY PLANS IN THE EVENT OF A TARIFF WAR.

The second development was that U.S. consumers were buying more throughout the quarter than expected. The only problem is that a lot of this extra buying was gasoline that had become significantly more expensive with the rise of the per-barrel price of oil.

The third factor was a lot of pent-up spending that took place right after the government shutdown ended. Remember that many thought another shutdown would occur only a few weeks after the end of the first one, and all those that were affected the first time bought far more than they usually did to be ready for the potential return.

The looming issue now is excess inventory now that business and consumers have spent heavily on contingency plans in the event of a tariff war. If that war really does emerge, that accumulated inventory will be very important. But if it doesn’t, lots of companies will be trying to get rid of what they have and thus not buying much in the way of additional inventory.
