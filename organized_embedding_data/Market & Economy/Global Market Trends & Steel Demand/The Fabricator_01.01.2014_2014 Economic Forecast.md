# 2014 Economic Forecast

[TARİH: 01.01.2014 The Fabricator]

Stumbling and Surviving for Another Year

Any minute now we will all be deluged with various iterations of prognostication directed at what might happen this year. In most cases, the economist hopes fervently that nobody remembers what is said now when the end of 2014 arrives. This forecasting thing is dangerous, and no amount of qualifying will keep people from giving the forecaster a hard time when things don’t quite play out as planned. Remember that the best definition of an economist is somebody that explains tomorrow why the predictions they made yesterday didn’t come true today. Have I said enough to cover my bases?

Dr. Chris Kuehl FMA Economic Analyst

The forecast for 2014 is broken down into four segments here:

1. The Fed and the whole financial community

2. The impact of politics

3. The reaction of the manufacturer and general business

4. The reaction of the consumer

All kinds of other techniques could be used to break this down, but these seem a good shorthand method by which to assess the likely future.

How About the Fed?

Let’s start with the Federal Reserve as not much else is roiling the stock markets these days. By all accounts the Federal Reserve in 2014 is going to be faced with some massive issues, and decisions will be falling into the hands of a group that doesn’t really have much experience with one another.

We all know that Janet Yellen is the new Fed chair replacing Ben Bernanke. The Fed is going to be a very different institution next year — mostly due to the selection of Stanley Fischer as vice chair. He was considered a candidate to replace Bernanke at one point and has vastly more experience than Yellen as he has been the head of the Bank of Israel since 2005 and was the number two man at the IMF in the 1990s. There will be other changes. Jerome Powell (the investment banker) is at the end of his first term but will likely get reappointed. Elizabeth Duke (the commercial banker) has already left, and Sarah Bloom Raskin (the regulatory expert) is taking a new position at the Treasury. Jeremy Stein (an economist) must leave or he will forfeit his tenured position at Harvard. This leaves only Daniel Tarullo (the attorney) and Bill Dudley (the New York Fed chair) as members of the “permanent committee.”

There is even consternation in the ranks of the Fed presidents scheduled to rotate on to the FOMC. It is the turn of Dallas, Philadelphia, Cleveland, and Minneapolis — Richard Fisher, Charles Plosser, Sandra Pianalto, and Narayana Kocherlakota. Pianalto is also retiring, so a replacement will have to be found for her, while Kocherlakota has shaken the Fed to its core by firing or reassigning the entire research staff of the Minneapolis Fed. Yellen is not facing an easy transition.

What is Yellen likely to do, and what will change as far as policy is concerned? In truth, not much. The likelihood is that all the Fed’s major actions will stay just as they are for the bulk of the year. There will be no interest rate hikes and no messing with anything like the reserve ratio or even the interest that banks earn on the money they have at the Fed.

What is likely to change is the QE III process, but even that will be very cautious. The markets have already built in an expectation that bond buying will decrease to the tune of maybe $10 billion a month by the start of the year. The real question is whether that trend will continue or accelerate. Given the expected negative reaction from the markets, small and regular cuts to the bond buying are likely, but even by the middle of the year the Fed will still be buying north of $60 billion a month. Much will depend on whether the reduction slows down the economy at all.

Banks have started to loosen up on credit, but that is partly because the Fed is still in a stimulating mood. It is expected that credit will loosen up very slowly and with frequent pullbacks if the outcome is not quite what was anticipated. The banks are still grappling with the Bank Reform Act and still not getting the kinds of loan applications they hoped for

Politics and Political Games

The second set of predictions are as depressing as any for the coming year. Fortunately, the discussion will be brief. For the third straight year the political issues affecting the economy will be serious enough to cause a real decline in the economy’s overall performance. The battle over the government shutdown in October was not solved in any way — simply postponed until January. The same with the debt ceiling — no decision was made in October, and now the whole mess lands in the laps of Congress in February. The pragmatists won one round in December as they were able to push a budget compromise through, which will avoid a government shutdown. One other thing to be aware of is that this is an election year and the control of Congress is at stake. Both parties have candidates with far more to fear from a primary than from the general election, and that means doing absolutely nothing to alienate the core. This will make dealmaking almost impossible, as the candidates will be rewarded for their obstinacy and punished for any hint of flexibility.

The political games will do nothing to help the economy grow and may actually do real damage, perhaps dragging the GDP growth rate down by half a point over the course of the year.

Manufacturing and the Business Community

The third set of forecasts involve the manufacturer and the general business community as a whole. The news through the last few months of 2013 was actually pretty upbeat. The year-end data on industrial production, capacity utilization, and productivity was respectable, and the data from surveys like the Purchasing Managers Index, Credit Managers’ Index, and the various offerings of The Conference Board all trended in a positive direction, leaving the impression that business was building some momentum going into the new year.

The one caution sounded by most everyone has been that much of the gain at the end of 2013 was provided by a dramatic inventory build, and now 2014 must come through with enough demand to deal with that inventory. The consumer is discussed in the next section, but from the manufacturers’ perspective, the issue is that most of the other conditions for growth are in place.

The accessibility of credit has improved, and there is more export demand than ever — from a variety of sources. Costs of raw materials are still generally down and labor costs are still low despite the fact that most companies are desperate for the right skilled worker. It is unlikely to stay that way for long.

By the middle of the year costs will be rising across the board. The producers of metal and other raw materials have been reducing production to bring supply in line with demand. Wages will rise for the people needed as they will be in short supply, and any pickup in business will make them that much more valuable. The bottom line is that business has maybe six more months of favorable conditions before the prices start to adjust upward.

Consumer Impact

Finally, the most fickle of them all, the consumer. Three things supposedly make the consumer feel ready to start spending again. They traditionally respond to housing prices as these make the homeowner feel a little more financially secure. They also respond to jobless rates because these reassure people that the job they have may last. Last, they respond (weirdly) to the price of gasoline. All three of these are trending in a positive direction, yet the consumer is not acting all that motivated.

We think consumers are not being affected by the political crisis that never ends and that they have become deeply skeptical about potential for real recovery. If that attitude persists, the first quarter will be very weak, and a big rebound in attitude is unlikely for the course of the year. This will lead to anemic growth of between 2.5 percent and 3.0 percent.

The conclusion is that 2014 is not going to be all that exciting in either a negative or positive way. It will be something akin to 2013 — something to be stumbled through and survived.
