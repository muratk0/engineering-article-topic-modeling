# Steel prices continue their slow, steady ascent

Steel buyers don’t seem bothered by historical highs

By

Kristen DiLandro

Even as domestic steel prices have risen to great heights, such as $1,000/ton for hot-rolled coil, steel buyers haven’t retreated from regular purchasing patterns.

Lima/iStock/Getty Images Plus

I

n Q4 2025, most steel market analysts and day-to-day market participants expected hot-rolled coil prices to peak at $1,000/ton.

Theories about why prices would hover around that mark varied. Some said President Donald Trump wouldn’t approve of high steel prices because he wouldn’t want a material price to be prohibitive for building projects. Others thought the market would not accept prices that tipped too much past $1,000/ton.

As of the second week in April, prices for domestically produced spot tons of steel sheet and plate have transcended the esoteric $1,000/ton price point. The average spot transaction price for hot-rolled coil the week of April 7 was $1,040/ton. For context, the average price of HRC price in the equivalent week of 2025 was $905/ton and in 2024 it was $835/ton. By now, few would disagree that tariffs are generally inflationary. First, they drive up the price of imported goods, which get passed on to consumers. Second, domestic producers raise prices on their goods to compensate for increased prices on doing business or just because they no longer compete with foreign products.

Domestic producers who relied on semifinished steel slab (an input product) from Brazil, for example, have been stymied by the drastic increase to their production costs. Domestic slab producers at integrated mills typically do not sell excessive tons of slab, and if they do sell slab, it isn’t a cost-effective substitute for mills that have relied on cheap Brazilian products. The result means fewer finished steel products in the market because theoretically there is less supply; tight supply traditionally translates to higher prices.

TARIFFS HAVING LITTLE IMPACT

Tariffs can have a far less significant impact on pricing if targeted products generally have a smaller impact on consumer price indices. Or, if foreign producers cut prices, paying a tariff continues to allow their products to remain competitive. The latter appears to be the case for some steel plate imports. A source on the West Coast said that importing steel plate from a reliable producer abroad allows his organization to maintain balanced inventory. The rub? Lead times are months out.

A different plate market source told Steel Market Update that his organization has remained competitive in the market because his organization buys plate from a Canadian producer. The market is so competitive right now that plate buyers with the flexibility to deal with long lead times are opting to import plate to secure deals that might have gone to competitors.

Tariffs alone are not driving the higher price points for products in the steel spot market, but it’s not totally demand either. While demand for some steel products looks bullish, it is not a demand-driven pricing environment alone that has pushed prices over "a grand per hot band," as some in the industry say.

Various domestic mills have experienced uncharacteristic setbacks that have limited capacity at various points throughout the end of 2025 and/or beginning of 2026. Mills have experienced temperature lows that have damaged equipment, unexpected outages, or equipment issues that have crimped their expected outputs. Those obstacles on the supply side have led to higher costs.

WHAT’S THE REAL SCOOP?

Unfortunately, domestic producers seem to have tightened their publicity policies around production-related news. Solid journalistic practice requires verified reports, not unfounded rumors. There you have the rub for steel market participants.

Buyers have little choice but to absorb price increases unless they are not in a position to wait for imported products. Many smaller purchasers of spot products are not in a financial position to pay for bonds and customs brokers. They don’t have the luxury of extended lead times and paperwork processing to become a licensed importer. Taking all of that into consideration, the spot market prices become the new normal for these buyers.

Understandably, mills prioritize the fulfillment needs of their contract customers. Strong demand from contract customers can shrink spot availability for other buyers. The result can mean very long lead times for spot buyers. In the current market, lead times have been extended to as much as six weeks for some customers.

Spot market participants also point out that the nature of pricing has changed in recent years. Rather than sending out notices advertising exponential price hikes on an occasional basis, many mills increase their prices more incrementally on a weekly or monthly schedule. The latter has been effective for raising prices without creating substantial backlash from buyers because increases do not radically alter projections in a way that huge swings once did.

Overall, buyers who have spoken with SMU are not overwhelmed by mill hikes or tariff-related price increases. More concerning for most is that end market demand is not as strong as sources would like. Most say they are kept up at night by the fact that there’s little diversity in the industries that are the sources of demand. Meanwhile, other buyers contend that having demand from shipbuilding and construction related to infrastructure projects and energy initiatives signals greater health in the overall market for them.

Prices for steel do not appear to be falling any time soon. However, as more domestic suppliers find their rhythm with restored operations, smoother supply chains, and stronger end market demand, spot availability could improve and lead times could shrink.
