# Manufacturing grows modestly, despite everything

Fabrinomics | Economic Insights

Fabricators navigate the fog of war

Dr. Chris Kuehl

Rangsarit Chaiyakun/iStock/Getty Images Plus

If there is to be a word of the year in 2026, it will likely be "uncertainty." There has been nothing but frantic change all year, and in periods like this, it can be challenging to determine the winners and losers. Some have clearly experienced growth while others are facing a dire future, and these positions seem to change on an hourly basis.

Hypersensitivity of markets and speculators seems to increase with every bit of news. Business leaders need to be able to make measured decisions, and the fog of war makes that difficult.

The PMI Surveys

April’s manufacturing purchasing managers’ indices (PMIs) were interesting because they were based on the latest survey information after the start of the Iran war. PMI respondents are reacting to the world as the world changes around them. As the operating environment changes, they must change with it.

The U.S. PMI from S&P Global bumped up to 52.3 from 51.6 in March. The manufacturing index remains in expansion territory, which is good news. Every major PMI commentary across all regions cited the ongoing Iran war as the primary headwind. Executives mentioned that the war has injected deep uncertainty into global supply chains. This is the single most common thread appearing in the U.S., Eurozone, UK, Japan, and Southeast Asia reports. Input price inflation is increasing simultaneously across geographies.

In the U.S., the Institute for Supply Management (ISM) Prices Index hit 78.3% in March, a four-year high; 17 of 18 industries reported higher costs. Eurozone input prices rose at their fastest rate since October 2022, while input prices in Japan rose at their fastest pace since August 2024. Southeast Asia input price inflation is now at its highest point since October 2022. Meanwhile, S&P Global input prices have been rising at their fastest rate since November 2022.

Headline PMI readings are being artificially inflated by longer supplier delivery times, which technically register as expansion but actually reflect disruption, not healthy demand. The U.S. Supplier Deliveries Index surged to 58.9%, the highest since May 2022. The S&P Global Eurozone commentary specifically warned that "PMI figures are misleading as supply shocks inflate delivery times."

Across the U.S. and Europe, manufacturers and their customers are actively stockpiling supplies and locking in prices before conditions worsen further. This pre-buying is artificially lifting new orders and production figures in the near term. The behavior also creates a pull-forward demand dynamic that may help Q1 data but suggests some risk of softness ahead.

Forward-looking sentiment is declining across most regions. Eurozone business confidence fell to a five-month low. U.S. firms are shelving investment and capital expenditure plans in reaction to the combined uncertainty of tariff policy and the Iran war.

ISM Chair Susan Spence noted that shifting trade policies (IEEPA legal challenges, Section 122 tariffs, Section 301 investigations) "make it difficult for companies to plan and make capital investments." Customers are also avoiding longer-term order commitments.

Automotive Remains Resilient

Every year for the past decade, there has been a prediction that automotive sales would dip dramatically, for a variety of reasons. There has been massive inflation in the auto sector, with the average price of a car hitting $50,000. Many remain concerned regarding the overall rate of inflation. Worries persist about increased levels of unemployment, the impact of supply chain disruptions due to tariffs, and now with the Iran war, the increased chaos in the oil markets driving the per-barrel price well past $100.

Despite all these negative trends, the auto sector has continued to hold steady and even expand. Positive drivers include everything from basic consumer desire for a new car to the fact that the average car in the U.S. is now close to 13 years old.

Banks are also willing to lend. Although car loan delinquencies are on the rise, it’s nothing compared to credit cards and student loans. Banks are still offering car loans because of the slow recovery of home loans. And with higher price jumps for used cars than new cars, and the rising cost of repairs, the prospect of buying a new car is more appealing.

Most expect this year’s sales to be around 16 million vehicles, which is not far off the pace set between 2016 and 2019 (with around 18 million vehicles sold annually). The most significant shift is that higher-end vehicles are selling more consistently than lower-end vehicles.

The most sought-after vehicles have been larger trucks and SUVs. If gas prices keep rising, the demand for these fuel gulpers will likely fade a little. It has been noted that consumers do not change their preferences until gas hits above $4.50 a gallon and stays there for an extended period.

Thousands of suppliers around the world contribute to the process of assembling a vehicle, and few industries are as important in terms of employment. This is why the status of the automotive sector is always top of mind among politicians. Some of the tariff and trade activity has been helpful, but some of it has been damaging.

Strait of Hormuz Chokes Helium Supply

Semiconductor production requires helium, and numerous ships carrying the element (at this writing) have been stuck in the Persian Gulf. Dealing with this new reality, various industries now face shortages, especially for low-profit chips. Many chipmakers have shifted their production to supporting high-margin data center, advanced manufacturing, biotech, and medical applications. Even cell phone producers are concerned about limited supplies of chips.

Liquid-helium containers can hold their contents only so long before warming and losing the helium through pressure-release valves. Qatar’s Ras Laffan complex was struck in early March by Iranian drones and missiles. As of March 30, hundreds of helium containers were still stranded in the Persian Gulf. Air Liquide has formally warned customers it may not be able to fulfill orders.

As a result, some price contracts have nearly tripled versus pre-war pricing. South Korean semiconductor manufacturers (primarily Samsung and SK Hynix) are in active spot-market scrambles trying to find supplies, potentially as a safeguard. Firms that produce helium will lose significant profits. Moreover, suppliers up and down the supply chain will be under pressure.

The loss of helium is specific to the type of container holding it. Some types of transport dewars have 90 days of static holding time, while large industrial storage dewars can hold helium between 200 and 350 days, depending on insulation integrity. Small lab dewars have static hold times as short as four days, while certain shipping containers common in biomedical and semiconductor logistics have as few as 22.5 days of hold time under normal conditions, and near-zero hold times under vacuum failure.

The fundamental issue here is that there is a great deal more coming from the Middle East and Gulf region than just oil and other petroleum products. We hear about fertilizer as it needs natural gas, but there are more elements to consider even beyond helium: bromine, antimony—the list goes on.

The Real Price of Oil

What we see in the futures market is interesting, but it doesn’t tell the real story about street-level prices for oil. As of early April, West Texas Intermediate was trading at $111/barrel and Brent was $109—though with world events as they are, those numbers will have undoubtedly changed drastically by the time you read this.

These are generally futures contracts, but buyers looking for oil on the spot market were paying $141/barrel in the Middle East and nearly $200 in Europe. Buyers were scrambling to find supplies, and U.S. exports of crude were surging.

One could look at it two ways. First, U.S. producers love this kind of bump. It will certainly give them a shot in the arm in what was otherwise a dismal forecast for crude oil prices in 2026. Remember that the Energy Information Agency predicted crude oil prices (if the market had been left to normal cycles and supply/demand impacts) to be in the $40s in Q1 and averaging about $55/barrel a barrel for all of 2026—and even less for 2027.

Obviously with the war, that’s completely off the table. But the flip side of this is that we haven’t likely seen the end of the run-up in prices, and again, duration is key.

Most of the market can absorb a short-term price spike; we’ve seen it before, and we can weather it. But if the duration is four to six months, real damage can be done to the broader market. The powers-thatbe know this, and some supply chain throttling is already taking place. The question is: How does this play out?

By press time in mid-April, news emerged about how the Strait of Hormuz might be opening, though the situation was in flux. Regardless, the threat is not likely to go away in the short term. We must calculate some continued pressure on oil supply into the outlook ahead. This is evolving; the fog of war is still in place, and conditions can change quickly.
