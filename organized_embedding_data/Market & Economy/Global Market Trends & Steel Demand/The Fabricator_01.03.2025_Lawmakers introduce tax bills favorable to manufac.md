s congressional leaders work towards crafting a budget reconciliation package, members have introduced stand-alone tax legislation aimed at extending critical tax provisions of the 2017 Tax Cuts and Jobs Act (TCJA), to be included in the larger tax reconciliation package. While the Senate is set to move forward with a two-step plan, with the first bill including border, energy, and defense and a second bill including tax policy, House Republicans are trying to build a single, large fiscal package.

As part of these important tax provisions, Section 199A, the 20% deduction of qualified business income for passthroughs, would be extended and made permanent, and the Section 179 small business expensing limit would be doubled from $1 million to $2 million. Additional tax bills that are set to be introduced include those to make permanent R&D expensing and to restore and make permanent 100% expensing, or bonus depreciation.

The introduction of these stand-alone tax bills is a step forward in the ongoing discussions for the potential inclusion of these critical tax proposals in a final budget reconciliation bill.

Trump Imposes 25% Tariffs on Steel and Aluminum Imports

On Feb. 10, President Donald Trump took significant action by signing proclamations that alter the steel and aluminum tariffs under Section 232 of the Trade Expansion Act of 1962. This move reinstates a 25% tariff on steel imports from all nations, including U.S. trade partners, while raising the aluminum tariff from 10% to 25%.

This decision aligns with directives in the president’s "America First Trade Policy" memorandum, which instructed the Departments of Commerce and Defense to conduct a comprehensive economic and security review of the U.S. industrial and manufacturing base. The goal is to determine if any import adjustments are necessary to safeguard national security, as well as to reassess existing exemptions and other related measures for steel and aluminum imports.

Starting March 12, the new tariffs will apply universally to steel and aluminum imports from all countries at a rate of 25%, effectively eliminating all previous "alternative agreements" that had offered exemptions and tariff rate quotas to Argentina, Australia, Brazil, Canada, Japan, Mexico, South Korea, the European Union, Ukraine, and the United Kingdom. Furthermore, the existing product exclusion process will be terminated immediately, with any current exclusions remaining valid until they expire, or reach their volume limits, or until March 12—whichever comes first.

These 25% tariffs also will extend to derivative steel and aluminum products. However, derivative steel items made outside the U.S. from steel that has been melted and poured in the U.S., and derivative aluminum articles processed in another country from aluminum that has been smelted and cast in the U.S., are exceptions. Notably, the 25% tariff on certain derivative aluminum products that are not classified under HTS Chapter 76, such as bumper stampings for vehicles and body stampings for tractors, will apply only to the aluminum content. The 25% tariff on these articles will come into force once the Department of Commerce establishes a system for assessing and collecting the duties.

These tariffs are anticipated to prompt retaliatory measures from other countries, with the European Commission already poised to convene to discuss a response aimed at safeguarding European interests.

Deregulatory Executive Order Issued

On Jan. 31, an executive order was issued to promote "prosperity through deregulation" by requiring federal agencies to identify at least 10 existing rules, regulations, or guidance documents to repeal whenever they seek to introduce a new rule or regulation. For fiscal year 2025, the total incremental cost of all new regulations must be significantly less than zero.

This builds upon the policy in Trump’s executive order 13771 from the first term, which included a two-to-one regulatory elimination requirement. Executive order 13771 faced legal challenges, though all were dismissed.
