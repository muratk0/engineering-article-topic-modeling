# What to expect soon, economically speaking

[TARİH: 01.12.2025 The Fabricator]

Fabrinomics | Economic Insights

How consumers relate to economic data

Dr. Chris Kuehl

One of the more honest economists, John Kenneth Galbraith, famously stated that there are two kinds of forecasters: those who don’t know and those who don’t know they don’t know. Never has this been a more accurate assessment as there has been an unusual amount of uncertainty over the last few years. Where are we now?

The classic economist’s response is, "It depends." Many consumer attitude surveys are quite negative as many are worried about the impact of inflation, the state of the job market, and the state of the global supply chain. At the same time, the raw data is painting a much less dire picture. The question is, has data not caught up with consumer sentiment, or have the consumers not caught up with the data?

First, a disclaimer: Economists are creatures of data. We base our judgments on the facts as we know them. Data does change, and some data sources are better than others, but an almost total lack of information makes assessments very questionable. As I write this in early November, the government is shut down, and there has been no government data in over a month. That complicates things. To be honest, I can’t tell you what is going on in the economy with any degree of confidence. I am reminded of my days as a Soviet specialist in the 1980s—dealing with data from the USSR that was 100% bogus or utterly incomplete and irrelevant. My hope is that by the time you read this, government activity has resumed.

Third Quarter Growth

With that disclaimer out of the way, I’ll interpret the data that’s available. The Q3 GDP data came in stronger than expected. The latest assessment from GDPNow has it at 3.8%, and that is nearly a full point more robust than the 20-year average. The unemployment rate remains at a record low level of 4.3%. That is barely higher than it was a year ago. We had widespread news of layoffs in October, which at this writing hasn’t been incorporated into the official unemployment numbers. Regardless, remember that rates up to 6% unemployment are considered "normal." The rate of inflation is certainly higher than it was a year ago, but it is only 2.9% (core is at 3.1%). The Fed’s preferred target for inflation is 2.0%, so this current level is not that far off that target. Retail numbers are strong as the holiday season gets into full swing, but concerns have emerged regarding how long the consumer will keep spending.

Part of what has been driving this divergence has been the division between sets of consumers. Economists have been calling this a "K" recovery. Those in the upper third of income earners are still spending aggressively, and that has kept retail growing. They notice inflation, but it has not affected their buying behavior much. In contrast, those in the lower third have been decked by inflation and have essentially been living paycheck to paycheck. That leaves the middle third, and thus far they are still spending as long as they think their jobs are secure.

In an era of labor shortages, many assume they can keep their job or find another one easily. The quit rate as measured by the JOLTS report is at 2.0%, and that is fairly high. It is a measure of the number of people willing to quit and search for another job.

What does this mean for fabricators? The brutal answer is we just don’t know. With the government shutdown, current economic data is sparse. We can guess and estimate and make assumptions, but we just don’t have the facts. That leaves analysts struggling to make sense of the current situation.

RVs and U.S. Manufacturing

Considering the lack of current data from the federal government, I’ve chosen to zero in on one sector of the economy: recreational vehicles (RVs). I’ve done this for a couple of reasons. The first is that it represents a manufacturing sector that’s almost entirely based in the U.S., though it’s still affected by tariffs; second, the market for RVs describes an important segment of the consumer population.

This has been a year of GDP variation, and often surprisingly so. The Q1 numbers were more miserable than expected, and many started to assert that recession was imminent. It turned out that this was the impact from huge levels of imports as businesses sought to beat the tariffs. Q2 returned to above normal with a rate of 3.8%, when all was said and done. Now, Q3 is slated to come in at 4.0%. This has been driven primarily by consumer activity as well as growth in nonresidential construction.

The Producer Price Index breaks the industry down in a number of ways. There is the Recreational Vehicle Dealers section, which stood at 160.33 at last available measure, and that is down from the 184 notched in May—and way down from the high point in January 2022. The fact is that the index is still far higher than it has been for over a decade. There was a major surge in demand related to the pandemic, and even as demand has cooled, it is still higher than in most years prior.

Has the RV customer changed? Ample evidence suggests a shift, but to where? It’s complicated. For years, most assumed that RVs appealed to an older generation that combined travel and retirement. That remains a key demographic, but now the data suggests that younger buyers are emerging as they see this as a way to travel more independently and cheaply. This is similar to the patterns in Europe as many younger travelers prefer the "camper van."

Thus far, the projections for RV sales in 2026 are solid, if not as robust as they were right after the pandemic. Two areas of demand do not attract as much attention as they should. One is the construction sector, where RVs are used as temporary housing. The second is a demand surge from emergency services, as RVs are used to house those affected by storms and other natural disasters.

It is not that this industry is not affected by tariffs, as parts and assemblies come from all over the world, but over 98% of RVs are made in the U.S., and a whopping 85% are from Indiana. The price hikes have been significant, but much of that is due to increased demand, and the fact remains that manufacturers are still struggling to keep pace with that demand. Most of the material that came from around the world has been replaced to some degree by domestic sources. This "largely U.S.-made" characteristic is actually a selling point for many RV consumers.

Obviously, an RV is a significant investment, and most consumers buy them through some form of loan. The Fed rate is not the rate that borrowers will encounter from banks or other lenders, but the setting of that rate will impact what the other rates will eventually become. The Fed has reduced rates twice this year (thus far), and by modest amounts of a quarter-point each. This hardly takes Fed rates into low territory, but it signals that there is concern about economic growth.

Many manufacturers have an interest in the RV sector, and of course there are many that are not connected at all. RV manufacturing is almost entirely U.S.-based, so it’s concerning that tariff and trade policies ostensibly designed to bolster U.S. manufacturing are actually doing significant damage to an industry that is nearly a purely American sector.

Innovation Drives Economic Growth

It has been said that the real job of an economist is to argue with other economists. Nobel prize winners are often engaged in some of the most obscure elements of the field, and there have been some very obvious biases involved. After all, this award is given by a very small group—the Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences.

This year the recipients are of more significance than in past years. Their work involves the role that innovation plays in economic growth, and that is a subject that preoccupies just about everybody in government and industry. The three winners are Joel Mokyr from Northwestern, Phillipe Aghion from College de France and INSEAD and London School of Economics, and Peter Howitt from Brown University.

Their work has many key points, but the top of the list is the observation from Mokyr regarding what it takes for innovation to drive growth. Growth from innovation requires three simultaneous developments: (1) a joint evolution of science and technology, (2) mechanical competence, and (3) a willingness to deal with disruptive change.

If you look at the world today, you might think all three of these prerequisites are missing. There has been an outright war on science in the U.S. and in many European nations, and very little effort to educate people regarding the advances in science. The issue of mechanical competence has been at the top of the list for manufacturers for decades.

This is the worker shortage. The education system in the U.S. and even in Europe has ignored the need for the mechanically skilled workforce that can implement the technology that has been developed. Despite years of warning of the demographic collapse at the end of this decade, very little has been done.

This research is important as it outlines what drove the great economic growth periods, and if we are ever to see another of these, there has to be a shift in behavior and expectations. With all the political drama over trade and tariffs, few pay attention to the actual needs of the manufacturing community. Granted, solving these problems will be time consuming and expensive, but they are far more relevant than import and export patterns. It always comes down to the same thing: training, training, training. The U.S. has neglected relevant education for decades, and now that technology is changing almost by the day, that failure has become acute.
