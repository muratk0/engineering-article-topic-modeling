GERMANY IN VIEW

Germany's export-dependent economy has been hit hard by the slowdown in global demand, while structural challenges are looming in the medium term.

ISMR SAYS:

"Germany's economic performance has been strong for the past decade, with the unemployment rate currently at a record low, and healthy public and private balance sheets. But its export-dependent economy has been hit hard by the slowdown in global demand, while structural challenges are looming in the medium term. At the same time, external imbalances remain large, partly reflecting the uneven distribution of the gains from growth," commented the International Monetary Fund (IMF).

Germany's manufacturing sector has experienced a sharp decline this year. After several years of real GDP growth averaging over two per cent annually, Germany's economy slowed sharply in the second half of 2018. This reflected a mixture of weak external demand and special circumstances affecting the auto and chemical industries.

"Structural challenges are weighing on Germany's potential output. Soon, the labour force is expected to shrink as the population ages, while productivity growth remains low. Adaptation to technological change and digitalisation is slow, while the energy transition poses important uncertainties for business investment," explained the IMF, pointing out several factors contributing to this:

Current Account: Rising savings by firms, together with fiscal consolidation after 2011, fuelled the rise in Germany's current account surplus. For much of the past two decades, as unemployment fell and exports surged, wage growth lagged behind and household purchasing power stagnated, particularly among the lower paid.

Reflecting this, a rising share of national income took the form of profits retained by firms, whose ownership is highly concentrated among the wealthiest households. As these households tend to save a larger share of their income, private saving increased and the current account surplus surged.

■ Fiscal policy: With many consecutive years of large fiscal surpluses, and the public debt ratio on a declining path, Germany's fiscal space is substantial. This fiscal space should be used fully to support potential growth through public investment in physical and human capital, and to provide further tax relief for low-income households which, along with stronger wage growth, would restore their purchasing power. These policies would boost potential growth, and help reduce external imbalances, said the IMF

Structural reforms: With the working-age population set to decline and widespread labour shortages, reforms to raise productivity and domestic investment are key to sustaining growth going forward. The focus, said the IMF, should be on upgrading coverage of nationwide high-speed internet, rolling out the e-Government platform, supporting venture capital and reducing uncertainties around the strategy for an ambitious energy transition programme.

Financial sector: Amidst the “low-for-long" interest rate environment, the German banking system needs to accelerate measures to shore up profitability, which has been persistently under pressure from high costs and slow progress with restructuring. Faster restructuring should be accompanied by an expanded macroprudential toolkit and enhanced data availability to address rising macro-financial risks, concluded the IMF.

## Manufacturing in focus

European and U.S. markets slumped after new data showed Germany's economy shrank in the second quarter, and an inverted US bond yield curve sparked fresh recession warnings. Germany's economy has suffered its worst downturn in almost seven years as a manufacturing slump deepens, raising pressure on the government to add fiscal stimulus measures.

“The economy was hamstrung in Q2 by the external sector. Exports contracted strongly and domestic demand was unable to cushion the downturn. Private consumption growth slowed noticeably and fixed investment contracted. Data for the third quarter, meanwhile, continues to signal diverging trends. Industrial activity remained in a tough spot amid severe weakness in the manufacturing sector. In addition, consumer sentiment averaged lower in Q3 compared to the previous quarter,” explained analyst, Focus Economics.

“On the upside, the unemployment rate hit a record low in July, while the services sector expanded robustly in July and August. Against this backdrop, the government unveiled a somewhat looser budget as the fiscal surplus is expected to narrow and announced, on 20 September 2019, a package of stimulus measures to fight climate change; the impact of the latter on the economy should be modest, however,” it added.

In Germany, unemployment is at a historic low. A global slowdown in growth, coupled with the dangers of trade disputes, has also affected manufacturing activity and investment and could affect services and consumption. Uncertainty – driven by trade but also by Brexit, and geopolitical tensions – are also affecting economic potential.

## Machine tool orders

Orders received by the German machine tool industry in the second quarter of 2019 were 22 per cent down on the same period last year. Orders from Germany fell by 28 per cent. Eighteen per cent fewer orders were received from abroad. Orders received fell by 21 per cent in the first half of 2019. Domestic sales were down 19 per cent in this period, with overseas sales 23 per cent lower.

"In the second quarter, orders are once again more than twenty per cent down on last year's strong figures," said Dr. Wilfried Schäfer, Executive Director of VDW (German Machine Tool Builders' Association). "The base effect can no longer disguise the global decline in investment in mechanical and plant engineering.”

The machine tool industry is not immune to the factors that are creating so much turbulence in the global economy as a whole and that are currently the subject of much discussion.

We are in the same boat as the other major international suppliers. Orders for machine tools have collapsed worldwide. Many countries are now having to pay the price for the almost daily international disputes being picked by politicians," added Schäfer.

The machine tool sector is a pure supplier of capital goods and equipment to industry, and as such is exposed to pressure from weak demand in the major customer industries, the engineering sector and the automotive industry. The latter is additionally undergoing a protracted period of uncertainty as a result of the tentative transformation process towards new drive technologies and is cutting back investment accordingly.

## Skills strategy

In terms of its skills strategy, problemsolving were above the OECD average and Germany ranks in the top 40% for the strength of its learning culture (according to the OECD). Germany is also very successful at activating the skills of the workforce with high employment and labour force participation rates, and a low share of youth not in employment, education or training (NEET).

“Despite this success, there continue to be areas in which Germany could improve. For instance, there is evidence of significant skills imbalances in the labour market. Germany ranks in the bottom 40% of the OECD countries for the alignment between skills supply and labour market demand. The OECD Skills for Jobs database identifies shortages in several skills and knowledge areas, linked to strong demand in sales and marketing but also in computers and electronics and mathematics knowledge,” explained the OECD.

“Some of these imbalances could be resolved by expanding and improving the tertiary attainment rate, which is lower than in many OECD countries, and by improving the inclusiveness of tertiary education. Germany has undertaken education reforms which have improved outcomes for disadvantaged youth. However, socio-economic factors still play a big role in participation in higher education,” it added.

Germany has, nevertheless, taken important steps to respond to many of these challenges. For example, legislation was passed in 2016 to strengthen continuing education and improve access for low qualified workers and long-term unemployed, and several upskilling projects have been implemented. The government also launched a strategy for Education in the Digital World, to improve digital skills and infrastructure, and National Decade for Literacy and Basic Education 2016 to 2026, to raise reading and writing competencies as well as basic education within the context of life-long learning.

## A SHARP FOCUS

Hidde van Hoeven, Hypertherm's VP of Global Sales and Marketing, lays out an exciting tableau of the company's achievements, transition and future aspirations to ISMR, including a recent waterjet acquisition.

Hypertherm designs and manufactures industrial cutting products and software for a variety of industries such as shipbuilding, manufacturing and automotive repair. Its product line includes cutting systems, in addition to CNC motion and height controls, CAM nesting software, robotic software and consumables.

The company's reputation for cutting innovation dates back over 50 years to 1968 with its invention of water injection plasma cutting. The 100 per-cent associate-owned company has a customer-driven ethos, with more than 1,800 associates along with operations and partner representation worldwide.

Hidde van Hoeven, its VP of Global Sales and Marketing, laid out an exciting tableau of the company's achievements, transition and future aspirations to ISMR, including a recent waterjet acquisition.

ISMR: Please outline any recent successes and achievements?

HvH: Firstly, 2018 was a record year for us in sales. After the global slowdown in 2015 and 2016, the business picked up nicely and 2018 was a really good year for us across the board and around the globe.

In April this year, we completed the acquisition of OMAX, a leading supplier of integrated waterjet cutting systems based in the U.S. (the number one waterjet supplier in the U.S. and number two globally). We are delighted that it has joined the Hypertherm family and we have broadened our range of waterjet cutting solutions through that acquisition and gained a leadership position in the U.S. market. We feel that we have more global reach in that portfolio now. OMAX is very well aligned with Hypertherm. It was founded by two engineers and has a real technical and engineering innovation focus. Waterjet is now more than 20% of our business.

The integration work is well underway on the OMAX acquisition. We decided to take 18 months/max. two years for its integration into the Hypertherm family.

This year, we are focused on four value/ work streams – HR, IT, R&D and Finance. We will be kicking off other work streams, including a combined commercial and business strategy, in January 2020. We are currently doing market research on the combined waterjet business strategy going forward. We also own a waterjet company in Minnesota which focuses on intensifier technology, whereas OMAX is focused on Direct-Drive waterjet pumps. Early next year, we will decide on the longterm strategy and start implementing an integrated commercial organisation for the overall waterjet business.

Another milestone for us is reaching 200 technology patents in the U.S. We have invested a lot into technology development since our inception in 1968 and, particularly over the last decade, into in-house intellectual property attorneys as well as our technology and product development programmes. We have made a real effort to file patents on IP around the globe. When you consider that, in 2012, we had 100 patents, you can see that we have made great strides in this area.

There is a strong initiative within Hypertherm to become more familiar with the end-users of our products.

ISMR: What are your views on the current global business climate for sheet metal cutting professionals?

HvH: 2018 was a great year for us. We have, in fact, had three really good years in a row. Later in 2018, going into 2019, we saw our growth slow a little, driven largely by global political uncertainty (especially the U.S.- China trade war) as well as uncertainty in financial markets and the investment climate.

We expect the business to slightly contract in the second half of this year and probably through the first half of next year. This is also driven by a slowdown in manufacturing, especially in countries such as Germany, Turkey, Italy and, of course, China, which has had an impact on the sheet metal market and related technologies.

We are, however, quite optimistic for the second half of 2020. Economic indicators now suggest that this will start picking up, probably in the late second quarter of 2020, and we expect moderate single-digit growth for the second half of 2020. 2018 was the second consecutive year of double-digit growth for Hypertherm, resulting in record revenue levels. In 2019, we again expect to see double-digit growth although this will be less driven by organic growth and more driven by acquisition.

ISMR: Please explain your strategic and technical mission and objectives?

HvH: There is a strong initiative within Hypertherm to become more familiar with the end-users of our products. We do not sell products directly to them, we work through partners. We are trying to establish more direct relationships with leading customers in selected industries.

If we understand the impact that the cutting application has on upstream and downstream manufacturing applications, we can create more value for the customer with our solutions. With our strong focus on R&D, we can engineer solutions to help customers and we have invested considerably in our R&D pipeline.

Geographically, despite the slowdown in the Chinese economy and the global effects of the trade war, we still feel that Asia offers plenty of opportunity (70% of steel consumption is in Asia) so we will continue to extend our presence in Asia over the next few years. Technically, one of our objectives is to broaden our focus to one or two other industrial cutting solutions for our portfolio (beyond plasma, laser and waterjet). We have an in-house advanced development team for technology development as well as a ventures group and a corporate development group which identifies potential acquisition targets and investment opportunities for us in new and different technologies. Over the next two-three years, we expect to make another significant acquisition.

## FACE TO FACE

ISMR: Which issues are of prime importance for your customers and how are you addressing these issues for them?

HvH: There are several key trends that we have identified from customers. More and more customers are investing in automation of the welding process. This requires consistent cut quality, constant service etc. We are trying to provide solutions for this, enabling customers to automate the welding process.

Another challenge for our customers is to find and retain skilled labour. The work force is ageing and skilled people in cutting and welding are retiring. Our customers are struggling to find skilled and motivated new operators and to retain those skilled workers. We are trying to inject more and more intelligence into our solutions software so that results become less operator-dependent. Driven basically by the same major trend, we see that customers are also investing more and more into automation, robotic automation and robots for applications such as material handling. They are also looking for solutions for thicker plate and other materials.

Initially, a lot of robots were used for pick and place (i.e. in the automotive sector). However, now we are using robots for certain tasks that are supplemented by humans. This is a very powerful combination and unlocks a whole different set of complex applications to be performed with some level of automation. This is a very interesting area of development that we are closely following.

ISMR: What is your strategic focus/ vision over the next few years?

HvH: One other developing area in the market is the Internet of Things and the connected factory. We launched the XPR generation of our fully automated plasma cutting systems in 2017. Like the XPR family, all the products that we plan to use in the future will all be equipped and be able to connect to the internet for users to gather data, perform predictive maintenance and continuously learn/adjust to improve output/performance. Users can identify, for example, how to increase the uptime of their equipment and maximise their manufacturing.

We feel that this area will play a greater role in industrial manufacturing in years to come and we are preparing our products/ solutions for this. Accordingly, we will be partnering with key platform and cloudbased data analytics providers on this in the near future.

ISMR: Which trends do you see developing in sheet metal cutting markets?

HvH: In sheet metal, we see the increased use of laser technology (it has become more capable and more powerful).

We have also noted the use of additive manufacturing technologies for metals and we are closely following this development. It is mostly still used for rapid prototyping and very small-batch, low-volume manufacturing at the moment but we can see this becoming more and more important in sheet metal manufacturing operations over the years to come.

ISMR: Where do you see the greatest challenges and opportunities?

HvH: We aim to ensure that we offer technologies and solutions that really help customers to improve their manufacturing operations and help us to develop in the right areas (through our customer initiative, for example).

Another challenge for us is to start offering more services, especially as Industry 4.0, connectedness and data analytics will require us to offer preventive and predictive maintenance and other types of services, over time. We have been a technology and productoriented company up to now so it will be important for us to make that vital transition.

Software will play an ever-greater role in industrial manufacturing over the next few years. We got into software just over a decade ago with the acquisition of a company called MTC Software (now called Hypertherm CAM). We also have software packages for programming robots offline, called RobotMaster. Software will play an ever-greater role in industrial manufacturing over the next few years and we are well positioned for this.

ISMR: Are there any products/ technologies that you have released or are releasing this year that you would like to discuss?

HvH: In the fully automated plasma cutting realm, our XPR platform was launched in March 2017. The XPR 170® was launched late last year and, next year, we expect to launch several new products on the light industrial and handheld plasma cutting side for sheet metal (also for fully automated cutting operations). We also expect to launch more products on the waterjet side next year. We are ramping up for some major product launches in 2020.

We recently announced the release of Robotmaster Version 7.1. It is a major update to the completely redesigned and advanced architecture of its Version 7 software released last year. In total, this new robotic programming software contains more than 40 new features, 500 improvements and dozens of bug fixes.

ISMR: Which exhibitions will you be attending this year?

HvH: Show visitors can find us at IMTS (via OMAX) and FABTECH in Chicago, USA.

ISMR: Is there anything else you wish to add?

HvH: Yes, we have an unusual, but very interesting, company structure. Hypertherm has been an 100% associate-owned company since January 2014, which drives commitment from everyone working at Hypertherm. We all get a share in the profits and a vested interest in the long-term success of the company, which means that we do the right thing for our customers. This structure, and ethos, differentiates us from other manufacturers and gives us a greater incentive for success.
