# Could a new political consensus be forged in steel?

[TARİH: 01.02.2025 The Fabricator]

Steel News

Timing might have been one of the biggest reasons why a Nippon Steel-U. S. Steel deal didn’t get done

MICHAEL COWDEN

With the Nippon Steel purchase of U. S. Steel scuttled by presidential decree, might Cleveland-Cliffs join forces with Nucor to make an offer? Cleveland-Cliffs could target the integrated mills, and Nucor could take ownership of Big River Steel, an electric arc furnace in Arkansas.

Nordroden/iStock/Getty Images Plus

I

’m not sure what to say about President Donald Trump’s potential tariff and trade policies that hasn’t already been said. Same goes for the ongoing saga that is Nippon Steel’s $15 billion quest to acquire U. S. Steel.

It’s fair to say, as U. S. Steel CEO David Burritt already has, that former President Joe Biden blocking the deal was ultimately all about politics and had very little to do with national security.

Putting rhetoric aside, here’s the timeline. Biden blocked the deal on Jan. 3. He initially gave Nippon and U. S. Steel 30 days (or until early February) to unwind it. The deadline was subsequently pushed back to mid-June.

As a result, the U. S. Steel sales saga continues.

It’s anyone’s guess what happens between now and then. Steel Market Update (SMU) has tried to remain neutral on the matter, which first burst into public view in August 2023, but to be honest, I thought Nippon was a good deal for U. S. Steel.

Yet Cleveland-based Cliffs remains adamant that it will acquire U. S. Steel—despite its market cap being lower than its Pittsburgh-based rival. How does that work? Some media reports suggest that Cliffs could work together with Nucor to do so.

We’ve seen something like that before. For example, when Steel Dynamics and AK Steel made a joint bid in 2014 for the assets of Severstal North America. AK Steel took the Russian steelmaker’s union-represented mills in the North, and Steel Dynamics got its relatively new nonunion electric arc furnace (EAF) sheet mill in Columbus, Miss.

Maybe we’ll see something similar again? Cliffs would get a wall of integrated mills along the Great Lakes. Nucor would finally get Big River Steel, the startup that it tried to block more than a decade ago. Big River Steel also happens to sit along the Mississippi River, right next door to other Nucor mills in northeast Arkansas.

That said, credible media outlets also reported back in 2023 that Esmark was in the running to buy U. S. Steel. That didn’t age well. So, again, we’ll see what happens this time around.

In the meantime, the lawsuits cometh. U. S. Steel and Nippon Steel filed one against the U.S. government. They filed another against Cliffs Chairman/President/CEO Lourenco Goncalves and United Steelworkers President David McCall.

What happens next might come down to the new presidential administration. President Trump is unpredictable. He endorsed H-1B visas after campaigning against immigration. Could he change his mind on a Nippon Steel-U. S. Steel deal as well?

Or perhaps Nippon Steel will decide to buy a part of U. S. Steel, such as its attractive mining and pelletizing operations, for example. And let’s remember that there is nothing stopping Nippon Steel from building a mill in the U.S.

Doing so would cost a lot less than $15 billion. We know that because South Korean steelmaker Hyundai is reported to be in talks to build a $6 billion sheet mill in Louisiana. Building could begin as soon as 2026 and production by 2029.

"The Past Is Never Dead"

While I think Nippon Steel’s deal was a good one for U. S. Steel, I’m also not surprised that it didn’t happen. (Or, at least, it won’t happen anywhere close to the original timeline.)

I was always a little leery of some early analyst predictions that the only hurdles to the transaction would be the usual regulatory ones. I suspected the issue would become political, especially in an election year. It officially did when Trump announced his opposition to it in January 2024.

I’m not surprised by what happened partly because I grew up in and around Pittsburgh. My dad, uncles, and grandfathers all worked in mills in the area. Some briefly, some for decades. (My dad paid for college by working at LTV, and J&L before that, during summers. That kind of opportunity no longer exists in our era of ballooning student debt.)

I also remember going to dirt track races, tractor pulls, and even a NASCAR race in Bristol, Tenn. I was shocked at times to see people carrying anti-Japanese signs—as recently as the early 2000s. They had nothing to do with the races, and they sometimes seemed to conflate Pearl Harbor, Sept. 11, and the decline of the steel industry in the 1980s, a time when Japan occupied a place in the popular imagination somewhat like the one China does now.

I was convinced that kind of stuff was from another era and would pass with time. But maybe I was wrong. "The past is never dead. It’s not even past," William Faulkner once wrote. It might be as true now as it was in the early 1950s, when Faulkner first wrote it.

When work at the mills dried up in the ‘80s and ‘90s, one of my uncles went back to farming, something he’d learned from his father. Maybe that was an example of what social scientists call de-industrialization? There was no work in the mills for my cousins, and I was pretty much told that getting into steel was a very bad idea. (I didn’t listen.)

Also, it’s not just steel that left town. My uncle who worked at Aliquippa Works had a pickup and a Volkswagen Rabbit. Why? Every farmer had a pickup, and the Rabbit was good for gas mileage and getting around town, especially when gas prices where high. It also had the added advantage of being made in western Pennsylvania, right there in New Stanton, Pa., as folks of a certain age might recall. Volkswagen, I was surprised to learn, was one of the first foreign automakers to set up shop in the U.S. But that plant, like a lot of steel production, closed in the 1980s.

That output was soon replaced (and then some) by automakers like Honda and Nissan, whose operations are predominantly nonunion. Some of you might remember "Gung Ho," a Michael Keaton movie (also from the 1980s) that riffed on this theme. Volkswagen, meanwhile, moved production to Mexico and to Tennessee, which was nonunion at the time.

"When Will Steel Come Back?"

I am still asked now and then by family members of a certain age, "When will steel come back?" I typically respond, "Well, it never really left."

First it moved west to places like the Chicago area. Over the last two decades, it’s increasingly moved south, with the rise of EAF steelmaking.

Of course, steel is still being made in and around Pittsburgh at places like Mon Valley Works and JSW Mingo Junction. You could even make a case that it’s coming back again with Nucor’s new sheet mill in West Virginia.

But the feeling of loss is still real in some places. Trump, who sometimes seems to be rooted in the 1980s, maybe had an intuitive sense of that, one that allowed him to better connect with voters in a swing region of a swing state.

So why am I getting back to politics? Business is not just revenue and profits, although those are very important. Business also is culture and history. The notion of a worker earning a pension or a student working at a mill to pay for college is mostly lost on my generation. But it’s something a lot of us from places like Pittsburgh; Weirton, W.V.; or Youngstown, Ohio, remember hearing about.

I don’t want to say it’s a lost Golden Age, but my mom can tell you how great it was to get by on powdered milk when her dad was on strike. It was a time when upward mobility seemed more the norm, or at least more within reach of most people. You definitely did better in the mill or at an auto plant than your father or grandfather did scraping by on a farm.

Losing such opportunities is a tough and personal story for a lot of families. Even though some people believe that America needs to be made "great again," I think it’s generally pretty good here, all things considered. Still, I can see why the notion, embraced explicitly by Trump (and less explicitly by Democrats), has an appeal.

A New Consensus?

In any case, a consensus appears to be emerging now that globalization didn’t work out as planned. Maybe the tortured timeline of the Nippon Steel-U. S. Steel deal reflects that.

I’m not exactly sure what to call what’s coming next. Nationalism? Isolationism? Everyone goes their own way-ism? Will this next era work out better for the U.S. and former steel towns? I guess we’re about to find out.

Also, to close this out on a positive note, it’s encouraging to see presidential administrations—whether Democratic or Republican—agreeing that manufacturing is essential for national security and for a strong middle class. You could make the case that those things are interlinked.

Yes, it might take years (and multiple presidential administrations) for some of the investments made in infrastructure under the Biden administration to pan out. Just like it has taken multiple administrations to reposition trade policy.

That’s OK. Political cycles come and go. Improving infrastructure and domestic manufacturing should be something we’re all in for the long haul.

In the meantime, it’s great to see companies like Nippon Steel and Hyundai seeing the U.S. market as a great place to invest.
