# FRANCE IN FOCUS

[TARİH: 01.03.2010 ISMR]

France is the fifth largest economy worldwide with over 65 million people. After Germany, France is the second largest consumer market in Europe and had a GDP of US$ 2,490 billion in 2009 (www.investinfrance.org).

However, due to the recession in 2009 and according to results released by the French Ministry of Economy and Finance, France's basic steel production and ferro-alloy exports totalled 7.07 billion euros, a drop of 37.48% from the previous year. France's exports of steel pipes and tubes from 2008 dropped 32.78%, cold rolled steel bars dropped 56.56%, cold rolled strip dropped 44.64%, cold drawn wire dropped 40.34% and metal structures and parts dropped 15.77% whilst imports of basic steel product and ferro-alloy imports totalling 6.31 billion euros, also dropped 46.79% from the previous year.

According to statistics released on 26 January 2010 by Insee, however, household consumption of manufactured goods rose 2.1% in December and 3.0% across the fourth quarter of 2009, the largest quarterly increase since 1999. The French Minister of Economy, Industry and Employment noted that this trend reflected 'not only the success of the premium breaks that spurred purchases of new vehicles, but also the satisfactory performance of all other items of consumption'. These figures reinforce the French government's analysis that budget support should primarily focus on business investment.

## STEEL PRODUCTION

BMI's France Metals Report opined that a modest rise in French steel production in Q209 as a result of inventory rebuilding and a temporary surge in car sales will not halt the inevitable decline in the industry, which is losing out to more competitive producers in the east. In the first seven months of 2009, France's crude steel output fell 40.8% year-on-year to 6.96mn tonnes. The steel industry recovered from its December 2008 low in H109 but crude steel production has not risen substantially above 1mn tonnes per month. July, output was down 37.1% year-on-year 1.1mn tonnes, though this was up by around 28% from January.

A similar trend was observed in the aluminium industry, where Rio Tinto Alcan reduced alumina output at its Gardanne refinery by 15% and slashed output at its Saint-Jean-de-Maurienne and Dunkerque smelters by early 2009. BMI estimates that aluminium output declined 6.3% year-on-year in 2008 to 495,000 tonnes.

Rising output has been dedicated to restocking. The European market situation for flat products remained unchanged throughout H109, characterised by weak demand and de-stocking with buyers only purchasing for immediate needs.

However, by mid-2009 there were signs of price stability, indicating that the market had reached its nadir. Demand for long products had stabilised at a low level with stainless steel longs growing, although prices were still under pressure in this segment. Demand for flats remained weak, with no significant improvement expected due in no small part to the collapse in the automotive industry.

The economic downturn hit demand in crucial end-user industries such as the automotive and construction industries. The recovery will be slow as the construction industry will continue to struggle - BMI's infrastructure team forecasts real industry value growth of -4% in 2010, before a recovery is staged in 2011.

The automotive industry received a shot in the arm due to the scrappage schemes launched across Europe, and the decline in the domestic light vehicle market for January-July was only 3.7% year-on-year as local producers were able to keep up their market share.

The termination of some scrappage schemes in European markets means that the boost will have only a temporary effect on the steel flats and aluminium industries. BMI reported that the steel industry should report crude growth of around 7.5% to 13.25mn tonnes in 2010, with hot-rolled output rising 6.4% to 12.46mn tonnes. In historical terms, these are still poor results and BMI does not envisage a return to pre-recession levels over the next five years, with some plant closures almost certain. By 2013, BMI says, crude steel output should be 16.15mn tonnes, which is still nearly 10% below 2008 levels. Aluminium should fare better, helped in part by its use in lighter and more fuel-efficient vehicles. By 2013, it adds, aluminium production should have recovered to 2008 levels, provided there is no closure of capacity.

## COMBATING THE CRISIS

Currently, France's metals industry employs 720,000 people - a mainly skilled labour force that can deal with modern methods of production.

However, Mécasphère; (December 2009 issue of the Fédération des Industries Mécaniques – FIM) suggests that France may be too dedicated to its European markets and may need to look at opportunities in other market areas such as aerospace, nuclear, ferry and rail transport, BTP, agricultural equipment, medical machinery, eco equipment etc. It believes that, by joining forces with other companies, French enterprises can look for international work 'as extended companies and consequently reduce their costs - this would allow French companies to deal with lower price competition'. Moreover, it adds, investment in research and development and better use of company's current depots would help with product development. So, it says, French companies that export over 50% of their products would be advised to research joint investment opportunities in order to compete successfully abroad.

To combat the financial global credit crisis, the French government made three major decisions and these changes helped France to improve her business environment and become the second leading recipient of foreign direct investment in 2008 after the US.

Firstly, it continued to inject capital into the banking sector throughout 2009 to negate the effects of the crisis. Secondly, its economy underwent some major reforms that had been introduced slowly since 2007 and finally, the government introduced the 'LME', 'Law to Modernize the Economy', in August 2008. This was set up to help businesses, by allowing more competition and creating a 'Single Competition Authority', thereby making France more economically attractive. The main changes for the economy were that:

• Labour laws were made more flexible as employees were given the right to work longer hours and therefore allowing businesses more freedom with their working week schedules.

• Tax cuts were introduced for businesses, individuals and expatriates. For businesses, new investments were made exempt from local business tax and this included all new investments and existing investments from the beginning of 2010. This meant a tax cut for companies of about 12.3 billion euros in 2010. Other changes included a change in tax laws for entrepreneurs, which became so popular that it had over 300,000 new applicants in the first year.

• Corporate laws were rectified and simplified, which made working and setting up an SAS or other company in France much easier. There was also a 'modernization of risk capital instruments: a new category of French private equity funds, in which the investment, commitment and partial buyback rules were based on the regulations governing the fund.' (FIM RAPPORT ANNUEL 2008 16 17).

Due mainly to these changes, France's steel production rose in January 2010 by 27.2% (World Steel Review, January 2010).

French industry, however, continues to be innovative in the face of the global economic downturn.

Visitor numbers at MIDEST, France's industrial subcontracting exhibition, recorded a significant 5.5% increase in visitor numbers – 39,710 professionals over the four days of the show - and the number of visitors from outside France, drawn from 78 countries rather than the 54 of 2008, increased by 13%. MAINTENANCE EXPO's visitor numbers rose by a more modest 1% to 2,998 over the four days of the show.

Therefore, despite the particularly difficult economic climate, the shows performed excellently in terms of visitor and exhibitor numbers. Exhibitors reported an increased presence of high-level customers and a wealth of new contacts.

## FACING THE FUTURE

The December 2009 issue of the Fédération des Industries Mécaniques (FIM's) 'Mécasphère' suggests that France needs a three-pronged attack to increase future market share.

• To invest more funds in existing companies; or lack of progress will be a distinct disadvantage

• To return to the real image of 'metal market cutting edge technology', in order to attract a young work force

• To strengthen ties with the Economic, Social and Environmental Council (CESE) to deal with the weaknesses in the semistructured metal industry and to ramp up future development. ISMR
