# AN EYE ON ALUMINIUM

[TARİH: 01.08.2017 ISMR]

The aluminium content in cars could continue its growth trajectory by as much as 30% in the next ten years.

From primary to recycling, the total demand for aluminium is increasing by an average of 2% each year. These figures, released at the ALUMINIUM 2016 trade fair in Düsseldorf, Germany, confirm aluminium to be the material of choice for a broad range of strategic applications.

Gerd Götz, Director General of European Aluminium, said, "Our forecasts for 2016 and 2017 show steady growth in both production and demand across the aluminium value chain in Europe. Clearly, aluminium is the preferred material for applications with strategic value for a sustainable economy, such as transport, packaging and buildings."

Following a difficult period, primary aluminium production in Europe has rebounded. European Aluminium data forecasts positive growth rates of 1.1% in 2016 and of 1.3% in 2017. This is despite the global challenge of significant overcapacities in countries such as China, where primary overcapacity is five times the size of EU production.

## A growing industry

European demand for semi-finished aluminium products is healthy. Flat rolled products, found for example in the production of cans and cars, were forecasted to increase by 2.4% in 2016 and 1.9% in 2017. Extruded products, used for instance in windows and machinery, are likely to see a 1.6% increase in 2016 and 1.2% increase in 2017. This healthy demand is against a backdrop of increasing exports of semi-finished products from China, which grew by 21% in 2015 and by 17% in 2014.

## Aluminium Auto Body Sheet (ABS)

In 2016, European Aluminium also published a new study on the aluminium content of cars. The study, conducted with Ducker Worldwide, uses data from automotive companies and suppliers, European Aluminium member companies and existing Ducker Worldwide data. It offered a revealing insight into how aluminium is being deployed in European cars.

Flat rolled products, found for example in the production of cans and cars, were forecasted to increase by 2.4% in 2016 and the study analyses the use of aluminium castings, extrusions, forgings and sheet for 33 component groups in a sample of 93 car models. This was subsequently extrapolated to cover the entire 2016 production forecast for EU28. It showed that in 2016, the average European car incorporated 150kg of aluminium, an amount expected to rise to nearly 200kg by 2025. Auto Body Sheet has the greatest potential for growth, with a predicted increase of 110% in the next 10 years.

"While the demand for aluminium Auto-Body Sheet (ABS) is growing fast, our industry is prepared to support the car industry in executing their lightweight vision. More than 1.1 billion euros were invested in ABS capacity over the past ten years and we expect to see a capacity increase of 65% over the next two years, according to our internal survey," said the association.

The study forecasts the aluminium content in cars to increase by up to 30% over the next ten years. This surge is mainly from rolled and extruded products, where Auto Body Sheet leads the growth with an expected increase of 110% over the same period. The growth is largely attributed to aluminium's role in lightweighting cars, thereby contributing to low emission mobility.

## Aluminium beverage can recycling reaches new highs

The overall recycling rate for aluminium beverage cans in the European Union, Switzerland, Norway and Iceland increased by 1.8% to a new record level of 71.3% in 2013. European Aluminium considers this result an important milestone on its path towards its voluntary recycling target for used beverage cans of 80% by 2020.

"From its first introduction more than 50 years ago, the aluminium beverage can has been an integral part of the circular economy. It is infinitely recyclable without loss of its properties and its value. This makes it the ideal packaging solution to help achieve the new ambitious EU recycling targets proposed for the years 2025 and even 2030," said European Aluminium.

Maarten Labberton, Director of the European Aluminium Packaging Group stresses the need for more efficient collection, sorting and recycling schemes in countries which have lessdeveloped recycling infrastructure:

Through its joint awareness programme with the can manufacturers, 'Every Can Counts', European Aluminium is addressing the collection and recycling of so called "out-ofhome" cans (cans consumed at the workplace, at festivals or other outdoor events). In 2015, Spain and Serbia joined 'Every Can Counts' and more countries are expected to adopt this model in the near future.

"Over the entire European continent (including other eastern European countries, Russia and Turkey) more than 28 billion cans have been recycled. Their metal stays in the European circular economy and remains available for the production of new aluminium products," explained European Aluminium.

"These collected cans represent a total amount of 400,000 tonnes of recycled aluminium. Recycling aluminium saves up to 95% of the energy used or the Greenhouse Gas Emissions (GHG) emitted to produce primary aluminium. This means that European aluminium can recycling avoids 3.2 million tonnes of GHG, which is equivalent to the yearly emissions of the population of cities such as Bilbao, Cardiff, Lublin or Nice."

## We analyse the advantages of precision cutting with plasma.

Method from an electric arc, constricted by a nozzle. The cutting process begins with a pilot arc which is ignited between the nozzle and electrode (cathode) by high voltage. The pilot arc has low energy and partly ionises the atoms between the plasma torch and workpiece.

As soon as the pilot arc touches the workpiece, the electric circuit closes and the main arc is ignited by the increased power. The high thermal energy of the arc and the high kinetic energy of the plasma gas melt the material and the molten material is driven out of the kerf. The small heat-affected zone and high cutting speeds are particularly strong advantages of this method. The result is a relatively clean, rapid cutting process.

Both oxy-fuel and plasma cutting systems have their advantages and disadvantages. Deciding which to use depends on factors such as material type and thickness, available power resources and the cost and location of the job. However, technology advancements are making plasma a more portable option than ever before.

## The benefits of plasma cutting

Precision plasma systems (high current density) are designed and engineered to produce the sharpest, highest quality cuts achievable with plasma. The torch and consumable designs are more complex, and additional pieces are included to further constrict and shape the arc. Multiple gases, such as oxygen, high purity air, nitrogen and a hydrogen/argon/nitrogen mixture, are used as the plasma gas for strong results on a multitude of conductive materials.

Plasma cutters are used to perform cutting and gouging operations, with the average hand-held system capable of cutting metal thicknesses of around one inch. Plasma typically requires a source for compressed air and a substantial amount of electrical power. One of plasma's greatest advantages is its ability to cut non-ferrous metals such as aluminium, stainless steel and cast iron.

Speed and precision cutting are also benefits of plasma, which typically cuts with minimal slag and can provide smooth cuts with a narrower kerf than that produced by an oxy-fuel system.

fuel torch. Plasma does not require the metal to be preheated before cutting, which saves time, and plasma cutters can also outperform oxy-fuel torches when cutting stacked metals. Faster speeds can be achieved on thinner metals with plasma, with minimal or no metal distortion. Plasma systems are also relatively simple to use compared to oxy-fuel systems, with the benefit of minimal clean-up.

Plasma cutting systems can offer benefits for cutting thinner ferrous and non-ferrous metals, including shaped metals — angles, channels and tubes, for example. Plasma cutters are also better able to cut large volumes of thin sheet metal and offer rapid grate-cutting capability. They do not require storing, handling explosive gases or dealing with an open flame, resulting in a safer job-site.

## An eye on cost and quality

In addition to the quality of the plasma cut, users attach high importance to the cost per cutting metre to maintain efficient operation of their production. Factors include cutting speed, gas consumption and the lifetime of consumables (e.g. gas usage, consumable nozzles, electrodes, garnet abrasive etc.) plus utilities (electricity, water etc.).

In some cases, amortised capital equipment cost may also be included as well as labour cost (such as for loading fresh sheets or unloading cut parts and scrap). Productivity or cut speed is the biggest influence on the real cost per part

The quality of cut surfaces when using thermal cutting processes is determined by the rectangularity and inclination tolerance and the average surface roughness. Additionally, the groove lag, the initial fusing of the upper edge and possible dross or drop formation at the bottom cutting edge of the work-piece can be taken into consideration.

In conclusion, plasma provides an optimal mix of cut quality, productivity and operating costs for mild steel, stainless steel and aluminium across a wide range of thicknesses.
