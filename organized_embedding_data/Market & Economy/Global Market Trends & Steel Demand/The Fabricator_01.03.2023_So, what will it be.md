# So, what will it be?

[TARİH: 01.03.2023 The Fabricator]

Fabrinomics | Economic Insights

A Recession or Soft Landing?

Dr. Chris Kuehl

Generally speaking, I place myself in the category of an optimistic economist. Everything is relative when one is part of the "dismal science," but I have consistently opined that we are facing more of a downturn in 2023 rather than a true recession. I still hold that we are more likely to see a soft landing than a deep economic retreat.

On the other hand (an economist’s favorite phrase), predictions from an economist are often better characterized as a warning rather than a prognostication. If certain trends do not reverse or certain actions are not taken, the economy could well sink into decline. Basically, it is a call to action (or sometimes inaction).

Inflation and the Labor Shortage

The chances for a next recession depend on three factors. How soon does it manifest? How long does it last? And what steps can be taken to limit it? Inflation remains the critical issue as this is what the central banks will focus on. At the moment, the rate of inflation remains consistently at two to three times the preferred rate. The Fed would like to see the personal consumption expenditures (PCE) price index between 2.0% and 2.5%, and at the moment it is between 4.0% and 4.5%. There are similar gaps in the EU, Japan, and other nations, and that means there are plenty of reasons to stay focused on inflation. There were several moves made during the 2020 recession that have complicated the inflation issue now. The savings rate soared as people were unable to spend, and that has left the U.S. with a $3.5 trillion overhang in savings—this is in spite of "revenge spending" that took place in 2021 and part of 2022.

The longer-term fear is that inflation issues have changed fundamentally. The average rate of global inflation had been around 2.0%, and most of the central banks spent the bulk of the last decade trying to get rates up to 2.0% as they had been languishing close to deflation level. The "new normal" may be closer to 4.0% rather than 2.0%.

This has many implications. Central banks will have a harder time cutting rates in the event of a recession. Governments that borrowed to stimulate will be punished by bond markets that grow far less tolerant of free spending. At the top of the list of drivers for this inflation is wages. The pressures that drove inflation in 2022 were based on supply chain breakdown and commodity prices.

Now the threat is labor, and for the simplest of reasons. The workforce is in rapid decline all over the developed world. In the U.S. alone, every Boomer will have hit retirement age by 2030 (74 million people). There is no quick fix. Robotics and automation will come to dominate, but that still means needing people who can run that technology. This is a problem the powers that be should have been able to see coming from the moment a Boomer generation child was born, but little was done. In fact, there have been active attempts to reduce population in most of the developed nations.

How Trade Has Changed

Three trends can be expected as far as global trade is concerned. The first is that reshoring will accelerate in the U.S. and in Europe. About $1 trillion in reshoring activity took place in the U.S. in 2022, and that amount is expected in 2023 as well. The limiting factor has been workforce availability, and that means that many of the companies heading back to the U.S. will rely heavily on robotics and automation.

The second trend has been labeled "friend shoring," and refers to the fact that trading with nations such as China will be made more complicated when there are political rivalries in play. More emphasis has been placed on nations friendly to the U.S., such as Mexico, India, and Vietnam.

The third trend is returning to multiple suppliers in order to reduce vulnerability. This translates into an aggressive diversification effort, and it also means adjusting to suppliers that may not be as easy to deal with as the primary supplier.

It is clear that global trade has been in transition since 2020. Assumptions regarding the extent of the pandemic influence were somewhat overblown, but patterns have been altered, and it is unlikely there will be a return to old patterns in their entirety.

Central Banks React

Most of the predictions regarding a recession in 2023 revolve around what the central banks choose to do. If they keep hiking interest rates to address inflation, the chances of a recession increase. Recently, the banks have started to slow the pace of these hikes. The Fed indicates it will hike by a quarter point at their next meeting. After that, they may take a break for a few months to see what happens. The Bank of Canada hiked rates to 4.5% and then indicated that was as far as they planned to go, even though they had previously hinted they could go as far as 5.5%.

Generally speaking, the world’s largest central banks tend to move in a similar direction. There are often differences in terms of pace and duration, but it is relatively rare that the banks differ on the fundamental assessment of the global economy. That is the situation that seems to have developed in the last few weeks as the Federal Reserve is painting a different picture from the one provided by the European Central Bank and the Bank of England. Granted, there are some major differences between these economies that can justify the differences between policies, but there are also some radically divergent assumptions when it comes to financial conditions.

The Fed is moving more definitively toward an assumption that the U.S. and global economies are headed for a "soft landing," or at least a far milder downturn than had been expected even a few months ago. This position has been made a bit more awkward by the latest employment data. Traditionally, the position of a central bank is that they hike rates until something in the economy breaks, and after that occurs the banks then set about lowering rates to fix what they broke. Once they believe they have "broken" enough of the economy, they will halt further rate hikes and wait to see if the inflation threat subsides. The tool that is used most often to determine whether anything has been broken is the rate of unemployment. Given the surge in job growth reported in February, it doesn’t appear that this has been broken. If there has been no increase in the jobless rate, what is convincing the Fed that a soft landing is possible? Why are the other central banks less convinced?

The Fed is of the opinion that labor rates are not as indicative as they once were. There are three factors that have played a role in this evaluation. The first is the chronic labor shortage. We have been hammering on this topic for years, and every industry sector has pleaded with the powers that be to do something, but to no avail. The reality is that there are some 10 million jobs on offer and only about 6 million who could conceivably take these jobs (if they had the necessary training and education). Companies are very reluctant to lay people off for fear they will not be able to find a workforce in the future.

The second factor is the growth of the gig economy. There are millions of jobs that can be done essentially part time or at least not formally. How are these workers to be counted? When they are asked in the household survey, they often assert they do not have a job as they are not formally employed.

A third factor is a reduction in immigration, legal and illegal. This is nearly impossible to measure (at least the illegal kind). It is clear that fewer migrants are finding their way into the workforce as pressure is applied to employers. The bottom line is that labor numbers are not as reliable as they once were. What, then, is the Fed using?

A combination of indicators show the economy has slowed in reaction to higher rates: decline in single family housing starts, deterioration of the Purchasing Managers Index readings, a slowdown in industrial production. There are also signals of reduced inflationary pressure such as lower-cost commodities, lower factory gate prices as measured by the Producer Price Index, reduced cost of shipping, and so on. The problem is that these measures are volatile. Gas prices have been on the rise lately, and there is no guarantee that supply chain issues are in the past.

The Fed thinks there is enough evidence to show a reduction in inflation and a reason to reduce financial sector pressure through higher rates. This is, thus far, not a position held by the European Central Bank (ECB) or the Bank of England (BoE). It must be noted that the ECB started its hikes slowly and only now is at 2.5%, roughly half of the Fed rate. The BoE has many more issues than either the Fed or the ECB due to the chaos of British politics over the last few years.

If the Federal Reserve stays true to its assessment of the coming economy and believes a soft landing is in the offing, the impact will be significant. It means an end to interest rate hikes sooner rather than later, and it would signal a more open credit market. It will be the Fed’s way of saying it had done what it set out to do.
