Congress finally passed what is called trade promotion authority (TPA), which requires the House and Senate to vote on any trade deal without being able to offer amendments. This is seen as a boon to the Obama administration as it negotiates a 12-nation Trans-Pacific Partnership (TPP) trade pact. (The TPP includes Australia, Brunei, Canada, Chile, Japan, Malaysia, Mexico, New Zealand, Peru, Singapore, and Vietnam.) The TPP won’t be presented to Congress until the end of the year at the earliest, if then.

Some elected officials worked hard to include strong language that targeted some Asian countries’ currency manipulation (intervention in monetary policy that helps to keep a currency below what should be its true market value), but in the end the TPA did not include any of that.

"The administration has also said that they would veto a bill that addresses currency manipulation in any meaningful way," said Caroline Behringer, Democratic communications director, House Ways and Means Committee. It is unlikely any anti-currency manipulation language gets into the final TPP document as well.

That said, many manufacturing groups were generally happy with congressional passage of the TPA, particularly after House Democrats threatened to scuttle the legislation mostly because of its failure to include trade adjustment funding for manufacturers hurt by imports. The adjustment piece was approved, eventually, as a separate piece of legislation.

Some elected officials worked hard to include strong language in the trade promotion authority legislation that targeted some Asian countries’ currency manipulation, but in the end the TPA did not include any of that.

A second, somewhat related piece of legislation also passed in late June. The Trade Preferences Extension Act of 2015 (H.R. 1295) is of particular relevance to the metalworking industry. The legislation makes it easier for the U.S. International Trade Commission (ITC) to decide that imports of one sort or another cause "material injury" to a U.S. industry. This is a requirement for the Commerce Department to establish tariffs on imported products.

U.S. Steel, Nucor, and some other steel companies were main backers of this bill. They also, not so coincidentally, have filed an important import injury case against China, India, Italy, South Korea, and Taiwan at the ITC. The suit concerns a common kind of coated steel used in automobile and construction industries. The trade preferences provision will make it easier for the ITC to get to a positive injury decision leading to import duties on some major steel product categories.

Manufacturing Groups Lobby Against Proposed Ozone Standards

Nearly 100 manufacturing associations, including a number involved in metalworking, sent a letter to President Obama on June 19 urging him to terminate the Environmental Protection Agency’s ozone reduction proposal. The EPA has proposed lowering it from the current 75 parts per billion to somewhere between 65 and 70 PPB. EPA is subject to a court order that requires a final rule by Oct. 1, 2015. The letter said, "The EPA’s proposed ozone rule could be the most expensive regulation in U.S. history."

EPA projects that in the U.S. 358 counties would not meet the 70-PPB standard and 558 counties would violate a 65-PPB standard. Under the proposed rule, states with nonattainment areas would be required to submit detailed plans to EPA indicating how they would reduce NOx and VOC emissions, which combine in the air and turn to ozone in the presence of sunlight.

The letter to Obama came three days after the House Energy and Commerce Committee held hearings on the proposed ozone rule. EPA estimates that annual costs to implement the ozone rule (excluding California) would be $3.9 billion to $15 billion for a 65- to 70-PPB standard in 2025.

"This proposed rule is a tough pill for the manufacturing industry to swallow. An ozone nonattainment designation would make it significantly more difficult for industries to invest and create businesses in communities across the United States," said Rep. Fred Upton, R-Mich., chairman of the House Energy and Commerce Committee.
