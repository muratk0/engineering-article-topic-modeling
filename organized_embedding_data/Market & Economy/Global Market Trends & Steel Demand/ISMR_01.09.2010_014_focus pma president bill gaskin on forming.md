# FOCUS PMA President, Bill Gaskin ON FORMING

[TARİH: 01.09.2010 ISMR]

PMA (Precision Metalforming Association) is the full-service trade association representing the US$ 113-billion metal forming industry of North America—the industry that creates precision metal products using stamping, fabricating, spinning, slide forming and roll forming technologies, and other value added processes. ISMR speaks to Bill Gaskin, PMA President, on his view of metal forming markets, trends and future targets.

In the second quarter of this year, compared to the first quarter, the average company added about 14% of its workforce but, considering the depth of the downsizing and layoffs in 2009, there is still quite a way to go before companies return to their former typical size.

So, within the manufacturing/metal forming segment of our membership, we have really seen some positive trends over the last year. We survey our members every month on how they feel about business prospects for the next three months. During the first week of August, 82% of our members said that their orders for the next three months would be the same or better than their orders over the last three months, which is pretty positive. Only 18% thought that their order levels would be lower. On the shipments side, numbers are not quite so rosy. 63% said that their shipments, for the next three months, would be the same or higher than for the past three months so we will see how that plays out.

There is a lot of talk in the US and elsewhere in the world that the recovery is softening a little bit now. In the US, they are talking about 2-3% GDP growth. The stock market in the US reflects the uncertainty at the moment. However, if we look at our indexes and measures (which are usually quite predictive of what is happening) – despite the uncertainty over consumer spending, the housing market, political issues and tax levels in the US – there is still a lot of solid economic data in our segment that says that we will continue to recover.

We expect orders and shipments to stay above the 12-month rolling average for at least the third, and probably the fourth, quarter of 2010. There is still a lot of excess capacity out there which is dampening demand for machine tools in our sector, which is a concern for suppliers to the metal forming industry. Companies that are making machinery investments are trying to make them wisely, with an eye towards automation and efficiency and maximising output as much as possible.

Because companies in the manufacturing sector were very aggressive about lowering their costs last year – they saw the downturn coming, reacted quickly and made hard decisions on workforce costs and increasing efficiencies – their profitability has been quite strong as business has returned this year.

I think it will take a couple of years for most companies to get back to where they were in terms of size in their peak in 2007/8. In the US, they are predicting slow growth over the next couple of years and there are concerns that we might double dip. We are expecting 2- 2.5% GDP growth. Inflation, right now, is not an issue and may not be for quite some time.

So far, year to date in 2010, our typical PMA member company has 54% higher orders and 43% higher shipments than they did for the first six months of last year

ISMR: Credit continues to be an issue for manufacturers – how has PMA helped its members with this?

BG: There is a concern about credit right now. Many companies which have tried to get financing to buy raw materials, re-hire staff or buy new automation equipment have had trouble getting loans from traditional lending sources. That mostly has to do with the condition of the financial sector in the US but is also a result of the 2009 experience, where many companies lost money and therefore do not meet the bankability requirements of private loans through banks.

This is generally a temporary impairment for many of these companies so we have been lobbying pretty actively for recognition that there is a whole sector of manufacturing (particularly small to mid-sized companies) that has been adversely affected by this. It does not mean that they are not viable companies going forward – it just means that they lost money in a very difficult economic period. However, it impacts their ability to borrow funds as well as the rate at which they can borrow the funds.

Financial regulators right now are demanding more scrutiny of the lending risk in the banking industry so one hand works against the other – the businesses that are temporarily impaired need financing but the credit regulators are requesting more caution from lenders. We have been trying to help our US elected officials understand that, in most cases, this is a temporary situation and the banking sector should put this in context from a historical perspective.

ISMR: Do you see growth trends in any particular regional metal forming markets?

BG: Most of our companies make metal assemblies and components and 90% of the business that our member companies do is in North America, with exports mainly to Mexico and Canada.

We certainly have seen earlier this year, with the weakened dollar, a growing trend towards increased global competitiveness. There are plenty of companies that serve the automotive industry, for example, who (with the restructuring of the automotive supply chain) are finding opportunities in Europe to supply their automotive customers. There are also more opportunities in the medical device manufacturing sector where a better cost structure here helps US companies be more competitive globally.

Mexico, of course, has had the biggest impact on the US as a regional market. There is a growing interest from large OEMs to have suppliers locate in Mexico. Some of our member companies have facilities or joint ventures in Mexico which is preferable to having the business move to China. We see Mexico as the biggest opportunity for export growth for us, even though it is also a competitor.

ISMR: Please outline current metal forming and fabricating trends, technologies and developments in the sector.

BG: The over-riding theme has to be‘lean’. Every company in the last 18 months has been getting lean – whether they wanted to or not – but most have really been focusing on doing that. Keeping costs down, eliminating workforce positions, improving efficiencies etc. – lean is a continuing and ongoing process.

On the equipment side, certainly anything relating to automation – ERP/MRP software systems, software as a service (SaaS)/cloud services etc. – has become more popular. I know several companies that have told me that they are not looking for new stamping lines but they are interested in ways to automate them and make them more efficient as well as improve tolerances and repeatability. This includes new ways to package parts and assemblies or reduce run volumes but maintain efficiencies (ie quicker setups, reassembled welding jigs for families of parts rather than just one dedicated part etc.).

These are the primary trends – increased automation and improved productivity. Certainly in the US, we continue to experience the shorter batch runs (hundreds of thousands of parts, rather than millions etc.). Stamping companies will buy lasers or turrets so they can be involved in shorter run production at the same time as making higher volumes of other parts. The fabricating segment of our metal forming industry did not dive as deeply during the economic downturn and has recovered more quickly than the stamping side (which tends to be more focused on automotive and higher volume work). There is a continuing shift towards more fabricating technologies.

For many years, companies were focused on just providing core technologies for customers. Today, that range of core technologies for customers has greatly expanded so that our member companies will try to partner or build a business relationship (mainly through the PMA) to provide whatever part a customer may need.

ISMR: Please give us some background history on PMA. Who are its members, how many do you have and how do you help them? Please also outline the benefits of membership.

BG: The PMA was founded in 1942 – we have been through three name changes since then. We started as the Pressed Metal Institute. In the mid-sixties, we became the American Metal Stamping Association and, in the mid-eighties, we became the Precision Metalforming Association. That really reflects the change over the last 60 years or so in the industry where the model in the 1940s and 1950s was for a company to be a metal stamper, making high volume parts only. Today, companies are solution providers who will also assemble and clean parts, as well as stamp them, and perform automated welding and other processes to serve customers.

Our membership has broadened to reflect this although it has become smaller over the last year and a half in the wake of the global economic downturn – we now have around 900 member companies (in precision sheet metal stamping, fabricating, spinning etc.). They serve virtually every market – automotive takes just less than half of the market for metal parts and components made by our member companies. Other segments include medical, defence, aerospace, appliances, telecoms etc. We therefore have a very diversified membership.

We have 17 local chapters to serve members throughout North America. We are very active in Washington and lobby on trade issues for members (such as the steel tariff, China currency issues etc.). More importantly, we have tried to create a better business climate in the US, trying to help companies obtain funds to innovate or for R&D (tax credits) and trying to get a lower corporate income tax rate, as well as lobbying on regulatory issues for members. We also publish MetalForming magazine and provide statistics and market / industry benchmarking data for members – we survey our members every month on their views of the market.

This year, we launched a new PMA.org website so that members can log in and build a profile, helping them to sell their products and services to others. We have also launched, in the last month, a new MyPMA Risk Manager software as a service website where members can access and share thousands of documents on human resources, training, environmental and safety issues that help them to better track and manage risk. We are hoping that this will become a core member service.

ISMR: What are your main objectives and targets over the next few years? How do you intend to achieve them?

BG: Personally, as president of PMA, I want to ensure that PMA is sustainable long term to meet the needs of the metal forming industry in North America. We were very financially healthy in the 1980s and 1990s and were able to accumulate reserves which have helped us particularly over the last couple of years.

Our current focus for the next few years is to return to sustainability including finding new ways to generate the revenues we need so that we can continue to provide services to members. We have substantially cut costs over the last couple of years but we need to rebuild and reposition ourselves for the future.

One of the ways we are doing this is to collaborate with the National Tooling and Machining Association in the US (the tool and die mouldmaking/precision automation machinery manufacturing association). It is a similar size to us and we have brought our advocacy efforts together so we are now branded as the NTMA/PMA OneVoice advocacy programme. Together, we are more effective than we could be by ourselves. The NTMA, and the Industrial Fasteners Institute, will now be based in our building outside Cleveland and we can cost share and work with each other to improve operational efficiencies. There is no loss of identity, no mergers – we will continue to serve our individual memberships.

We also want to continue to ensure that our industry has the technical and management information it needs and to promote the idea that manufacturing is critical for long term success in the US. It is a message that is being echoed back to us from Washington – Senators and Congressmen who were traditionally not pro-manufacturing are now recognising (over the last year and a half) that manufacturing now needs to be rebuilt in the US.

ISMR: How are preparations going for Fabtech and what do you have planned?

BG: We combined the METALFORM show with Fabtech / AWS Welding exhibition as a service to the industry and to help our exhibitors cut costs. It is now the largest showcase for metal forming equipment and technology in the US. It is being held in Atlanta from 2-4 November this year. There are about 1060 exhibitors this year and we are expecting around 20,000 visitors.

This year, we are adding a new section on metal finishing to the show. There will be 93 presentations over the three days on metal forming technologies along with management-oriented workshops. We are running one on global diversification and manufacturing trends this year. There will also be sessions on the medical sector, aerospace and wind energy sector opportunities.
