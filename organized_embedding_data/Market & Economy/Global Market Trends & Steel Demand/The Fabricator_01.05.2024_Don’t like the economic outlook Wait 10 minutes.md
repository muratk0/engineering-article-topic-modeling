# Don’t like the economic outlook? Wait 10 minutes

[TARİH: 01.05.2024 The Fabricator]

Fabrinomics | Economic Insights

Dr. Chris Kuehl

Once again, economists are expressing gratitude for the existence of meteorologists as they make us look good. Beyond that, the economy now resembles Kansas weather. If you don’t like it, don’t worry. It will change by the end of the day.

We’ve been steadfastly asserting that a recession is imminent for five quarters, and every quarter brings solid growth numbers. The annual rate of growth for 2023 was 2.5%, and that’s basically the 20-year average. The growth in Q3 was spectacular at over 5.0%. So far, the growth in Q1 of 2024 is close to 2.8%, according to data from the Atlanta Fed.

There have been four drivers for the economy over the past year, and three are still going strong. The main lever has been consumer spending, and there’s little sign of a slowdown. Retail sales were up 0.6% in January and up 0.8% over January 2023. The second driver has been government spending, and all that infrastructure money is now making its way into the economy. The third driver has been nonresidential construction, and it has been on a near-exponential trajectory due to all the transportation-related activity. The only driver that has faded has been inventory-build, with most companies now back to normal levels as far as their sales-to-inventory ratios are concerned. This seems to add up to a robust year, at least for the first half.

Supply Chain Concerns

Supply chain issues are not as severe as they were during the pandemic, but they have become more complicated and difficult to assess. The issues are now geopolitical as well as weather-related. The first concern developed over a year ago as the drought in Central America dried up the lake that makes up the bulk of the Panama Canal route. A system that once carried more than 35 ships a day has been limited to around 20 and often fewer. Before this drought, the Panama Canal saw 40% of global freight pass through.

The impact has been severe in terms of logjams at West Coast ports and reduced activity for Gulf Coast operations and those on the eastern seaboard. There is no sense of when this will end because the rains haven’t started yet.

Next up is the crisis in the Red Sea. This has had a limited impact on U.S. shipping because this route primarily brings goods from Asia to Europe and vice versa. The part that does affect the U.S. is in commodities because this is a route taken by oil tankers as well as ships carrying food. The issue is not that the Houthi terrorists have been able to attack that many ships; it is a matter of insurance. The industry is no longer willing to cover ships transiting that region.

As if that weren’t enough, there’s the Baltimore port issue. There are many alternative ports on the eastern seaboard, and ships have already rerouted, but that has created additional congestion at these alternatives. Baltimore was key for shipments of cars from Asia and Europe, and that sector will be hard pressed to find alternatives.

Overproduction in China

Unlike the supply chain issue that developed during the pandemic, the concern is not in terms of production. If anything, the supply chain is dealing with the opposite issue—too much production from China. The Chinese economy is dealing with deflation, and that has collapsed prices internally as well as for exports. The Chinese government is very reluctant to cut production as this would create unemployment problems. Therefore, industry just keeps producing despite reduced demand, and that surplus is dumped on the global market.

These goods that no longer have demand in China are once again clogging the ports in the U.S., Europe, and other Asian nations. The supply chain index had been showing a calmer situation but has been worsening lately—not to the same extent as a few years ago, but the upward trend is not as solid. The Baltic Dry Index has been dropping steadily since the first of the year. It fell to a six-week low at the start of April as overall shipping volumes have been down. That is probably a good trend for the moment given the congestion threats.

Drifting Inflation Rates

Inflation rates have started to drift back up. It doesn’t constitute a crisis by any stretch because the rates are still close to what the Federal Reserve has indicated is its target. The Fed looks at data from the Personal Consumption Expenditures (PCE) index, and it is sitting at 2.5%. However, a few weeks ago, it was as low as 2.3%. (

.)

The driver for this expansion of inflation has been wages. The latest comments from a key Fed official have some predicting interest rates will not fall as expected at the end of this year. Chris Waller essentially pointed out that there is no urgency as long as jobless rates are low, economic growth is solid, and there is still a threat of inflation. Many economists have come to the same conclusion and suggest rates may stay high into 2025.

So, What Is the Fed Funds Rate, Anyway?

The debate over whether to cut the Fed Funds Rate is in full force. Note that the Fed Funds Rate is simply the rate that banks charge each other for overnight loans. It does not automatically make other rates change, but they usually do within weeks. The rate is set by the Open Market Committee, which consists of the Fed governors plus four regional Fed presidents who serve rotating one-year terms. Today, the committee is well balanced between "hawks" and "doves."

The prime rate is what banks use to set their other rates—everything from mortgages to car loans and business loans. It is almost always three points higher than the Fed Funds Rate (which is currently between 5.25% and 5.5%). That leaves the prime rate at 8.5%. Another significant rate is the mortgage rate. The national average for a 30-year fixed mortgage is 7.5%, which is down from the 9.2% rate noted at the start of 2023. The mortgage rates are also affected by the bond market, and this has been slightly more favorable of late (and therefore, the prime rate could also come down to 7.5%).

The mantra of the central bank is that they hike rates until they break something, then lower those rates to fix what they broke. This is in the quest to control inflation. The challenge now is that it’s unclear the Fed has managed to break anything. Jobless rates are still at historic lows, well below 4.0%. The wage hikes are what has been driving inflation, and wages are going up because there is a shortage of workers.

The Fed is not feeling any real urgency to cut rates as long as the unemployment rate is this low. This is why there are conversations among the Fed hawks regarding the timing of any rate reduction. The thinking now is that the Fed might not start cutting rates until 2025, and if they do cut in 2024, the reductions might be less than many have been assuming.

Industrial Production Edges Up

Industrial production numbers improved slightly this month. In February, the manufacturing side of this data jumped by 0.8%, and overall industrial production improved by 0.1%. This contrasted with January numbers, which showed a decline of 0.5%. Much of the January deterioration was attributed to weather as extreme cold affected production. The capacity utilization numbers are still hovering just short of what would be considered ideal, between 80% and 85%.

The latest iteration of the Purchasing Managers Index (PMI) shows global manufacturing has started to recover. In January, 23 nations had readings under 50, which is in the contraction zone. Just 12 remain, most of them Europe. The U.S. has been in the expansion zone for two months. In January, the reading was 50.7, and in February it hit 52.2. Canada is still in contraction at 49.7, but that is closer to expansion than they have been in months. Mexico is in expansion territory with a reading of 52.3. India still leads the majority of nations at 56.9.

What Does This Mean for Manufacturers?

Good question! The economy is actually doing pretty well. Jobless numbers are down, inflation rates are falling, and consumers are spending. Does this mean there is nothing to worry about? Of course not! Each of these positive trends has its own Achilles heel. What if layoffs start? What if worker shortages persist and keep driving wage inflation up? What if all that mounting consumer debt catches up and people stop spending $50 on a cup of coffee? This bad news does not seem imminent, but it can’t be dismissed either.
