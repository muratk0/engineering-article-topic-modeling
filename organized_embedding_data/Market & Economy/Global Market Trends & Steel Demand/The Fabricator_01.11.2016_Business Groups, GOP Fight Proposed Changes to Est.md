T

he estate tax continues to be a deeply divisive political issue, the latest evidence of which was a letter from 41 Senate Republicans in late September asking the U.S. Department of the Treasury to abandon plans to effectively increase the number of family businesses subject to the estate tax.

The Treasury Department proposed a rule in August that would increase the computation of the size of an estate by eliminating an exemption that applies to family members who hold a minority interest in a business. By doing so, more estates would qualify for the tax.

The estate tax is currently 40 percent of the value of the transfer. However, only transfers by an individual in excess of $5.45 million and $10.9 million for married couples are subject to the tax.

The GOP senators made their views known after a wide swath of private sector trade groups, led by the National Association of Manufacturers (NAM), sent a similar missive to Treasury.

"At the heart of the August proposal is the flawed concept that a minority valuation discount should not apply in family-owned businesses because a family acts as one entity regardless of who actually has control," explained Dorothy Coleman, NAM vice president of tax and domestic economic policy. "Indeed, IRS is unfairly targeting family-owned enterprises based on the erroneous assumption that families operate in complete unanimity."

Elimination of the estate tax has long been Republican dogma. In April 2015 the House passed a bill that would have eliminated the estate tax entirely. Democrats didn’t support the bill, and it was never taken up by the Senate.

Republican presidential candidate Donald Trump has said he also would eliminate the estate tax. On the other hand, Democratic nominee Hillary Clinton has proposed increasing the tax rate to 65 percent in stages with that rate applied to estates over $1 billion.

The Treasury’s proposed regulation has a 90-day comment period. It cannot be finalized until November at the earliest. But if Trump wins the election, the Treasury could finalize the rule before he takes office in January 2017. If Clinton wins, Treasury will be under no pressure since theoretically her Treasury Department will be at one with President Obama’s.

New Incentives for Small-Business Retirement Plans

The Senate Finance Committee has passed legislation that would extend a generous tax incentive to small businesses that start a qualified retirement plan and would add a new tax credit for them when they automatically enroll participants.

The Retirement Enhancement and Savings Act goes to the floor of the Senate with an excellent chance of passage because it was approved unanimously by Democrats and Republicans on the Senate Finance Committee. However, no companion bill has been introduced in the House.

Present law provides a nonrefundable income tax credit for qualified startup costs incurred by a small employer that adopts a new savings incentive match plan for employees individual retirement account (SIMPLE IRA) or a more traditional simplified employee pension (SEP) IRA, provided that the plan covers at least one non-highly compensated employee. The credit is the lesser of a flat dollar amount of $500 per year or 50 percent of the qualified startup costs. The credit applies for up to three years beginning with the year the plan is first effective or, at the election of the employer, the preceding year.

The bill passed by the Finance Committee changes the calculation of the flat dollar amount limit on the credit. The flat dollar amount for a taxable year could be as low as $500 for those businesses with few expenses linked to setting up this type of employee retirement program to as much as $5,000 for those companies with a large number of employees not considered highly compensated that might participate in these retirement programs.

Employers could get another $500 credit if the plan includes automatic enrollment. A third $500 credit is available if companies add automatic enrollment as a feature to an existing plan.
