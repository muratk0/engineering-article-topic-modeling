# The many reasons for a recession

[TARİH: 01.11.2023 The Fabricator]

Fabrinomics | Economic Insights

Dr. Chris Kuehl

There had been an assumption that the U.S. economy would manage to keep dodging the recession bullet, but lately there have been more reasons to worry. The U.S. is now, at long last, possibly headed for that recession.

The strike against the U.S. Big Three, the shutdown stupidity, and myriad other disputes will likely pull as much as a point off GDP growth in the fourth quarter.

Bloomberg and others have been issuing reports detailing six reasons to think that a recession could now be in the offing. This is my interpretation of these warnings.

1. Calls for a soft landing peak just before a hard landing.

It has been compared to the athletic director praising the football coach just hours before firing him. The assertions of an end to the recession threat seem to encourage people to quit worrying about it. One can look at the decisions to shut down the government and launch major strikes as an example. "What is there to worry about—we avoided recession?"

2. Fed hikes are about to hit hard.

Now is not the time to go into gory detail on economic theory, but there are many who assert that the neutral Fed Funds Rate is not 1.0% but 2.0%. There is much to say about this, but the important aspect for this discussion is that it would mean the Fed rates didn’t really start affecting the economy until February of this year, and now is when there has been a palpable reaction. There is most definitely a financial sector crisis brewing.

3. NBER readings are signaling.

The National Bureau for Economic Research is the arbiter of calling a recession, and they do not do this until there has actually been one. They do not say you are in a recession; they tell you that you have been in one. But they look at a number of factors, including measures of income, employment, consumer spending, and factory output. All of these are starting to flash red to some degree.

4. Shocks to the system.

This is where the self-inflicted wounds come in. We have a collection of politicians willing to tank a fragile economy with a shutdown that could drag as much as a point from GDP growth. We have major union actions threatening the loss of billions every week, and it is not just the UAW. Pilots are close to action, healthcare workers are striking, and many other sectors are moving in that direction. These are enough to plunge the economy into reverse all by themselves. You can add in a host of other factors—oil price spike, end of student loan moratorium, end of pandemic support. The latest of these shocks stem from the sudden war in Israel as terrorists in Hamas launched the largest attack in their history.

5. Household spending.

There have been many jokes regarding the impact of Beyonce and Taylor Swift, but the reality is that consumers have been spending aggressively and running up their debt. There is over $7 trillion in credit card debt right now, and $70 trillion in total consumer debt. This has started to dry up, and when the consumer can no longer afford to spend $500,000 on concert tickets, the economy loses a major driver.

6. Credit squeeze.

The data from the senior loan officers shows that over half are seriously restricting loans, and at a pace not seen since the 2008 financial crisis. The inability to borrow was what triggered that recession as business could not invest and could not hire.

Obviously, all of these factors affect the manufacturer upstream or downstream. The threats come from higher input costs triggered by all this ferment, and now there is worry about the demand side as consumers continue to feel the pinch of higher prices combined with economic uncertainty.

One set of economic data keeps pointing in a positive direction, however, and that is employment. The estimate was for a decline in jobs. Instead there was an addition of 339,000, and the jobless rate stayed at 3.8%. As long as people think their job is secure, they spend, even on big ticket items such as vehicles and homes—even as the prices for these items soar.

Long-Term Bond Trends Reflect Future Worries

Recently there has been some interesting activity affecting the term premium in long-term bonds, and it warrants some commentary. The bottom line is that the term premium appears to be positive for the first time since 2017.

The question is, why the shift? There are any number of suggestions. One possible explanation is that expected rate volatility is higher as there is an expectation that inflation volatility is likely to be higher. The latest data on jobs in the U.S. has analysts expecting more growth, and that gives the Fed more room to hike rates.

A second rationale is more uncertainty regarding U.S. solvency and political stability. There is a deepening fear that politics in the U.S. is utterly out of control and dysfunctional—held captive by extremists on both the left and right.

A third area of concern is the supply of treasuries. Year after year of deficit spending has made for massive treasury issuance, and it is already the second highest on record (more than in 2020). To make matters worse, there has been a decline in demand for these treasuries as both Japan and China have reduced their purchasing and have even been selling off parts of their portfolio.

Usually, buyers such as the Fed, commercial banks, and foreign banks are not all that concerned about price, but right now the Fed is trying to tighten, U.S. banks are trying to reduce duration risk, and the demand for treasuries overseas has diminished. Overall, the balance of risks for bonds has shifted as the four-year bull market has come to an end. Finally, there is the sense the Fed is not done with that quantitative reduction and will not be for a long time.

Long-term bonds were very popular in the period when rates were very low, and when the investors started to shy away from them and favored the short-term offerings, this was the inversion process that often corresponds to a recession. It is basically the bond buyers assuming that an economic slowdown is imminent, and, therefore, they should start bailing out of those long-term bets.

Remember that a downturn had been predicted as early as Q3 of last year. This avoidance has been down to factors such as expanded exports, robust consumer behavior, and more than a little bit of good luck. This streak of success has apparently annoyed the "leaders" in Congress, unions, and much of the corporate community.

Given the anemic forecasts for these quarters, the U.S. is now heading for that long-predicted downturn, and we should all know exactly who to blame. The business community and the consumer has been resilient up to now, but with the prospect of millions of people going unpaid, that resilience is about gone. To be honest, this is disgraceful. The fact is that many of these issues really need to be dealt with, but not by demagogues and hysterics.

Attack on Private Equity Model

For decades, the driving force behind anti-trust activity has been the "consumer welfare" model. The trigger to pursue action against business was whether their actions were harming the consumer. The mere fact that private equity was involved in consolidating business was not considered a bad thing in and of itself. This was the "buy and build" strategy.

Private equity is concentrated in smaller businesses—over 85% of that investment goes to businesses with fewer than 500 employees. It has been especially active in industries that are highly fragmented and cost-intensive. It is very hard to achieve any sort of economy of scale in these sectors, and that ends up costing consumers.

The Federal Trade Commission under Lena Khan is advocating a very radical new theory to motivate anti-trust action, and sectors such as healthcare have become targets (as well as high tech sectors). These new approaches will severely restrict capital flow. Critics have emerged from throughout the economic community. Larry Summers was treasury secretary and director of the National Economic Council in the Obama administration, and he describes Khan’s efforts as a" war on business" and suggests it constitutes "a substantial risk to consumers and healthy markets."

The reality is that private equity supports over 12 million workers and 44,000 businesses as well as the retirement money for 34 million public servants. Khan’s approach is antiquated and rooted in theories that were debunked decades ago.

A great deal of the investment money that is directed at the manufacturer is from private equity. The fact is that 75% of U.S. manufacturers are small businesses with fewer than 25 employees. This attack is far more politically motivated than rooted in economics or competitiveness.
