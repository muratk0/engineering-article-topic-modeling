# The great ownership transfer

[TARİH: 01.06.2026 The Fabricator]

Tech Talk

And the coming manufacturing capacity vacuum

Wachirapong Sukkasemsakorn/iStock/Getty Images Plus

A

quiet transition is underway in American manufacturing. According to a recent report from McKinsey & Co., roughly 6 million privately held businesses employing more than 60 million workers are expected to change hands or close in the coming decade. Manufacturing is heavily exposed to this transition. McKinsey estimates that approximately 71,000 manufacturing firms could exit by 2035.

Many of these businesses will not be sold. They’ll just close. That should concern anyone who depends on the American industrial base.

For many small manufacturers in particular, the owner is simultaneously the estimator, process engineer, production manager, quality lead, maintenance expert, and institutional memory of the company. When these seasoned operators retire, the country permanently loses experience that’s critical for the health of the U.S. manufacturing economy.

On the other hand, this massive transition creates opportunity. As legacy business owners retire, both new and incumbent manufacturers can absorb that demand. This will create a marketplace with fewer manufacturers that tend to be much larger.

LOST EXPERTISE IS A GENERATIONAL CHALLENGE

As I write, Stanley Black & Decker just announced that it is closing its Connecticut plant—the factory that makes its famous yellow tape measures. It’s retiring the product altogether, favoring a double-sided design that is currently manufactured in Thailand.

This is a shame, but is anyone truly surprised? We’ve already seen a massive loss of manufacturing volume and expertise in the U.S. I know that sounds harsh, but we aren’t anything like the manufacturing superpower we were entering World War II. The manufacturing industries that survived until today are, I believe, dynamic and healthy. But certain capabilities were offshored and lost altogether. High-volume, commoditized, and consumer-facing products—like tape measures—have been most likely to migrate overseas. Our political embrace of globalization made that basically a guarantee.

We could discuss at length whether that has been a net positive or negative for the U.S., but I’m no economist. I’ll just say that it’s concerning when we lose critical manufacturing expertise to international rivals. It would be nice if we could all "just get along," but that’s clearly not how it turned out.

The 71,000 manufacturers at risk of closing in the next decade are the ones who survived, generally by carving out niches where overseas competition could be minimized and where regulation, regional logistics, IP protection, and speed are important competitive factors. Now we might lose critical capacity and skill even there.

This is a real challenge when so much of our manufacturing capability is fragmented across small shops. As a rule, the smaller the business, the less attractive it is for buyers who might carry the torch. Meanwhile, when much of the expertise lives with the owner, even an acquisition will be of little help.

Much of American manufacturing still runs on undocumented tribal knowledge accumulated over decades. Countless businesses rely on highly manual workflows held together by a small number of deeply experienced people. In many cases, those systems do not scale well because they were never designed to.

This creates fragility in the industrial supply chain. As thousands of owners retire, it’s likely that we’ll see pockets of severe capability shortages emerge even as demand grows. It’s wild to think that this could happen because we simply, and quietly, "forget" how to make things here. It might be extremely difficult to bring it back. The more complex and specialized the manufacturing, the more true this is.

THE OPPORTUNITY OF A CENTURY

It’s tempting to despair at the coming capacity and skills gap. I get that. It looks bad. U.S. manufacturing is in some markets already on its heels, gutted by cheap overseas competition, weaponized "free trade," and laissez-faire trade policies. Much of our expertise has already left the U.S. If we lose tens of thousands more of the seasoned manufacturers who survived the last decades, it’s going to be a problem.

But our marketplace isn’t static. It’s dynamic and responsive. People are motivated and smart, and if there is an opportunity to create value and generational wealth, people will jump in and fill the gap. Yes, it takes time to create expertise, but frankly, losing four decades of experience to retirement does not mean it will take a new operator four decades to get it back. The learning curve involves fast knowledge gain early on and increasingly incremental gains long term.

The lesson is this: if there’s a crisis, people will step in and learn fast. And those people will become wildly successful. It’s very rare for structural, technological, and demographic shifts to align to create new opportunity, but that’s exactly what is happening right now. As manufacturing companies close, it will create a capacity vacuum that yields increased demand for new and incumbent manufacturers who stick around.

This will give them rare leverage. Usually, commoditized manufacturing creates slim margins and poor cash flow. It makes manufacturing look like an unattractive business. But when shops are busy, they’ll have more ability to prevent margin compression and say no to unfavorable payment terms.

Manufacturers in the coming decade will tend to be more profitable and have better liquidity. This will, of course, attract more competitors over time. That’s how it’s supposed to work, and that’s how our market will correct itself.

The manufacturing marketplace will also consolidate in some ways. This may come in the form of acquisitions, but I believe the biggest impact will come from scalable direct manufacturers who are positioned to grow fast and absorb demand as it arrives. Historically, many fabrication businesses grew until management overhead, quoting bottlenecks, scheduling chaos, or quality drift slowed them down. Adding more work often meant adding disproportionate layers of people and coordination. Complexity became the limiting factor.

Modern manufacturing systems change that equation. The next generation of scalable manufacturers will be characterized by great systems, clean operations, modern equipment, and in-house software as core operational competency. Software is important because it allows manufacturers to coordinate complexity at scale.

However they manage it, manufacturers who are able to absorb demand will grow to fill the capacity vacuum even as it is created. Long term, this will create a marketplace containing fewer competitors. Consolidation will be functionally limited by the size and diversity of the marketplace: we won’t see a dozen manufacturers put everyone else out of business. Instead, those 71,000 manufacturers might become 5,000 or 10,000 in the coming decade or two, and they’ll be a lot bigger on average.

Therein lies the opportunity. Manufacturers that want to thrive in the coming decades need to figure out how to eliminate equipment, systems, and labor bottlenecks; and capture market share as other businesses age out and close. Manufacturers who do this will become enormously successful.

Overall, I’m wary about the risks but bullish on the potential. Now is the time. This is the manufacturing opportunity of the century. A relative handful of companies will step up to the plate and knock it out of the park. The only question is, who will that be?
