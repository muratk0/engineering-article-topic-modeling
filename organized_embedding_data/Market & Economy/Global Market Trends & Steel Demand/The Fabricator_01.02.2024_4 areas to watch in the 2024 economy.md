# 4 areas to watch in the 2024 economy

[TARİH: 01.02.2024 The Fabricator]

Fabrinomics | Economic Insights

Dr. Chris Kuehl

There is considerable difference of opinion regarding GDP growth rates in 2024. Some predict a shallow recession in the first and second quarter (-1.0% in Q1 and -0.7% in Q2). Others are not quite as dismal but see growth at less than 1.0%. This is coming after a pretty robust 2023, but several factors are expected to play a bigger role in the coming year. Inflation is still high and interest rates are expected to stay elevated until mid-summer. Consumer debt has been rising fast—it’s currently at more than $70 billion. The last of that pandemic money has been distributed, and there has been some impact from the resumption of student loan payments.

On the positive side, unemployment remains low, but there are some concerns building here as well. In November, the Employment Trends Index from The Conference Board dropped again in November, signaling a cooling labor market.

As we head deeper into the first quarter of 2024, fabricators have plenty of economic factors to consider, both positive and negative. This is what I see ahead in four key economic areas.

1. Interest Rate Policy

For several months the Federal Reserve has been chanting the mantra of "higher for longer," and that will be their position through at least the first half of 2024. It is true that inflation has dipped a little, but it is still well above the ideal level set by the Fed.

Traditionally, the central bank raises rates until it breaks something and then sets about lowering rates to fix what broke. The prime indicator of whether the Fed has broken enough is unemployment. When that rate goes up, the assertion is that the economy has slowed enough. Right now, the U6 rate is as low as it has been in years. The Bureau of Labor Statistics releases six cuts of its data, and U6 is the most complete as it picks up the discouraged worker and the involuntary part-time worker. It is sitting at 6.7%, and the long-term average is 10.1%. As long as that rate is this low, the Fed will have room to raise rates if they deem it necessary.

Beyond that, there is the fact the economy grew at 5.2% in Q3, and Q4 growth is expected to be at 2.3%. These are not numbers that suggest imminent recession, so the Fed is not feeling intense pressure. It seems likely that rates will start to decline slowly by the middle of the year—maybe three or four quarter-point cuts by the end of 2024.

2. Insights on Inflation

Some interesting theoretical conversations have been taking place that could affect how the Fed reacts to inflation. It has been assumed for years that the "neutral" Fed Funds rate is 1.0%, and that is what prompts the Fed to set its inflation target at 2.0%. There is mounting evidence suggesting the neutral rate might be higher. The neural rate means that anything over that rate slows the economy and anything under those levels stimulates it. If the neutral rate is higher than assumed, the Fed could shift its inflation goal to 3.0%.

As measured by Personal Consumption Expenditures (PCE) Price Index, inflation is just slightly over 3.0% (at the sixmonth level as of September). It is close to 4.0% on an annual basis, and core rates rose a little in the last month. Housing and wages are the primary inflation drivers, and most don’t expect this to change anytime soon. The factors that have lowered inflation threats have been energy and food. Neither is counted in figuring core inflation rates as these are intensely volatile. Inflation will keep falling in 2024, but any shift in energy or food will drive prices back up in a hurry.

The highest inflation rates have been seen in housing (24% increase), which can depress demand. According to Lending-Club data, 60% of households are still living check-to-check, 38% believe they are in worse financial health than they were a year ago, and 62% are very or extremely concerned about the economic outlook. There is still a significant difference according to what income level a consumer is in. The lower third is very stressed by inflation, and the upper third has barely felt the pressure as they have seen income hikes that have kept pace. The middle third is the group to watch as they struggle to keep up with higher prices.

3. Bright Spots and Recession Threats

Economists have been predicting recession for the last five quarters and have yet to be on target. In Q3 of 2023, the assumption was a decline of over 1.0%—what we actually got was growth of 5.2%. Now we have economists predicting a shallow recession at the start of 2024 (negative 1.0% in Q1 and negative 0.7% in Q2). But that is hardly a universal opinion as many have watched these dire predictions fail.

Some signs of weakness are emerging, though. For instance, after a surprising surge in activity in November, the Empire State manufacturing index showed deep contraction in early December. This could be the impact of the UAW strike. Growth in November was on top of strong production, output, shipments, and what sounds like recovery after the automotive sector was idle for a couple of months.

But now we have at least one example of what the rest of the industry could look like in the next couple of months as the impact of the restart begins to soften. The index came in at -14.5 points, down from a positive 9.1 in November. This is the first monthly survey report that we get each month, and it shows some broader weakness across some of the remaining segments of manufacturing.

Was all that growth and investment that drove activity in 2023 just a short-term reaction to leftover pandemic pressures? There was reshoring, fixing a broken supply chain, and spending all that extra pandemic support cash. All played a big role in boosting demand for manufactured goods. Is there any of that demand left? Will more routine business behavior resume? The questions abound.

At the same time, fabricators are benefiting from sector-specific bright spots, including continued demand in defense spending. In fact, with two wars raging, including one of the biggest in Europe since World War II, defense spending has surged around the world. Congress passed $886 billion in additional appropriations for building equipment, munitions, and host of spending tied to Ukraine, Israel, and bolstering preparedness in the South China Sea.

Defense manufacturers provide wide offerings—fasteners to coatings, core materials, metals, even services like consulting—and many will be positively impacted by this surge in spending. And remember, it’s worldwide. Even adversaries are ordering products and materials at a rate faster than they have in the past 60 years, some of which will be fulfilled by unknowing vendors two or three times removed from the original buyer.

Some of this spending is secretive. There are traces of it, and we can find it in the broader macro-data at times, but we don’t always have a current barometer of how fast it is hitting the market and how fast it is multiplying across the economy. This is one of the three key legs of the economic stool, and it just got nearly a $1 trillion shot in the arm.

Regarding GDP growth in general, the more optimistic assessment is growth around 2.0% (close to the 20-year average of 2.5%). The two factors that have kept us out of a downturn thus far have been consumer spending and the inventory build. If the consumer starts to falter under that $1.8 trillion credit card debt, their contribution could falter fast. If businesses stop inventory accumulation, the economy also starts to retreat. At the moment, few indicators point to an imminent recession as corporate investment remains robust and other growth indicators are positive.

4. Unemployment and Worker Shortage

The jobless numbers are historically low, and there have been job gains every month despite the predictions of decline. The U6 rate is as low as it has been in years, and the U3 rate is still well under 4.0%. (Remember when 6.0% unemployment was considered low?) The very low rates have been propelling wage inflation, and the fact is that worker shortages will dominate next year and for many years to come. The quit rate is as high as it has been in years. Lately there have been some high-profile layoffs, but in general the job market remains extremely tight.

Three out of the four of these forecasts would have to be considered positive for the manufacturer, and the fourth is a chronic problem that has been with the industry for a long time and is likely to be a concern for a lot longer. It is obviously a good thing that inflation has started to decline in some key areas, and it is a good thing that recession threats have faded to a degree. It is because of these developments that the Fed can start thinking about the reduction of rates, and that is a good thing as well. The labor force issue is the most vexing, and there are simply no good options now or on the horizon.
