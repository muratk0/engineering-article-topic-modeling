Steel, aluminum prices not expected to decline in 2015

By

MetalPrices.com

staff

Steel

North American steelmakers and steel buyers remain cautiously optimistic about market conditions despite some recent weakening of sheet steel prices and overall steel industry output. U.S. raw steel production slipped to an average of 75 percent capability utilization in the mid-fourth quarter, down from a multiyear high of 80 percent three months earlier. Hot-rolled coil prices averaged $645/net ton free on board (FOB) the steel mill, down from $675 three months before, and cold-rolled sheet slipped to $765/ton from $790/ton in the previous quarter.

Buyers said they expected continued upward pressure on prices from industrial demand. However, recent high imports of finished steel products may be affected by recent trade rulings.

The U.S. Department of Commerce canceled a suspension agreement preventing the imposing of antidumping duties on Russian hot-rolled coils in mid-October. This means duties from 73.59 percent to 184.56 percent would come into force by mid-December. Market sources said the ruling was expected to close the U.S. market to Russian suppliers, but suppliers from Mexico, Brazil, and Turkey were among the steelmakers who should be able to fill the void left by the Russian departures. Russian hot-rolled coils accounted for more than 500,000 tons of imports in the first eight months of 2014, compared to just 23,000 tons in the same period a year earlier.

Buyers also said the suspension agreement cancellation could result in a psychological boost for U.S. mills that were trying to pull back some existing low-priced supply agreements and that also have been working on trade cases targeted at Chinese cold-rolled coil and coated products.

Ferrous Scrap

Ferrous scrap prices lost their footing in the fourth quarter, and some dealers are worried they could keep tumbling into the first quarter of 2015. Weaker export demand led the decline and dragged both offshore and domestic prices for No. 1 heavy melt and shredded scrap to their lowest levels in more than two years. Exporters blame increased competition from cheap Chinese rebar and semi-finished billet, as well as steady gains in the value of the U.S. dollar. Chinese steelmakers have been flooding world markets with steel.

Export sales account for roughly 20 percent of the ferrous scrap generated in the U.S. each year. In the past decade, sales have doubled to an annual average of 23.1 million tons and have become a bigger determinant of prices and demand. When “the docks,” as scrap dealers in the coastal regions refer to them, start cutting prices as severely as they did in October, it ripples through the country.

Prices paid for the bellwether heavy melt at the U.S. East Coast docks slipped to as low as $270/ton and were below $250/ton on the West Coast by late October. That set the pace for the domestic scrap market. Steelmakers along the Eastern Seaboard cut their shredded price by $20 to $320/ton and offered more of their scrap to inland steel mills at discounts. A price war was ignited with local dealers in those regions trying to meet lower offers from the East.

MetalPrices.com

expects this to continue until the export price reductions end. The average price of shredded steel trading at the end of October was $355/ton delivered to some mills in the Chicago area. The price is expected to drop another $20/ton or $30/ton by year’s end. By comparison, shredded was trading at $440/ton in many of the regional markets in the Midwest and the South at the beginning of the year. Some traders believe the price will be at $340/ton or less by Q1 2015.

The approaching winter and the threat of a repeat of last winter’s snowstorms and colder temperatures is the wild card that could benefit scrap dealers and bolster prices. Weather plays a key role not only in the flows of feedstock to scrapyards, but also in the shipment of prepared scrap to the steel mills. Last winter, for instance, scrap shipments by rail were delayed by as long as two months to some mills because of the weather. That forced several mills to scurry and look for scrap closer to home, shifting pricing power to the hands of the scrap dealers.

Nonferrous Metals

On one hand, both aluminum and zinc prices essentially are supported by a supply deficit, and going into 2015, premiums for both metals are expected to increase. On the other hand, nickel, lead, and copper prices are being driven by speculative funds. Regardless, there are compelling fundamentals overhanging the nickel market specifically. Supporting nickel prices in the near term are the Indonesian and Philippine governments’ threat to ban future exports of nickel ore and to promote internal investment by upscaling the value of the metal by processing the ore into a nickel-iron product. China imports nickel from both countries to meet its stainless steel production. Support for higher prices in the latter half of 2015 is driven by expectations of a looming deficit. Prices could be supported well into 2016 until Indonesia’s nickel pig iron industry is in full production.

Currently European and U.S. stainless steel consumers have adequate inventories and are not in the market aggressively. Inventories of stainless steel scrap are building, with price indications for 18/8 scrap in Europe between $1,400 and $1,430/metric ton, and for 316 at $2,000 to $2,050/metric ton. Asian prices have been softening recently by $100/metric ton and are now in line with European prices. European nickel foundry premiums are softer from this past spring and late summer after reaching $235/metric ton. Premiums currently are quoted at $185/metric ton.

Copper prices have slumped since the beginning of Q3 2014 as the supply of both scrap and copper cathode increased. Copper cathodes on warrant have only sellers, and lower premiums are being tested with each transaction.

Aluminum will be moving to a supply deficit in 2015, and the markets currently are showing increased premiums for prompt P1020 and 6000 series billet, both in the U.S. and worldwide.
