Could investigations into China’s unfair trade practices be next?

OMAR NASHASHIBI

T

he House Select Committee on the Chinese Communist Party has sent a letter to the administration calling for an investigation of China’s unfair trade practices. In the March 5 letter to U.S. Trade Representative Jamieson Greer, Attorney General Pam Bondi, and Homeland Security Secretary Kristi Noem, Chairman John Moolenaar, R-Mich., and Ranking Member Raja Krishnamoorthi, D-Ill., urged the Office of the U.S. Trade Representative (USTR) to investigate China’s violations of U.S. trade laws under Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974.

Moolenaar and Krishnamoorthi highlighted the trans-shipment of goods through third-party countries as one of the primary methods China employs to "circumvent tariffs and duties, evade customs enforcement, or obscure the origin of products produced in whole or in part with forced labor."

This letter from the House Select Committee coincides with USTR’s ongoing review of unfair and nonreciprocal trade policies and practices from other countries, as directed by President Donald Trump in the "America First Trade Policy" memo and the "Reciprocal Trade and Tariffs" memo. USTR has solicited feedback from stakeholders on information "on a country-by-country basis, to assist the U.S. Trade Representative in reviewing and identifying any unfair trade practices by other countries, and in initiating all necessary actions to investigate the harm to the United States from any nonreciprocal trade arrangements."

The USTR review is due to the president on April 1. Following this, USTR, the Commerce Department, and other relevant agencies "shall initiate, pursuant to their respective legal authorities, all necessary actions to investigate the harm to the United States from any nonreciprocal trade arrangements adopted by any trading partners," according to the president’s reciprocal trade plan memo. Subsequently, the agencies will submit to the president a comprehensive report on any proposed remedies.

25% Tariff on Steel, Aluminum Take Effect

On March 12, President Donald Trump’s administration began imposing a tariff rate of 25% on imports of steel and aluminum globally, without exemptions for countries or a process to request an exclusion. Sources in Washington expect that each nation or bloc of states such as the European Union (EU) will continue to work with the Trump administration to suspend the tariff on their country’s imports of steel or aluminum. However, the Commerce Department is not indicating that it intends in the near term to restart a process allowing companies to request an exclusion from paying the tariff on a specific imported steel or aluminum product. As of this writing, the tariffs apply to all countries, including Canada, Mexico, EU, Japan, South Korea, and others.

The same action on steel and aluminum taken under the Section 232 national security law applies a 25% tariff on 167 derivatives of steel and 123 aluminum derivatives. The products covered by the new tariff action, which started March 12, are identified using their Harmonized Tariff Schedule code, an eight-digit number harmonized globally.

White House Rescinds NEPA Regulation

The White House has revoked its National Environmental Policy Act (NEPA) implementing rule. In accordance with Trump’s Jan. 20 executive order titled "Unleashing American Energy," the White House Council on Environmental Quality (CEQ) rescinded the rule in a Feb. 25 interim final rule published in the Federal Register notice and issued guidance directing agencies to follow their individual NEPA regulations.

The revocation follows two court rulings that determined the CEQ does not have the authority to establish binding NEPA rules. In a November 2024 decision, the D.C. Circuit Court of Appeals concluded that the CEQ lacks statutory power to create NEPA regulations, asserting that the "CEQ regulations, which claim to dictate how all federal agencies must comply with the National Environmental Policy Act, are

ultra vires

," meaning they exceed legal authority and are unenforceable.

Additionally, the District Court for the District of North Dakota on Feb. 3 vacated the Biden administration’s 2024 Phase 2 NEPA rules in response to a lawsuit brought by 21 attorneys general.

The interim final rule revoking the NEPA implementing regulations takes effect on April 11.
