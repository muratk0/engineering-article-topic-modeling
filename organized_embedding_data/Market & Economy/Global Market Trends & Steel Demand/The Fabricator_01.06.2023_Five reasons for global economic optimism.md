# Five reasons for global economic optimism

[TARİH: 01.06.2023 The Fabricator]

Fabrinomics | Economic Insights

Dr. Chris Kuehl

It is a bit of a worn-out joke, but it remains pretty accurate. If you put five economists in a room, you will get five opinions (six if one is from Harvard). It would seem simple enough to query economists regarding the chances of a recession this year, but when this question was posed to those involved in the Chief Economists Outlook from the World Economic Forum, there was no clear answer: 45% thought a recession in the next couple of quarters was likely, and 45% thought this downturn was unlikely.

The range of GDP predictions for the world is from less than 2.0% to as high as 4.0%. The U.S. predictions run from a negative 2.0% to a high of almost 3.0%. Why the wide variability? It all comes down to how aggressive the central banks are expected to be and how resilient the consumer and the business community turns out to be.

Glass Half Full and Half Empty

The optimistic view holds that central banks have reached a "breather stage." This means they have done about as much rate hiking as they think the economy can handle at this stage. It is not that they think they have vanquished inflation; they are well aware that it is still a major issue. The U.S. is still sitting on a Personal Consumption Expenditure (PCE) reading of around 4.5%, and the Fed wants that down to 2.5%. The reality is that rate hikes take between 12 and 18 months to have a major impact, and the central banks will now wait to see if they have done enough.

If the latest hike is the last for a while, the sense is that the economy can still expand. It is true that a federal funds rate of 5.0% is much higher than it has been in recent years, but historically it is not all that unusual. If the brakes are not pushed any harder, there is still an opportunity for business to access money and grow, and there is still an opportunity for many consumers to spend.

The pessimistic view asserts that inflation is no closer to being under control and that the central banks still have a long way to go. They point primarily to the labor market. There are too few people to take the available jobs, and those with the right skills and training are in desperately short supply. That makes them expensive. Replacing them with robots is even more expensive (at least in the short run). The level of unemployment remains very low, and the economy keeps adding jobs at a rapid clip.

Beyond the impact of the labor market, idiotic political games threaten to make all this worse. The fight over the debt ceiling is the height of craven political gamesmanship. The time to determine how much the government should be spending and how much revenue it needs to raise is during the budget process. That is what a budget is for. Passing a budget and appropriating the money means a decision has been made. Waiting to decide whether to honor that decision is beyond destructive, and this fight can’t possibly be happening at a worse time. If there is a recession in the next quarter or two, the "leaders" in Congress will be to blame.

Just as with all the other sectors of the economy, the industrial community needs to know what potentially lies ahead. If there is a recession in the immediate future, there will be a desire to reduce output and reduce investment and hiring. But if there is growth in the offing, the manufacturer has no desire to lose market share to a competitor. The worst situation a business can face is one of uncertainty.

What About Stagflation?

Stagflation is supposed to be technically impossible. One either has slow growth or one has inflation. By definition, these are supposed to be mutually exclusive. Inflation is generally driven by faster growth that creates a disconnect between supply and demand. Unfortunately, there have been periods in the past when growth has been slow and inflation has been a problem anyway. One of those times seems to be now. The growth numbers that are coming in from the U.S., Europe, and other nations are anemic and even going into the negative, but inflation is still roaring along despite the efforts of the central banks to slow things down.

The last time there was a significant threat from stagflation was in the 1970s, and the remedies eventually deployed were brutal. The Nixon administration ended up imposing wage freezes and price controls to force the end of the inflation surge. That technique was intensely controversial at the time, and it would be impossible to pull off today.

The ingredients for a classic stagflation are still not in place, but the risks are rising. The economy is showing signs of stagnating as growth numbers show. There have been predictions of negative growth in the next three quarters (although that has not been a universal opinion). If this takes place, the economy will indeed meet the criteria for a recession, and the main questions will be how deep and how long.

Glass-Half-Full View Might Prevail

It is not that I have decided to don my pair of rose-colored glasses. There is plenty to worry about regarding the future of the global economy. The potential for a hard economic landing remains, geopolitics has become a major factor as far as economic growth is concerned, supply chain shocks are still an everyday concern, and many indicators still point to recession – everything from bond inversions to inventory overload. There is no sense of wild optimism, and now is certainly not the time for complacency. That said, many other factors are far more positive than they are being given credit for.

The major economies of the world are not in recession

, despite the grim warnings we’ve heard for months. That includes the U.S., EU, India, China, Japan, U.K., and South Korea. This is despite the fact that interest rates have been rising fast and aggressively. Remember that hiking rates to deal with inflation is supposed to trigger a recession – that is the intent. All of these nations were expected to be in recession by now, and some were anticipating a severe recession by now, including the EU and U.K. Thus far they have shown remarkable resilience. This is not to say that they will not fall into this decline in the months to come, but avoiding it this long is good for the prospects for a shorter and shallower recession than was originally feared.

The supply chain crisis has started to fade.

It is not gone by any stretch, but the most recent index reading from the New York Fed’s supply chain pressure index is now well below its historical average. The other positive element here is that this supply chain has become more secure and diverse as the near total dependence on China has eroded considerably. The reshoring to the U.S. and Europe has built up business in these nations, and many other developing nations have become sources for supply. China will recover a percentage of their lost business — but not all of it, as many companies are more concerned about the future.

The energy crisis never quite materialized in Europe.

As the winter advanced in 2022, there were extremely dire predictions of recession based on the assumption that energy prices would skyrocket and people in Europe would be forced to choose heat or food. The winter was far milder than expected, and new sources of fuel were developed. Europe now sits on more gas supply than they have ever seen at this time of year, and prices are falling. Even OPEC moves to cut production have not been able to send oil prices into high gear. There are plenty of factors still driving inflation, but for the time being, there is not a lot of activity spurred by energy costs.

China is coming out of the COVID lockdown at last.

It has taken longer than expected, but the pace has improved. Even as the world tries to untangle from China, it remains the world’s second largest economy and still provides a great deal of product to the developed nations. Most important, the production in China requires purchasing of commodities and materials from other nations. The developing world depends on that Chinese demand, and now there is a sense that it will come back. Growth rates are close to the target of 5.0% set by Beijing.

The OPEC attempt to drive up oil prices at the behest of the Saudi Arabians and Russia has fizzled.

The price per barrel has stayed in the $70s, and expectations were that it would climb to the $90s and even to $100 a barrel. The reality is that non-OPEC nations such as the U.S., UK, Canada, Mexico, and others made up the difference. The U.S. is set to hit record levels of production, and so will Canada. The other OPEC nations have been ignoring the restrictions, and it seems that all Saudi Arabia managed to do was weaken their grip on the cartel.

Of course, the status of the economy changes extremely rapidly. That is especially true at this stage as so many drivers of the global economy are in flux. The current situation could (and probably will) change in an instant, but for the moment the sense is that there will not be a deep or long-lasting recession, and it is entirely possible that one will be avoided altogether.

The ingredients for a deep crisis are simply not there. If one looks at the 2008 recession, it was abundantly clear that everything that could go wrong had indeed been going wrong. The banking sector was not just dealing with a few bad banks and nervous depositors; it had crushed itself with bad decisions. The consumer was in shock and the government was very slow to react. The situation in 2023 is serious to be sure, but the business climate now seems to lack the ingredients to spin out of control.
