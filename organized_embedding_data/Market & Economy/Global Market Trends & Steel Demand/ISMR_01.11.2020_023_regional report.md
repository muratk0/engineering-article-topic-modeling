LATIN AMERICA IN FOCUS

“The economic potential of Latin America is undeniable. It is an area with a great wealth of human and natural resources. Latin America displayed a surprising dynamism and did not devolve into crisis. Ongoing societal and economic reinvigoration continues as the area pushes towards increased competitiveness and productivity and towards the further alleviation of poverty,” said analyst, SIS International Research.

“Economies in Latin America are increasingly characterised by high growth rates, abundance of commodities and resources, increasing stability and growing populations. Some growing industries in Latin America include consumer goods, food and beverage and retail, due to the rising per capita income in many of these countries. Another rising industry is construction and real estate due to the heavy emphasis on building infrastructure. Tourism is increasingly showing an upward trend, which can become a major part of an economy’s GDP every year.”

Latin America also hosts some of the world’s most dynamic renewable energy markets. In addition to the historical role of hydropower and pioneering moves with biofuels, countries in the region are significantly scaling up other renewable energy sources such as wind, solar and biomass-based electricity.

## The region is set to experience a sharp downturn this year as lockdowns pummel activity

The impact of COVID-19

“The fast-spreading COVID-19 pandemic is set to push the region into its deepest recession in modern history this year. The region is set to experience a sharp downturn this year as lockdowns pummel activity. Considerable job and income losses will hit household spending, while heightened economic uncertainty will hammer investment activity. Moreover, disrupted value chains and low demand for commodities will squeeze exports, while weak fiscal positions further cloud the outlook,” commented analyst, Focus Economics, in September 2020.

Latin America and the Caribbean has been hit particularly hard by the COVID-19 pandemic, with an expected contraction of 9.3 percent this year—its largest recession on record – according to the IMF (International Monetary Fund). The region has been impacted by the direct effect of the lockdowns and spillovers from the rest of the world through lower commodity prices, remittances and tourism as well as capital outflows. As the pandemic subsides and the world economy recovers, the IMF is projecting a partial recovery in the region of 3.7 percent in 2021.

“The policy response in the region has been swift. Central banks have intervened effectively, using all tools of monetary policy, through lower rates and expanded balance sheets. Given the scale of the crisis and unprecedented uncertainty, governments have had to also deploy fiscal measures to strengthen the health system, protect the most vulnerable and support employment and otherwise viable businesses,” commented IMF managing director, Kristalina Georgieva.

“For example, Argentina and Paraguay have cash and food programmes for disadvantaged families; Chile, Peru and Colombia are deploying wage subsidies and guarantees and loans to ailing SMEs. We have seen Ecuador—in a particularly difficult situation—targeting the most vulnerable. So far, the Caribbean countries have been successful in containing the virus better than others, and in expanding support for the most vulnerable.”

She also advised Latin American governments to strive for effective public policy interventions, stepping up testing and tracing efforts, delivering clear communications on health developments and policies, and reducing risks of contagion by diversifying transfer mechanisms, and increasing digital delivery—especially in the megacities of Latin America. She also urged a continuing focus on reforms to support competitiveness and growth.

The IMF has doubled access to emergency funding, and approved requests from 70 countries for emergency financing, with disbursements totalling around US$ 25 billion dollars. This includes about US$ 5.5 billion of total financing to 17 countries in the Caribbean, Central America and South America. It also approved access to new Flexible Credit Line facilities to Chile and Peru, and renewed Colombia’s line, expanding precautionary lending to the region to US$ 107 billion.

“We will also continue our tremendous engagement with the region on capacity development and policy advice. As we live with the pandemic, governments must be agile and start planning for the eventual recovery,” concluded Kristalina Georgieva.

## Ecuador in focus

Revenue collapsing, while spending surges, is by now a familiar narrative of how the COVID-19 pandemic is hurting governments’ finances across the globe. For Ecuador, a South American country of 17 million, this double whammy was compounded by a sharp fall in oil prices, one of its main exports. The result was devastating for the economy.

Despite the difficulties, the Ecuadorian government took decisive actions to mitigate the adverse impact of the pandemic on households and firms. This included creating a special cash transfer programme, expanding the social safety net to more vulnerable people and temporarily broadening unemployment insurance. People were allowed to delay certain payments (such as tuition, utilities and health insurance). Temporary price controls for basic food items were also implemented.

To support Ecuador’s stabilisation and recovery programme in the short term and help its economy become more dynamic and competitive in the medium term, the IMF’s Executive Board approved a new US$ 6.5 billion Extended Fund Facility arrangement on 30 September 2020.

## An eye on productivity

In 1990, Latin America’s average GDP per capita was a little over a quarter of the United States’ income level, while emerging and developing Asian countries’ GDP per capita was only five per cent. In 2019, Asian countries had grown fourfold, but Latin America was still at the same level.

What explains this weak relative income growth? Since Asia has twice the investment level of Latin America, it is tempting to blame low growth on low investment. But Central, Eastern and Southeastern Europe casts doubt on this narrative, having achieved faster growth than Latin America with lower investment than Asia.

“In a new working paper, we compare the experiences of these three regions (before COVID-19) and conclude that Latin America is poorer because of lower levels of human capital and productivity, not investment,” commented the International Monetary Fund. “In the longrun, it is not more input (labour and capital) that generates growth, but productivity (how much more output can be produced with the same input) in the same amount of time.”

Strong governance and good business climate matter for productivity growth. The White Paper shows that countries with higher human capital and better governance and business climate tend to be richer than those with low scores on these variables. High human capital alone is not sufficient: the analysis in the paper shows that countries become rich only when governance also improves.

“Our paper argues that countries won’t grow faster and close the income gap with richer parts of the world without improving human capital, governance and business environment,” said the IMF.
