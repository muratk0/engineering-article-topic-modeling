# DEFENCE OF THE REALM

[TARİH: 01.08.2013 ISMR]

ISMR analyses trends and opportunities for sheet metal manufacturers in global defence markets

In tight budgetary environments, according to global analyst Frost & Sullivan, global defence industries are experiencing another threat to their growth: unexpected competition arising from rapid innovations in Commercial-Off-The-Shelf (COTS) technologies. As traditional defence markets no longer offer reliable growth opportunities, global defence integrators are being forced to develop business in adjacent markets including new geographies, industries and end-users.

Maturing countries such as Brazil, Russia, India, China and Saudi Arabia amongst others are ‘transitioning markets’ with significantly higher growth rates in C4ISR spending over the period 2012-2021. These countries have demand for integrated Land C4ISR systems such as armoured vehicles with on-board computing systems. There is also increasing demand for special mission airborne systems that provide situational awareness and superiority.

“Adjacent markets such as Security and Transportation also provide opportunities for defence organisations, as they keep improving value for money for the end-users by using less expensive, Commercial Off-The-Shelf (COTS) technologies and innovative support models. Defence majors could also benefit from providing restrictive products, which are in demand in the transitioning markets, such as self-protective and electronic warfare suites, unmanned systems and advanced simulation equipment,” added Frost & Sullivan.

## Latin America

“It has become common practice for advanced markets to sponsor defence capabilities in transitioning markets to both parties’ advantage. For example, the US-Brazil Defence Strategic Dialogue aims to keep Brazil under US influence, while Brazil benefits from technology transfer. This has also been the main driver for US Foreign Military Sales (FMS) across the Middle East and APAC region,” explained F&S.

Defence majors’ go-to-market strategy should also emphasise forging true partnership in ‘transitioning markets’. “Those companies who are willing to share/license technologies and form joint ventures with local participants with the aim of selling to third countries, besides the home market, will be well positioned to succeed in transitioning markets,” added Mr Ahmed, F&S analyst.

Brazil continues to lead the way in Latin American defence expenditure. Forthcoming and ongoing programmes mean that Brazilian budgets over the next decade will be higher than those of the next five biggest Latin American spenders combined. Overall budgets can be deceptive, however, as new procurements are limited by high personnel and maintenance requirements.

New analysis from Frost & Sullivan, “Revenue Opportunities and Stakeholder Mapping in the Latin American Defence Market”, estimates that cumulative defence expenditure on new equipment procurement in Brazil, Chile, Colombia and Peru will amount to US$ 69.1 billion between 2011 and 2020. The majority of these procurements will be in Brazil.

“While a lack of big-ticket programmes in smaller countries does not necessarily entail a lack of opportunity for defence companies, Western manufacturers willing to export may be disappointed by challenging markets,” noted Frost & Sullivan industry analyst, Chiara Ball. “However, significant opportunities exist, especially for those willing to help governments’ further industrial development by creating domestic or regional bases.”

Latin American countries’ stocks currently comprise a lot of Cold War and even WWII-era platforms. Long overdue modernisation drives are now being pushed by governments, and the global recession’s comparatively light touch on the region is helping them to do so.

Global defence integrators are being forced to develop business in adjacent markets including new geographies, industries and end users.

“Some countries’ defence forces are struggling with internal security problems,” added Ball. “The need to modernise is acute and is the main driver of defence procurement in the region.”

In many countries, decades of economic fragility and instability coincided with transitions to democracy following military dictatorships. The economic and cultural result of this was institutionalised neglect of military capabilities, which is now changing according to F&S. However, despite the overall economic growth of many of these countries, overall budgets are still too small to match the overwhelming requirements.

End-users in Latin America are extremely price-conscious, in many cases even preferring to buy second-hand platforms and domestically refurbish and upgrade them. There is an increase in the popularity of modular platforms, and demand for upgrade components and packages will remain high.

“Governments in these under-developed countries look favourably on defence procurements with clear domestic industrial and technological benefits,” concluded Ball. “In order to foment incentive, companies should enter the market via partnership with local companies or by establishing a domestic manufacturing base.”

## Middle East and Africa

Modernisation programmes and the procurement of advanced platforms have lent momentum to defence markets in Africa and the Middle East. This is particularly true for the Gulf states of Saudi Arabia and the United Arab Emirates which are looking to develop their military capabilities in the light of civil unrest and international instability.

New analysis from Frost & Sullivan, “The Middle East and Africa Defence Market Assessment”, finds that the market earned revenues of US$ 29.67 billion in 2012 and estimates this to reach US$ 45.49 billion in 2021.

“Political resolve to maintain up-to-date systems drive opportunities in support services and upgrades, especially in long range strike capabilities and air platforms,” said Frost & Sullivan aerospace and defence research analyst, Yasha Izadpanah. “The highest growth is expected in the command, control, communications, computers, intelligence, surveillance and reconnaissance (C4ISR) and support in service segments, with compound annual growth rates (CAGR) of 4.5 per cent and 6.7 per cent respectively between 2012 and 2021.”

While the successful acquisition of these contracts may depend on the ratification of offset agreements, defence companies are finding it difficult to fully implement offset conditions, curbing market growth in the Middle East and Africa. The market is further restrained by arms regulations in Western countries, which limit the licensing of military technology to the regions.

“Defence suppliers must also establish a presence in smaller countries where procurement opportunities for modest budgets are available,” proposed Izadpanah. “Return-on-investments may be limited in the short term, but demand in countries like Morocco, Kuwait and South Africa is likely to increase rapidly in the long term.”

The African continent has also emerged as a strong potential market for the South African aerospace and defence industry. South Africa boasts a well-developed aerospace and defence industry. Steadily increasing local demand for air travel, including low-cost air travel, will provide growth to the aerospace industry. At the same time, relatively untapped markets in the rest of Africa will offer new opportunities for the defence industry.

## Modernisation markets

Despite austerity measures implemented by most Western countries, the military Support in Service (SIS) market seems to be relatively immune from the effects of the economic recession. Amongst SIS segments, the modernisation sector is one of the most stable and promising in terms of revenue growth. According to recent Frost & Sullivan analysis, the military modernisation market will increase in value from US$ 14,837.1 million in 2011 to US$ 18,031.5 million in 2020.

“Decreasing defence procurement budgets and the rising prices of new complex combat defence platforms are resulting in end-users looking to modernise equipment rather than replace it,” explained Frost & Sullivan industry analyst, Dominik Kimla. “The Ministries of Defence (MoDs) are trying to manage existing air defence platforms fleet more efficiently, and balance cost savings with the higher availability rates from lesser fleet numbers. Therefore, many more life extension programmes are expected in the coming years.”

The highest revenue for the defence industry, said F&S, will be generated by the air segment of the military modernisation sector that will exhibit a Compound Annual Growth Rate (CAGR) of 2.6 per cent from 2011 to 2020.

The air modernisation segment will also increase its share in the global military modernisation expenditure from 41.7 per cent in 2011 to 43.3 per cent in 2020.

North America, said F&S, would keep its position as the top region in terms of air modernisation defence spending, mainly due to the United States. It is expected that the regional market will experience a stable 1.5 per cent CAGR increase during the 2011-2020 period. Frost & Sullivan research also shows that APAC, Middle East and Latin America will experience the highest CAGR growth at 4.5, 4.2 and 4.3 per cent respectively for this period. In particular, the size of the market and high CAGR growth will make the APAC and Middle East countries very promising markets for suppliers of military air modernisation packages.

“At the country level, it is noteworthy that after the US, Russia will have the second biggest air modernisation budget, worth US$ 3,821.4 million, during the analysed period,” highlighted Mr Kimla. “Russia’s high rank in modernisation efforts is the result of huge state investment into the military to cover the modernisation gap after the collapse of the Soviet Union.”

The top defence companies should be aware of growing competition, particularly from Tier-1 firms which are more and more active in the military modernisation market. Therefore, the major defence companies need to adopt new business models to capitalise on aftermarket services.

“Since MoDs are increasingly looking for complex but compatible solutions, defence companies should offer modernisation packages as an element of the total solution type. This will be the most efficient way forward, both from supplier and end-user perspectives. In doing so, the development of local industrial footprint is also a vital point to consider for suppliers,” advised F&S.

“Another recommendation for market participants is the adoption of commercial off-the-shelf solutions to reduce costs of modernisation packages,” summarised Mr Kimla. “In the near future, the industry should also be prepared to manage new demand for retrofit of a growing number of unmanned systems.”

## Complex requirements that place increasing demands on efficiency are mission-critical challenges for tube forming applications

This tube bending cell based on a Unison all-electric machine has just made its 30-millionth bend at Olicana Products, the UK-based tubular parts manufacturer.

Form-bound bending procedures, such as press bending or rotary draw bending, are used to form a tube workpiece into the shape of a die. Straight tube stock can be formed using a bending machine to create a variety of single or multiple bends and to shape the piece into the desired form. These processes can be used to form complex shapes out of different types of ductile metal tubing. Freeform-bending processes, such as three-roll-push-bending, shape the workpiece kinematically so that the bending contour is not dependent on the tool geometry.

Generally, round stock is used in tube bending. However, square and rectangular tubes and pipes may also be bent to meet job specifications. Other factors involved in the tube bending process are wall thickness, tooling and lubricants needed by the pipe and tube bender to best shape the material.

A tube can be bent in multiple directions and angles. Common simple bends consist of forming elbows and U-bends. More complex geometries include multiple two-dimensional (2D) bends and three-dimensional (3D) bends.

## Adapting to complex requirements

Traditional hydroforming is used to manufacture complex-shaped parts from tube blanks, but form blowing is becoming an increasingly common method. Form blowing entails a boron steel tube blank first being heated up to 1000 degrees C. It is then placed in a tool and formed by blowing in air or nitrogen under high pressure so that the blank expands. The method is similar to that used during hydroforming, with the main difference that gas is used instead of fluid. Form blowing has comparatively low investment costs which makes it especially suitable for manufacturing parts in short series.

Complex requirements that place increasing demands on efficiency are mission-critical challenges for tube forming applications too. This is particularly true for shipbuilding. The high investment in the tooling needed for forming machines has been an unnecessary cost driver – until now.

Engineers at transfluid Maschinenbau GmbH have developed an efficient solution for the tool-independent forming of tube ends with the company’s first CNC rotary forming machine in 2011. With a second, new process the company is now marking a further milestone in forming – without the tooling complexity. Engineers have now developed a new economical process for forming tubes of up to 325mm in diameter – a flaring machine that does not require special tool inserts.

“Benefits include a lower investment in – and stock of – tooling, minimum set-up times and a manufacturing process optimised to the cycle-time because the shape is sometimes achieved in two forming stages – or the flange is fitted to the tube end by welding,” explained the company.

Special, cost-intensive tooling, says the company, has now become a thing of the past. Transfluid specialists also recently designed an intelligent automation system for heater spirals with lateral connection points for tubes with diameters of ½” to 1¼”. The automation system ensures quality and leak-tightness in a rapid 90-second cycle.

## New systems and upgrades

Orbitalum expanded its range for pipe cutting, bevelling/squaring and orbital welding with its new boiler-pipe processing machine, BRB 2 Full Auto. The pneumatically-operated machine complements Orbitalum’s WIG pipe-in-sheet weld heads. It is also economical and ensures high levels of precision and quality when shortening and welding thousands of pipes on heat exchangers. During maintenance and service, defective pipes can easily be “milled out” of the pipe sheet.

Tube specialist Unison recently launched a new generation of its all-electric bending machines featuring mechanical architecture and control software improvements that substantially reduce tubular part fabrication times. The re-design reduces the time required for all the intermediate handling and movement of the tooling configuration tasks that the machine performs before and after a bend, by as much as 40%. As there may be four or five such auxiliary movements for each bending move, as well as changes of tooling dies, these savings can add up to very large productivity gains – particularly for companies fabricating tubular parts with several bends.

The new machine architecture will now be fitted as standard on all of Unison’s all-electric bending machines, which are available for tube/pipe diameters from 16 to 220mm (5/8 to 8.5 inches).

“Higher productivity is important for virtually every tube bending machine user but wherever production is in high volume – such as in the automotive or furniture manufacturing sectors for example – the gains can be very significant” said Unison’s managing director, Alan Pickering.

The UK manufacturer of all-electric tube bending machines has now opened a new facility in Scarborough. Today, around half of Unison’s projects include the development of special features and software for purposes such as integration with CAD systems and to simplify application-specific programming, and/or the provision of machines with integrated peripherals in the form of handling automation, washing machines, cutting machines etc. Unison strongly views its future in this ‘integrated manufacturing solution’ segment of the tube bending machine market.

At EuroBLECH 2012, TRACTO-TECHNIK (TT) displayed innovative technology for bending and processing pipes and tubes; high-precision measuring systems for quality control and reverse engineering as well as intelligent software solutions for pipe shop automation, workflow optimisation and lifecycle management for piping.

“With our new servo-electric CNC multi-radii pipe bending machine, TUBOTRON 25 MR, pipes from 4 to 25mm (1”) OD can be rapidly precision bent. The machine has an exposed and compact bending head and enables production of complex pipe figures at a bending speed of 170°/s,” explained the manufacturer to ISMR. “The multi-level tooling concept enables bending of a pipe with different radii or pipes of different diameter without intermediate tool exchange. Main applications are in aerospace technology as well as in the hydraulic, automotive and furniture industries.”

For pipe end processing, TT showcased the new mobile TUBOFORM A, an axial-forming machine for the production of pipes up to 20mm OD in small- and medium-sized series. The forming tools (clamping jaws and forming punch) are individually manufactured according to the job. The unit is suitable for applications such as high-precision flaring of brake pipes or forming of corrugations for hose connections.

Other manufacturers such as companies in the BLM Group (Adige Spa, BLM SpA and Adige Sys. SpA), Addison McKee, Bendco, TRUMPF etc. also produce a wide range of tube processing equipment and innovations.

## Laser for tube

Last year, The Laser Cutting Company of Sheffield, a subcontractor specialising largely in tubular fabrication, took delivery of the UK’s first TruLaser Tube 7000 tube laser cutting machine. Although this is its fourth tube laser on-site, it was the first from TRUMPF.

“The arrival of the TruLaser Tube 7000 not only brings us up to date, it nearly doubles capacity and fortifies our strategy of acquiring the most efficient machinery available,” says the company’s chairman, Jon Day. “There are three factors that secure business in this market: a competitive price, the right quality and on-time delivery. The fact that the machine can also accommodate open and square section beams and channels also boosts our flexibility.”

The new TruLaser Tube 7000 cuts tubes and profiles with large diameters and wall thicknesses without sacrificing productivity. It can process tubes and profiles up to a diameter of 200mm (optionally 250mm), while wall thicknesses of up to 8mm in mild steel can also be accommodated.

Using a laser for tubular designs enables new designs to be created. For instance, specific recesses in the corners for bent edge joints help to produce rounded corners in a rectangular tube, thus simplifying subsequent manufacturing stages. The forces for subsequent bending are kept low and the weld joints reduced to a minimum. A laser tube cutting machine is used to add these recesses quickly and, more importantly, automatically. And since it operates without contact, the laser also produces no distortions in cutting out the keyhole-shaped holes to attach the supporting fabric.
