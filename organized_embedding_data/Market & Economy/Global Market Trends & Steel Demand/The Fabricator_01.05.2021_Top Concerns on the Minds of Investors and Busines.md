I

n just a few short weeks the concerns that most occupy the minds of investors and businesspeople shifted dramatically. In December of last year, the only thing that seemed to matter was the pandemic and now that has faded to perhaps number four. The top three concerns now are familiar ones: inflation, fiscal policy, and regulation. The expectation now is that inflation will be an issue by the summer and beyond and it is expected that taxes will rise at some point. There have already been waves of new regulations showing up.

The discussion has now shifted to the need to upgrade and repair the national infrastructure and the Biden plan is an ambitious one calling for an additional $2 trillion in spending. The size of the U.S. debt is already far beyond what has been deemed sustainable — now sitting at close to 110% of the U.S. GDP. Debt service is close to $400 billion a year and makes up the fifth largest category of the federal budget (behind Medicare, Medicaid, Social Security, and the Defense budget). If there is to be anything close to the $2 trillion outlined for infrastructure, there will need to be tax hikes to pay for it. Where would these taxes likely appear?

IN DECEMBER OF LAST YEAR, THE ONLY THING THAT SEEMED TO MATTER WAS THE PANDEMIC AND NOW THAT HAS FADED TO PERHAPS NUMBER FOUR.

The conversation has revolved around hiking taxes on corporations and wealthy people. The plan thus far is to hike the corporate rate to 28% from the current 21%. That is less than the 35% that existed before the 2017 tax cuts but the average rate for OECD nations has been 25% and in most of the EU the rate has been 19%. The U.S. would be back to having one of the highest corporate rates in the developed world. Of course, there are hundreds of ways to tax, and every nation sports a complex web of VAT, sales tax, "revenue enhancements", user fees, and the like. Comparing is hard but the bottom line is that taxes will have to rise if the infrastructure ambitions are to be realized.
