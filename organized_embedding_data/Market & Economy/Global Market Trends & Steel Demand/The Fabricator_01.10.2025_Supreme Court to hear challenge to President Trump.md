# Supreme Court to hear challenge to President Trump’s IEEPA tariffs

[TARİH: 01.10.2025 The Fabricator]

Around Washington

As tariffs remain in the news, the administration releases its near- and long-term regulatory priorities

OMAR NASHASHIBI

T

he Supreme Court has agreed to review a major trade case challenging President Donald Trump’s use of the International Emergency Economic Powers Act (IEEPA) to impose tariffs, setting oral arguments for the first week of its November 2025 session, which begins on Nov. 3. Under the court’s scheduling order, opening briefs are due in the coming weeks, ensuring an expedited path to a decision on the scope of presidential authority in trade matters.

The justices took up the case after the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit ruled on Aug. 29, in a 7-4 decision, that IEEPA does not authorize the president to levy tariffs—a power reserved for Congress. That decision upheld a lower court ruling striking down tariffs of 10% to 41% on imports from multiple countries. The appellate court stayed its judgment until Oct. 14, keeping the tariffs in place while the administration sought Supreme Court review.

For businesses, the financial stakes are significant. By mid-August, the government had collected about $72 billion in duties under the challenged tariffs. If the Supreme Court affirms the lower courts, importers are expected to pursue substantial refunds, though the administration could turn to other statutory authorities or seek new congressional authorization to reimpose tariffs.

The case also carries long-term policy implications. A ruling against the administration would mark a clear boundary on presidential use of IEEPA in trade disputes, while a ruling in its favor could expand executive flexibility in future economic confrontations. Notably, the litigation does not affect Section 232 tariffs on steel, aluminum, copper, and automobiles, which the Supreme Court has previously upheld under a different statute.

USTR Opens Comment Period Ahead of 2026 USMCA Review

The Office of the U.S. Trade Representative has launched the public comment process for the upcoming 2026 joint review of the United States-Mexico-Canada Agreement (USMCA). The first review of the trade pact will take place on July 1, 2026, six years after the agreement entered into force.

USTR’s Federal Register notice, published on Sept. 17, invites stakeholders to submit written comments on how the USMCA is working in practice and where improvements may be needed. The agency is seeking input on several key areas, including the overall operation and compliance of the USMCA, the effectiveness of the Competitiveness Committee, and recommendations for forthcoming joint review negotiations. It also seeks feedback on North America’s investment climate, U.S. competitiveness and technological leadership, and strategies for bolstering economic security, including responses to non market economic practices.

Comments and requests to testify are due by Nov. 3, and a public hearing is scheduled for Nov. 17 in Washington, D.C. A rebuttal comment period will follow for one week after the hearing concludes.

Trump Administration Releases 2025 Spring Unified Agenda

On Sept. 4, the Trump administration published the Spring 2025 Unified Agenda of Regulatory and Deregulatory Actions, providing a snapshot of the federal government’s near- and long-term regulatory priorities. The agenda outlines expected timelines for proposed and final rules across multiple agencies that impact manufacturers.

Among the near-term actions, the Environmental Protection Agency expects to issue a final rule in September to repeal the 2009 Greenhouse Gas endangerment finding, the Department of Commerce plans to finalize a rule in November terminating certain Section 232 investigations, and the Department of Defense is preparing an interim final rule on procedures for implementing the National Environmental Policy Act.

Also in 2025, the administration is expected to release an October proposal further clarifying the definition of "waters of the United States"; issue a September final rule adjusting acquisition-related thresholds for inflation; and in December release a proposed rule updating the Occupational Safety and Health Administration’s lockout/tagout standard and propose rulemaking addressing joint employer liability under the Fair Labor Standards Act.

DoD Finalizes CMMC Rule, Extending Cybersecurity Requirements Across Defense Supply Chain

The DoD has issued a final rule amending the Defense Federal Acquisition Regulation Supplement to formally implement the Cybersecurity Maturity Model Certification (CMMC) program. Published on Sept. 10, the rule takes effect Nov. 10 and cements cybersecurity certification as a condition for eligibility on DoD contracts.

Under the new requirements, contractors must hold a current CMMC at the level specified in their contract and maintain it for the duration of performance. The rule also requires contractors to register their CMMC Unique Identifier in the Supplier Performance Risk System and submit annual affirmations of compliance. DoD has signaled that the rollout will be phased in over three years, giving companies time to adjust.

The changes will have a direct impact on subcontractors and manufacturers within the defense industrial base. The rule requires CMMC provisions to flow down to all subcontractors handling Federal Contract Information or Controlled Unclassified Information. Subcontractors unable to demonstrate compliance risk being excluded from awards, while misrepresentation of cybersecurity status could trigger False Claims Act liability.

EPA Proposes to End Greenhouse Gas Reporting Program

The EPA has proposed ending the Greenhouse Gas Reporting Program, a rule that has required large industrial facilities to track and report emissions since 2009.

The program currently applies to facilities that emit 25,000 metric tons or more of carbon dioxide equivalent per year, covering 41 industrial sectors including petroleum refining, chemicals, cement, steel, and power generation. More than 8,000 facilities nationwide are subject to the rule, which requires them to submit detailed data on fuel use, process emissions, and other greenhouse gas sources.

The proposal, released Sept. 16, would eliminate reporting requirements for 46 of the 47 source categories currently covered, leaving only municipal landfills subject to the rule. The EPA estimates the change would save regulated entities $2.4 billion over the next decade in compliance costs. If finalized, facilities would not be required to submit data after the 2024 reporting year, and the 2025 reporting deadline would be extended from March to June 10, 2026.

Industry groups have long criticized the program, arguing that it imposes significant paperwork burdens without directly reducing emissions. However, environmental advocates and some states argue that ending the program would sharply reduce emissions transparency, making it more difficult for companies, investors, and policymakers to track industrial emissions trends.

The proposal is now open for public comment through Nov. 3, with a virtual public hearing scheduled for Oct. 1. Manufacturers and other stakeholders are expected to weigh in as the EPA considers whether to finalize the repeal.
