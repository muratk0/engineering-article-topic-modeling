fter witnessing the most amazing political swing in the history of the U.S., you might be asking yourself, "What does this have to do with the steel industry and manufacturing?"

It is still too early to tell, and it may be months before we get an answer to those questions. However, one thing is clear: The voters in the Rust Belt states, those states that were proud steelmaking and manufacturing centers in years gone by, are waking up today with hope that those industries will return.

President-elect Donald Trump made trade, manufacturing, and the steel industry a cornerstone of his agenda. What changes are actually coming for the industry is not well-known as this article is written.

Foreign trading companies and their U.S. customers are waking up not knowing how difficult it will be for them to bring in steel from foreign countries. The domestic steel industry has done a good job of blocking countries such as China and Russia from "dumping" steel into the U.S. In early November the U.S. Department of Commerce (DOC) issued a number of rulings that supported the domestic steel industry and punished those importing steel into the country. The DOC ruled against cut-to-length plate exports from Austria, Belgium, China, France, Germany, Italy, Japan, Korea, and Taiwan.

Also in early November, the DOC announced that it had initiated an investigation into whether China was attempting to circumvent antidumping (AD) and countervailing (CVD) duties levied against them by sending steel to Vietnam to be converted into either cold-rolled or coated steels, such as galvanized, and then shipping it to the U.S. In the past the process of converting steel substrate from one product designation to another (hot-rolled to cold-rolled or cold-rolled to galvanized) was enough to change the country of origin. This "significant transformation" rule, recognized by the world community, has been in effect since the late 1980s but may soon no longer be accepted by the Commerce Department. This would have far-reaching implications for both trading companies and manufacturers in the U.S.

In our opinion, fabricators and manufacturing companies are going to have more difficulty getting steel, especially those products that the domestic steel industry does not like to produce. The steel-using community can expect to pay higher prices for their steel in November, December, January, and possibly beyond. Steel Prices on the Way Up

In our last installment in

The FABRICATOR

("Flat-rolled steel spot prices keep falling," October 2016, p. 42), we spoke about our surveys and, in particular, about service center flat-rolled steel spot pricing. Over the past eight years, we have begun to recognize that when service center spot prices of flat-rolled steel are falling from month to month or week to week, there comes a point that we call "capitulation." Capitulation is that point when 75 percent of the service centers are reducing prices, which devalues their inventories and cuts into their margins. When we get to the 75 percent level (determined through our surveys that are conducted twice per month), steel distributors are then receptive to supporting price increases from the domestic steel mills.

Capitulation was achieved in October, and price increase announcements from the domestic steel mills followed shortly thereafter. In

Figure 1

, the red bars depict the percentage of steel service centers reducing their spot prices to their customers. As you can see, capitulation was achieved in early October and the domestic mills followed with price increase announcements, which are the black ovals above the graphic.

AK Steel is used as our benchmark for price announcements because it does it publically and publishes the announcements on

www.aksteel.com

in the news section of the website. The most recent survey, conducted the week of Nov. 7, shows a complete reversal of service center spot prices, which are construed as being very supportive of the steel mill price increases.

U.S. Steel made the first price increase announcement on Oct. 21, but Arcelor-Mittal USA followed up with its own announcement, making it the leader in the club house as of Nov. 7. It provided base price guidance to its customers, taking hot-rolled to $520/ton ($26.00/cwt) and cold-rolled and galvanized to $740/ton ($37.00/cwt), plus applicable extras.

From our perspective, which comes from daily contact with buyers and sellers of flat-rolled steel around the country, we see price momentum as being in favor of the domestic steel mills. Our expectation is for it to remain that way for an extended period of time.

Right now several factors are supporting higher prices. Ferrous scrap, which is feedstock used by the domestic steel industry to produce new steel, is higher-priced because of strong scrap exports and better order books at the domestic steel mills. Around the world, inputs used to make steel like iron ore and coking coal are undergoing price increases, which are affecting world prices. In addition, as we mentioned previously, many countries are being blocked from sending hot-rolled, cold-rolled, coated steels, and plate to this country, which creates stronger domestic demand, pushing prices higher.
