# The FAB 40: Fabricators report record levels of demand, but can they find the workers to meet it?

[TARİH: 01.06.2018 The Fabricator]

By Tim Heston

t’s one thing for a $10 million custom or contract metal fabricator to predict a 10 percent sales growth this year; it’s quite another for a fabricator that brought in $314 million. That prediction comes from MEC, the largest on the

FAB 40

list. Most significant is that other large fabricators, BTD and Ironform among them, report similar predictions.

Of course, predictions change all the time. Some projections end up being conservative, others overly optimistic. Thing is, a few significant players blew past their 2017 projections. BTD predicted $191 million in sales last year, but actually earned $197 million. Standard Iron & Wire Works predicted to end 2017 at $75 million in sales and turns out it reached $84 million. Robinson Metal projected to end last year at $62 million, and it reported $65 million. Then there’s MEC, which predicted $301 million in 2017 and ended up at $314 million.

It’s difficult to generalize, considering

FAB 40

participants change over time. But no one could argue that for many companies on this year’s list, the good times continue to roll.

Positive Outlooks Dominate

The FABRICATOR

launched the

FAB 40

in 2010, and since then it has provided a window into the financial performance of the mostly private custom metal fabrication business.

Like in other industry surveys, such as the Purchasing Managers’ Index from the Institute for Supply Management,

The FABRICATOR

keeps the comments that come with

FAB 40

submissions anonymous. But this year more than previous ones, fabricators’ comments were especially glowing.

"We are excited about 2018 and beyond. The year started strong and it’s continuing. The outlook from our customers is also extremely strong."

"We expect to see a seasonal lull in the summer, then double-digit growth in the back five months. [We see] some very strong demand."

"We’re steadily growing with current and new customers, as demand continues to increase in their markets. We continue to add capacity in machines, labor, and space."

"2018 is turning out to be very strong. We added a new company to our portfolio in 2017 and just closed on another acquisition in March 2018."

One thing absent from most comments was what has put the metal industries in the headlines of the broader business press: material prices and tariffs. According to anecdotes and interviews (including some used in the pages that follow), raw material prices are of course a concern, both for steel and, especially, aluminum.

"[Regarding aluminum], the U.S will always be dependent to a significant degree on imports, as the U.S. lacks the raw material for smelting—bauxite. The impact of the steel tariff has been discussed at some length … [but] much less attention has been paid to the impact of higher aluminum prices." So stated Chris Kuehl, economic analyst for the Fabricators & Manufacturers Association Intl.

Thing is, many fabricators seem to be taking the tariffs in stride. Metal prices already had been on the rise, after all; the tariffs just add another wrinkle of complexity. Moreover, everyone has known about the issue for some time. Shops have been strategizing for months.

A greater concern, one that muddies all the rosy predictions, is the one everyone has talked about for years: Can the industry find the people it needs to continue growing?

"The lack of new people entering the field with the needed skills has been a complaint for decades," said Kuehl in a recent newsletter, "and now the issue has reached crisis levels, with baby boomers retiring at a pace of 10,000 per day—close to 3.5 million a year."

During the recession people were available to hire, but they just didn’t have the right skills. Now, with unemployment at record lows and retirements at record highs, fabricators can’t seem to find employees—period. If a fabricator wants to grow, and yet it can’t find enough people to grow organically, a company acquisition may well be the easiest path.

Ryan Raber, MEC’s vice president of sales and marketing, described the problem like this: "We’re still a contract manufacturer, and we need people to get the job done."

Fabricators can’t automate themselves out of this problem. It’s true that automation, both physical and informational, has transformed this business. Software has automated many front-end processes, from quoting to order entry and even scheduling (though the last remains the most challenging to put on pure autopilot). Software streamlines the back-end processes too, including accounts receivables and collections.

For the shop floor, fabricators can buy automated storage and retrieval systems, robotized press brakes and welding systems. But custom fabricators live in a high-product-mix world, and they still need people to move components from one station to the next.

Custom metal fabrication isn’t semiconductor manufacturing, an industry that has skewed the government’s manufacturing productivity statistics for years, with its "blazing pace of technological change," as a recent article in

Quartz

pointed out. "When the computers subsector is aggregated with that of other manufacturing subsectors, the adjustment makes it seem like the whole of manufacturing is making more goods than it actually is."

Indeed, according to the Bureau of Labor Statistics, the metal fabricated products subsector has had less-than-stellar labor productivity, dropping 4 percentage points since 2014, continuing a trend that began in 2011. This perhaps can be traced to the labor crisis and hint at the fact that automation by itself can’t make a fabricator more productive.

So where do fabricators go from here? Unlike other businesses—particularly global corporations—fabricators remain solidly rooted in their communities. You can hold a teleconference and email an idea or a CAD file, but you can’t email a fabricated part. Indeed, as the company profiles on the following pages show, the health of the local community intimately affects the quality of a fabricator’s workforce.

As the

FAB 40

points out, and what many outside manufacturing miss, opportunity in metal fabrication abounds, and this translates into a boost for the local community. The demand is there from customers, even as they face external challenges like rising material prices and tariffs. Fabricators just hope they can hire enough people to meet that demand.
