# More evidence of a modest economic retreat

Fabrinomics | Economic Insights

Economists are notorious for offering a set of excuses regarding their missed forecasts as fast as they offer the forecast, but lately there has been an unusual variety in terms of these prognostications. Many assert the recession is imminent and will be as deep or even deeper than the collapse in 2008. Others cite evidence that the recession will be little more than a slowdown for a quarter or two. One of the more recent versions of this optimistic assertion comes from the Brookings-

Financial Times

tracking index. This is published twice a year, and the latest version was released last month in advance of the meetings of the International Monetary Fund and World Bank.

It notes that economic growth was significantly faster than had been expected in several key nations. There was growth in the U.S., UK, Eurozone, China, Japan, India, and many other nations, both developed and developing. Business and consumer confidence remained higher in all of these nations despite headwinds created by high inflation and interest rates.

These reports have added to the general sense of confusion, and it seems that economic forecasts are more reflective of the personality of the forecaster than the actual performance of the global economy. At the same time that reports from the World Bank are trending in a generally more positive direction, the head of the organization has been all gloom and doom.

Kristalina Georgieva has been asserting the global economy is in the worst shape seen since the 1990s. Meanwhile, Eswar Prasad, senior fellow at Brookings, points out that China is well on the way to recovery and that the U.S. has continued to show solid signs of growth.

Has Inflation Peaked?

Inflation is still nearly double the preferred rate. The preferred measure of inflation is the trimmed mean personal consumption expenditure (TMPCE) index. It is generally more accurate than the more commonly cited consumer price index (CPI), but the data lags by almost three months. By mid-April we finally were getting a sense of the January rate of inflation. It seems that inflation has peaked as many predicted at the end of last year. The last reading had PCE at an annual rate of 5.0%, the lowest since September of 2021 and lower than had been forecast (5.3%). As recently as June of last year it stood at nearly 7.0%.

This is obviously an improvement but still far from the range between 2.0% and 2.5% the Fed would prefer. If one looks at this data alone, it would be simple to assert that rates would continue to rise. The mantra of the central bank is that rates are hiked until something breaks, and then rates are subsequently lowered to fix what was broken. In most years, the measure of whether there has been enough breakage has been the rate of unemployment. Once there has been an impact on the job market with extensive layoffs, the sense is that the economy has slowed enough.

Unfortunately, the job data has been far less reliable than in past years. Two factors have contributed to this. The primary issue is the chronic worker shortage that has affected nearly every business. Employers have been struggling to find appropriate workers for years now, and they are losing the experienced members of their workforce at an accelerating rate. By 2030, the entire boomer generation, 76 million people, will have reached retirement age. There are simply not enough people to replace them, and that makes companies extremely reluctant to lay anyone off – even if there has been enough of a business slowdown to warrant it. The second factor has been the rise of the gig economy. Millions of people are now "quasi-employed" in jobs such as ride sharing and food deliveries. We are not even sure how to count people in these positions as they often assert they are not actually employed.

Primary Metals Forecast

The Armada Strategic Intelligence System (ASIS) now forecasts primary metal industrial production to increase by 10.9% in 2023. This volatile sector is being driven heavily by changes in raw material availability and prices. As a feeder industry into other segments of industrial production, it is heavily driven by activity in other sectors. The new model still shows a stronger midyear recovery period followed by flattening through the end of the year and into 2024.

Global inventories of many commodity metals are still near decade lows. China is reopening after ending zero-COVID policies much quicker than expected. The low global inventories of many commodity metals will put pressure on materials and push prices higher than expected. The model is picking up some of this theme through Q3 of this year and is now showing a much stronger start to 2024.

What’s Next for Fabricated Metals?

The fabricated metal sector is largely dependent on other sectors of manufacturing and, just as we see in those models in the ASIS, this sector is now showing 2023 pressure as well. The sector is expected to now contract by 4.93% in 2023. The first two quarters of the year are showing contraction, falling by a sharper 3.3% and 1.4% respectively. That will be followed by growth in Q3 of 1.2% followed by a softer Q4 with a contraction of 1.4%. The long-range outlook has weakened of late. It’s now expected to dip below the long-term trendline and continue to stay there. The unsettling aspect of the model’s outlook is that it is not yet showing a rebound in the tail-end of the curve. Other models are showing some mild recovery in early 2024, but this particular set of data is not getting enough of a lift to grow. Remember that the accuracy on these models is best over the next six to nine months; the long-range forecasts change considerably as market conditions warrant.

Tightening Credit, Raw Material Issues

Credit tightening continues to be the story in Q1. Bank tightening has accelerated in the last 30 days and across many sectors. The percentage of banks tightening lending standards is the highest since the Great Recession.

Raw material shortages are still on the radar. This is still one of the biggest risks to the outlook, and those risks could accelerate in the second half of 2023. Data still shows accelerating raw material shortages in early February. Global stocks of everything from copper to aluminum, zinc, nickel, and lead are still showing inventories below 10-year levels. Of concern is that this is occurring while countries like China and many parts of Europe are essentially shut down for various reasons.

So, Now What?

Some of the larger forecasting firms are pushing their recession forecasts to the second half of 2023 and are now calling for a shallow recession (in which many industries may not even notice the downturn). Q1 GDP was still trending too strong, with the latest GDPNow trending data from the Atlanta Fed showing growth of 2.5%. Again, some industries are already in a sectoral recession.

What is a business analyst to do with all this? Is the recession coming or not? It is important to note the patterns of economic analysis. Economists react to the data they are presented with, and there is a great deal of it. Every economist seeks out the data they most trust and base their opinions on that data. They’re also well aware that people are more likely to forgive a forecast that was too bleak than one that was too optimistic. If one predicts gloom and the reality is more upbeat, people will be relieved, but an upbeat prediction that turns sour angers people who had been counting on that progress.

At this point, the data seems to suggest that global economic growth has been sustained despite the scourge of higher inflation numbers, a banking crisis that has made the sector more cautious, geopolitical tensions, and the lingering damage from the pandemic year. I suppose the cautious conclusion is that this growth is real enough, but it remains fragile. Many assert the year will be recession-dominated, but I am still mildly optimistic.

I suppose the message is, essentially, don’t panic. There is evidence that central banks have succeeded in getting the slowdown they have been aiming for as they hike rates. At the same time, there is enough pent-up demand and resilience in the economy to keep growth from plummeting into negative territory. Remember the goal of the Fed (and other central banks) is not to trigger recession. They simply want the inflation rates to come down, and unfortunately that often brings a recession along for the ride.
