## MARKETS AND TRENDS

Trade of goods and services as a percentage of GDP has almost tripled in Latin America in the last 25 years. Latin American countries that opened their markets to international competition during the last decade have not been more vulnerable to the global economic crisis. Instead, trade and financial openness have been accompanied by the creation of resilience mechanisms that allow more space to fund counter-cyclical policies. Fiscal and monetary measures, however, should be sustainable and not compromise macroeconomic fundamentals; nor should they reverse the progress made during the past five years in reducing poverty.

Latin American and Caribbean countries have not escaped the global financial crisis, but have weathered the storm more successfully than in past downturns, due to better macroeconomic management, more efficient structural policies and a vigorous business sector. However, important challenges lie ahead for the region.

Although not the largest, Latin America is the fastest growing US regional trade partner, with the exception of Africa, which has had strong export growth based largely on the rise of petroleum prices.

Between 1996 and 2007, total US merchandise trade (exports plus imports) with Latin America grew by 137% compared to 110% for Asia (driven largely by China), 114% for the European Union, 294% for Africa and 120% for the world.

China has invested billions into mining in South America and replaced the United States as Chile’s top trading partner. The rich reserves of copper and iron from South America’s Andean ridge are attractive to China.

## AN EYE ON BRAZIL

Capital investment and domestic consumption are the main driving forces behind Brazil’s rapidly developing economy. Opportunities for technology innovation and an abundance of labour have created favourable economic conditions for foreign direct investment (FDI) in Brazil, especially in the manufacturing sector.

Furthermore, proximity to North American markets and strategic access to untapped South American markets, local demand, skilled labour and increasing R&D opportunities (Brazil is differentiating itself by offering highly skilled engineering and design talent with low cost labour) are helping Brazil to gear up to be the next hot destination for EMS outsourcing. A strong crack down on piracy has also positioned Brazil as a more favourable manufacturing destination.

While its economic growth is outpaced by other countries such as India and China, its potential wealth of opportunities has made Brazil a strong contender for OEM and EMS investment. The Brazilian economy is estimated to grow at 7.3% in 2010.

The Brazilian government is actively encouraging foreign investment. It has also been focusing on improving overall infrastructure. Brazil’s participation in the MERCOSUR, a South American free trade group, is expected to further elevate its strategic position.

The local aerospace supply chain in the

Brazilian market is small (around 350 companies). Major projects in the pipeline include the Embraer project for larger passenger jets (and Embraer’s expansion), a decision on the Air Force multirole fighter jet FX2 and a decision on the Emraer KC-390.

Demand for wireless communication devices and next generation mobile phones has boosted demand and in turn increased the growth of the communication sector in Brazil.

The country is also becoming a hot market for the automotive industry and this trend is expected to grow in the next five years. The demand for automotive electronics is further spurring additional growth.

## STRONG POTENTIAL

Countries such as Brazil, Argentina and Mexico have proved attractive to date for manufacturers. Accessibility to a large base of skilled labour at low costs and tax planning advantages are driving manufacturers in mature markets (such as the US and Europe) to collaborate and partner with manufacturers in markets such as Mexico and Brazil.

In addition, proficiency in design and easier design collaboration, and relative strength in build-to-order environments in these markets is further driving collaborations especially in segments such as home appliances and other products (with large weight-to-value ratios).

“Latin America is a region brimming with energy and promise,” Mr. Strauss-Kahn, managing director of the International Monetary Fund, said earlier this year. “In this post-crisis world, Brazil, Peru and the other emerging economies in the region are well positioned to build on their recent strong macroeconomic track record, and to engage prominently in the global economic policy debate.”

## GOODBYE TO SHEET SKELETONS

The work sequences for modern punching machines are highly sophisticated nowadays: fast, processdependable and often automated. But there are a few exceptions. The sheet skeleton, says TRUMPF, is a risk for process reliability because parts can get caught on it. In addition, unloading the skeleton is a separate work step requiring either the machine operator to move the unwieldy leftovers into a container or use loading and unloading machines. This takes time and uses too much material.

TRUMPF has now introduced an alternative. Skeleton-free processing is a feature that will soon be available on the high-end TruPunch 5000 and the new generation of TruPunch 3000 machines. This innovation will launch at EuroBLECH.

This new method relies fully on common split cuts. But extensive programming is no longer needed to make them because any possible geometric configuration is already programmed into the machine software. This enables the sheet layout to be improved and prepares for the necessary “release strokes”. The parts are initially processed in sequence and then freely punched prior to discharge or removal. Parts or scrap are rotated using a separating tool with a bi-level stripper to facilitate discharge via the chute. The remaining strips are also completely crushed. The processing time per part stays the same.

The new method increases material efficiency by an average of 10 per cent, says TRUMPF, potentially saving thousands of euros since materials represent the majority of part costs in punch processing. A great deal of manufacturing energy is saved as well because less sheet metal is used. The new development meets the overall TRUMPF goal of managing resources as sparingly as possible. The sheet skeleton-free processing strategy also saves space because various peripheral equipment for cutting scrap and its disposal is no longer necessary.
