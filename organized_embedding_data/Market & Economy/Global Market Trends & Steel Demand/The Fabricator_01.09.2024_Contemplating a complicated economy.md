# Contemplating a complicated economy

[TARİH: 01.09.2024 The Fabricator]

Fabrinomics | Economic Insights

The complex aftereffects of a K-shaped recovery

Dr. Chris Kuehl

Is the economy at a transition point? Are we headed for a soft landing, or something worse? Change is afoot, and much of it reflects a complicated broader economy, one that’s great for some and quite challenging for others. What’s behind all this? Read on.

The K Economy

Since 2020 the United States has undergone a K-shaped recovery that has shown a stark difference between those in the upper third of income earners and those in the lower and middle thirds. The upper third is loosely assumed to be those who earn $100,000 and above. Those in the bottom third earn less than $50,000. That leaves the middle in the $50,000 to $100,000 range.

These are admittedly generalizations, as there are all kinds of variations within these categories. The observation has been that those in the upper third are not feeling much of a pinch yet, and their spending levels reflect this. It is their activity that has been propelling the economy—in the U.S. as well as the rest of the world. This has created a situation where the growth numbers look solid but obscure the fact that almost half the population is struggling to make it from paycheck to paycheck.

The impact of this division is starting to manifest in the performance of certain business operations. McDonalds just reported its first drop in global sales since 2020, and there have been similar deteriorations for merchants such as Walmart.

The bottom line is simple enough. The lower two-thirds are reacting negatively to higher and higher rates of inflation, and they are becoming increasingly nervous regarding their level of job security. The rates of unemployment have not yet started to rise, at least in most nations and in most sectors. That doesn’t mean there is no increase in trepidation.

Casual decisions are no longer casual. Prices are high enough to cause some careful consideration of worth. Even as a business asserts that it has only increased by a small margin, there is the same issue that surrounds taxing. One small tax hike is repeated dozens and dozens of times, and these accumulate. If every business is hiking prices (and they are), the net impact on the consumer is significant. It forces choices, and more often than in the past, the decision is to reject spending. Walmart has noted that people are no longer buying anything other than necessities. Even cheap fast-food outlets are no longer that cheap, and people opt for eating at home.

While it is true that inflation rates have fallen to levels that satisfy the central bankers, actual costs remain very high as they surged at the end of the pandemic and have never fallen back. The average consumer is grappling with much higher costs, and that influences their decisions.

Businesses serving the upper third of income earners have seen little sign of distress as those consumers are still spending. But those aimed at the other two thirds are likely to see deteriorating demand. It is tough for most manufacturers, as they have limited contact with the end consumer. If you are making car parts, it will matter whether that part is destined for a Mercedes or a Nissan or a Hyundai. These reactions to inflation also affect the workforce as they have seen their buying power steadily erode. That leads to desires to seek out better paying job opportunities.

What About Inflation?

A central bank raises rates until it breaks something, then lowers them to fix what broke. The central bank is tasked with mutually exclusive goals: maintain full employment through encouraging growth and keeping a lid on inflation that usually accompanies that economic growth. In reality, it is impossible to accomplish both goals at the same time. It is a matter of choosing which threat is the most immediate and urgent.

Central banks (such as the Fed) worry more about inflation than a recession. A recession doesn’t hurt everyone as there are always "recession-proof" industries and there are always companies that do better than their rivals during a downturn. Inflation affects everyone, rich and poor. The low-income population can’t keep pace with the price hikes, but even the wealthy are affected as their money loses value. The central bank will tolerate more recession than it will tolerate inflation.

Put yourself in the shoes of the Federal Reserve members voting on what to do with interest rates. You know that changing rates will not immediately impact the economy; it takes almost 12 months for rate cuts to have an impact. So, they are trying to figure out what the economy looks like in the middle of 2025. They still see wage inflation, and that is the most persistent kind. They also see signs of a slowing economy, and that would suggest a rate cut sooner rather than later.

Employers Gaining the Upper Hand Again?

Has there been a shift in the balance of power? This is not a geopolitical reference; it refers to the relationship between the employee and employer. As the pandemic emerged, there was already a labor shortage looming simply due to retirement. The situation grew more and more serious, and that gave employees an edge they had not had in decades. Suddenly there were few applicants for most jobs, and employers were expected to pay high wages as well as offer a host of incentives to attract the potential worker. At the same time, the workers hired were less qualified and appealing (generally speaking).

Signs of a dramatic reversal are emerging, as there are far fewer offerings from the business community and far more applicants. As recently as 2023, there were only two applying for a job and now the average is 10 to 15. This has meant lower wage offers and much reduced benefits and accommodations. This has been the toughest year for new graduates to find work in the last 10 years.

The employer is regaining control as there are fewer job opportunities listed, and each of these offers are seeing more applicants. People do not have to lose their job to worry about joblessness. Just the suggestion their job might be at risk will start to alter behavior, making them more likely to save or at least limit their purchasing.

Skill Scarcity Persists

Of course, as any hiring manager will tell you, just because the balance of power has shifted to the employer doesn’t mean the skilled worker shortage has magically gone away. The worker shortage has been cascading through the economy in a very negative way for years, and very little has been done to address it.

If anything, those delightful politicians have taken steps to make the problem worse. It is not just me that keeps bringing this issue up—I hear these laments at every meeting I attend, regardless of industry. The problems created by the worker shortage are obvious, and there have been a whole series of reactions, each of which brings their own set of issues.

The first response has been to pay people more so that the necessary employees could be acquired. This means higher labor costs, and often the productivity levels fail to match the higher wages. Those higher wages also mean that prices for the product must increase, and that is why wage inflation has been driving overall inflation.

The second response has been to replace people with machines and technology, and that is not cheap either. Again, does the investment pay off with greater productivity? Given the fact that the latest edition of the Forming and Fabricating Job Shop Consumption Report indicated that FMA respondents were operating at 65% capacity, the answer is no.

Response No. 3 would be increased reliance on workers from elsewhere in the world. That either means more employment of immigrant labor or more outsourcing, and the powers that be are diligently interfering with both. It may well be justified to reduce U.S. dependence on outsourcing, and it makes sense to control immigration, but that means there must be attention to the worker shortage through a massive investment in training and education—not the hesitant and piecemeal approach now practiced.

To address the training deficit will require a wholesale rethinking of the education system. We need massive investment in new trade schools. Right now, there are 12 traditional universities and colleges for every trade school. The secondary schools should reintroduce what used to be referred to as vocational training, as only 6% of secondary schools now have these programs. Companies need to do their own training, but they also need support as they will be employing people who are not contributing to productivity for a year or more. These are most often local political decisions—state government, city government, school boards. The education system no longer seems oriented toward serving its local community, and that

must

change.
