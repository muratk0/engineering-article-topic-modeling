There has certainly been no shortage of bad and worse news over the last several months. To maintain any sense of optimism in the face of all this makes one feel like something of a fantasist. Although the preponderance of the evidence is negative, there have been some little glimmers of hope.

The markets seem to have shrugged the whole thing off and have returned to highs not seen since the outbreak started. The latest editions of the Purchasing Managers’ Index (PMI) and the Credit Managers’ Index (CMI) are thoroughly in growth territory and have regained almost all of what was lost. Housing starts is showing a surge of interest in markets all over the country, and retail numbers have been climbing for the last three months. Not that these gains off set the massive increase in unemployment, the surge in eviction rates and bankruptcies, and the desperate situation that states and cities are in now that they have seen their revenues nearly eliminated. The truly interesting story at this point is why some of these indicators are trending up while so many others are still in the deep doldrums.

In fact, there is not really one explanation for these pockets of good news. This is not due to some massive recovery in the overall economy as most of the general data remains bad. The GDP collapse in the second quarter has been followed by another negative quarter in Q3, and that is the definition of a recession. The jobless numbers are still in double digits. Some of the good news really is good news, and some of these indicators reveal something else.

There is the stock market, for example. In April and May, it fell like a rock and erased all the gains that had been made in the years since the 2008 recession. Now it has recovered most of that loss, and some have tried to assert that this indicates all is now well in the economy. By this time most are well aware that the markets really do not follow the logic of the overall economy and haven’t for years. The factors that are driving the markets right now include a surge in interest from the bargain hunters and contrarians that are attracted by low prices for stocks they believe will recover. Also, a great deal of foreign money is surging into the U.S. markets as the other global markets are in much worse shape. This recovery in the markets is not really a reaction to a strong economy — in many ways, it is a hedge against a weak one.

The rebound in the PMI and CMI may be related. The manufacturers were stunned by the speed of the decline, and in many cases they were left with large inventories they suddenly could not sell. As the lockdown eased a little, they began to sell off some of that inventory while they debated over whether to increase production. It was not clear that demand would sustain additional output, and few had any idea what lockdown decisions would be. By the middle of the summer, that inventory had started to diminish, and it was necessary to resume production. Ideally, additional demand will be developing and these lockdowns will not be reimposed, but these are both key assumptions. If that demand doesn’t keep improving and the lockdowns reappear, the growth noted by the PMI will stall again.

Housing starts are surging by over 22% from last month, and that has provoked widespread enthusiasm, but starts are still down by 11.0% from last year, and this latest increase is likely based on the fact that mortgage rates are at record lows as that tends to attract the new homeowner while the market performance is pulling in the high-end buyer. There is also a lot of shifting as people seek homes farther out into the exurbs and multifamily housing loses some appeal. This may be a long-term trend, but it might also be short-lived. Time will tell.

The bottom line is that these good news reports are fully legitimate and may well signal the economy is more resilient than it has been assumed to be. It is also important to look at each of these reports individually to determine what might be responsible for the movement, and the same caution has to be exerted when looking at the negative news. Everything in the economy has been affected by the pandemic to one degree or another, but this reaction is not the same for every sector.
