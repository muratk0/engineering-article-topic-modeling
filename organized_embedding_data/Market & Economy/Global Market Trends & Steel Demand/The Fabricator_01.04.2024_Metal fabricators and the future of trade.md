# Metal fabricators and the future of trade

[TARİH: 01.04.2024 The Fabricator]

Fabrinomics | Economic Insights

Dr. Chris Kuehl

Global trade is, in essence, a case of whose ox is being gored. Who suffers and who benefits? That depends. Many in the manufacturing community would loudly support massive tariffs on China, India, Mexico, and Luxembourg if it came to that. These foreign exporters have been driving these U.S. producers out for years. On the other hand, others rely on those imports to keep their own costs down, and they know that those high tariffs will be paid by them. The consumer just wants the greatest variety at the lowest cost and could not care less where that product comes from or who made it.

Our Consumer-Centric Economy

Trade always generates controversy, because there will always be winners and losers. The U.S. has long been seen as a champion of trade, but this was never a matter of being altruistic or concerned about the economies of other nations. The U.S. trade policy has long been consumer-centric due to the simple fact that over 70% of the U.S. GDP is linked to consumption.

The goal has been to provide the U.S. consumer with the best and cheapest products and services from around the world. Unfortunately, this meant U.S. companies were unable to compete with these low-cost platforms, and thousands of companies were lost. Now there has been a shift in political attitude, and efforts are underway to rebuild the industrial infrastructure. This generally means blocking imports with tariffs, regulations, and other restrictions.

The cost this time will be carried by the consumer. If all the suggested tariffs and barriers were implemented (and it is unlikely they will be), the U.S. consumer will pay between $10,000 and $15,000 this year for the same goods they purchased in 2023.

The Big Shift

The U.S. has started to shift toward a production-centric trade model that discriminates against imports while bolstering domestic production. This has long been the position of the Democrats, but the Republicans favored more open trade. Now it is also the GOP that has tilted toward protecting U.S. production and manufacturing. This will come at a cost to the consumer.

If all the tariffs and restrictions that have been suggested were actually implemented, consumers would pay between $10,000 and $15,000 more this year for the goods and services they bought last year. This would instantly ramp inflation back to near double-digit levels. It is very unlikely that all these trade restrictions would develop, but every one that does will impact the consumer. It is essentially a choice of favoring the consumer or favoring the producer.

The FMA member and other metal fabricators are caught in the middle of this shift. Many U.S. based manufacturers will welcome the relief provided by limiting foreign imports, but many of these same manufacturers import parts and assemblies. These will get more expensive. The U.S. manufacturer will likely hike prices as their costs are obviously higher than those in these other nations. Experience has revealed that manufacturers do not lower their prices as much as they could, and are content having prices slightly below those that overseas competitors can offer with tariffs. The bottom line is that imports get costly for the consumer as well as for businesses—and that process spikes inflation again.

It is more than apparent that U.S. manufacturers need some sort of assistance if they are to compete with those low-cost platforms in nations such as China, India, Vietnam, and Mexico. In the great scheme of things, it may be more effective to promote training to alleviate the worker shortage or provide incentives to produce rather than resorting to tariffs (taxes) and regulations. These are far more complicated fixes. The bottom line is that tariffs will be used in the coming year to shift industrial policy.

Accusations of Dumping Rise

At the same time, there has been growing concern that China will try to export its current deflation problem on the rest of the world by dumping its products on the global market. There has been a sharp decline in prices for goods in China, and that has exporters seeking some kind of outlet for their production given that the domestic consumer has remained reticent.

The U.S. has warned that any sort of deliberate dumping will trigger a response. The challenge in reacting to dumping is that it is very hard to prove. The definition of dumping is selling at less than the cost of production, but the fact is that production costs are legitimately lower in China than in U.S. or Europe. This is why China emerged as a global manufacturer in the first place. If prices and wages are falling in China, the costs of production fall with them, and that allows Chinese exporters to sell cheaply. That is not dumping. The U.S. will have to make the case that producers are selling at a loss, and that is hard to do.

China in the Year of the Dragon

From the moment Xi Jinping took control of China, he has had a clear goal: reduce dependence on the U.S. China’s ascendance to the position of No. 2 economy in the world was based almost entirely on the fact that China became the key platform for manufacturing consumer goods destined for markets in the U.S. and Europe. This dramatic shift had immense implications for both the U.S. and China. The great exodus of U.S. manufacturing capacity was attributed in no small part to the wave of imports that came from the low-cost production platform in China. It also made China’s economy very dependent on the U.S. market. That constrained the actions of the Chinese leadership as the U.S. always had leverage.

Meanwhile, the level of foreign direct investment in China has fallen to levels not seen since the 1990s. The direct investment liabilities are a gauge of investor interest, and it totaled $33 billion in 2023. That is an 82% decline from 2022 and the weakest level since 1992.

A combination of factors account for this stunning reversal. The Chinese consumer has been moribund, but the bigger issue is that export activity has plummeted as China’s trading partners have become more distant and cautious. China is not the low-cost platform it once was, and that prompted supply chain managers to seek alternatives. Geopolitical tensions have also contributed to the reduction in interest. The fact is that most of China’s growth over the years has been due to western interest in investing and producing in China, and that interest has faded considerably.

As the flow of western investment has dried up, Xi still wants to free his nation from western pressure and influence. He has counted on the development of a consumer base that would replace that dependence on the U.S. and European markets.

So, how is the Chinese consumer doing? The Lunar New Year, launching the lucky Year of the Dragon, has long been an excuse for the Chinese consumer to splurge. It is a time to travel back to ancestral homes, to buy new clothes and gifts for those one visits. The workplaces shut down so that people can observe the holiday, and evidence thus far suggests that the consumer has been doing just that. Retail sales, very slow since the middle of last year, suddenly showed signs of recovery. Still, the majority of this spending has been on travel and eating out. There has not been much evidence of big-ticket purchasing, and the business community has not joined in this splurge.

Overall, the Chinese consumer has been hammered by the lockdown and other factors, and they have not been able to step up. The new approach is to shift Chinese trade emphasis to the developing world. But there’s a problem: Developing nations do not have the consumer power the U.S. has. The average U.S. per-capita income is over $70,000. The average in the less developed nations is less than $15,000, and some nations are far below that. These nations want to produce the same stuff that China produces. Meanwhile, China has tended to behave like the colonialists of old. As a matter of fact, that is how many in these nations refer to them. They take what they want and do little to help these nations develop.

The changes in China have been significant, and they have developed quickly. The nation has been under stress for several years now, and these challenges are piling up. Nobody is talking about China replacing the U.S. as the No. 1 economy these days. The talk now is about whether China manages to pull out of this series of slumps. They are not likely to be overtaken by No. 3, Germany, or No. 4, Japan, but they are slipping further behind the U.S.
