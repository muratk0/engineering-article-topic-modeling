# Growing fatigue from the tariff game

[TARİH: 01.07.2025 The Fabricator]

Fabrinomics | Economic Insights

Other issues get pushed aside as trade dominates the news cycle

Dr. Chris Kuehl

"Tariff fatigue" is a term I have been hearing a lot lately. People are growing weary of the constant coverage and the constant variation in policy. The most common business reaction now is to essentially throw one’s hands in the air while delaying as many decisions as possible.

A bigger problem is other important business and economic issues get pushed aside as everybody becomes preoccupied with the daily gyrations over tariffs and trade. Three of these "other" issues stand out.

The first is the workforce problem, which has not gone away and has actually become more complex. The overall jobless rate remains at near record lows, at 4.2%, but for recent college grads it is at 6.0% and for many majors that percentage is in the double digits.

More than 7 million people are still seeking work, so how is it that the labor shortage is still such a major problem? It is basically the disconnect between what the business community needs and what skills are possessed by potential job seekers. The bottom line is that there may be plenty of people looking for employment, but they are not hirable. Some estimate it takes between 12 and 24 months to train an inexperienced person, and most companies simply do not have that kind of time.

The second issue that has been ignored is infrastructure. In the frenzy to cut spending, there has been an acceleration of neglect: antiquated ports and airports, bad highways, failed power systems, and more. These breakdowns and inadequacies cost the economy tens of billions of dollars every year. Much has been made of China’s advantages in global competition, with its focus on low-cost labor and loose regulations, but the most important advantage has been the staggering investment in first-class transportation support as well as robust financial engagement by the government. The U.S. has been graded D- by the American Society of Civil Engineers—especially as it involves energy and transportation.

The third area of concern involves engagement in the technologies expected to dominate the future. China has engaged in many of them, including electric vehicles, alternative power sources, robotics, aerospace, medical technology, and AI. The U.S. is fast losing much of its "first mover" advantage in these sectors as there has been lukewarm support from the government and caution in the private sector.

In the past year there has been outright opposition to many of these innovations coming from the political powers that be. Given the persistent threat of a workforce crisis, the solution must be technological, and the U.S. has become very reluctant to move away from traditional patterns. When one looks at the predictions circulating around, the U.S. is woefully behind. By 2030 it is expected that one in three Japanese households will have a robot assistant in the home. AI is expected to eliminate millions of entry-level white-collar jobs, and the U.S. is not prepared.

Motivations that Drive Policy

Policymakers have justified tariffs for three reasons. The first is an attempt to recover some of the manufacturing base the U.S. has lost over the last several decades. Manufacturing in the 1960s and 1970s accounted for almost 25% of the nation’s GDP; that share has shrunk to 10%. Imports played a major role in this decline, but it was not the only reason for all the job loss (some 20 million in the 1990s alone). Technology replaced a great many of those industrial jobs, but so did outsourcing and imports.

The thinking now is that restricting imports would allow the recovery of the industrial sector. Restricting imports or making them more expensive will help, but there are many other changes that would need to take place, changes the manufacturer has been asking about for decades: labor force expansion, infrastructure support, assistance in developing R&D, and regulatory and tax relief.

The second motivation that has been offered for the tariffs focuses on national security. The U.S. has become dangerously dependent on other nations for key supplies and resources. Most chips used in our weapons systems come from imports. These usually are supplied by nations allied with the U.S.—but not always. Of course, these chips also support the entire U.S. tech sector, and there have been efforts to stimulate domestic production of these devices.

But the issue goes beyond chipmaking. The U.S. is way behind in developing solar panels, electric vehicles, and rare earth commodities. It is assumed that placing tariffs on products from other nations will encourage the expansion of U.S. production, but that process is complex and slow at best.

The third stated motivation for a tariff policy is that it provides room to negotiate. Trump has a reputation as a dealmaker, and the assumption is that he can use the tariff threat to get want he wants from other nations. Take the issue of drugs coming from Mexico. He threatened high tariffs on Mexico if it did not step up its efforts to halt the flow. The tariff on China is based on demands that China should address the export of chemicals used in the production of fentanyl.

The challenge with this motivation is that it is all based on who has the leverage. Sometimes that is the U.S. and sometimes it isn’t. The U.S. is nearly always on the demand side of this equation, and China is generally on the supply side. The U.S. needs and wants what China and others produce, but these nations need access to the U.S. consumer. The question is, who needs whom the most? To the U.S. business community, the most vexing part of the tariff situation is the constant fluctuation. A tariff threat is issued that would radically change the supply chain, and then a week or two or three later it is rescinded. No business decision can be made under these conditions, so most companies just go into a period of stalling and delaying. Even worse, these tariffs are ephemeral. They have all been created by a stroke of the presidential pen, and they can all be undone the same way. Trump constantly changes his mind, but the bigger worry is what happens when he leaves office. When Biden took over from Trump, he negated 40% of the executive orders signed by Trump, and when Trump came back to power, he undid 40% of Biden’s. No company will invest in producing something if they think they will lose that competitive edge in a few years.

At the end of May came yet another dramatic shift. The U.S. Court of International Trade (established by the Constitution) held that using the International Emergency Economic Powers Act of 1977 was not lawful or justified. This would negate numerous tariff decisions that Trump has made since the start of the year. This triggered immediate appeals and counter appeals. Nobody has a sense of where this goes, but it is clear the legal wrangling will dominate for months. The practical reality is that business has no clear idea what to expect, and that supports paralysis and extremely short-term actions.

Some Historical Context

The tariff debate has become highly political (as has just about everything else). It is hard to assess the actions with any kind of dispassionate analysis. There are some basic points to be aware of. The first is that U.S. companies have been competing in a fundamentally unfair environment for years, and much of this was a deliberate decision by the U.S.

At the end of World War II, the U.S. sought to rebuild the global economy by opening our consumer economy to the world. This would allow recovery in Europe and Asia and the growth of the developing world while providing the U.S. consumer a wide choice of goods. For decades this worked to our benefit, but it also caused the loss of production jobs as U.S. companies struggled to compete. Tariffs are one method designed to return manufacturing to the U.S., but as tactics go, tariffs are clumsy at best and generally easy to dodge and circumvent.

Many other methods can be used to support U.S. export activity and protect domestic producers. Non-tariff barriers include everything from quotas to regulatory barriers that favor domestic operations. These tactics have been used by many developing nations for years and were often referred to as "infant industry" tactics. The idea was to protect the emerging industry until it could compete on its own.

Industries in the U.S. are hardly infant, but they have been pushing uphill for market share against nations that often exploit their advantages (cheap labor, loose regulation, government support). The U.S. has tried to deal with these disadvantages through institutions such as GATT (later the WTO), but these are slow processes at best, and many companies have been seeking quicker solutions.

If support for manufacturing stops with tariffs, the goal of bringing production back to the U.S. will not be met. There are many adjustments that need to be made, and unfortunately, most expect little substantial progress.
